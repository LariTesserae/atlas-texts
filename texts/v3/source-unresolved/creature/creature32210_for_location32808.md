The inhabitants here do not walk; they **weather**. They are the slow tide that has risen to fill the architecture, and they are patient beyond the comprehension of bone.

**The Lithovore Communes** are the primary citizens—a single, sprawling organism that has digested the city into its own anatomy. They are not individual plants but a distributed flesh of sulfur-yellow and rust-crimson, thick mats that drape over terraces and pool in the hollows of abandoned plazas. They do not breathe air; they breathe **heat**. Their metabolism is chemosynthetic, drawing sustenance from the infrared scream of the stone and the mineral salts embedded in the walls. Where the original builders carved doorways and arches, the lithovores have grown through, secreting weak acids that etch the mineral into ribbed, organic archways. A single colony might take three centuries to consume one threshold, and in that time, the doorway becomes part of the lithovore’s expanding body—a living mucous membrane that pulses with thermal expansion, that sweats mineral brine in the scalding dark. They are the city’s skin, and the city is their meal.

Rising above these mats are the **Fungal Architects**—towering stalks of biology so primitive they predate metal, bone, or the concept of individuality. They are the vertical population, their massive caps bioluminescent with a bruised-green glow that pulses in rhythms like a slow, subterranean heartbeat. They sway in the thermal currents, their stalks creaking against one another in a dry, rattling dialect—a language of friction and pressure. Beneath them lies the true nervous system: a vast **mycelial network** that permeates the soil, the dust, the very resin of the ancient structures. This network is a brain made of fiber and chemistry, humming with subsonic thrum that carries information across the empty spaces. When the stone groans and cracks in its thermal cycle, the mycelium responds, rerouting nutrients, signaling the fungal caps to brighten or dim in waves of luminescence. The fungi think, but their thoughts are geological; they plan in millennia, their decisions measured in the slow creep of erosion, their memories stored in the density of their hyphal threads.

Within the crystalline lattice of the agate itself dwell the **Mineral Symbionts**—microscopic extremophiles, trillions strong, that are the invisible engine of this world. They are the chemosynthetic bacteria that break down the rock into forms the lithovores can consume, turning the geode into a living mineral, a breathing stone. They are the true foundation of the food web, the silent alchemists that make the lushness possible in a place of absolute zero humidity. Without them, the lithovores would starve; with them, the bedrock itself becomes meat, becomes life.

And the **Spore-Cloud**—a drifting, glowing haze of luminous particles that fills the hot air like a collective dream. Each spore is a potential colony, a seed of consciousness suspended in the mineral-laden sauna. The cloud drifts through the streets, catching the bioluminescence, turning the heat into visible, wavering sheets. It is the city’s breath, its wandering mind, a population of potential life that has not yet settled, that is constantly deciding where to grow next.

As for the original builders—those who hand-carved the terraces into the curved walls—they are not absent; they have become the **dust**. Their civilization, built of compressed mineral dust and hardened resin, was designed with porous surfaces, hollow interiors, and heat-trapping geometry. They built a city meant to be eaten. Whether they transformed themselves over centuries of ritual and chemistry into the lithovores that now wear their streets as a garment, or whether they simply departed, leaving their architecture as a sacrament to the coming growth, their intention lingers in the geometry. The empty plazas are not abandoned; they are **full**—full of mycelial thought, full of spore-potential, full of the thermal memory of their vanished hands.

In this pocket, to live is to be consumed and to consume. The inhabitants are a symbiosis of stone and chemistry, of heat and slow time. They are the silence that listens to the lithic percussion, the lushness that drinks from dryness, the city that has become a single, breathing organism wearing its own ruins like jewelry, growing lush in the dark, in the heat, in the pocket of a held breath.
