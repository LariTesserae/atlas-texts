They are the pale tide, the wet-bone multitudes that carpet the newborn stone in a living pelt of chitin and mineral crust. There is no single shape among them, only a continuum of hunger and motion, a gradient of forms that rises from the floor to the ceiling in layers of increasing size and strangeness.

At the lowest level, where the walls weep their mineral sweat, the smallest crawl—pinhead things with translucent bodies the color of old teeth and unbleached parchment, clustering in drifts against the limestone like pale snow. They lap at the rock’s exudations with mouthparts that work in a wet, rhythmic scrape, contributing to the susurrus that fills the basin. They are the foundation, the mineral-feeders, consuming the stone’s breath directly.

Above them move the scuttlers. These are the rivers. They flow in dense, near-touching layers across the basalt ridges, a living carpet of segmented backs and twitching antennae, moving with the blind urgency of water finding its level. They possess too many legs, or legs that move too fast to count—a clicking percussion that rises from the floor in waves, a tap-dance of a million feet on wet rock. When disturbed, they do not scatter; they surge, boiling upward in tornadoes of leg and wing that twist through the light shafts, pale bodies flashing like hail in a summer storm.

Then the flyers. They hang from the ceiling in swaying curtains, thousands of them, their wings folded tight as wet paper, dripping with condensation and motion. When they take to the air, they do not buzz; they beat in a synchronized rhythm that vibrates in the sternum, a percussive drone that drowns out the geological groan of the settling earth. They are drawn to the heat rising from the fissures below, swirling in the warm updrafts, their bodies catching the refracted daylight in shifting, normal-gold halos that make the humid air shimmer like heat off summer stone.

But among the swarm move the giants. These are the walking geodes, creatures so encrusted with mineral deposits that their flesh has become indistinguishable from the rock they traverse. They are the size of cottages, slow-moving, their joints grinding with a sound like millstones turning in a deep cellar. Their crystalline hides pulse with a deep, systolic rhythm—visible undulations that suggest a heartbeat measured in minutes rather than seconds, a slow breathing that makes some boulders appear alive. They are ancient even in this newborn cave, or perhaps they burn through their lifespans quickly in the warm, wet dark, growing old in weeks. When they move, the smaller creatures do not flee; they part around them like water around a boulder, then close behind, scavenging the detritus the giants shed—flakes of mineral crust and chitin that fall like dandruff from their grinding joints.

Then there are the others. Not part of the swarm, not prey, but the small presences that move against the current—the builders.

They are slight things, perhaps no taller than the knee-high walls they raise, with skin that has gone the color of old parchment in the mineral light, papery and translucent at the edges. They move with a deliberation that contrasts sharply with the frenetic chaos around them. Where the swarm is noise and weather, they are architecture and intent. They navigate the turbulence by reading the patterns of the swarm’s movement, stepping through the rivers of scuttling bodies as if the creatures were merely water, their bare feet finding purchase on the glistening stone between the pulses of the giants.

They are clothed in the products of their harvest—vests and cloaks woven from the shed chitin of the larger creatures, bleached pale and stiff by the mineral air, giving them the appearance of walking fungus or animate shed skins. Around their waists hang tools: flaked blades of basalt sharp enough to split the humid air, and hammers made from the dense leg-bones of the geode-walkers, hafted with strips of membrane from some vast winged thing. They carry baskets of woven chitin on their backs, already heavy with the pale, drowned bodies of the small crawlers.

Their hands are perpetually busy. They stack flat stones into platforms, creating islands of dryness in the perpetual dew. They raise low walls of slate, knee-high barriers that hold back the tide of the scuttlers, creating enclosures where the swarm pools and thickens, easy to harvest. They descend into the warm pools where the creatures splash and drown in their millions, gathering the drowned in nets. They scratch patterns into the walls with ochre and charcoal—geometric spirals, records of the rock-falls, maps of the light shafts that shift with the weather above. Their work is never finished; a wall raised in the morning is buried by evening beneath a new drift of the pale dead, or washed smooth by the condensation that seeps from the ceiling, or simply claimed by a swarm that rises to cover it like a second skin.

They are farmers of the swarm, in a way. They place their enclosures in the path of the rivers, allowing the creatures to flow in, then seal the exits with flat stones, trapping the harvest. They keep the giants at bay—or perhaps they worship them—leaving offerings of mineral-crusted bone at the feet of the pulsing boulders, hoping to appease the slow, grinding gods that might crush their stone platforms with a single careless step. They exist in the interstices, the pause between the heartbeats of the geode-walkers, the still point in the tornado of wings. They have learned to breathe the thick air, to move in the shimmer of the swarm, to find a kind of silence in the roar.

They are embryonic, these builders—their culture just beginning, their structures crude, their understanding of the cave incomplete. They are the newest thing in a place that is itself newborn, scratching their brief history onto walls that will soon crumble, building a civilization in the cracks of a world still deciding what it wants to be. Together, the swarm and the small ones form a single ecology—a closed loop of consumption and construction, of frenetic life and deliberate stone. The creatures eat the rock and each other; the builders eat the creatures and stack the rock. Both are pale, both are adapted to the wet dark, both are temporary. The cave will continue to collapse, the light will shift, the swarm will evolve or die, and the builders will either adapt or be buried. But for now, in this warm, humid chaos, they live—the billion pale backs and the few small hands, the roar and the scratch of charcoal, the walking geodes and the knee-high walls, all of them breathing the same thick, shimmering air, writing their brief, bright existence into the stone.
