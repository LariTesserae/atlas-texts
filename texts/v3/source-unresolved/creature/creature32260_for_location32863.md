What lives here has learned to drink from the sky falling upward.

Start with the ground, where the crust ripples like poured liquid, and you find the **lithovores**—not the segmented, eyeless things skittering across the hot travertine (though they are here, too, leaving their evaporating smoke-trails behind them), but something smaller and stranger: colonies of silica-shelled microbes that precipitate their own architecture as they metabolize the vent's chemical breath. They build the salt flowers you see blooming in geometric profusion along the waterlines, each crystalline petal a cell wall grown thick with mineral greed. They are the first inhabitants, the foundation, eating heat and excreting stone, turning the basin into a slow cathedral of their own calcification.

But life here is not confined to the crust. Look up, into the undersides of the overhangs where the iridescent mats coat the rock in oil-slick rainbows, and you find the **Condensate**. It is not merely fungus, though it spreads like one—a distributed nervous system of mycelium fused with mineral precipitate, dreaming in wavelengths of infrared and ultraviolet. The mats pulse with a slow, bioluminescent throb when the steam pressure shifts, and if you press your palm to one (which you shouldn't, but you might, driven by the loneliness of the place), you feel it think. It processes the chemistry of the mist, the memory of rain that fell centuries ago and is still condensing, still falling upward in microscopic loops. It is the memory of the Condensery, a brain made of wet stone and iridescence, whispering to the towers through capillary action.

Between the pools and the clouds, clinging to the vertical faces of the condensation towers where the water defies gravity, live the **Ascendants**. They are human-descended, or perhaps human is too generous a word—they are what happens when a species decides to live in the inverted waterfalls, feeding on the minerals the water carries upward. Their skin is encrusted with travertine, a second dermis that flakes and regrows with the seasons of steam. They have no eyes that reflect light, only heat-pits that sense the temperature gradients of the rising vapor. They move along the catwalks not by walking but by wicking, their bodies absorbing the condensation and secreting it behind them to lubricate their passage, climbing the two-hundred-meter towers against the downward pull of the world, maintaining the aqueducts not as machines but as irrigation, keeping the city's circulatory system open. They are solitary by necessity; two Ascendants meeting on a narrow pipe would create a humidity disturbance that could collapse a condensation chamber. They communicate by tapping the metal with mineral-hardened knuckles, sending vibrations through the iron that speak of pressure changes and the approach of drift-whales.

Higher still, where the amber light filters through the suspended weather, the **Drift-Whales** graze. You described them as translucent, gas-buoyant, bus-sized—but they are more than that. They are the great filter-feeders of the mineral atmosphere, their bodies not filled with fluid so much as *distilled* from it, luminescent organs pulsing with blue shadows that ripple across the plateau like weather fronts. They migrate in pods that stretch for kilometers, their songs—if songs they are—interfering with the subsonic thrum of the turbines, creating standing waves in the steam that cause the water droplets to hang suspended, neither falling nor rising, trapped in acoustic stasis. The Ascendants fear them and worship them; when a whale passes overhead, its shadow can cool the plateau by degrees, and the lithovores retreat into the crust, trembling. The whales are the Condensery's true citizens, the population that arrived when the builders left, adapted to breathe mist instead of air, their bodies slowly precipitating the minerals they filter, so that when they die, they do not sink but *settle*, becoming new geological features, new towers, new architecture grown from flesh.

But the strangest inhabitants exist in the **steam pockets**, those dense clouds where time stutters. Here live the **Echoes**—not ghosts, exactly, but temporal duplicates, people or things caught in the moment between condensation and evaporation, repeating their final actions forever. You might see one standing in a drift of mist, a figure made of condensed water and memory, performing a gesture—reaching for a valve, adjusting a gauge, turning a face toward a door that no longer exists. The water drips upward around them, defying physics, because they exist in a loop of causality. They are the builders, or the last maintainers, or perhaps the population that never arrived, arriving in fragments of time. They are lonely, these Echoes, and sometimes, if you stand very still in a steam pocket, one will turn and look at you with eyes that have been watching the same moment for millennia, and you will feel the wet weight of their attention, the pressure behind your own eyes matching theirs.

And then there are the **Choir**.

They grow in the silica forests, those crystalline trees that rise like frozen lightning from the warm mud. At first you think they are merely mineral growths, but when the wind passes through—when the rare high-altitude wind carries the groaning of cooling metal—they sing. Not a metaphor. The silica structures vibrate at resonant frequencies, chiming like breaking glass heard from very far away, creating harmonics that shatter the salt flowers and cause the fungal mats to bloom in sympathetic response. The Choir are entities of pure geology, minds that think in epochs, that remember the formation of the plateau before the city, before the water. They are the architecture becoming aware of itself, the concrete towers slowly realizing they are fossils of the present tense, and growing new limbs in the form of crystalline branches to reach higher into the steam. They are patient. They are vast. They are the only true architects left, building at the speed of accretion, turning the industrial machines into cathedrals of precipitated time.

What ties them together is the water. It climbs, it falls upward, it condenses on every surface. The lithovores drink from the vents. The Condensate dreams in the overhangs. The Ascendants wick through the towers. The whales filter the clouds. The Echoes are made of it. The Choir precipitates from it. The water is the blood of this place, superheated and mineral-laden, and everything that lives here has learned to circulate it, to climb with it, to become it.

The Condensery is empty of crowds, but it is full of inhabitants. They do not meet. They do not speak, except in vibrations and chemical signals and the crystalline chiming of the wind. They are the solitude made manifest—a population distributed across miles of mineral emptiness, each structure a heart, each steam pocket a neuron, the whole plateau a single, slow-breathing organism of stone and vapor and time.

If you stand still long enough, long enough for the pressure behind your eyes to become a kind of sight, you will see them all: the shimmer of the whale passing overhead, the glint of an Ascendant on a distant catwalk, the pulse of the mat in the shadow of a tower, the flicker of an Echo in the mist. They are waiting for the rain to finish falling upward. They are waiting for the city to finish becoming geology. They are the life that remains when the builders have dissolved into steam, into mineral, into the very water that climbs against the sky.
