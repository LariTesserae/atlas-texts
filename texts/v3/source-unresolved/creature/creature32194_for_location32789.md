The first inhabitants arrived with the pouring of the foundation, hitchhikers in the coolant and the aggregate, or perhaps released deliberately as the waters rose to claim the ceramic and alloy. They are the biofilm—a living patina spreading across surfaces too new to have known sediment, thin as breath but tenacious as intent. Where the stone is sharp-edged and pale, the film darkens it to a soft umber or a faint, bioluminescent green. These are chemosynthetic architects, feeding on the minerals leaching from the concrete, on the ozone and metal tang of the water, on the heat bleeding from un-rusted turbines. Near the artificial thermal vents, they glow—not with any memory of sunlight, but with the cold fire of chemical conversion, a constellation of microscopic stars pinning the walls of this young world to the concept of life.

Above them, anchored to the terraces and the skeletal scaffolding, grow the engineered kelp. They are monuments to intention, planted by hands that have either withdrawn or remain hidden within the factory complexes, tending the systems that keep this ocean breathing. Tall, black-green ribbons that sway in currents no natural sea has ever known, they are the only softness in a landscape of knife-edges, the only vertical gardens in a world of horizontal terraces. They are largely ignored by the grazers, as the silence between them suggests, but they are not empty. They host entire neighborhoods: tiny mollusks clinging to their fronds like barnacles on a shipwreck, protozoa drifting in the mucus they secrete, nematodes threading through the holdfasts. They are the world’s lungs, however meager, converting the machine’s exhaust into oxygen in a place where oxygen is a byproduct of industry rather than a gift of atmosphere.

Then there are the crustaceans. Dog-sized, but wrong—their bodies are flattened, armored with plates that click and grind against the metal with a sound like hail on a tin roof, but smaller, rhythmic, a percussion section to the machinery’s bass roar. They are the benthic janitors, the first wave of the recycling economy, the recyclers of rust and coolant. Their shells are unmarked by time because there has been no time—no sediment to wear them smooth, no predator to have cracked them yet, no slow geological patience to soften their edges. They scrape the bacterial mats with mandibles like chisels, their legs—twelve, fourteen, too many to count—finding purchase on the geometric ridges and the crisp concrete seams. They are creatures of vibration; when the great pumps pulse through the planetary plumbing, they feel it through the floor, a heartbeat they have learned to synchronize with, mating in the thermal plumes that rise like invisible rivers, laying eggs in the silt of construction debris that still settles from the making of the world.

The fish come in schools, silver and armored, their scales overlapping like the plates of some ancient warrior, reflecting the diffuse blue-white light in flashes that confuse the eye. They are built for this specific water—dense, mineral-heavy, cold, tasting of ozone. They do not dart or hide; they spiral, synchronized in gyres that cut through the haze, their bodies flashing like coins dropped into a bottomless well. They navigate by the thermal highways, the stratified layers of warm and cold that the artificial vents create, riding the currents that pulse through the vast chambers. They feed on the particulate snow—the microscopic metal flakes, the chemical precipitate, the organic detritus of the filter-feeders and the dead of their own kind. Their voices are high, thin whistles that cut through the roar of the turbines, a language of pressure and pulse, signaling the location of food, of mates, of the next processing node in their endless migration.

Hunters patrol the shadows beneath the structures. The eel-things—segmented, long as a diver’s arm or longer, eyeless or with eyes like black beads sunk deep in translucent flesh—move through the darkness under the factory complexes, through the negative space between the pumping stations. They are not blind; they sense the electrical fields of the machinery, the vibrations of swimming prey, the chemical signatures of fear and of the thermal plumes. They strike in the spaces between heartbeats, between the pulses of the circulation systems, their bodies flexible enough to bend around the sharp angles of the pipes, to coil in the hollows of the un-weathered metal, to wait in the cathedral of emptiness for the silver flash of a school to pass.

And drifting near the outflows, where the processed water gushes back into the vast hollow with force, are the filter-feeders. Translucent, bell-shaped, they are the size of umbrellas or small cars, hanging in the nutrient-rich plumes like living lanterns. They strain the water for the detritus the machines shed—the skin cells of the biofilm, the scales of the dead fish, the fine particulate of industrial output, the chemical snow. They are ghosts, pulsing slowly, their bodies like glass, refracting the artificial light into rainbows that have no sun to cause them. They are the world’s immune system, the cleaners of the machine’s exhaust, converting waste back into biomass, their translucent tissues holding the accumulated heavy metals in suspension, turning poison into flesh.

Suspended between them all, invisible to the eye but thick as soup, is the plankton of the industrial age—a microbial rain of archaea and engineered protists, feeding on the thermal plumes and the chemical leachate, grazing on the bacteria, being grazed in turn by the larval stages of the crustaceans and the young of the fish. They are the drifting particulate made alive, the snow that falls upward in the currents, the base of the food web built not on sunlight but on the exhaust of engines.

These beings live in a world without weather, without seasons, without the softening touch of deep time. They exist in a perpetual noon, a steady chill, a constant roar. They are the children of a closed loop, born into a cathedral of negative space where the only god is the machine, and the machine is kind, providing heat, motion, and the endless rain of nutrients. They are young, all of them, adapted to sharp edges and mineral weight, thriving in the hollow places where the ocean stretches continental and endless, finding in the emptiness not loneliness, but room to spiral, to click, to drift, to hunt—to be, in this freshly made world, the first and only true natives.
