The inhabitants are not a species but a weather system—a distributed, chattering intelligence that moves through the amber gloom in waves of articulated chitin and wet gleam. They are the Resonant, though they have no name for themselves; they are simply the motion that fills the silence between the land’s tectonic sighs and the structures’ subsonic drone.

Each individual is a contradiction: part crustacean, part circuit-board, part something that gestates in the warmth of cooling basalt. Their bodies are armored in plates that shift between pearlescent opal and oxidized copper, segmented by joints that click with the precision of clockwork but flex with the give of sinew. They have too many limbs—six, eight, twelve—each ending in delicate grippers that can adhere to the glassy crust or peel back the crystalline lichen to drink the moisture trapped beneath. Their wings, when they unfold, are membranes of living liquid crystal that beat at frequencies high enough to make the humid air itself shimmer, creating visible standing waves in the moisture-laden atmosphere.

They do not walk so much as flow. Because the air is so thick, so saturated with the breath of the newborn earth, flight is indistinguishable from swimming. They drift and surge through the mist in schools that darken the dim light, their passage leaving ripples in the condensation that hang in the air like disturbed jelly. When they land, they do not settle; they perch on the humming structures—the ribcage half-swallowed by stone, the pulsing dome, the spire that leans against gravity—and plug themselves in, literally and figuratively. Their thoraxes open like dark flowers to reveal ports that match the circuitry of the architecture, and for a moment, creature and structure share a current, a pulse of heat and data, before the creature detaches with a wet *snap* and launches back into the soupy air.

They are the gardeners, yes, but also the immune system, the construction crew, and the nervous system of this place. The sparse vegetation—the single stalks of grass-like sensors, the fungal blooms that drink the mist—does not grow despite them but because of them. The creatures carry spores in specialized chambers, depositing them in the cracks of the cooling stone where the geothermal heat is right. They tend the crystalline lichens, stroking them with antennae that spark with static, harvesting the condensation they collect and feeding it back to the plants in a closed loop of moisture and mineral. They are chemosynthetic farmers, tending an ecology that runs on the heat of the world’s birth rather than the distant, bruised sun.

Their life cycle is measured in the rapid weather of the place. They hatch from eggs laid in the warm glass where the crust is thinnest, emerging as soon as the stone cools enough to support their weight but while it is still soft enough to mold to their bodies. They live fast—hours, perhaps a day—growing, mating in frantic aerial dances that add to the roaring chorus, and dying as the crust shifts beneath them or as the next burst of geothermal steam flashes their pools to vapor. Their corpses do not rot; they calcify, becoming part of the mineral matrix, their metallic shells contributing to the new geology. They are born of the place and they return to it, accelerating its becoming.

They are loud. Not with voices, but with presence. The clicks are their language, a staccato code of pressure and intent. The whir of their wings is a constant, maddening overtone. The wet tearing sound is them feeding—perhaps on each other, perhaps on the fungal blooms, perhaps on the very air, filtering microbes and minerals from the mist. They harmonize with the structures, their biological frequencies locking with the subsonic thrum of the architecture until the whole bounded space vibrates with a single, complex chord—a song of construction, of terraforming, of a world still deciding what it wants to be.

They are the only true residents. The structures are their tools, or their gods, or their exoskeletons extended into architecture. The land is their cradle, still forming around them. And they are the breath, the motion, the hunger that ensures this place does not settle into stone, but remains forever in the act of becoming.
