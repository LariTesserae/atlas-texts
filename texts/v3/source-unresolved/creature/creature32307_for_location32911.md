The inhabitants are the **Still-Ones**—though “inhabitant” suggests a creature that moves through a space, and these do not move. They are the encrusters, the grafted, the crystalline colonies that cling to the alloy floor and the flanks of the frozen engines. They are the only life here, sparse as breath on glass, and they exist in a state that is neither growth nor decay but something more absolute: a *persistence without process*.

They appear, at first, to be mineral formations—pale blue hexagonal plates that overlap like the scales of a fish that has forgotten how to swim, or gray-green crusts of lichen that have learned to etch themselves into the metal. But look closer, and the boundary between geology and biology frays. These are **biomechanical grafts**: patches where organic tissue has been sutured into circuitry, where a tendril of living matter has fused with a coolant pipe or a turbine casing. The flesh is not rotting, not desiccating into leather; it is *suspended*, held in a state of arrested animation by the cryogenic stillness. You can see the faint, arrhythmic pulse of a capillary network beneath translucent skin, beating in time with nothing—not the roar, not the light, not even its own heart. The circuitry embedded within it glows with a dim, internal luminescence, a slow stutter of electricity that matches the frozen blink of the machines’ indicator lights.

They do not drink. There is no water to drink, no soil to root in, no rain to catch. Instead, they **leach**. Microscopic filaments—part hypha, part conductive wire—extend from their crystalline matrices into the fine mineral dust that coats the empty pipes, into the oxide layers flaking from the alloy grating. They chemosynthesize, drawing energy from the non-aqueous chemistry of the place, from the salts and metallic residues that precipitated out long ago and have never been disturbed. The constant, flat light from the luminous ceiling feeds them too, but it feeds without warming, a photosynthesis of pure revelation. They are cold-blooded in a world that has no blood, warm-blooded in a world that has no warmth.

They are spaced with a terrible deliberation. You can walk for minutes—hours, perhaps, in the timelessness of this place—across the grating, past the stacked vertebrae of the great engine, past the conveyor belt frozen mid-load, and see nothing but the pristine metal and the distant, hulking machines. Then, in the deepest shadow cast by a coolant reservoir, you will find one: a cluster of crystalline lichen no larger than a palm, its surface fractal and faintly luminescent, gray-green with veins of pale blue that pulse with a light of their own. It is alone. There are no neighbors within sight. It does not reach toward anything. It simply *is*, a node of arrested life in a network of perfect stasis.

And yet, they are the only witnesses. The fauna level is 0.2—a rounding error, a vacuum—but not quite zero. There are things here that are almost animals. Perhaps they are spores, dormant in the dust, waiting for a water that will never come. Perhaps they are the machines themselves, viewed through a biological lens: the great engines as leviathans with hearts of pistons caught at the apex of their stroke, the pipes as desiccated veins, the roar as the white-noise hurricane of a circulatory system that has reached perfect, screaming equilibrium. The vegetation clings to these leviathans like barnacles on a sleeping whale, or like moss on a statue that breathes. They are parasites and symbiotes both, feeding on the minerals the machines exude, sustained by the light that the machines’ eternal hum somehow generates.

But there are others. In the deepest shadows, where the light falls at an oblique angle and the cold is most absolute, the biomechanical grafts take on a different aspect. Here, the fusion of flesh and circuitry is more complete, more *intentional*. You might see the suggestion of a face pressed against the metal, eyes open but unseeing, mouth slightly parted as if caught mid-scream—but the scream is the roar, the eternal drone that fills the pocket. These are the **Grafted**, perhaps the last workers, perhaps a new species born from the wedding of human intention and industrial material. They do not move. They do not age. They stand—or lie, or cling—in the shadows between the industrial monoliths, their gray-green skin encrusted with crystalline lichen, their veins threaded with fiber-optic cables that carry no signal but the eternal, frozen stutter of the machines. They are sustained by the constant light and the minerals of the constructed earth, existing in a state of perpetual, humming wakefulness. They dream, perhaps, in a language of static and cold. They dream of a time before the stasis, or of a time after, but the dream never changes, because time does not pass here.

They are the garden without pollinators, the factory without workers, the museum without curators. They are the only thing in this pocket-scale universe that is *wild*—not in the sense of untamed nature, but in the sense of being self-sustaining, autonomous, a life form that has achieved a state of perfect, screaming equilibrium. They do not grow, but they accumulate, layer upon layer of mineral secretion, a slow accretion that is the only change in a world where nothing changes. They are young—0.7 on the scale of erosion, pristine, fresh from the manufacturing line—yet they have the weight of eternity. They were placed here, or they grew here, at the exact moment the universe paused at its completion, and they have been witnessing the nothing-change ever since.

Above them, the ceiling glows with flat, honest brightness. Below them, the grating holds firm. Around them, the machines sing their eternal, unvarying song. And they—the crystalline lichens, the biomechanical grafts, the sparse vegetation clinging to the cold surfaces—they simply *are*, in their arrested animation, the only living things in a place where life has achieved its final, frozen form. They are the inhabitants of the pocket: gray-green and pale blue, low and encrusting, spaced so far apart that each one is an island, each one a universe, each one a silent, patient witness to a time that has stopped, but never stopped being loud.
