They are the Pale Chorus, first and foremost—a population of frogs so numerous they seem less like individuals than like a weather system, a biological mist clinging to every dry surface above the waterline. Their skin is translucent as wet parchment, stretched over visible ribcages, their hearts beating in naked, rhythmic syncopation with the drip of condensation from the ceiling. They possess no eyes that reflect the jade gloom; instead, dark, lidless pools absorb the light. But their throats are magnificent—pulsing sacs of membrane that distend and contract with a precision that mimics machinery, though they are purely flesh. They do not croak randomly; they have learned the stone. Each frog occupies a specific harmonic niche in the chamber’s architecture: the lowest drones cluster in the deep center, half-submerged in the black-glass pool, their bodies vibrating with a subsonic hum that makes the water’s surface tremble; the brighter, more staccato trills perch on high ledges near the oculus, tiny and jewel-like, their calls bouncing off the curved limestone to create overtones no single throat could produce. When the rain swells the aqueduct and the water level rises, the frogs shift their positions, migrating up or down the walls to re-tune themselves to the altered resonant frequency of the air column, adjusting their songs so that the chord never resolves, never breaks. They breed in the warm shallows, laying strings of eggs that resemble pearls in the silt; the tadpoles hatch already quivering, their first movements synchronized with the standing waves, as if born into the music.

Above them, in the upper reaches where the light filters down in a diffuse, greenish column, hangs the Parliament of Bats. They are fewer—perhaps a few dozen—but they are the elders, the silent auditors. Their wings, when they unfold, do not merely beat the air; they test it, emit echolocation clicks that ride the same curved surfaces as the frog song, mapping the chamber in three dimensions through returning echoes. They hang in clusters near the oculus, wrapped in their own wings like gray shawls, their respiration slow and deep. When they drop to hunt, they dive not for insects—there are none here—but for the water-striders that skate the central pool, snatching them mid-pattern with a precision that suggests they are conducting an orchestra, cueing the silences between the frog notes. They are the high-frequency shimmer at the edge of hearing, the listeners who remember.

In the deepest part of the basin, where the pool becomes a well of black glass and the floor is a century-thick accumulation of organic silt, the Eels writhe. They are the ancestors, the deep ones, eyeless or nearly so, their skin the color of wet shale and slate. Their bodies move with a sinuous, electric grace that generates the low-frequency drone underlying the entire hymn—a bass note felt in the sternum rather than heard. They feed on the dead: frogs that have sung their final note, bats that have fallen from the clusters, the constant organic rain of skin and scale that settles from above. In consuming the deceased, they stir the silt, releasing nutrients that feed the algae, which in turn feed the frogs, completing a circuit older than memory. They are the memory of the place itself, moving through the accumulated bone and shell of generations, their bodies charged with the mineral electricity of the deep water.

On the surface of the central pool, the Water-Striders move in strict geometric formations. They are the geometers, the mathematicians of the resonance. They skate not randomly but along the hexagonal patterns that ripple across the black glass—standing waves created by subsonic vibrations—their six legs touching the peaks and troughs of the water’s self-listening, reading the invisible architecture of force. They are the rhythm section, their movements creating tiny, percussive splashes that the chamber amplifies into a counterpoint to the frog chorus, a staccato tapping that answers the sustained drone.

Between the striders and the eels drift the Newts, living jewels of orange and jade, slow-moving and deliberate. They are the philosophers of the basin, neither singing nor listening but simply drifting, their presence a visual rest in the cacophony, their bodies absorbing the green light and re-emitting it as a faint, internal glow.

And beneath it all, clinging to the upper ledges where the stone remains dry enough, grows the Velvet—the moss and lichen and algae that form the skin of the place. It is a mineral garden, a velvet nap drinking the condensation that beads on the walls, holding the damp against the limestone. Pale roots dangle from cracks above like nerve fibers, tapping into the moisture, feeding the green world that exists above the floodline. This vegetation is sessile, patient, feeling the tremor of the frogs’ songs through the rock itself, growing in the flow-stones and runnels that spiral against the current in patterns that make the eye water.

But the truest inhabitant, the one that fills every volume and animates every other, is the Water itself. It is not merely a medium but a presence—a weeping, listening, remembering entity that perspires from the stone, that carries the sound waves, that erodes the walls into the spirals and hollows that house the frogs. It enters through the primitive aqueduct, a slow pulse from the spring outside; it exits through the lower sluice, a breath. It is the blood of the place, and in it swim the gametes of the frogs, the spores of the moss, and the dissolved memory of the builders—those hands that knew only stone, antler, and fiber, who are now part of the architecture, their intention lithified into the limestone blocks, still resonating, still singing through the throats of the pale frogs, keeping the perpetual, deafening hymn alive.
