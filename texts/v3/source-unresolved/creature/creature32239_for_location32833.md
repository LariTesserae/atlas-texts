They are the Clickers, though they have no name for themselves, no concept of singularity. They exist as a weather system, a warm-blooded tide that fills the lagoons with a density so complete it obscures the water itself. Billions of them: translucent, many-jointed, eyeless as pearls, their bodies like living glass filled with mineral broth and the pale, pulsing dark of organs that have no need for light. They drift not by choice but by the slow, tidal respiration of the basin, carried on currents that rise from the geothermal pulse below. Where they gather—on the stilted legs of the factories, along the rusted rims of discharge channels, in the shallows where the water laps against the mineral crust—they form a writhing carpet, a silent (save for that susurrus, that soft percussion of chitin clicking against chitin, a sound felt in the sternum rather than heard) undulation that blurs the line between the many and the one. They are a single organism that has forgotten how to die individually.

Each creature is a filter, a living sieve. They possess no eyes, yet they navigate by the gradient of heat, by the chemical signature of the microbial films, by the pressure of the warm flow against their segmented flanks. Their limbs—dozens of jointed, pale appendages—wave constantly, tasting the water, straining the mineral-rich broth that the factories return to the lagoons. They do not hunt; they do not flee. They simply *are*, processing the poison and the nourishment with equal indifference, their metabolisms racing to match the heat, breeding in the discharge channels where the water is hottest, birthing young that are already adults, already dying. Their lifespan is measured in days, in hours at the peak of the thermal surge. When they die, they do not float; they sink, dissolving into the warm mud, their translucent bodies breaking down into the very nutrients that sustain them.

Beneath them, anchoring them, is the Film. The extremophiles. They are the slow inhabitants, the patient ones, the painters. Where the Swarm moves, the Film spreads. It is a bacterial slime, an oily patina that crusts the exposed earth in faint, shimmering colors—white, pink, ochre—the mineral world rendered in living pigment. They do not swim; they colonize. They root in the cracks of the concrete, in the porous leaching of the old infrastructure, in the saturated mud where the shoreline drowns gradually into lagoon. They are the memory of the place, recording the chemistry of the water in their layered mats, turning the corpses of the Clickers into the crust that will, in time, become new ground. They are pre-vegetative life, indifferent to the vegetable kingdom, content to be the foundation upon which the faster, brighter, clicking multitudes dance.

Together, they form a closed breath. The Film feeds the Swarm; the Swarm dies and feeds the Film. There are no plants here to photosynthesize, no green things to bridge the gap between mineral and animal. Instead, there is only this loop: heat rising from the earth, condensing on the pipes, dripping as warm rain, seeping into the lagoons to feed the Film, which explodes into bacterial bloom, which is grazed by the Clickers, whose bodies in turn sink to nourish the Film again. They live in the dim, diffused light of the steam vault, but they do not need it. Their world is one of touch and warmth and chemical signature—a world of heat and flow, of soft clicking and silent, oily color. They are the true citizens of the basin, the ones who have made the hollow bones of the city their reef, their nursery, their grave. When the turbines groan and the steam hisses, it is the sound of their environment breathing. When the condensation drips, it is the rain that waters their invisible gardens. They are the life that persists in the absence of green, the overwhelming, patient, clicking heartbeat of a mineral world.
