They are the **Resonant**—or the **Ten-Year Folk**—though names, like the foundations beneath their feet, refuse to settle. They are a people who have learned to live inside the patience of stone, to make their home in the brief, violent youth of a world that is still deciding whether it wants to be land or sea.

Their bodies have adapted to the upside-down light. In the green-blue twilight that filters up from the luminous depths, their eyes have grown large and dark, catching the phosphorescence of the plankton-world and holding it like small, steady lanterns. Their skin is pale, almost translucent, marked not with ink but with a living patterning that mimics the lichen and moss—biological camouflage against the wet stone, a second skin that breathes with the humidity. They move with the slowness of the landscape itself: a gesture takes three seconds longer than it should, a step settles into the cliff-face with a deliberation that seems to consult the rock before committing. They do not walk so much as *negotiate*, their bare feet reading the temperature of the basalt, the subtle tremor of the cooling obsidian, the places where the mountain is still dreaming itself into shape.

They are **architects of impermanence**. A house in Vorn is a decade’s lease, no more. They build with hand-carved stone and timber lashed with vine-fiber, raising terraces that cling to vertical faces like the roots of the bioluminescent plants beside them. When the mountain shifts—and it shifts constantly, lifting a terrace skyward by inches each year, tilting a staircase into a new angle—they do not resist. The **Stone-Listeners**, the elders who keep the city’s memory, hear the change in the geological hum first: a new frequency in the rain’s song against the cliffs, a shift in the resonance that vibrates through their teeth. Then the people dismantle what has been lifted too high, carry the timber down, and begin again on a fresh face of rock, treating the ground as a temporary agreement, a conversation rather than a foundation.

Their technology is one of **pressure and patience**. They possess no wheels, no forges that burn with metallic hunger. They work copper—rare, precious, hammered flat with stone tools until it rings like a bell—into thin sheets for cisterns and the occasional blade. They hunt the silent, translucent-winged things that nest in the highest crags with stone-tipped spears, moving so slowly that the prey has time to turn and look before the strike falls. They fish the glowing depths with woven reed traps, but they also leave offerings: small discs of worked copper dropped into the warm water for the great serpentine forms that move below with the slowness of the landscape itself, shapes never fully seen, only glimpsed as shadows against the phosphorescent dark.

They are **dense**—1.6 presences per volume, packed into every niche, every ledge, every crevice that offers purchase. They live in layers, houses stacked upon houses like geological strata, connected by suspension bridges that sway in rhythms matching the pulse of the plankton below. Their city breathes: the wooden walkways creak as the timber swells with the constant mist, expanding and contracting like lungs; the clay cisterns fill with the harvested breath of the sea, the mist condensed into drinkable water. At night—if night can be named in a world of perpetual twilight—the settlement glows with the verdant bioluminescence of the plants that grow among them, their leaves pulsing with the same rhythm as the drifting lights below, so that Vorn becomes a second archipelago of green fire, visible from the depths as a constellation of slow, warm stars clinging to the black flanks of the rising mountains.

They are **cultivators of the dark**. They grow fungus in the crevices where no light reaches, harvesting it with stone knives. They tend the broad-leafed plants whose sap pulses with internal light, not as crops but as collaborators, for the plants knit the raw geology into a single organism, their vines thicker than a man’s waist draping between cliffs and holding the city together. The people move through this jungle with familiarity, their skin brushing against fronds that drink directly from the saturated air, sharing the same slow, patient metabolism.

Their language is **subsonic**. Sound here is 1.3—subdued, intimate. They speak in frequencies felt in the teeth rather than heard, harmonizing with the low, resonant tones of the warm rain striking unweathered rock. Their words are few and heavy, weighted with the knowledge that a sentence might take a minute to form, that meaning accumulates like sediment. They have no written script; instead, they write in **arrangement**—the angle of a terrace, the depth of a cistern, the specific pattern of a suspension bridge’s lashings tell the history of a family, a decade, a shifting mountain. To read Vorn is to read a poem in three dimensions, carved from living stone.

They are **children of the mist**. Born into a world where the air is liquid enough to drown in, they learn early to drink the condensation, to harvest the rain that falls constantly, soft and persistent. They know the paradox of their home: that you can thirst in a place where water is the default condition, that the sea is everywhere and yet absent like a desert, that the light comes from below so shadows fall upward and the sky is only a rumor of brightness behind the weight of the warm atmosphere.

They are the first intelligence to arise here, and they know it. They are the **consciousness of the emerging earth**, the witness to the slow birth of mountains. They measure time in geological pulses—a decade is a heartbeat, a century the span in which a cliff-face visibly shifts. When they look at the stone, they do not see permanence; they see a future. They see the ledge that does not yet exist, higher and sharper, where their grandchildren’s grandchildren will stand, looking down at the luminous deep, feeling the mountain rise beneath their feet, continuing the conversation with a world that is still learning how to be solid.

They are the **Vornites**. They are the temporary. They are the song of water on young stone, made flesh, made city, made prayer—clinging to the flanks of a world that has not yet finished being born, and singing to it, patiently, as it rises.
