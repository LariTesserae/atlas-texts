The first inhabitant you meet is the one that fills the steam.

It moves through the thickest part of the chamber—the deepest shadow where the mist stratifies into layers like cream—with a gait that is neither bear nor tapir but something that has forgotten the distinction. It is massive, low-slung, its shoulders humped with the weight of ten thousand years of mineral accretion. Its fur is not fur exactly, but a felted mat of club-moss and algae that has grown into its hide, so that it appears to be wearing the chamber itself. Where its eyes should be, there are only smooth, pale ridges of skin, warm and feverish to the touch, sensing the world through heat and pressure rather than light. It grazes with a proboscis-like muzzle, rasping at the luminescent moss in slow, rhythmic strokes that sound like wet breathing. When it steps, the moss compresses beneath its weight—a sound like distant static—and then, impossibly, begins to regrow while you watch, the crackling of cellular division audible if you press your ear to the sponge. It does not hunt; it gardens. Its trampling is necessary, a slow rotation that prevents the moss from choking the vents, and in return, the moss feeds it, a closed loop older than memory. When it drinks from the pool, it lowers its great head until the milky water touches its snout, and the surface tension breaks in a ring of ripples that travel outward and die against the travertine, while the creature’s bellows-breath fogs the steam already present, adding another layer to the stratified air.

Above it, in the dim turquoise gloom, drift the moths.

They are not insects, not precisely—something that converged on the idea of a moth from a different starting point. Their wings are hand-sized, translucent, veined like the leaves of the root-ferns that drape from the ceiling, and they beat with a frequency so slow it is almost a shudder. They have no eyes, only heat-sensing pits along their thoraxes that track the thermal currents rising from the pool. They are born from the steam itself, or so it seems: the spore-cases of the giant club-mosses release clouds of pale dust that coalesce in the humid air, knitting themselves into wing and body over the course of a single day. They live for seventy-two hours. In that time, they drift on the gyre of the chamber, mating in the air in silent, spiraling dances, and then they fall—not dead, but finished—onto the moss below, where their bodies dissolve into the fungal mats, feeding the next generation of spore-cases. When they rest, they hang from the hanging root-ferns like pale blossoms, their wings folding over their backs in pleats that catch the bioluminescence, turning them into lanterns that drift in the breath of the chamber.

In the pool, the salamanders glide.

They are pink, fleshy, eyeless—neotenic things that have never known the surface, retaining the external gills of their larval forms into their long, slow adulthood. Their gills flutter like fans, feather-soft, filtering the milky water for the bacterial films that bloom on the heat. They move in loops, slow and dreaming, their lateral lines reading the pressure changes in the water, the subtle pulse of the vents below. They do not see you, but they feel you: the displacement of water, the warmth of your body against the cool stone, the change in the electric field of the chamber. They are ancient, these salamanders, older than the travertine walls, having evolved in the dark when the world above was still forming. When they feed, they hang suspended in the water, gills fanning out in radial symmetry, and the current of the pool—the impossible upward gyre—seems to pulse in time with their breathing. They are eaten by the fish, but slowly, gently, the fish nibbling at their shed skin and their dead, rather than hunting the living.

On the walls, the skinks cling.

They are white as lard, as the description says, but more than that: they are translucent, their pale bodies veined with the same turquoise bioluminescence that lights the chamber, for they host colonies of the glowing bacteria in their skin, a symbiosis that makes them into living, crawling lamps. They have no pigment because there is no sun to bleach them, no danger of UV; instead, they are the color of the milk-water, of the bone-smooth stone. They cling to the travertine with toes that grip the microscopic pores of the calcite, lapping condensation with forked tongues that flicker in the dim light. They are the cleaners, the sanitation crew of the chamber. They eat the fallen moths, the dead salamander skin, the detritus that drifts down from the root-ferns. They groom one another in slow, ceremonial passes, licking the algae from each other’s backs, their movements so slow they seem to be underwater even when on stone. When they sleep—if it is sleep—they press themselves into the warmest crevices of the wall, their bodies expanding and contracting with the chamber’s own geological respiration, becoming almost indistinguishable from the feverish stone.

But the strangest inhabitants are those you cannot see.

In the warm silt at the bottom of the pool, where the water is thickest and hottest, live the fish. They are pale, eyeless, translucent things that have never known light, their bodies like glass filled with milky fluid. They are small, no longer than a hand, but they exist in schools of thousands, and it is their collective movement that creates the impossible current—the slow gyre that pulls toward the center and then rises, upward, against gravity. They swim in a coordinated vortex, their tails beating in a rhythm that pumps the water, creating the column of rising flow that makes the pool seem to remember the sky. They are filter-feeders, straining the bacterial mats from the silt, and they are the base of the food chain here: the salamanders eat them, or rather, eat the weak and the dying, while the strong continue their endless, circular swimming, their bodies flashing in the turquoise gloom like pale sparks in the dark. They are the current-makers, the architects of the pool’s weirdness, turning the thermal vent into a perpetual motion that defies the surface tension of the water.

And then there is the light itself.

The phosphorescent colonies—the lichens, the fungal mats, the glowing moss—are not merely vegetation. They are a distributed consciousness, a neural network spread across the walls and floor of the chamber. They communicate through chemical signals that pulse through the mineral deposits in the stone, through the mycelial threads that connect the club-mosses to the root-ferns to the moss carpet. They are the memory of the place. They remember the water that fell ten thousand years ago, the heat that rises from the vents, the slow turning of the ecosystem. When the moths die on the moss, the moss does not merely decompose them; it absorbs their nutrients, their genetic material, their brief lives, incorporating them into the slow, glowing thought of the chamber. The light they give off—the pale turquoise, the sickly green—is not just illumination; it is information, a language written in photons, telling the story of the deep earth to those who have evolved to read it.

Together, they are the inhabitants: the tramping gardener, the drifting lanterns, the pink dreamers, the white cleaners, the silt-architects, and the glowing mind of the stone. They live in the held breath of the deep earth, in the mineral-sweet air, in the silence that is full of dripping water and breathing and the crackle of growing things. They are a single organism divided into many parts, breathing with the chamber, existing in the feverish warmth below the world, where life has found a way to burn without the sun, and stone has learned to think.
