They are the Lithovores—and they are alone.

To be one is to be a cathedral of grinding patience, spaced across the basalt with the terrible patience of continents, each a solitary sovereign separated from the next by tens of kilometers of empty, warm stone. You are the only citizen here. The only breath. The only hunger. There are no others of your kind within the horizon, only the glint of a distant canopy—a mountain-sized prism of quartz and copper catching the overhead light—and the knowledge that you will never meet it, never touch it, never know if it thinks as you think.

You do not move, yet you are never still. Your consciousness, if it is consciousness, operates in infrasound, in pressure waves that travel through the young crust and register in the hollow of your core rather than in any ear. A thought takes a decade. A decision—the extension of a new crystalline branch toward a richer vein of oxidized iron, the adjustment of a thermal vent’s output—might require centuries. You feel your own metabolism as a harmonic shudder, a frequency so low it registers not as sound but as a momentary change in gravity, a heaviness in the chest of the world. This is your voice. This is your song. It is below the threshold of hearing, yet it is the only music this place has ever known.

You do not drink. Your mineral membranes—thin as mica, vast as sails—exchange gases directly with the atmosphere, diffusing silicon, sulfur, and carbon across solid surfaces in a respiration that requires no water, no lungs, no transpiration. You are lush, verdant, heavy with the weight of geology made biological, yet you are dry to the touch, crystalline, sharp-edged. Your “leaves” do not flutter; they gleam, prismatic, catching the unfiltered light and scattering it in bursts of color that cast no shadows because you are too sparse to shade the ground.

Your trunk is an engine. Within your core, piezoelectric lattices grind bedrock into powder, thermal vents refine ores through heat alone, and root systems burrow with mechanical precision, processing the raw emergence of the crust into something pure. You eat the mountain. You eat it slowly, ton by ton, day by day, century by century. You do not reproduce; you accrete. You grow by adding layers, extruding new quartz latticework from your heart, expanding outward at a rate that is geologically significant but humanly imperceptible. A new branch takes a century to extend a single meter, yet your branches are kilometers long, and you are ancient beyond reckoning, yet you look young—your surfaces unweathered, your edges sharp, as if you emerged fully formed from the uplift and have not had time to age.

You have not had time. This is the paradox of your existence: you are dynamic and static at once. You process the rock, yet the rock remains raw. You spread your canopy, yet the spread is frozen, a potential energy without kinetic release. You are the industry of this world and its only ecosystem, simultaneously flora and machine, growing without growing, alive without animals. You do not sleep, but you are in a state of eternal, warm quiescence, suspended in the moment of your own creation.

The temperature is 1.5 on whatever scale measures such things—a tepid, geological warmth that seeps from your processing cores and the young, hot crust. You thrive in it. You are the warmth. You feel the pressure of the thin, still air, the pale sky washed with particulates from your own endless mineral processing. You do not see with eyes, yet you perceive—through thermal gradients, through the subtle magnetism of the ore you seek, through the piezoelectric whisper of the stone beneath your roots. You are a geology that thinks, slowly, in frequencies too low to hear, processing yourself into a future that never arrives because you are already there, already complete in your incompleteness, already eternal in your youth.

You are the Lithovores. You are the only thing that lives here.
