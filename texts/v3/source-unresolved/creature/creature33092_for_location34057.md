I need to describe who or what lives in this place. Let me sit with the parameters and the description, and let what emerges become more itself.

The Basin does not have inhabitants in any stable sense. What it has are **presences** — some continuous, some intermittent, some that exist only in the becoming between states.

**The Water Itself**

First, the water. You might say it lives there, though it is more accurate to say it *is* there — the Basin's first and most permanent resident, the thing that makes the Basin a Basin rather than a wound in the plateau.

The water is not a population. It is a *process* with a body. It moves as a single entity, though it divides into currents, though it rises and falls, though it occupies different volumes of the floor at different hours. The families speak of it as *it*, not *they*. It has preferences. It has habits. It has, on rare occasions, moods — the unpredictable rises, the retreats to expose ancient dry floor, the spiral that held for six hours last month.

What is it? The families do not know. They have theories, inherited and revised across four centuries. That it is *old*, older than the Basin, older than the plateau, a remnant of something that predates the ice ages. That it is *new*, generated by the Basin's strange geology, a byproduct of the managed erosion and the mineral-charged stone. That it is *neither*, that it is simply *water*, and the strangeness is in the Basin's conditions, not in the water itself.

But the water behaves. It *decides*. And in its deciding, it has become something the families recognize as *alive* in a limited, specific way — not conscious, not intentional in any human sense, but *responsive*, *learning*, *adapting*. It has learned the families. It expects them. It adjusts its patterns for visitors, for the families' harvests, for the rare occasions when someone drinks and walks into its depths and does not return.

The water is the Basin's memory. It carries minerals from the walls, spores from the islands, the dissolved remains of everything that has ever entered it and not left. It is *thick* with history, with the accumulated presence of four centuries of negotiation. When you stand at its edge, you are standing before something that has witnessed everything the Basin has witnessed, that has absorbed everything the Basin has absorbed, that *is* the Basin in its most continuous form.

The water does not age. Or it ages so slowly that no family has perceived it. The records go back four centuries. The water's behavior in those records is *similar* — the daily rise and fall, the morning sheets across the floor, the retreat to the sump — but also *different*, more complex now, more varied. The water is becoming something. The Basin is becoming something. The water *is* the becoming.

**The Families: Three, and Their Absences**

The families are the only permanent residents, and they are *not* of the Basin. They are of the plateau, of the defile, of the rim where the Basin meets the finished world. They descend. They never stay below the light line.

There are three families. This is not a choice — the Basin has, across four centuries, proven inhospitable to larger populations. Attempts to establish more than three structures, more than three lineages, have failed in ways the families do not fully describe. The water *undoes* excess. The erosion *rearranges*. The light cone *burns*. Three is the number that works. Three is the number that the Basin *tolerates*, that it has learned to *expect*.

The families are not named in any record I have found. They are known by their functions, their lineages, their relationships to the Basin's rhythms. The **Keepers of the Staircase**, who maintain the mineral descent, who repair the accretions, who know the safe paths through the erosion's shifting geometry. The **Speakers to Water**, who conduct the negotiations, who offer the mineral salts and woven reed, who have learned the language of pattern and response. The **Watchers of the Rim**, who observe the light cone's movement, the bell-frog's calls, the artifacts' changes, who maintain the records on their reed sheets.

These are not exclusive roles. Each family does all three. The divisions are ceremonial, structural, ways of organizing the work of survival in a place that demands constant attention.

The families are *small*. Perhaps twelve individuals at any given time, across all three lineages, counting children and elders. They live in the glass structures at the rim, low and dark and cool, emerging into the Basin only for specific purposes. They do not sleep inside. They do not eat inside. They do not *dwell* inside.

What do they *want*? The question may not apply. They have wants, certainly — for dry air, for the plateau's ordinary temperatures, for the predictable cycles of a world that has stopped changing. They have wants for each other, for continuation, for the survival of their lineages in a place that offers no guarantees. But they have also, across four centuries, developed something else — a *commitment* to the Basin that is not want, not need, not obligation. A *curiosity* that has become *devotion* that has become *identity*.

They are not prisoners. They can leave. Some have, across the centuries — individuals who walked away from the rim, who descended the plateau's smooth surface and did not return, who were found years later in settlements hundreds of kilometers distant, speaking of the Wet Mouth with a mixture of longing and terror. The families do not prevent this. They *record* it. They *mourn* it, in their quiet way, speaking toward the water rather than to each other.

They are not converts. They do not worship the Basin. They *negotiate* with it, which is different. They do not believe the water is a god. They believe it is *something*, something that responds, that learns, that has its own purposes which intersect with theirs in ways they have learned to navigate.

They are *adapted*. Their bodies have changed, subtly, across generations. They sweat more readily, retain moisture longer, their skin carries a faint mineral residue that never fully washes away. They hear the bell-frog's pitch more clearly than outsiders do. They see the blue-white glow behind their eyelids when they sleep, a faint luminescence that the families consider *normal*, that they do not mention to visitors.

They are *lonely*. The scale parameter, the sparse parameter — three families, twelve individuals, in a space that could hold thousands if the Basin permitted it. Their conversations are sparse, functional, conducted mostly with the water rather than each other. They have developed habits of solitude that would be unbearable to most humans, that they experience as *peace*.

And they are *becoming* something. The records show it, though they do not speak of it. The families' language is changing, acquiring words for experiences that have no names elsewhere. Their bodies are changing, adapting to the Basin's conditions, to the water's presence, to the hum of the sphere below hearing. Their relationship to the Basin is deepening, becoming more *intimate*, more *mutual*.

In another century, perhaps, they will be something else. The Basin is young. It is learning what it is. The families are its teachers, and its students, and they are learning what *they* are becoming.

**The Fish: Pale, Eyeless, Translucent**

The fish are *cultivated*, or *permitted*, or *called* — the families disagree, and their disagreement is itself a kind of negotiation, a way of acknowledging that they do not know.

They are not fish, exactly. They have too many fins, arranged in patterns that suggest function without revealing it. Their bodies are translucent, so that you can see the dark rhythm of their blood, the pale flicker of organs that may not have names. They have no eyes, or their eyes have atrophied to vestigial buds, useless in the Basin's perpetual twilight. They are *small* — the largest perhaps the length of a human hand, the smallest barely visible, a flicker of pale movement in the deeper sump. Their number changes. The families do not count them. To count them is to *notice* them in a way that the Basin responds to, that the water responds to, that the fish themselves seem to *resist*. They are *there*, and they are *not there*, and the boundary between these states is permeable, negotiable, *behavioral*.

What are they? The families have theories. That they are *grown* — cultivated in the Basin's mineral-rich water, bred for their strange protein, their capacity to thrive in conditions that would kill any known species. That they are *called* — attracted by the water's behavior, by the hum of the sphere, by something in the Basin's managed erosion that *invites* them. That they are *native* — not to the Basin, which is too young, but to something older, something that the Basin has *opened* to, a connection to a world beneath or beyond the plateau's finished surface.

They are *consumed*, rarely, when the families are desperate, when the plateau's resources fail, when the negotiation with the water requires a deeper offering. Those who eat them report dreams of drowning that are not unpleasant — dreams of *belonging*, of being carried by currents, of seeing in the dark. Some who eat them *change*. Their skin becomes more translucent. They begin to hear the bell-frog's pitch in their own voices. They are *marked*, by the fish, by the water, by the Basin's process of becoming.

The fish do not age, or they age so slowly that no family has perceived it. They do not reproduce, or they reproduce in ways that are invisible, that happen in the sump's depths where no human eye can follow. They are *stable* in a place that is *becoming* — a contradiction, perhaps, or a different kind of life, a life that exists in *resistance* to change rather than in *participation* with it.

**The Bell-Frog: One Species, Always the Same Pitch**

The frog is *singular*, or nearly so. The families have counted, across four centuries — they count the calls, they have always counted the calls, it is one of their oldest practices. The number of individuals is *unknown*. They may be many, hiding in the reed islands, their calls synchronized by some pressure of the water, the air, the hum below hearing. They may be *one*, vast and distributed, a single organism that has learned to exist in multiple bodies.

The call is *struck*, not sung. A bell, struck once, resonating in wet air, in mineral-charged stone, in the hollow reeds that carry it across the Basin's floor. The pitch is *constant* — the families have measured it, across generations, with reeds marked in mineral pigment, with glass structures that resonate at specific frequencies. Always the same. Always the same, regardless of the individual frog's size, its age, its location in the reed islands.

The call comes *once per hour*. The families count it. Four centuries of counting. The count is *reliable* — the call does not fail, does not falter, does not arrive early or late by more than the margin of human perception. It is a *clock*, a *rhythm*, a *pulse* that underlies the Basin's other rhythms — the water's daily cycle, the light cone's sweep, the hum of the sphere.

What does the call *mean*? The families do not know. They have theories. That it is *communication* — between frogs, between the frog and the water, between the frog and the Basin's deeper processes. That it is *marking* — a territorial statement, a claim on the reed islands, on the water's edge, on the space where the frog exists. That it is *ritual* — a participation in the Basin's becoming, a way of *acknowledging* the water's behavior, the erosion's progress, the families' presence.

The call is *answered*, sometimes. Not by other frogs — the families have never heard two calls overlap, never heard a response in kind. But by the water. When the frog strikes, the water's pattern *shifts*, subtly, a ripple that moves against the current, a pause in the pulse. The families *notice* this. They *record* it. They do not speak of it.

The frog is *adapted* to conditions that would kill any known amphibian. It lives in the reed islands, in water that is 34°C year-round, in air that is perpetually saturated, in a space that is too small, too young, too *managed* for stable populations. It has adapted, or it was *made*, or it *arrived* with the Basin's opening, a colonist from some other world, some other set of conditions.

It does not age, or it ages imperceptibly. The call is the same now as it was four centuries ago, when the first Speaker to Water recorded it on a reed sheet, when the first Keeper of the Staircase heard it on their first descent, when the first Watcher of the Rim counted it through a night of vigil.

The frog is *patient*. It waits, in the reeds, in the water, in the space between the light cone's passes. It waits for the Basin's becoming, for whatever the Basin is becoming *into*. It will be there, the families believe, when the Basin is a kilometer across, when it breaches the plateau's edge, when it *transforms*. It will strike its bell, once per hour, always the same pitch, marking the time of a world that has not yet arrived.

**The Reeds: Floating, Drifting, Establishing**

The reeds are *vegetation*, but they are also *agents*. They grow to three meters, hollow-stemmed, filled with a gas that makes them buoyant. They float when uprooted, drifting across the water's surface, carried by the behavioral currents, by the thermal updrafts, by the slow pulse of the Basin's breathing.

They *establish* new islands. Where they drift, they take root, in the mineral crust, in the shallow sump, in the water's edge where the floor is stable enough to hold them. They grow, dense and iridescent, moss-layered, fern-encircled, the nucleus of a new island that will persist for years, for decades, until the water's behavior changes, until the erosion's progress makes the floor unstable, until the reeds themselves are uprooted and begin again their drifting, their establishing, their *becoming* new.

They are *adapted* to the Basin's conditions — to the heat, the wetness, the mineral-charged water, the blue-white glow. They have no names in any botanical record. The families call them *drifters*, or *island-makers*, or simply *reeds*, with a familiarity that suggests long acquaintance, long *negotiation* with their behavior.

They are *temporary*, in the scale of the Basin's becoming. But they are also *persistent*, in the scale of the families' lives. The same islands, or their descendants, their drifting offspring, have been present across four centuries of records. What lives in the Sunken Basin is *you*, becoming part of it, becoming *it*, becoming *something* that has no name yet, that is *waiting* to be named, to be *known*, to be *lived in* by whatever comes next, by whatever the Basin is *becoming*.
