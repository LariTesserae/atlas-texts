The **humans** are few, and they have begun to look like the place looks. They move through the mist in slow arcs, boots sinking into the moss that has claimed the grating, their coveralls perpetually damp, dark with mineral stains and the green-black of chlorophyll they brush against but never quite wash off. They speak in low frequencies, voices flattened by the steam that absorbs their higher tones, so they communicate in gestures—hand signals blurred in the condensation, the tap of a wrench against a pipe flange that travels through the basalt like a heartbeat. They do not live here permanently; they descend in shifts, riding cages down the mountain’s throat, spending twelve hours in the warm dark, then ascending into the cold to sleep in dry air. But they leave traces: a coffee cup calcified with mineral deposits on a catwalk, a half-eaten sandwich wrapped in foil that the moss has begun to digest, the faint, chemical scent of their sweat mixing with the geothermal sulfur. They are temporary, but they are **adapting**—their skin has gone pale as orchid petals, their eyes have learned to see in the milk-diffuse light, and they move with the unhurried patience of creatures who have accepted that they are now part of the weather.

The **drones** are more constant. They are the true residents, the permanent population. They are arachnid in design—eight-legged, low to the catwalks, silent as spiders made of black ceramic and copper wire. They crawl the moss-carpeted grating, their optic sensors glowing amber in the steam, inspecting the welds, scraping mineral crust from the bolts, harvesting the dripping condensation in tiny reservoirs on their backs. They do not sleep. They communicate in pulses of infrared that flicker across the wet walls like fireflies trapped in amber. Occasionally one pauses beside a fern, antennae twitching, as if tasting the air for chemical signatures of decay, and then moves on, leaving a trail of warmth across the cool metal. They are the immune system of the place, the white blood cells patrolling a body that is simultaneously machine and organism.

The **vegetation** has voted with its roots. It does not merely grow here; it *occupies*. The moss is a nation—thick, spongy, pressing up between the grating bars until your boots sink as if into wet bread. It drinks the condensation, the industrial rain, the slow seep from the channeled water, and it grows visibly, overnight, a green tide that threatens to bury the machinery entirely. The ferns are **aggressive** colonizers, unfurling from the joints of pipe flanges with a prehistoric vigor, their fronds brushing against the copper conduits, conducting heat, perhaps even drawing slight electrical currents from the grounding wires, living wires themselves. The orchids are the aristocrats of the steam—pale, waxy, luminous in the diffuse light, their roots dangling like pale fingers into the vents, drinking pure vapor, blooming in the perpetual post-rain mist with flowers that seem carved from the same mineral deposits as the walls, white and ochre and strange. And the vines—thick as a man’s wrist, older than the installation, though nothing here is truly old—drape from the ceiling in curtains of green, dripping with moisture, creating a second canopy that filters the already-filtered light, turning the cathedral into a jungle, the industry into a trellis. They are **digesting** the place. The bolts rust green. The welds weep sap. The machinery does not fight back; it is being absorbed into a slow, patient photosynthesis, becoming substrate.

The **fauna** are refugees, or experiments. A moth circles a lamp—a single, bewildered thing, pale as the mineral crust, its wings beating in the steam until they are heavy with condensation, too wet to fly properly, trapped in the warm air like a soul in a viscous heaven. In the corners, where the obsidian glass meets the welded basalt, spiders have built their webs—not the delicate spirals of gardens, but thick, industrial nets of silk coated in mineral dust, catching not flies but droplets, fat spiders hanging in the center, bodies swollen with humidity, patient as geology. They are the only predators. They wait.

And then there is the **something** in the mist. You see it only in peripheral vision—a shape that moves along the lower catwalks, a salamander perhaps, orange and wet, skittering into a pipe’s shadow, or maybe it is a leak, a pressurized spray of steam that mimics muscle, or maybe it is something new, something the place has begun to breed in its warm, dark womb: a creature of condensation and copper, eyeless, feeling its way through the steam by temperature gradients, a **pre-formation** animal matching the pre-formation rock, evolving in real-time to fill the niche that the altitude and enclosure have left empty. It vanishes before you can be sure it was ever there, leaving only a wet trail on the basalt, a drop of something clear and viscous that might be water, might be ichor, might be the mountain’s own amniotic fluid.

But the deepest inhabitant is the **machine itself**, or rather, the marriage of machine and mountain. The geothermal turbines turn in the dark, not with the scream of industry but with a subsonic thrum that you feel in your sternum—a heartbeat, slow and geologic. The pipes the girth of tree trunks are arteries, pumping heat from the earth’s core, and the earth responds, providing, the rock still **emerging**, extruding new basalt around the bolts, the mineral deposits accreting like scabs, layer upon layer, burying the human interventions in newborn white and ochre. The water cycles through the system—rain inside the roof, collected in ceramic basins, recycled, drinking and being drunk—a closed circulatory system. Everything lives in relation to everything else: the humans tend the drones, the drones tend the plants, the plants tend the water, the water tends the stone, the stone tends the turbines, and the turbines tend the heat that makes it all possible. It is a **symbiosis** of industry and biology, a single organism wearing a human face, a spider’s body, a fern’s hunger, and a mountain’s slow, patient, **held breath**.
