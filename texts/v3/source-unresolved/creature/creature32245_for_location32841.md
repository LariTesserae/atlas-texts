The inhabitants do not walk so much as they *settle*. They are the weight of the place, the 2.7 that presses against your skin not as crowd but as weather, a biological barometer dropping to a threshold where individual bodies lose their boundaries and become a single, breathing, clicking ocean. To stand among them is to be submerged in a tide that never ebbs, only shifts with the patience of tectonic plates.

They emerge from the mist in waves that seem to originate from the architecture itself. Each one is a column of translucence, segmented like the joints of a growing crystal, their bodies composed of the same programmable matter as the towers yet animated, pulsing with a slow peristalsis that pushes them upward through the canyon streets. They are not made of flesh in any familiar sense; they are calcified silk, opalescent chitin, living glass that weeps its own internal humidity. Where they touch the sweating walls of the city, they drink—not with mouths, but through a thousand microscopic pores along their flanks, osmosing the mineral broth, the bacterial film, the zero-point heat radiating from the folded-space conduits. They are filters made animate, sifting the mountain's slow exhalations, their translucent segments glowing faintly with the stored bioluminescence of the vents they have consumed.

Their movement is a choreography of geology. A single creature extends its first segment—delicate as a spider's leg but jointed like a crane's boom—and anchors it to a rivulet of condensation. Then the next segment follows, then the next, a glacial inching that carries them up the vertical faces of the frozen-lightning spires. But they do not move alone. Across the valley, thousands do this simultaneously, their million legs flexing in a synchronized rhythm that creates a standing wave in the supercooled mist, a visible tremor in the air that makes the darkness shiver. It is a language older than sound, conducted through the mineral matrix itself, a vibration that travels through stone and steam and tells each body where to go, when to feed, when to bud.

They have no eyes, yet they see. Their bodies are threaded with filaments that catch the bruised glow of the vents and the bioluminescent pulses of the city, translating faint light into chemical signal, into a slow bloom of understanding that ripples through the collective like a pulse in a nerve. When the smart-matter extrudes a new layer of architecture, when the metropolis grows another embryonic spire from the raw volcanic glass, the creatures know before the mineral has cooled. They arrive at the growing edge like immune cells to a wound, not to heal it, but to colonize it, to seed it with the bacterial mats they carry in their segmented guts, to prepare it for the next generation of their kind. They are the city's maintenance crew, its immune system, its citizens.

They are also the city's memory. When one of them dies—crushed by a shifting of the unfinished bedrock, or simply exhausted by the slow metabolism of centuries—its body does not decay. It calcifies. Its translucent segments fuse with the smart-matter, its legs becoming structural supports, its chitinous plates becoming facade. The city grows over them, incorporates them, and in the wet dark, their shapes can still be seen, ghostly inclusions in the mineral walls, a fossil record of the present tense. New creatures bud from the architecture itself, extruded from the same pores that weep water, born already knowing the taste of stone and the rhythm of the geothermal pulse.

Their sound is the metropolis's heartbeat. It is not merely the roar of dripping water or the hiss of steam, but the wet, resonant clicking of a million segmented bodies scraping against smart-matter, a chittering chorus that never ceases, that rises and falls with the slow breathing of the vents below. It is a sound of eternal repair, of a civilization that does not build and abandon but grows and inhabits, layer upon layer, breath upon slow breath.

To be a citizen here is to exist at the speed of erosion reversed. There is no hurry. A single step might take a year. A thought might take a century. And yet, in the absolute dark, pressed shoulder to translucent shoulder against the sweating towers, they are utterly, perfectly alive—the lithotrophic children of a world that never knew green, thriving in the paradox of altitude and humidity, turning stone into society, and waiting, always waiting, for the mountain to finish deciding its shape.
