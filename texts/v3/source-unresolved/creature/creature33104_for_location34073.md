The people of Varnmass are *quiet*. Not chosen—grown. A person born here learns in infancy that sound carries strangely, that a voice raised in anger or alarm will travel wrong, flat, arriving at the wrong place as something else entirely. The town teaches silence the way other places teach speech. A child who shouts is not punished but *corrected*—brought to the platform's edge, made to listen to how their own voice dissolved into the mist, how it came back wrong, or didn't come back at all.

They are *pale*. Not from lack of sun—the sun is close here, but filtered, scattered, reaching them as warmth without the harshness that burns. Their skin is the color of wet stone, of reed-stem, of the clay they dig. They tan, briefly, in the dry season, then fade again to the gray-green pallor of people who live in moisture, who drink water that carries the minerals of young rock, who breathe air thick enough to leave a film on the skin. A person from the lowlands would find them *sickly*. The people of Varnmass find the lowlanders *loud*, *red*, *dry*—words that are not quite insults, merely observations, the way one might note the color of a stone.

Their bodies have adapted to the terrain. They are *low*—not short, precisely, but compact, built close to the ground. The walkways are narrow, the platforms crowded, the doorways of reed-mats hung low. A tall person hits their head; learns to stoop. The children are born small and grow slowly, their bones knitting in the mineral-rich water, in the reed-seed flour, in the fish they eat when the water offers them. They are *strong* in the way of people who carry loads through shallow water, who pole boats, who cut reed with a blade that must be sharpened daily against the silica in the stems. Their hands are broad, their fingers long and *sensitive*—they can feel the tilt of a platform, the difference between mud that will hold and mud that will swallow, the moment when a post has found stone or will sink forever.

They move *slowly*. Not from laziness—there is no laziness here, the work is constant, the maintenance endless—but from *necessity*. The walkways are hinged, sliding, they give underfoot. The platforms settle at different rates. A person who moves quickly is a person who will stumble, who will find themselves in water unexpectedly, who will be *swallowed*. The town teaches patience. The reed-cutter waits for the sap to withdraw. The potter waits for the clay to reach the right consistency. The builder waits for the wood to season, to tighten its joints, to become *honest*. To rush is to build something that will fail, that will sink, that will be reclaimed by the mud. The people of Varnmass have no word for *hurry*. They have words for *soon*, *after the water drops*, *when the mist burns off*, *in the dry week*—time measured by conditions, not by hours.

They are *many* and *few*. Four hundred, perhaps, but scattered across three hollows, connected by cuts that take an hour to traverse. A person might live their whole life in one hollow, know the platforms in the next flat only by rumor, by the sound of voices carried wrong across the water. The town has no *center*, no gathering place, no market. Trade happens at the margins, where platforms meet walkway, where a person from the clay pits might encounter a reed-cutter, exchange goods without speaking, leave bundles on agreed stones, retreat. The town is a *network* without nodes, connections without meeting. And yet they know each other. The tilt of a platform, the particular creak of a walkway, the rhythm of a pole in water—these identify a person as surely as a face. A mother recognizes her child's footsteps on the reed-mat. A neighbor knows when a platform is settling faster than it should, when its occupants are in danger of being *swallowed*.

Their faces are *weathered*. Not old—the town has no elderly, or few, the work wears people down, the wetness takes its toll, a person who reaches fifty is *remarkable*, talked about, their platform visited by those who wish to learn their techniques, their particular way of reading the mist. But weathered, yes. The moisture marks them. Their hair is *thin*, falling out from the mineral imbalance, the lack of protein, the constant damp against the scalp. They go bald young, or gray, or *both*—patches of white amid the thinning, a coloration that makes them look *older* than they are, that makes a person of thirty appear sixty to lowland eyes. They do not care. Appearance is not *currency* here. To be seen is to be noticed, to be noticed is to be counted, and the town's survival depends on a kind of distributed presence, many small presences, none of them dominant.

They have *no names* for themselves as a people. Not *Varnmass*—that is the place, the hollows, the wetness. They call themselves *the settled*, *the platform-people*, *those who wait for the water to drop*. In the lowland tongue, traders call them *the drowned*, *the mud-folk*, *the silent ones*—names given from outside, from misunderstanding, from the fear that comes from a place where sound behaves strangely, where people move without making noise, where the light is wrong and the colors muted and the silence *complete* at night. The people of Varnmass do not resent these names. They do not *hear* them, mostly. They have no concept of *dignity* as the lowlanders understand it, no *pride* in their difference. They are simply *here*, simply *being*, simply *waiting* for the next rise of the water, the next cutting of the reed, the next firing of the kilns.

Their *religion*, if it can be called that, is *attention*. They do not worship the water—they *live* with it, as one lives with a breathing thing, a presence that must be read, anticipated, respected. The sweating stone is not prayed to; it is *channeled*, *directed*, *used*. The mist is not divine; it is *read*, its thickness and movement and color telling the weather, the mood of the day, the *possibility* of work or rest. There are no temples. The cisterns, the sluices, the aqueducts—these are the sacred spaces, maintained with care, repaired with reverence, for they hold the water that is life and death both, that rises to drown and falls to reveal, that is *always* present, *always* moving, *always* finding its level.

They have *no children*, or few. The town is not *fertile* in the biological sense. The water is marginal, the food uncertain, the work exhausting. A woman who bears a child is *remarkable*, talked about, her platform visited, her methods of survival studied and imitated. The children who are born are *protected*, kept close, taught early the sounds they may make, the movements they may risk, the *patience* they must learn. A child who shouts is corrected. A child who runs is pulled back. A child who does not learn to read the mist, the water, the settling ground—*drowns*, or is abandoned, or simply *fails to thrive*, and the town does not mourn as the lowlanders mourn. The town has no word for *grief*. Loss is *measured*, recorded in the knots of reed-fiber, in the tilt of a platform now vacant, in the silence where a voice once was.

And yet they *persist*. Four hundred, perhaps fewer, perhaps more—the town does not count. They persist because the hollows are here, because the water is near the surface, because the reed grows a meter in a season, because the clay is diggable, because the ridges provide the dry ground for kilns and for the midden-slopes and for the *perspective* that allows them to see themselves, to measure their own slow sinking, their own slow filling, their own *duration* in the young, wet, waiting world. They persist without *hope*, without *progress*, without *story*. They are the town that has no name for itself, no myth of its founding, no narrative of its future. They are simply *here*, in the hollows, on the platforms, in the silence, *waiting*—for the water to rise, for the water to fall, for the mist to burn off, for the day to end, for the night to come, for the slow, imperceptible change that will, in ten thousand years, make this place a meadow, and the town a memory, and the people *gone*—but not yet. Not yet. The water is still near the surface. The reed is still growing. The platforms are still tilting, still settling, still *holding*. And the people of Varnmass are still *here*, still *quiet*, still *waiting*, still *being*.
