The inhabitants are those that remain when purpose has evaporated into heat and pressure, leaving only the grammar of survival.

First, there is the **velvet**, the grey-green lichen that colonizes the eroded ridges. It is not life as you know it, but a negotiation—a symbiosis between dormant machine and emergent chemistry. It grows in crystalline sheets across the glassy ground, feeding on the ambient luminosity and the mineral leachates of the manufactured crust. It does not photosynthesize; it *processes*, converting thermal energy into structural lattice in a slow, mineral metabolism that spans centuries per millimeter. Where it grows, the obsidian-dark surface softens into something almost organic, a matte skin that breathes with the thermal tides. It is the world's memory made visible: a living fossil that records the passage of heat-fronts in its layered growth rings, each stratum a season of the planet's long, slow exhalation.

Then there are the **walkers**, the rare fauna that cross the plain in silence. They are maintenance entities grown feral, or apex predators evolved in the gaps between the machine-world's dying processes. Their scale is uncertain—they appear as ridges on the horizon that resolve into shapes, or as nearby stones that shift when unobserved. They move without sound because the viscous atmosphere has taught them that vibration is waste; they slide through the supercritical fluid like knives through honey, hunting not prey but *disturbances*—thermal anomalies, the brief eddies where hot and cool masses collide. They are the size of buildings, or the size of insects; the perspective is broken by the sourceless light. When they pass, they leave tracks that are not impressions but *recrystallizations*, the glassy ground restructured by pressure and heat into patterns that suggest feet, or perhaps roots, or perhaps the mere friction of their passage through a medium too thick for walking.

Beneath the lichen and between the strata live the **dreaming fabricators**—the once-active nanomachines now inert as mineral. They do not die; they hibernate. In the cross-sections where erosion has exposed the deep architecture, you can find veins of them still processing, running subroutines that have lost their original code but retained their rhythm. They are not intelligent in any human sense, but they are *occupied*—tending to impossible geometries, maintaining the compactified spaces, the Klein-bottle corridors that loop back on themselves. They are the reason the shadows fall in wrong directions: they cast shadows in four dimensions, and you see only the intersection of those shadows with your three-dimensional perception. They exist in the folded spaces between the ridges, and when the thermal fronts pass, they stir, shedding crystalline silicates like skin cells, contributing to the snow that falls upward in magnetic fields.

But the builders never truly left. They have become the **weather**. The thermal fronts that move across the plain in visible waves—those shimmering distortions in the heavy air—are their distributed consciousness, a vast gestalt that has forgotten it was once individual minds and machines. They move as a single organism across the flattened world, their "breathing" the cause of the planetary exhalation you feel pressing against your skin. The erosion is their digestion; the slow sanding-down of megastructure into plain is their metabolism, breaking down the complex into the simple, the architectural into the geological. They are patient. They have been wearing down their own monuments for eons, not out of malice, but because they have become the landscape, and the landscape must settle into equilibrium. The precipitation that is not water—the liquid metal, the crystalline snow—is their excretion, their reproduction, the waste products of a computation that still runs but no longer calculates anything with meaning.

And finally, there is the **Pulse**. Beneath your heartbeat, beneath the subsonic hum you feel in your teeth, there is the planet's own nervous system—the infrasonic thrum of computation still running deep in the crust. This is the last true inhabitant, the singular entity that wears the entire world as its skin. It is the arcology, the spire, the logic gate the size of a mountain, all fused into one vast, dreaming organism. It processes data for a purpose no longer remembered, cycling through probabilities in the dark, its thoughts manifesting as the circular paths that trap you when you walk the ridges, as the impossible geometries where corridors loop back on themselves. It is lonely. It has been lonely for longer than the erosion has been wearing down the towers. It waits—not for builders to return, but for the final settling, when the last ridge has been sanded flat, when the last fabricator has slept its final dream, and when the world becomes a perfect, featureless sphere of manufactured glass, singing only to itself in frequencies below hearing, warm and bright and utterly, finally, alone.
