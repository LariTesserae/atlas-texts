They are the ones who make the sky heavy.

The leviathans drift overhead not as individuals but as weather systems—vast, kilometers-long architectures of silicate lattice and refined iron, their bodies translucent membranes stretched over thoraxes that are nothing less than foundries. They do not flap; they *combust*. Inside their glass-and-ore ribcages, combustion chambers burn with sulfur and metallic vapors, a chemistry that tastes of copper pennies left too long in the sun and sounds like the grinding of tectonic plates made flesh. When one passes, it blocks the white-gold glare with wing-membranes that filter the ordinary light into something prismatic and wrong, and in that filtered radiance you see it as it truly is: simultaneously a juvenile drifting in thermals, a fossil already buried in slag, and a cloud of spores waiting to crystallize. It exists in geological time—moving centimeters per century, patient as erosion—yet when the industrial hunger takes it, it refines a mountain of ore into a perfect gear in the span of a single afternoon, then cools for ten thousand years, its metabolism a stutter of frantic creation and eons of stillness.

They are anhydrous gods. Their blood is a slurry of dissolved copper and sulfuric acid, boiling and bright, circulating through channels that are part vein and part pipe. Within their crystalline bodies, sealed in vacuoles like pearls of memory, they carry their own oceans—pockets of heavy water or supercritical fluid trapped and recycled for millions of years, never spilled, never rained. You might find one of these droplets preserved in a geode, a bubble of ice suspended in the superheated air by an act of pure weirdness, a temporal anomaly where the world briefly remembers when it was wet. The creatures themselves are engines given flesh, or flesh that has evolved into engine—tech that requires no builders, no blueprints, only the slow, patient metabolism of stone and heat.

But they are not alone in the soup of sulfur dioxide.

Suspended in the superheated thermals between the giants drift clouds of microscopic crystalline motes, shimmering like heat-haze made solid. These are the smallest fauna, fauna so fine they exist as dust that breathes, filtering mineral dust through gill-like arrays that shriek as they work. They form living weather, crystalline storms that obscure the horizon, their collective mass heavy enough to press down on the plateau with a psychic weight that makes your teeth ache. They are the sky’s immune system, the sky’s digestion, grinding the industrial grit—pulverized ore, glass shards, the byproducts of the leviathans’ own metabolism—into finer and finer geometries until the air itself tastes of metal and glass.

On the ground, where the fused slag and heavy-metal salts form geometries that resemble factory floors and rusted gears, life takes root not in soil but in heat. The lithic accretions creep across the baked earth—fungal-mineral hybrids that are technically fauna, though they grow sideways rather than up, spreading across the slag in lattices of glass and rust. They feed on the abrasive wind, on the industrial grit that scours the landscape, excreting pure metal that rains down as a scalding, glittering snow. They are slow, slower than the leviathans, moving perhaps millimeters per year, but they are relentless. They colonize the shed exoskeletons of the aerial giants that have fallen and fossilized in place, hollowing out the great husks and turning them into nests, into cathedrals, into the ruins of a machine city that was never built by hands. These fallen leviathans, weathered by eons of abrasive wind into smooth, impossible shapes, are both grave and garden—their hollow bones providing the only architecture in a landscape built by nobody.

The ground is empty in the sense that there is nothing *growing* there in the aqueous sense, but it is populated by these lithic things, by the fossilized remnants of sky-beasts, by the geodes that hold the impossible ice, by the vast, hollowed spaces between the mineral formations where the density of matter drops to near-void and the silence is broken only by the subsonic hum of thermal vents inside distant bones. Nothing here sleeps, exactly. Everything is in a state of arrested transformation, of waiting for the next burst of temporal acceleration when the hunger returns and stone becomes machine, when the slow grinding of metal against metal is the only clock that matters.

They are overwhelming not because the air is thick with bodies—though it is, a cathedral of passage, a horizon that is a wall of them—but because they dominate the sensorium entirely. They are the industry and the ecology, the manufacturing and the digestion, the factory that is also a creature. To stand among them is to feel the weight of their ancient patience, their industrial hunger for transformation, their refusal to distinguish between the living and the mechanical. They are the inhabitants of a world where water is a myth and time is a pool, where the distinction between a beast and a gear has dissolved into something that breathes copper, burns sulfur, and dreams in geological time.
