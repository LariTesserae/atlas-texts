I need to describe who or what lives in Veth-Morai. Let me let this emerge slowly, not rushing to a single image.

---

There is a woman who has lived in the Mistward for sixty-three years, though she does not count them that way. She counts them in *levels*—the high level of the cisterns in the fruiting year of the grey-cap, the low level when the moths came thick and the fog did not lift for eleven days. Her name is Morai, which is also the name of the older settlement below, which is also the name given to her by her mother, who was given it by her mother, who received it from a grandmother who claimed to have heard it from a stone. She does not know if this is true. The stones here are young. They have not been listening long enough to have names for things.

She wakes before the drip. Not before the *sound* of it—that is always present, the percussion of water finding its way down—but before the *pattern* of it changes from night to day. In the night-drip, the water moves slow, almost reluctant, each droplet a small decision. In the day-drip, it moves with purpose, with the slight acceleration of warmth meeting stone, of condensation beginning its work of erosion, of return. Morai knows these differences the way other people know the faces of clocks. She does not need to see the sky. The sky in Veth-Morai is not a thing you see. It is a thing you *feel* against your skin, a pressure, a moisture, a coolness that has no direction because it comes from everywhere.

Her house is in the Mistward, which means it leans. All the houses in the Mistward lean, slightly, toward each other, as if sharing warmth, as if conspiring. Hers leans toward the house of a man who died in the low-level year, three years past, and whose house now belongs to his daughter, who is too young to maintain the leaning, who lets her walls grow straight and true and *wrong*, and who will learn, eventually, that straight walls in the Mistward crack, that they do not breathe with the building, that they fight the damp and lose.

Morai's walls are woven mat over timber over basalt, the basalt laid by hands that had no iron, that used stone to shape stone, that trusted friction and patience. She has added to these walls. Everyone adds. The additions are always upward. Never outward, because outward means into the space between houses, the space that must remain, the space that keeps the city from becoming too warm, too dry, too *tight*. She has added a room for drying moss. She has added a room for the fermentation vats, which must be kept at the exact temperature where the fungi thrive, where they fruit, where they are harvested and dried and bundled and sold to the Dryward, which is too dry for fungi, which buys them because the Dryward has forgotten, or never knew, how to make things from damp.

She is a *moss-warden*, though the title means less than it sounds. She does not own the moss. No one owns the moss. It grows on every north-facing wall, every shaded stair, every surface that holds moisture and does not shed it. She harvests it. She knows which walls produce which moss—the soft, pale, slow-growing kind that makes the best insulation; the thick, dark, rapid kind that burns long and smoky; the rare, almost luminous kind that grows only where warm stone meets cold air, where condensation is heaviest, where the wall itself seems to sweat. She knows these walls. She has named them, though she would not tell you the names. They are not for telling.

The moss-wardens are a guild, or something like one. They do not meet. They do not need to. The moss tells them what the others are doing. A wall that has been harvested too heavily grows back slowly, grudgingly. A wall that has been harvested at the right time, in the right season, in the right phase of the cistern-level, grows back lush, *grateful*. The moss-wardens read this gratitude. They adjust their harvests. They communicate through the moss itself, through the slow language of growth and recovery, through the economy of damp that they have learned to speak.

---

There is a child in the Dripward. The child has no name that anyone uses. The child is called *Small*, or *the Small one*, or *the one who does not go up*, because the child has never been to the Mistward, never been to the Dryward, never been higher than the floating platform where the house stands, where the house rises and falls with the pooling, where the stilts sink into mud or stand on stone depending on the level, depending on the season, depending on the *breathing* of the place.

The child was born in the Rising. This is known because the mother remembers—the water climbing the walls, the gutters flowing upward, the child emerging into a world that was momentarily *wrong*, momentarily inverted, momentarily showing the rules that usually hide beneath the surface. The mother did not see it clearly. She was, as mothers are, occupied with other things. But she felt it—the air moving against her skin, the droplets on her arms traveling upward, toward her shoulder, toward her face, *toward the ceiling, which was also, in that moment, a floor*. She remembers this. She tells the child, sometimes, when the child asks why the Dripward is the Dripward, why the houses float, why no one builds solid foundations here.

The child does not understand. The child has never known a house that did not float. The child has never known water that did not move in both directions, given the right hour, given the right pressure, given the accumulated breath of a city that has nowhere else to go. The child plays in the gutters. The child catches condensation frogs, pale and voiceless, and holds them until they warm in the hand, until they *thirst*, until they must be released. The child has learned not to be frightened by the Rising. The child was born into it. The Rising is, to the child, simply *night*, simply the time when the water goes up, when the platform creaks differently, when the mist is thick enough to swim in, to breathe, to *become*.

The child will grow up, if the equilibrium holds, as all children in Veth-Morai grow up—learning that the world is stable, that stability is not the same as sameness, that the damp is not a problem to be solved but a condition to be inhabited, a *medium* like water for fish, like air for lungs, like time for memory. The child will learn that the Rising passes. The child will learn, as all children in the Dripward learn, not to be in the gutters at the wrong hour, not to be where the water goes up when the breath of the city exhales, when the pressure differential builds, when the accumulated moisture of centuries finds its moment of inversion.

But the child will remember. Children always remember. The child will carry the Rising into adulthood, into the work of managing water, of maintaining the balance, of being a *water-warden*, which is another guild, another non-guild, another set of people who communicate through the cisterns, through the levels, through the slow rise and fall that is the only weather that matters.

---

There are the *old ones*. This is not a title they use for themselves. It is what the young call them, when they speak of them at all, which is rarely. The old ones live in the Dryward, or in the highest parts of the Mistward, where the wind scours and the stone is merely cool, merely patient, merely *waiting*. They have lived long enough to remember when the terraces were different, when the Dripward extended lower, when the Mistward reached higher, when the Dryward was smaller, was *provisional*, was the edge of settlement before the equilibrium was understood, before the rules were learned, before the city knew how to breathe without tipping into too dry or too wet.

They remember the changes, which were not changes in the human sense—no wars, no fires, no plagues that swept through and altered the population—but changes in the *relationship* between the city and the damp. The old ones remember when the moss grew differently, when the fungi fruited at different times, when the condensation frogs were louder, or quieter, or *different*, some species that has since gone, or changed, or become so rare that only the old ones remember its particular silence, its particular presence on warm stones.

They are not historians. They do not write things down. The written word in Veth-Morai is precious, is used for trade records, for the accounting of moss and frog-leather and high-altitude water, for the contracts that bind the wards to each other. The old ones speak. They speak to each other, in the Dryward's thick-walled houses, in the small windows that keep the damp out, in the warmth that is *managed*, that is earned, that is precious because it must be fought for against the cool that presses always from outside.

What they speak of is the *texture* of the place. The way the mist feels different now, slightly, imperceptibly, a change that has accumulated over decades, over a lifetime, a change that no single person can name but that all the old ones can feel, can *taste* in the air, can hear in the drip of water that has changed its rhythm, its *pace*, its relationship to stone and timber and skin.

They speak of children who were born in Risings, who carry the inversion in their bodies, in their dreams, in their *way of moving through the world*. They speak of the cave bears, which have grown smaller, paler, *tamer*, over the generations, as the city has learned to placate them, to feed them, to make them *neighbors* rather than threats. They speak of the bog deer, which have grown bolder, which descend lower now, which must be diverted by militias that are more *procession* than *force*, that guide rather than drive, that understand that the deer are following the condensation, following the moisture, following the *logic* of the place that they do not question.

They speak, sometimes, of the Rising. Not as children speak of it—as wonder, as play, as *night*—but as something that has happened to them, that they have survived, that they carry in their joints, in the way their bones ache when the pressure changes, when the breath of the city builds, when the water prepares to climb. They speak of it as a *visitation*. As something that comes. As something that will come again, because the equilibrium is not a given but an achievement, a constant negotiation, a balance that must be *maintained* against forces that would overwhelm it.

The old ones do not fear the Rising. They have seen it too many times. But they *respect* it. They leave the Dripward at the wrong hours. They sleep with ropes, with weights, with the resigned acceptance of people who have learned that the world contains rules they have not yet mapped, that the map is always incomplete, that the territory always exceeds the map.

---

And there is the city itself. The city as *inhabitant*. The city as the thing that lives in the terraces, that breathes through the cisterns, that dreams in the condensation, that *wakes* in the Rising, that returns to its equilibrium by morning, exhausted, satisfied, *continuing*.

The city is not a metaphor. It is not "the people who live here." It is the *built thing*, the layered construction, the accumulated weight of basalt and timber and woven mat and clay render and lime plaster, the network of gutters and sluices and aqueducts of hollowed log, the circulatory system that moves water from roof to cistern to gutter to sump to evaporation, the *breath* that condenses on every surface, that pools in every low place, that climbs, occasionally, *upward*, against the grain, against the simple physics that the builders constructed their lives around.

The city lives in the damp. The damp lives in the city. They have grown together, layer by layer, generation by generation, until the distinction between *settlement* and *condition* has become meaningless, until the city is not *in* the damp but *of* it, *for* it, a solution to the problem of moisture and cool and young stone, a *way* of being wet that has learned to persist, to replicate, to continue.

The city does not change. This is its strangeness, its gift, its *curse*. The terraces have been built and rebuilt, but each rebuilding reproduces the last, because the conditions that made the first building necessary have not altered. The condensation is constant. The temperature is constant. The young mountains erode too slowly to matter on any human timescale—they will be here when the last human memory of their sharpness has faded, still sharp, still young, still eroding.

And yet the city *breathes*. In the deep hours, when the mist is thickest, when the condensation frogs are silent and the stone lizards have withdrawn to their warm cracks, the city exhales. The water in the cisterns moves against the gradient. The gutters flow uphill. The damp on the walls gathers into droplets that travel *up*, defying the simple physics, revealing the *other* physics, the physics of pressure and temperature differential and accumulated breath, the physics that the builders have constructed their lives around but have never fully mapped.

This is what lives in Veth-Morai. Not just the people, the moss-wardens and the water-wardens and the old ones and the children born in inversion. Not just the fauna, abundant at 2.1, the condensation frogs and the mist moths and the stone lizards and the bog deer and the cave bears. Not just the vegetation, lush at 1.7, the moss and the ferns and the dripwood and the fungi, the slow minds of the damp.

But the *equilibrium itself*. The frozen balance. The city that has stopped changing and, in stopping, has become something else entirely—a place where the world breathes in and out, where water climbs and falls, where the boundary between inside and outside, between built and natural, between human and damp, has become *permeable*, has become *negotiable*, has become the very *substance* of living here.

What lives in Veth-Morai is *adaptation without end*. The city has adapted to the damp, and in adapting, has become damp. The people have adapted to the city, and in adapting, have become *patient*, have become *still*, have become able to live in a world that does not change, that does not *offer* change, that offers only the narrow variation of more condensation or less, of fog that lifts or fog that stays, of cisterns that run high or low.

They are not unhappy. This is important. The stillness of Veth-Morai is not depression. It is not stagnation. It is *equilibrium*, a state where every input is matched by an output, where the water that falls is the water that evaporates, where the heat that rises is the heat that condenses, where the living and the dead and the built exist in a balance so stable that it feels, to those who live within it, like *eternity*.

And yet—they are not *finished*. The Rising happens. The equilibrium is not a given but an achievement, a constant negotiation. The city breathes. The water climbs. The world contains rules they have not yet mapped.

What lives in Veth-Morai is *anticipation without hope of fulfillment*. Not hope—hope implies change, implies the possibility of something better arriving. What lives here is *attention*. The attention of people who have learned to read the damp, to read the mist, to read the level of the cisterns, to read the *breath* of the city itself. They attend to the equilibrium. They maintain it. They know that it is fragile, that it could tip, that the condensation could become too much or too little, that the young mountains could, in some far future, erode to roundness, could change the drainage, could alter the whole *system*.

They attend. They maintain. They continue.

This is what lives in Veth-Morai. The city and its people, the damp and its creatures, the equilibrium and its guardians. All of it breathing together, in the mist, in the cool, in the permanent dampness that lives in the joints of stone, in the grain of timber, in the lungs of everything that breathes.

All of it *still*. All of it *unusual*. All of it, at 0.0 dynamic, *continuing*.
