They are the **Arbortects** first—the hosts, the landlords, the slow dreams of a mountain that refused to die. They do not merely grow here; they *are* here, their calcified bulk the only geography that matters. Each one is a solitary consciousness thinking at the speed of erosion, its nervous system not neurons but the vascular channels that carry sap and pneumatic pressure alike. They remember the weight of being a peak, and in their slow, grinding expansion upward—visible if you watch long enough—they seem to be reaching for a height they lost, stretching their fossilized limbs toward a sky that has forgotten their ambition. They weep constantly, a saline transpiration that feeds the condensation pools below, and in that weeping they speak, though the language is older than sound, a subsonic grinding of stone against stone that you feel in your teeth more than hear.

Then there are the **Grafted**—not machines, not plants, but the *interface* between them. The industry did not invade; it was *grafted*, spliced into the woody hollows until the boundary dissolved. Now they are a third kingdom: beings of rust and verdigris, of gears that turn with tectonic inevitability and pistons that breathe steam in rhythm with the Arbortects' slow pulses. They are the maintainers, the metabolizers, the ones who keep the lights on in the dark chambers of the fruiting bodies. Their "skin" is a patina of oxidation that blooms and recedes like a fungal infection, and their minds—if they have minds—are distributed across the pneumatic tubes, thinking in pressure differentials and the hiss of valves. They are patient. They have learned the plant's patience, or the plant has learned the machine's hunger, and in the marriage, they have become something that requires neither soil nor electricity alone, but the *friction* between them.

In the condensation pools—where water defies gravity and rises upward to cling to overhangs—live the **Mist-Born**. They are pale, drifting things, neither insect nor spore but perhaps the Arbortects' motile immune cells, or their children, detached and wandering. They exist in the thin, buoyant air, heavier than they should be, lighter than they appear, drifting in the tidal waves of mist that roll across the plateau. They photosynthesize the strange, gauze-diffused light, and they cast shadows that fall in directions no sun should allow. They are the plankton of this high-altitude sea, grazing on the mineral dust of the flattened stone, reproducing in the hollows where the built environment has taken root.

Between the Arbortects, across the kilometers of empty silence, move the **Brass-Winged**. They are birds, or were, or are clockwork approximations of birds—entities with feathers of beaten brass and gears for hearts, whirring past on thermal currents that shouldn't exist in air this thin. They fly wrong, their wings beating against a physics that has thinned, their trajectories curving in arcs that suggest they are navigating not by wind but by the magnetic memory of the mountain that once was. They are the couriers, the only things that cross the vast distances, carrying spores in their hollow bones, delivering messages written in rust flakes from one Arbortect to another.

Deep within the hollows, in the dark where the steam engines chuff and the great gears grind, there are rumors of the **Hollowed**—small, pale figures that tend the boilers. Whether they are human remnants, or calcified tissue that has learned to wear brass like clothing, or simply the shadows given temporary form by the reality fracture, is unclear. They might be the last caretakers, or they might be the Arbortects' dreams of caretakers, given enough time to coalesce into shape. They move in the spaces between the grinding teeth of the machinery, lubricating joints with sap instead of oil, their forms half-merged with the verdigris, becoming part of the mechanism they serve.

And finally, there is the **Erosion** itself, the final inhabitant. It is not a creature but a process that has achieved appetite. It lives in the grinding creak of the Arbortects' expansion, in the shedding of calcified plates that fall like distant thunder, in the wearing of the plateau ever flatter. It is the consumer that waits at the end of the world, patient and mineral, wearing the stone down even as the plants grow taller, ensuring that the tableland of bones remains a tableland, grinding the past into the dust from which the condensation rises.

There are no humans here, or if there were, they have become indistinguishable from the rust, the mist, or the slow, photosynthetic machinery. The inhabitants are the symbiosis itself—the plant that built a city in its own flesh, the machine that learned to photosynthesize, the water that climbs upward, and the silence that presses against the ears like deep water, broken only by the organ-pipe drone of wind through hollow chambers and the creak of a world that refuses to stop growing, even at the end of its life.
