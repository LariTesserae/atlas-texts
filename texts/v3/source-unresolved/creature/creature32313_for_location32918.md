The insects are the first to make themselves known—not as individuals, but as a *presence*, a shimmering at the edge of perception. They are moths that never knew the caterpillar, born already winged and translucent, their bodies veined with filaments of copper that have grown into their flesh like vascular tissue. They cling to the warm iron surfaces of the pistons and pipes, their feet equipped with microscopic hooks that grip the oxidized metal. Their wings beat in a frequency that matches the drone of the engines exactly, creating a standing wave in the air, a interference pattern of motion that appears as a silver shimmer. They do not fly from pillar to pillar; they hover, suspended in the thermal updrafts, beating their wings not to travel but to *maintain*—to keep the vibration alive, to feed the resonance. They draw sustenance from the electrical charge conducted by the moss, their bodies acting as living capacitors, glowing from within with a soft, pulsing luminescence. They exist in swarms that have no beginning or end, clouds that shift and reform in patterns that repeat with mathematical precision, their numbers constant, their configuration eternal. They are the static frenzy—motion without progress, life as pure vibration.

In the hollows of the great columns, nestled between the meshing teeth of the primordial gears, dwell the Pale Ones. They are small, eyeless, their skin like wet parchment, gray-white and faintly luminescent in the flat gray-green light. They have no need for sight; the world here does not change. They are creatures of the crevice, adapted to the heat and the constant tremor—their bodies contain sacs of viscous fluid that act as shock absorbers, cushioning them against the eternal vibration of the machinery. They move only as the gears turn, their elongated, segmented bodies fitting perfectly into the gaps between metal teeth, locked into the rotation like keys in a lock. They graze on the mineral crust that accretes on the iron, on the conductive lichen, on the quicksilver condensation that beads upon their skin and never falls. They are born and die at identical rates, their populations fixed, their life cycles synchronized with the rotation of the turbines below. They exist in the spacious void between the pillars, in the emptiness where the air hangs heavy—small, pale, and utterly still, the stillness at the heart of the roar.

Above, in the upper alcoves where the vascular tissue forms natural vaulting and the light grows dim, roost the Drift-Birds. Or perhaps they are bats, or perhaps something older than either—creatures with wings like membranes of translucent skin stretched over elongated, grasping fingers, the skin veined with a network that mirrors the ceiling above. They hang suspended from the fibrous wood, not sleeping but existing in a state of torpor that is not quite sleep, their hearts beating at the same rhythm as the pistons, their breath synchronizing with the breathing of the chamber itself. They fill the void with their presence, dozens of them, motionless as statues carved from the same pale wood as the pillars, yet alive—their chests rising and falling in perfect, repeating loops. They do not hunt or migrate; they simply hang, filtering the damp air through the membranes of their wings, catching the moisture that refuses to fall, existing in the upper dark where the gray-green light is thickest. They are the roosting life, the upper ecology, suspended in the eternal moment.

And beneath them all, or perhaps *as* them all, is the thing that breathes. The chamber breathes—a slow, tidal respiration that moves the vascular walls, that pulses the glowing fluids through the xylem-pipes. The inhabitants are this breath made manifest. The insects are the cilia, sweeping and resonating; the Pale Ones are the antibodies, maintaining the equilibrium, the climax state; the Drift-Birds are the lungs, expanding and contracting in the damp. Together, they form a single, vast organism that has reached its final, perfect form—a symbiosis of organic and mechanical so complete that neither can be said to live within the other. They are the inhabitants of the end, the dwellers in the time-invariant moment, living in the loud, unchanging hum of a world that has forgotten how to change, existing in perfect, roaring stillness until the erosion finishes its final, patient work and even the memory of motion fades into the dark, yielding floor.
