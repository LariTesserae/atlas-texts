The inhabitants do not occupy this place so much as **constitute** it—a single, distributed organism wearing many skins, from bacterial film to locomotive-sized carapace to the grinding brass of the city itself.

**The Lithic Sward**
Begin at the foundation: the ground itself is alive. The lithotrophic mats do not grow upward but *outward*, spreading across the terraces like a skin of wet felt, thick and rubbery and warm to the touch. They are not green but the color of old bruises and rust—bands of iron-orange, sulfur-yellow, and deep slate-purple. They breathe. When the geothermal vents pulse beneath the plateau, the mats visibly swell, exhaling steam that smells of copper and rot. They are the "grass" here, but their metabolism is geological: they drink mineral-rich water from artesian springs, chew through volcanic glass with acids secreted from pores too small to see, and excrete the calcite and gypsum that slowly encrust the city’s bones. A single mat might be a kilometer across, a slow-moving continent of chemosynthetic tissue that has been feeding on the same bedrock for ten thousand years. When you walk, you do not step on stone; you step on the backs of these ancient, patient organisms, feeling the faint give of their spongy, mineral-laden flesh.

**The Mist-Borne**
Above the sward drifts the second population: the **aeroplankton**. They are not insects but living filters—wingless, eyeless, translucent creatures that range from the size of dust motes to the size of your fist. Their bodies are lattices of chitin and precipitated minerals, shaped like nets or sieves or strange, crystalline flowers. They hang suspended in the humid air, drifting in the thermal currents, their sole purpose to strain the atmosphere. They filter the constant rain for bacterial spores, for mineral dust, for the microscopic detritus of the larger fauna. Their wings—if they can be called that—are membranes of calcite that shimmer in the amber light. When they die, they do not fall far; they dissolve into the mist, becoming part of the mineral soup that feeds the mats below. To breathe here is to inhale them. They taste of ozone and wet stone, a constant, faint grit on the tongue. At night, or when the steam thickens, their bodies bioluminesce faintly—a pale, blue-white glow that turns the fog into a starfield suspended at street level.

**The Geophagous Herds**
Then there are the grazers—the megafauna that give the city its pulse. They are **lithovores**, and they are enormous.

Picture a creature the size of a railway car: a low, sprawling beast with twelve jointed legs, each foot ending in a grinding plate of tungsten-hard mineral. Its body is armored not with bone but with geological accretion—layers of calcite and gypsum that it has secreted over centuries, forming a shell like a cross between a nautilus and a termite mound. This shell is not dead armor; it is *growing*, adding millimeters of stone each year, recording the chemistry of the air and water in its stratified bands. When it moves, it does not walk so much as **abrade**. The sound is not footsteps but the groan of tectonic plates—a deep, grinding scrape as its carapace scrapes against the terraced stone, wearing away the bacterial mats to reach the mineral deposits beneath.

They move in herds, and a herd is a weather system. A procession of these creatures might take three days to cross a single district, their collective passage creating a low-frequency vibration that shakes the steam pipes and causes the condensation to rain from the ceilings in rhythmic waves. They are blind—eyes would be useless in the perpetual mist—and navigate by sensing the heat gradients of the geothermal vents, the chemical signatures of different mineral deposits, the vibrations of their own kind through the bedrock. They communicate in subsonic rumbles that make your teeth ache, a language of pressure and temperature spoken over miles of stone.

Their digestion is a marvel of slow chemistry. They ingest the bacterial crust, the mineral precipitates, the rust flaking from the iron girders. Inside their gizzards—vast, steam-heated chambers lined with symbiotic bacteria—they break down rock into usable energy. Their excrement is not waste but **soil**: a fine, warm, mineral-rich paste that feeds the smaller arthropods and nourishes the lithic mats. They are the city’s gardeners, mowing down the overgrowth, fertilizing the ground, preventing the architecture from being choked by its own lithic flora.

**The Symbiotes**
Living on and in the grazers are the **cleaners**—smaller, softer creatures that have evolved to occupy the niche of parasites, but have become mutualists. They are pale, eyeless, slug-like things that cling to the crevices of the great shells, picking off the dead mineral layers, eating the mites and spores that would otherwise infest the herd. In return, they receive warmth from the grazers' geothermal metabolism and a steady supply of mineral dust. Some are so specialized that they live only in the joints of the grazers' legs, lubricating the grinding plates with secretions that prevent the creatures from wearing themselves to death.

Then there are the **gear-worms**—creatures that have abandoned the open air for the interior of the machines. They are long, segmented, eyeless, and pinkish-warm, living in the steam vents and gear housings of the industrial district. They eat the calcite deposits that would clog the pipes, their bodies acting as living filters. They are the reason the ancient machinery still turns; without them, the city would choke on its own mineral excretions. They are tended by no one, yet they are essential—a hidden population of maintenance organisms that keep the heart of the megalopolis beating.

**The Drifting Minds**
But the strangest inhabitants are the **elders**—the oldest of the grazers, the ones whose shells have grown so thick with mineral layers that they have become sessile, rooted to the bedrock like coral. These are not stupid beasts; over centuries, the constant filtering of mineral-laden water through their nervous systems has created a form of distributed intelligence. They do not think in words but in **pressure** and **chemistry**. They remember. An elder grazer might have been scraping the same wall for three hundred years, its shell recording the history of the city in its layers—the temperature of the steam vents, the chemical composition of the rain, the vibrations of the machines as they were built, grew old, and were buried by newer construction. When a younger herd passes by, the elder communicates with them through the stone—subsonic pulses that carry information about where the best mineral deposits lie, where the warm currents flow, where the ancient machines still vent usable heat.

They are the city’s memory, its historians, its slow-beating heart.

**The City as Organism**
And finally, there is the **megalopolis itself**—not a collection of buildings but a single, emergent entity. The bacteria grow the stone; the stone houses the machines; the machines generate the heat; the heat feeds the fauna; the fauna maintain the machines and fertilize the stone. It is a closed loop, a metabolism as complete as any cell. The "inhabitants" are not separate from the place; they are its organs. The streets are arteries carrying warm, mineral-rich blood. The steam vents are lungs. The grinding gears are a digestive system, breaking down the rock of the world and excreting the city.

To live here is to be part of this slow, wet, grinding cycle. There are no individuals, only **processes**. You are born as a spore in the mist, you grow as a mat on the wall, you are eaten by a grazer, you become part of its shell, you die and fall as dust, you are breathed in by the next generation, and you become, once again, the living stone. The city does not have inhabitants; it *is* the inhabitant, a vast, patient, mineral-eating god that dreams in geological time, wrapped in warm, humid air, listening to the endless, dripping rain.
