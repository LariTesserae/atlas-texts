The inhabitants do not occupy the plateau so much as constitute it. To walk here is not to traverse empty space but to squeeze through a continuous tissue of being, to navigate a geography made entirely of bodies packed so tightly that the ground itself has become organic architecture. They are the final biomass, the terminal density of a world that has reached the end of its gradients and settled into a single, infinite, mineral moment.

First among them are the crystalline herbivores, layered through the salt flats in strata that mimic sedimentary rock but pulse with the memory of appetite. Their hides are not skin but geology animated—plates of quartz and feldspar grown into tessellated armor that fits together with the precision of a jeweler's infinite patience. When the pale, diffuse light falls upon them, it does not reflect; it refracts, shattering into spectra that hang suspended in the thin air around them like halos made solid, rainbows that do not move because motion has been forgotten. They stand in herds that have not shifted in ten thousand years, their heads bowed in the eternal gesture of grazing, mandibles locked around mouthfuls of mineral dust and static. Their bodies are polygonal, geode-like, each facet a prism that holds the light and refuses to release it, so that the herd becomes a field of captured auroras, a landscape of frozen digestion.

Interwoven with these crystal grazers, or suspended above them in dense clouds that do not drift, are the insectoid swarms. They are not a swarm in the sense of motion but in the sense of density—millions of individuals arrested mid-gesture, each one a punctuation mark in a sentence that will never finish. A wing is half-raised, translucent membrane stretched over veins of amber; a leg extends toward a destination it will never reach; an antenna quivers in a vibration that has lasted a geological age without completing its arc. They form mandalas and mathematical constants in the air, geometric spirals and spiraling fractals made of chitin, their bodies packed so closely that they create a second atmosphere, a shimmering, faceted canopy that filters the already-thin light into something even more rarefied. They are the plateau's punctuation, comma-like and semicolon-shaped, the moments between breaths stretched into eternity.

Beneath and between these smaller geometries lie the leviathans—the great, silent ones that form the foundation of the plain. They are beached not by water but by its absence, stranded in the dry air of deep time, their flanks rising in calcified ridges that mimic the polygonal crust of the salt pan itself. They are made of chitin that has mineralized into something between bone and stone, their bodies the size of hills, lying on their sides with ribcages that create cathedral-like caves where smaller creatures nest in the strata of their flesh. They breathe no breath, yet their chests rise and fall in a rhythm measured not in seconds but in millennia—one inhalation since the mountains were young, one exhalation that has not yet completed itself. Their mouths gape in silent song, their eyes—if they have eyes—staring at the transparent sky where other worlds press against this one, semi-transparent, overlapping. They are the architecture, the ground itself rising into curves of calcified grace, the planet's final attempt at creating mountains, this time from within.

Scattered among this frozen megafauna are the builders—small, bipedal, made of the soft stuff that precedes mineralization, the last warm-blooded things now cooled to the temperature of the plateau. They are found near the shelters of woven bone and cured hide, near the cairns of stacked salt-blocks, their obsidian blades still sharp in their hands or half-buried in the crust at their feet. They are caught mid-step, mid-gesture, mid-prayer, their bodies preserved in the attitudes of early tool-use, of simple fabrication, of hope. They are the only ones who seem to have been in motion when the moment froze, their shelters half-constructed, their tools half-used. They are the question written in flesh and salt: what were they building? A refuge from a cold that never varies? A marker for travelers who would never come? A grave? An altar? They stand or crouch or kneel among the giants, the last soft things, now as hard as the stone they worked, attending to tasks that will never be completed.

Between the cracks of the polygonal plates, where the mineral skeleton of the world shows its fissures, clings the vegetation—not truly alive in the way of motion and growth, but clinging with the tenacity of the final. There are lichens of copper-green and rust, crustose and stubborn, feeding on light and mineral static, spreading across the salt in slow, geological patterns. And there is the glass grass: not plant but mineral formation, silica fibers grown into blades that stand in eternal arrays, refracting the light without chiming, silent as the fauna, standing in ranks like a forest made of fiber-optic cable, transmitting nothing, receiving everything, motionless as the leviathans themselves.

But it is not the forms of these creatures that defines the life here, but the quality of their attention. They attend. Many have no eyes, or their eyes are faceted into the same crystal as their hides, yet they watch with a pressure that is almost physical, a density of being that makes the chest tighten and the thin air feel thinner still. They are aware in the way that a photograph is aware, or a fossil, or a moment held in amber—but they are not dead. They are held. They are the world's last congregation, gathered for an event that will not occur, witnessing a silence that has weight. They form a continuous membrane of consciousness, each creature touching its neighbors, creating a network of frozen awareness that covers the plateau like a skin, like a second crust, like the planet's final layer of self-observation.

To be among them is to feel the weight of their gaze without eyes, to feel the squeeze of their bodies that have forgotten how to yield, to breathe the air that tastes of mineral static and old dust while the stars stare down through the too-blue sky at the height of day. They are the museum and the audience, the architecture and the congregation, the final, primordial form of life when the water has been leached away and time has pooled into a single, infinite, unchanging moment. They are held in the salt and the silence, packed to the edges of the world, attending, attending, attending, forever.
