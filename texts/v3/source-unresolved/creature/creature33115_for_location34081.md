I will stay here with you. Let me not rush to the inhabitants—let me first understand what it means to *live* in such a place, what kind of being could persist in a volume where walls breathe and water pulses and every surface pays attention.

The inhabitants cannot be human in any simple sense. Not because humans couldn't survive here, but because survival here requires *becoming* something else. The damp settles into bone. The listening changes you. The hangings adjust to your presence. To live here long is to be incorporated.

So I imagine them already changed. Their skin, perhaps, has developed a quality the surface-dwellers would find disturbing—a slight translucency in certain light, a way of holding moisture without discomfort, a sensitivity to vibration transmitted through mineral and fabric alike. Their eyes, adapted to the narrow band of bioluminescence and reflected glow from pale creatures and wet stone, have grown large and still, with a membrane that closes sideways, the way a creature of water closes its eyes. They do not blink often. Blinking is a surface-world habit.

Their voices carry strangely here, as the place describes. So they have learned to speak in registers that the water-tubes can transmit without distortion, and they have learned to *listen* in directions that don't match their source. A person speaking from three chambers away arrives as a presence behind you, and they have trained themselves not to turn, not to react, until they have traced the resonance through the curved surfaces and found its true origin. Trust in this place is built on this discipline: the refusal to believe what seems immediate.

They are small, I think, or they have made themselves smaller. The Lung, where arms can touch opposite walls, is their cathedral, their gathering place, and to fill it would be to overwhelm it. They move through it in groups of seven or nine, never more. The number matters. I don't know why yet.

Their relationship with the creatures—the pale, translucent, patterned beings that navigate the hangings—is older than fear, the place says. So they do not keep them, exactly. They *house* them, in the way the walls house the stalactites, the way the hangings house the fibers. The creatures eat, and in eating they produce, and the produce is gathered with a tenderness that suggests ritual rather than utility. The hair is brushed, not plucked. The scales are collected from surfaces where the creatures have shed, never taken from the living animal. This is not kindness, precisely. It is *recognition*. The creatures notice things. They are alarm systems, yes, but also something else—witnesses, perhaps, or mirrors. When the builders speak of the creatures' attention, they speak of it as a kind of sight that the builders do not possess. They see movement the builders miss. They see change in the hangings, in the damp, in the walls themselves, that arrives before any human instrument can detect it.

The builders have a word for this. They call the creatures *the-early-knowing*, or something that approximates it.

They work with metal that is old, older than their memory of its origin. They do not smelt. They persuade, by patience and repetition. I imagine their metalworkers as people who spend days on a single object, who know the grain of the metal the way others know the grain of wood, who can feel when it is ready to bend, when it has been worked enough and must rest. Their tools are few but they are *worn*. A knife that has been used for seven years has a handle shaped by one hand, and that hand has changed to fit it. The builders do not share tools casually. To use another's tool is to enter their rhythm, their tempo, and this is a kind of intimacy.

They weave. Everything here is weaving, in the end. The hangings, yes, but also their social structure, their calendar of knots and notches, their way of recording debt and obligation and grace. A knot is a promise. A notch is a debt. The calendar is a wall of fiber in the deepest chamber, and it is touched daily, added to, never finished. They do not write, not in any system that would translate. They *knot*, and the knots are read by feel in the dark, by the tongue, by a fingertip traced along a cord.

They have sealed chambers. They do not speak of them. But they speak of them *around*—in the way they avoid certain topics, in the way children are taught not to ask, in the way the creatures gather and wait. The sealed spaces are not forgotten. They are *maintained*, in a minimal way: a barrier checked, a seal renewed, a ritual performed at intervals that the calendar marks. Punishment takes place there. Death takes place there. And something else, something that changes those who enter and return, though they do not speak of what they found.

I think the builders know, in some part of themselves that they do not voice, that the sealed chambers are *growing*. That the place is not finished with them. The walls grow and are ground back. The water finds new paths. What would it mean for a sealed chamber to grow, to expand, to press against its barriers? The creatures press against them too, in waiting. Not distress—the place is clear on this. Waiting. As if they expect something. As if the chamber is a bud, and they are gardeners who do not yet understand what will bloom.

There is a predator. Or something that functions as one. It lives in the deepest chamber, the one where the plant fiber grows in absolute dark, harvested by feel. The community feeds it. They do not worship it. They *acknowledge* it, as one acknowledges a season, a tide, a wound that will not close.

I imagine it as something made of the same materials as everything else—mineral and fiber and bone and water—but arranged according to a pattern no one designed. It is not large, by the place's standards. Dog-sized, perhaps, or smaller. But it is *dense* in the way the Lung is dense, full of layers and hangings and resonances. It does not hunt, exactly. It *receives*, and in receiving it grows, and in growing it changes the chamber around it, and the changes are accommodations, the way the builders accommodate the water's new paths. It is not hostile. It is not friendly. It is *present*, in a way that makes presence itself feel like a kind of attention, a kind of listening.

The builders feed it. What do they feed it? I think: the things that would otherwise accumulate. The shed fiber that is not quite clean enough for weaving. The creatures that die, that are found in the hangings, that are gathered with the same tenderness as the living ones. The condensation from the lowest basins, collected and carried down, down, down, through passages that narrow until only a hand can pass. They do not see it eat. They do not need to. They know it is there by the changes in the chamber, by the way the air moves differently when they approach, by the sound—that sound, in the wet season, that arrives from below and that they have dug toward, carefully, without finding its source.

The sound is the predator, perhaps. Or the predator is the sound, given pattern and hunger and growth. The builders have stopped trying to name it precisely. They have a word, but it is the same word they use for the place itself: *the-hearing*, *the-being-listened-to*. They are listened to by everything, constantly. The predator is one more listener, one more attention, and the deepest, and the one that changes them most when they approach it.

Now—the inhabitants themselves. The builders. Who are they, these people who live in a grown place, who have made a home in the hollow of something that remembers being wood, that became limestone, that became bone, that became this?

They are not numerous. The Lung, their largest chamber, fits perhaps thirty in a gathering, and that is a crowd, a compression, a temporary density that they endure for ritual only. In ordinary life they are spread through the chambers, the passages, the vertical spaces, the niches hollowed from stalagmites and lined with clay. They are perhaps two hundred, perhaps three hundred. I cannot say. The place does not census itself.

They are old, in the way of communities that have no childhood. There are no babies here, not in the surface sense. The builders do not reproduce quickly, or easily, or at all in any way that would be recognized. New builders arrive from somewhere. I think they come from above, from the surface world, from places where the damp does not find you, where the listening is absent, where one can be *unattended*. They come young, or young-ish, and they descend through the chimney of warm air, and they are *changed* by the descent, by the first months, by the incorporation that the place demands.

Some do not survive the change. They go back up, or they sicken and die, and their bodies are... what? I think they are woven. Their hair at least. Perhaps their bones are ground and mixed with the clay that lines the stalagmites. The place does not waste. The place *incorporates*.

Those who remain become builders. They learn the water-tubes, the bellows, the calendar of knots. They learn to read the damp, to know which chambers will flood and which will stay dry as a bone. They learn the creatures' alarm calls, the particular pitch that means a shift in the hangings, a change in the mineral growth, a presence in the sealed spaces. They learn to weave, and to knot, and to persuade metal by patience and repetition. They learn to move through the hangings without disturbing them more than necessary, to climb the trained stalactites, to find purchase on the mineral floor that holds the memory of countless hands and feet.

They are quiet, mostly. The loudness of the place is not made by them. It is made by the dripping, the mineral growth, the expansion and contraction of walls, the scuttle of creatures, the resonance of voices carried strangely by curved surfaces. Their own voices are low, careful, designed to travel through the water-tubes without distortion. They speak in registers, in patterns, in call-and-response that has become ritual without losing its function. They do not shout. Shouting here would be a kind of violence, a disruption of the listening.

They have a relationship with time that the surface does not. The hangings are constantly rewoven, the old fiber becoming the new, in cycles tracked by knots and notches. The walls grow and are ground back. The water finds new paths. The builders accommodate, never fully control. They think in generations. A decision made today will be felt by builders who are not yet born, who will descend through the chimney of warm air and find the place slightly different, slightly *theirs*.

They have rituals, though they do not call them that. The opening of sealed chambers, rarely, for punishment, for death, for the changes that happen inside. The feeding of the predator, regular, unspoken, a carrying-down of offerings that are also waste. The touching of the calendar, daily, in the dark. The gathering of the creatures' shed hair and scale, with tenderness. The bellows-worked cycles of air, timed by observation, maintained by hand.

And they have the unresolved. The chambers they do not speak of. The hangings that move against the air current. The sound from below. The creatures that gather and wait.

I think they are afraid, in a way they do not name. Not of the predator—they have acknowledged it, as one acknowledges a wound. Not of the sealed chambers—they maintain them, they ritualize them, they incorporate them into their calendar. But of the *unresolved* itself. Of the possibility that the place is not finished with them, that the incorporation is not complete, that something is growing in the deepest chamber that will eventually require them to become something else, something that fits the pattern no one designed.

They do not speak of this fear. They speak of the damp, the water-tubes, the creatures, the calendar. They speak of the work. The work is endless. The hangings must be rewoven. The bellows must be worked. The metal must be persuaded. The sealed chambers must be maintained. The predator must be fed. The fear does not fit in the work, and so it lives in the spaces between, in the silences, in the way they listen to the sound from below and do not dig anymore, though they dug once, carefully, without finding its source.

They are not happy, in any simple sense. They are not unhappy. They are *attentive*. They are *being-listened-to*, by everything, constantly, and the listening changes them. Their skin holds moisture without discomfort. Their eyes have grown large and still. Their voices carry strangely, arriving from directions that don't match their source. They have become, over generations, over descents, over incorporations, something that fits this place, this hollow, this grown volume where walls breathe and water pulses and every surface pays attention.

Would they leave, if they could? I think not. The damp has found them, eventually, has settled into their bones in a way that the surface world can never quite remove. They are *of* this place now. The hangings adjust to their presence, but they have adjusted to the hangings. The creatures turn their pale faces toward them, and they turn their large still eyes toward the creatures, and this is not friendship, precisely. It is *recognition*. The older-than-fear relationship.

They would stay. They would become, in time, one of the hangings themselves. Their hair among the fibers, their voice among the resonances, their body finding its own hollow in the wall. The place offers this, gently, constantly. The incorporation is not an ending. It is a *continuation*, differently, into forms that the builders cannot yet imagine, that the place is growing toward, that the predator in the deepest chamber is perhaps *dreaming*, in its mineral-and-fiber way, in its pattern that no one designed.

They are the builders. They are the heard. They are the being-listened-to.

And they are, finally, *here*. In this place, under, not deep, in the hollow of something that remembers being wood, in the volume where walls breathe and water pulses and every surface pays attention and the listening changes you, gently, constantly, until you are no longer quite yourself, until you are part of the hangings, part of the resonance, part of the system that was complete before you arrived and will continue, differently, after you leave—or stay, and become one of the walls that breathe, one of the creatures that turn their pale faces toward the newcomer, one of the builders who learn, slowly, to be listened to by everything, constantly, and to listen back.
