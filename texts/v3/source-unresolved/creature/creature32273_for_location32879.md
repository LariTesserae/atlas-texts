Start at the bottom, with the **Crust**. It is not merely ground; it is a single, continent-scale organism reduced to a photosynthetic patina, a lichenous mat only centimeters thick but denser than stone (2.9). Within its grey-green tissue, stacked like geological lasagna, live the **chemolithotrophs**: archaea that eat iron and sulfur, bacteria that split hydrogen from the mineral matrix, and anoxygenic phototrophs that harvest the blinding 2.6-light not to split water—there is none—but to oxidize sulfur compounds, excreting elemental sulfur as waste. They are the primary producers, drawing carbon from the bright, thin air and nitrogen from the stone itself, fixing it into amino acids through enzymes that operate at near-freezing temperatures. The crust is ancient, weathered to an erosion-state of 2.2, a pavement of fused bone and pumice where the dead have become the living. It transpires—a slow, mineral exhalation of supercritical CO₂ that forms a ghost-film over the Veldt, a breath that smells of ozone and rust.

Then there are the **Towers**. They are not buildings; they are **megafauna**. Each one is a grown cathedral, a kilometers-tall organism of silica-secreting cambium and metal-reducing bacteria, a plant that has learned metallurgy. Inside their flanks, ribbed with vascular pipes, pulses a grey fluid: ammonia mixed with pressurized mineral slurries, the blood of this world. They respire through chimney-stacks that are actually fruiting bodies, venting steam and spores into the screaming wind. Their roots—drilling organs of patient, glacial hunger—grind into the mantle, extracting feedstock while the reflective crystalline leaves on their surfaces track the ultraviolet sun like heliostats, converting light into chemical energy through sulfur-based photosynthesis. They are not conscious in any way you would recognize, but they possess a **distributed sentience**, a ghost-memory of design spread through their cambium networks—a slow, mineral intelligence that remembers where the bedrock is richest and where the ammonia flows.

Scurrying across the metallic bark of these giants are the **Cleaners**: insectoid janitors the size of fists, swarming in chattering clouds. Where the Towers drip—where liquid ammonia cascades down their flanks in cryogenic streams—cluster the **Ammonia-Feeders**. These are the closest things to aquatic life in this anhydrous world. They live in the rivulets of super-cold ammonia, their bodies composed of membranes that remain fluid in solvents that would shatter terrestrial cells. They are filter-feeders, sieving the ammonia flows for mineral particulates and the occasional spore, their bodies translucent and pulsing with bioluminescent chemistry that glows pale blue in the blinding light. They are small, hidden in the shadows of the Tower bases, but they are the recyclers of the liquid, breaking down the organic detritus that falls from above.

Above them, in the thin, pale membrane of the upper atmosphere, drift the **Wind-Riders**: aerial plankton, vast colonies of buoyant organisms that photosynthesize while suspended in the 2.9-scale gales. They are living gas-bags, filled with hydrogen or ammonia vapor, their surfaces membranous and reflective to protect against the UV. They ride the continental storms that circle the Towers like hurricanes around skyscrapers, seeding the Veldt with spores when they descend, or being carried to new Towers to colonize their heights. They are the clouds of this world, the floating gardens that connect the Towers in a web of genetic exchange.

And there are the **Hunters**. Sparse, patient, almost invisible against the grey-green crust, they are the apex predators of this mineral ecology. Eyeless like the grazers, they hunt by sensing the vibrations of the herds, by tasting the chemical signatures left in the ammonia film, by feeling the heat differential of a warm-blooded (or warm-ammonia-blooded) body against the freezing stone. They are long, low, and many-legged, moving through the crust like eels through water, their bodies armored against the wind that would strip flesh from bone. They take the grazers, yes, but they also take the Cleaners, descending upon the Towers like locusts to strip the bark bare when the Towers grow too old, accelerating the collapse that feeds the crust.

When a Tower dies—and they die often, in landslides of mineral and meat—the **Recyclers** arrive. These are not a single species but a procession: first the ammonia-feeders colonize the fallen ammonia pools, then swarms of chemolithotrophic bacteria invade the vascular pipes, digesting the grey fluid. The crust itself knuckles upward, thickening, sending out rhizoids that penetrate the fallen giant’s calcified shell, digesting it over decades. The Grazers come to grind the bones, the Cleaners (now scavengers) strip the metal, and the wind carries the spores to new ground. Nothing is wasted. You do not breathe oxygen as your primary oxidizer; you respire sulfur, iron, and carbon dioxide. You do not see with eyes, but with vibration and chemical taste. This is what lives here: not a civilization, but a **metabolism**—a planetary-scale organism that thinks in minerals, dreams in ammonia, and grows in the blinding light of a world without water.
