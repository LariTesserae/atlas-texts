The primary inhabitant is the city itself—though “inhabitant” suggests a separateness that does not exist here. It is a slow, calcified metabolism that learned to want shelter and, in wanting, grew walls. Whether it was a civilization, a single vast organism, or a symbiosis of chemosynthetic bacteria and coral-like polyps that achieved a kind of architectural sentience, the distinction has eroded away. What remains is a living geology: three hundred meters of porous calcium carbonate honeycombed with burrows, weeping mineral-rich water upward in reverse waterfalls, being dissolved by the warm acid of the deep and rebuilt by accretion in the same breath. It is a body that sleeps in equilibrium, its surface constantly flaking and renewing, its plazas drifting with anemone-like filter-feeders that pulse with the rhythm of its tides. The city does not think, but it metabolizes; it does not remember, but it preserves the shape of intention in its rounded, weathered geometry.

Around and within this sleeping giant move the colonists—the true current population, descendants of nothing that built these walls, inheritors of a ruin that has become a reef.

The fish are the most numerous, yet they exist as a single distributed consciousness. They are blind, their eyes reduced to vestigial scales, pale as the marine snow that falls around them like warm ash. They move as one silent body through the canals, navigating not by sight but by pressure gradients, by the chemical signatures of the stone, by the subtle shifts in the dense broth. Their flanks scrape against the eroded walls without a sound—the water is too thick for acoustics—and they graze the orange and black bacterial mats that stain the architecture, harvesting the chemosynthetic rivers that flow visibly through the currents. They are the city’s skin-shedders, its exfoliators, eating what the structure weeps and leaving behind only the smooth polish of their passage.

Then there are the isopods, scavengers the size of dogs, their many jointed legs clicking softly against the mineral plazas. They are armored for the pressure, their segmented shells reflecting the cold blue-green pulses of bioluminescence that drift past. They move with a deliberate, geological patience, picking through the drifts of silt, consuming the detritus of a thousand deaths, the fungal mats, the fallen leviathans that have sunk to the floor. They are the city’s janitors, its recyclers, turning waste into the raw material of new accretion. When they die, they do not float; they settle, becoming part of the ooze, their calcium shells contributing to the slow rise of new towers.

Suspended in the water column between the avenues hang the eels—eyeless, translucent, drifting like ribbons of living glass. They are the city’s nerves, sensing the chemical gradients, the thermal plumes rising from cracks in the crust. They feed on the suspended bacteria, the clouds of chemosynthetic life that turn the currents into visible rivers. They are almost ghosts, part of the architecture’s negative space, hanging motionless for hours until a shift in the broth sends them undulating away in search of richer pastures. Some say they are the descendants of the original builders, evolved into something that no longer remembers the act of building; others say they are the builders themselves, transformed over ten thousand years of cultivation into pure, drifting function.

Above them drift the leviathans—creatures like whales but wrong, with too many mouths ringing their undersides or too few eyes clustered on their flanks. They are the weather of this place, vast filter-feeders that strain the dense, living water for sustenance, their passage creating subsonic thrumms that vibrate in the bones rather than the ears. They move between the towers with a buoyancy that defies their mass, their bodies vast enough to obscure the blue-green pulses of the plankton. They sing, but the song is felt, a deep vibration passing through the warm, thick dark, a communication older than sound, older than the city itself. They live for centuries, perhaps millennia, growing slowly, becoming geological features in their own right, their bodies eventually sinking to the silt to feed the isopods and the worms.

But the true citizens are microscopic. In the honeycomb of the walls live the worm-things, secreting the calcium carbonate, maintaining the porous stone, eating the erosion and shitting out the architecture. They are the city’s immune system, its constant repair crew, working in the dark, turning the city’s decay into its growth. The walls themselves are stained with chemosynthetic bacterial mats, orange and black, living rivers that convert the chemicals seeping from the stone into biomass, into the visible currents that feed the fish. And everywhere, suspended in the viscous broth, is the bioluminescent plankton—clouds of blue and green fireworks that ignite when disturbed, turning the absolute dark into a slow, drifting constellation.

The fungal vegetation spreads across the silt floor in slow, spreading rings—rubbery, dark mats that feed on the minerals exhaled by the stone. They are ancient, established, part of the city’s metabolism, growing at the pace of millimeters per century. They do not photosynthesize; they chemosynthesize, drinking the warmth and the chemicals, spreading outward in a slow, patient hunger.

To live here is to exist in a state of perpetual buoyancy, where movement is governed by the density of the liquid, where time moves at the pace of erosion. The inhabitants do not build; they inhabit. They do not speak; they sense through pressure and chemical signature. They are adapted to the womb-deep heat, the pressurized dark, the constant rain of marine snow. They are the feast that the city hosts, the filter that cleanses its pores, the life that has colonized the grown ruins of a cultivation that outlived its cultivators—or became indistinguishable from them.

There are no builders left, only the built, and those who have made the built their home. The city is always becoming what it already is, and its inhabitants move through it like a slow tide, silent, warm, and utterly at home in the liquid dark.
