What lives here does not have names you would recognize, nor would it recognize yours. It has grown into the spaces your kind built and abandoned, and it has made those spaces into *living* in ways your kind never intended.

---

The inhabitants of the Drowned Hundredth Floor are **distributed**. They do not gather in cities, though the old architecture suggests they once might have. They exist as *processes* more than as individuals—colonies that extend through corridors, through walls, through the water itself. A single "organism" might occupy three floors, connected by fungal networks that pulse with chemical signals, that share nutrients, that *remember* where they have grown and where they have been damaged. When you cut through their membranes, they do not bleed. They *reseal*. They *re-route*. The wound becomes a new passage, a new opportunity.

The **shell-bearers** are the most visible. Crustacean-things, as you might call them—though their shells are not calcium but something softer, something that grows with them, something that incorporates the minerals of the old water directly. They range from the size of your fist to the size of your forearm, and they do not walk so much as *climb*, gripping the grown surfaces with articulated appendages that have learned the texture of lichen-crust, of kelp-membrane, of the rare patches of exposed metal where something has scraped the growth away. They are slow. They are *patient*. They feed on the detritus of the ecosystem—dead kelp, dead fish, dead anything—and in feeding, they become food. Their shells, when they die, do not dissolve. They *accumulate*. In places, the floor is nothing but shell, layer upon layer, compressed into something new, something *geology-in-progress*.

The **drift-fish** move through the flooded levels in schools that behave as single organisms, though no individual fish directs the others. They are pale, almost translucent, their bodies adapted to water so thick with mineral that it buoys them without effort. They feed on chemosynthetic bacteria, on the colloidal memory of concrete, on the constant slow rain of organic matter from above. They have no eyes that you would recognize—only sensors that read pressure, temperature, chemical gradient. They *feel* you passing. The school parts around you, reforms behind you, and you are alone again in water that remembers your shape.

But there are **others**. The ones who made the tools you find. The ones whose hands tied knots in sinew, whose hafts articulate still, who *use* this place with intention.

They are **tall**. Not human-tall, but *stretched*—adapted to move through water that is half-air, half-liquid, through corridors where the waterline shifts with the thermal pulse, where the environment demands constant negotiation between breath and buoyancy. Their bodies are *amphibious by design*—membranes between digits, nictitating layers over eyes that see in the green-gold spectrum of bioluminescence, skin that can extract oxygen from water or air, switching as needed, *patient* with the switching. They do not speak, not as you would recognize speech. They communicate in **pressure changes**—subtle shifts in the water around them, in the vibration of surfaces, in the chemical signatures they release into the slow-moving currents. You would feel them before you saw them, a *thickening* of the water, a presence that alters the acoustics of a corridor.

They are **cultivators**. They tend the kelp-analogues, prune them, graft them, direct their growth through the structure's original plumbing. They harvest the drift-fish, not with nets but with *gardens*—enclosed spaces where the fish enter and cannot find their way out, where they are fed, where they breed, where they are eventually taken. They maintain the **dry levels**—the sealed compartments where air is trapped, where they can work with shell and bone and fiber, where they make their tools, their lines, their blades. They have learned the structure's **rhythm**, the pulse of the thermal vents, the cycle of the light, the slow breathing of water in and out through fissures they have mapped over generations.

They are **not numerous**. The density here is sparse, and they are *sparser*. Perhaps a dozen hold the entire vertical extent, perhaps two dozen, scattered through forty storeys of growth and water and the memory of purpose. They do not gather. They do not need to. Their communication is **chemical and vibrational**, long-range, slow, *patient*. They meet where the work requires it—a blocked passage to clear, a new growth to establish, a boundary to negotiate with the shell-bearers or the drift-fish or the **something larger** that patrols the deep shafts.

That something larger. You hear it sometimes. A *thrumming* in the structure itself. Too big for any room. Coiled around the central spine.

It is **old**. Older than the cultivators, perhaps. Older than the building's human purpose, though it lives *in* that purpose, has made the deep shafts—the vertical spaces the humans never used, the pressure-equalization columns, the waste conduits—into **its** territory. It moves through water-filled space, and its movement creates pressure waves you feel in your sternum. It does not hunt, not as you would understand hunting. It *filters*. It passes through the deep levels, and in passing, it takes what it needs—drift-fish, shell-bearers, the occasional cultivator who has ventured too deep, too curious, too *fast*. It is not malicious. It is **hungry**, and it is *patient*, and it has learned to wait in water that holds everything in suspension, that preserves, that *delays*.

The cultivators know it. They avoid the deep shafts. They mark the boundaries, leave offerings of kelp-growth in the transition zones, and they *listen*—always listening—for the thrumming that means it is moving, it is near, *retreat*.

---

And at the top. At the hundredth floor. Where the rain falls.

There, the dynamic changes. The water is fresh. Thin. *Violent*, compared to the slow patience below. The kelp grows stunted, struggling against the interface. The shell-bearers are smaller, more fragile, their shells thinner, less armored.

But something lives here too. **Rain-catchers**. Cultivators who have adapted, who have climbed the full height, who have learned to survive in air that is *too thin*, too *dry*, too *loud* with its silence. They are fewer. They are *strange*, even to their own kind below—paler, more delicate, their membranes thinner, their pressure-sensitivity *acute* in air that does not transmit vibration as water does. They tend the stunted kelp, harvest what the rain-zone can provide, and they *watch* the sky.

They watch for **ships**. For **falling things**. For the **surface world** that they have never known, that they can only imagine, that they prepare for with tools and lines and the accumulated patience of generations.

They are the ones who might, someday, climb higher. Who might reach the sky itself. Or who might, someday, find that the sky is not what they imagined—that it is vast and grey and *normal*, and that the drowned world below was the *strange* thing, the *unusual* thing, the thing that made sense only in its own slow, patient, growing logic.

They are the **edge**. The boundary between the drowned and the dry, the deep and the high, the known and the imagined.

And below them, the structure continues. The city continues. The slow, patient, growing world that has learned to live in the memory of human purpose, that has made of that architecture something *itself*, something that will outlast the rain-catchers, that will outlast the shell-bearers, that will outlast even the **something larger** in the deep shafts, coiled and waiting and *hungry*.

The water holds them all. The growth surrounds them all. The structure remembers.

And time, patient as only deep water can be patient, moves on.
