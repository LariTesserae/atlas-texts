The primary inhabitants are not singular but saturated—a botanical density so complete it reads as a single organism, a green lung filling the concrete thorax of the place. They are the feral descendants of whatever was intended to be cultivated here, now escaped, perhaps mutated by the strange turquoise light filtering through thirty centimeters of motionless water, grown thick and strange in the warm, chemically enriched broth.

First, there are the **drapers**: ferns hanging from the overhead conduits in cascades of prehistoric architecture, their fronds unfurling in the humid stillness with a slowness that borders on the meditative. Each frond is a fractal study in green, and they do not sway—there is no wind in this cathedral, only the imperceptible tremor of cellular expansion. They are the elders here, ancient lineages that remember when the mountain was seabed, now colonizing PVC and steel with the same patience they once reserved for stone. They photosynthesize in the diffuse, shadowless glow of the grow-lights, their chlorophyll drinking in photons that hum with electric current, converting light into cellulose in a silence broken only by the subsonic thrum of the pumps below, a vibration felt in their tissues rather than heard.

Then the **carpet-makers**: mosses in thick, emerald velvet that cushion the walkways and soften the sharp concrete. They are the diplomats of this place, secreting mild acids that begin the slow work of erosion, turning the industrial edges into something approaching soil. They hold the moisture in their spongy bodies, creating microclimates of saturated humidity against the sweating walls, reproducing by spores that drift in the heavy, motionless air like green dust, settling into every hairline crack in the new concrete, germinating in the white, crystalline mineral deposits that the walls sweat out.

The **thirsty vines** are the adventurers, the explorers. Thick as a wrist, they descend from every grating and pipe joint, their tips quivering—not with wind, but with the cellular urgency of reaching, always reaching toward the diffuse glow above. Their roots dangle into the warm water, submerged forests of pale, hair-like filaments visible through the glassy surface, drinking the chemically treated liquid that seeps into your skin if you wade in. They drink deeply, bathed in this bath-temperature broth, and they grow visibly, extending centimeters over the course of hours, a jungle expanding in fast-forward but measured in millimeters, always stretching toward a sun that does not exist beyond the glass.

But beneath the visible green lies the true population: the **microbial multitudes**. The water, though it may be sterile of fauna, is far from empty. It is a living soup, a biofilm coating every surface—the pipes, the pump grates, the concrete walls—a slick, iridescent skin of bacteria and archaea that process the nutrients, that keep the water from stagnating, that are the invisible workforce of this ecosystem. Algae bloom in the clogged intakes, green slime that chokes the machinery, while colonies of diatoms etch delicate patterns into the still surface. These are the first colonists, the alchemists who turn the chemical broth into something biologically active, who prepare the water for the roots, who form the true skin of the contained sea.

There are no pollinators here, no insects to carry pollen, no birds to disperse seeds, no fish to graze the algae. The reproduction is vegetative, asexual, a cloning of success. The plants propagate by runners, by fragmentation, by spores that settle on the damp concrete and germinate in the mineral sweat. It is a monoculture in the strictest sense—genetically identical or near-identical, a single genotype expressing itself in fern-frond and moss-cushion and vine-thicket. They are all, in essence, the same organism, a distributed intelligence of chlorophyll and cellulose, communicating through chemical signals dissolved in the water, through root exudates and pheromones, through the slow, pulsing exchange of nutrients and waste across the root-mats that choke the pumps.

They live in a state of suspended animation punctuated by growth. Their metabolism is geologic—slow, patient, adapted to the weak gradients of this place where nothing rushes and the water level rises only a millimeter per day. They do not need to hurry. With no herbivores to eat them, no predators to fear, they expand into every niche, every channel, every basin. The silence is theirs; it is the sound of a world without consumption, only accumulation. The only movement is the rise of the water, and the corresponding rise of the plants, their tips always extending toward the humming lights, reaching for photons, drinking the warm blood of the machine.

They are waiting. The ferns wait for the erosion to soften the walls into soil. The mosses wait for the concrete to crumble into loam. The vines wait for the water to finally ripple, for a current to carry their clonal offspring to new ground. But mostly, they simply grow, saturated and still, a green heart beating subsonically in the throat of the mountain, the sole inhabitants of a flooded cathedral, breathing out oxygen into the humid, motionless air, patient, packed, and utterly alone in their verdant dominion.
