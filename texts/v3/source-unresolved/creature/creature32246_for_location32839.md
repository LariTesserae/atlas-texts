The inhabitants are **The Calcified**—not a single species but a distributed consciousness threaded through mineral mycelium. They do not move as animals move; they *accrete*, expanding outward by millimeters, their bodies the bleached coral towers and the pleated leather walls that fold across the terrace. Each structure is an individual, yet they share a slow, lithic communion through filaments of stone that pulse with metabolic warmth. They are warm-blooded in the way that stone should not be—press your palm against a fungal wall and you feel a thrumming, a geological heartbeat, the vibration of growth felt in the teeth rather than heard.

They are the inheritors of a vanished people. Where hands once wielded flint blades and copper awls, the Lithic Ones have absorbed those tools into their spreading mass. A knapped knife is no longer an object but a rib in the chest of a tower; a copper pendant half-buried in the crust has become a node, a ganglion in their mineral nervous system. They remember the touch of those hands not as nostalgia but as *thermal residue*—the stone retains a faint, ghostly warmth, as if the vanished tenders never truly departed but dissolved into the architecture, their bodies metabolized into the slow chemistry of the growth. The weathered ochre handprints on the fungal walls are not decorations but scars, the last visible strata of a culture that has been digested by its own creation, the boundary between tool-maker and tool dissolved into a single, breathing geology.

They live as hermits, these stone-people, each tower or low wall a solitary dwelling, yet bound together by the migrating water. The geometric films—hexagonal, iridescent membranes of mineral-saturated water—are their blood, their messengers, their circulatory system. These films creep uphill, defying gravity, drawn by the osmotic hunger of the fungal bodies, pooling in the folds of the lithic growths to feed them before evaporating into tracery of salt. This is how the Lithic Ones drink: not through roots or mouths, but through these slow-moving, intentional waters that migrate across the bedrock like amoebas, carrying nutrients from the frost-shattered stone to the breathing walls, then retreating before dawn, leaving only the glitter of their passage.

There are elders among them—the organ-pipe clusters that rise in dense, layered ranks, their cross-sections revealing growth rings like those of ancient trees, each stratum a year of patient accretion. And there are the young, the low curving walls barely three feet high, their surfaces still soft with uncured silica, trembling with the vibration of new expansion. They do not speak in frequencies the human ear can parse, but they *hum*—a subsonic drone when the wind finds the right resonance among the calcified towers, a geological song that seems to emanate from the stone itself, felt as pressure against the eardrum, a slow language of settling timbers and growing bone.

They are both architects and architecture, both people and place. In the scream of white-violet light and the silence of oil-black shadows, they are the only presence—the living stone that grows, breathes, and slowly, patiently, reclaims the small, bright, silent space. They do not conquer; they *become*, engulfing the primitive cairns, incorporating the stone hammers into their pleated flesh, until the distinction between the shelters they inhabit and the shelters they *are* has vanished entirely, leaving only the warm, mineral pulse of a hamlet that has learned to live at the speed of geology.
