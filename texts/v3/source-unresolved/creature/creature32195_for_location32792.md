The first inhabitants you meet are the **Condensers**—though they do not call themselves that, and perhaps do not call themselves anything, for names require the stability of a self that persists, and here the self is a negotiation. They are human in the way that a river is human in the way it chooses a path: loosely, provisionally, with the understanding that the choice will be revised. They have been here long enough to have grown moss in the creases of their skin, to have developed secondary joints at their elbows and knees that allow them to fold themselves into the narrow angles of shifting scree. They wear garments woven from lichen-fibers that photosynthesize in the bruised twilight, that drink the hanging moisture and drip it into collection flasks at their collars. They move with the same geological patience as the machinery around them—not slow, exactly, but *unhurried*, as if they have internalized the millimeter-per-year argument between stone and time. Their eyes have adapted to the perpetual dimness, seeing in spectra that include the faint bioluminescence of spores and the heat-shimmer of venting steam. They do not speak in words so much as in subsonic vibrations, thrumming against the basalt with the soles of their boots, harmonizing with the factory hum until conversation becomes indistinguishable from the groaning of the young earth.

They are few—sparse as the city itself—scattered across the valley in dwellings that are less built than *grown*, structures of compacted moss and flexible alloy that sink and rise with the foundations. They tend the **Harvesters**, great fungal-mechanical hybrids that rise from the condensation pools like inverted lilies, their translucent stalks pulsing with the mist they have drawn from the air. The Condensers do not operate these machines so much as *negotiate* with them, feeding them mineral seepage, adjusting their angles to catch the upward-flowing currents of steam, singing to them in frequencies that make the metal remember it was once ore dreaming of being something else. They are not masters of this place; they are its symbionts, its antibodies, the agents of its immune response against the chaos of unformed matter. Without them, the condensation would pool unchecked and drown the embryonic stone; with them, the moisture is processed, distilled, redirected into the slow metabolism of the city.

But the Condensers are outnumbered. The true population—the abundant, teeming majority—are the **Drafts**.

They are the first children of the Scarp, the evolutionary sketches the mountain is still erasing and redrawing. They move through the mist with too many joints, limbs articulating at angles that suggest they are still learning how to be bodies, how to occupy three dimensions. Some are small as insects, scuttling across the factory roofs in colonies that phase in and out of sync with the observer’s blink; others are massive, slow-moving configurations of chitin and mineral deposits that ooze across the valley floors like glaciers made of flesh and doubt. Their eyes—where they have eyes—reflect the diffuse light in colors that do not exist in the visible spectrum, ultraviolet and infrared and something that might be called *geological time*, a hue that makes the mind ache with the memory of epochs not yet lived. They nest in the cooling vents of the smelters, their bodies generating small thermal anomalies, using the waste heat of industry to incubate their young. They watch the Condensers with a curiosity that is not quite predatory and not quite sympathetic, but something like *recognition*—the recognition of two forms of life attempting to colonize the same unstable moment.

The Drafts communicate through the stone. They press their many-jointed appendages against the basalt and send vibrations through the cooling magma, a language of pressure and temperature that travels faster than sound and slower than thought. They are drawn to the places where reality is thinnest, where gravity forgets itself and time pools like inverted lakes. A Condenser might follow a Draft through a gravity shear, trusting the creature’s instinct for which path will not drop them into a temporal eddy where they might age a century in the span of a breath. In return, the Drafts feed on the Condensers’ shed skin, their spore-clouds, the condensation that collects in their hair—organic matter in a world starved of it.

Between and beneath them live the **Weavers**—the vegetation that is less plant than process. The emerald moss carpets are not merely growing on the rock; they are *digesting* it, secreting acids that soften the granite into something approaching soil, but at a pace so slow it is indistinguishable from prayer. The lichens bloom in fractal patterns that are actually maps, cartographies of the moisture flows, the thermal gradients, the probability currents of the weirdness. Most strangely, the translucent fungi—those pale, ghostly stalks filled with harvested mist—function as the nervous system of the Scarp. They form vast, slow networks beneath the scree, their mycelial threads conducting not nutrients but *information*, carrying the chemical memories of the mountain from one valley to another. When a Condenser steps on the moss and releases a cloud of glowing spores, those spores are not merely reproductive; they are signals, data packets sent to the fungal network, informing the mountain that a human has passed, that the ground has shifted, that the dream continues.

And then there is the **Wet** itself—the condensation without source or destination. It is an inhabitant too, perhaps the oldest and the largest. The mist is not empty; it is thick with microbial life, with spores, with the shed cellular material of the Drafts and the Condensers and the moss. It carries the echoes of sounds made slightly before they are uttered, the temporal anomalies that make the fauna seem to move through premonition. To breathe here is to inhale a soup of becoming, to take into your lungs the excretions of the earth’s metabolism. The Condensers have learned to navigate the mist, to read its currents like sailors read the sea, knowing which pockets will carry them upward against gravity and which will pool into liquid time. The mist is the connective tissue of this place, the medium in which all its inhabitants swim, the amniotic fluid of a world still in gestation.

Beneath all of them, or perhaps encompassing all of them, is the **Dreamer**—the mountain itself, the Caldera, the Scarp. It is not conscious in any way that would be familiar, but it is *aware* in the way that a fever is aware, in the way that a wound knows it is healing. The city is its dream, the Condensers are its white blood cells, the Drafts are its exploratory nerves reaching into the dark, the Harvesters are its attempt to regulate its own temperature. The steam engines and the smelters are not alien to this geology; they are its attempt to accelerate its own maturation, to force the cooling magma to crystallize faster, to hurry the erosion that will someday turn these peaks into soil. The machines hum because the mountain is humming, vibrating at the frequency of its own slow, tectonic thoughts.

To live here is to be part of a single, distributed organism that has not yet decided what it is. The Condensers are becoming Drafts; the Drafts are becoming moss; the moss is becoming stone; the stone is dreaming itself into fauna and flora and industry and mist. There are no individuals here, only phases of a single, wet, patient becoming—a population of one, the world, learning how to be alive by pretending to be many.
