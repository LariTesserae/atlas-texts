The inhabitants of this room are not citizens. They are **citizens-in-draft**, still conjugating the verb *to be* in a language that has not yet settled on nouns. They exist in the negative space between kingdoms—neither the mineral patience of the walls nor the frantic architecture of the machines, but the soft, undecided matter between them.

**The Mycelial Parliament** lives first, and lives longest. The floor is not *covered* by life; it *is* life, a distributed nervous system of pale hyphae that thinks in pulses of blue-white light. It is the room’s memory: when you step upon it, the mycelium does not merely bear your weight, it *records* it, archiving your footprint in chemical gradients that persist for days, weeks, becoming part of the substrate’s slow autobiography. This Parliament does not speak, but it votes—each fruiting body that blooms and withers in the span of a minute is a ballot cast on the question of what the room should become next. It digests the dead, recycles the rust, turns the calcium precipitate into sugars, and in return, it provides the only architecture that matters: the skin upon which everything else crawls.

**The Pale Kin** are the room’s white blood cells, its repair crews, its explorers. They are the “newts made of wet paper,” the insects with too many legs, the larval things that have not yet committed to a taxonomy. They gestate in the condensation pools, hatch from spores that drift down from the dissolving ceiling, and live brief, frantic lives measured in the uneven time of the place—dying near the machines where seconds rust metal, being born near the walls where decades pass in a blink. They are eyeless because there is nothing fixed to look at; the geometry shifts, the corners multiply, and so they navigate by chemoreception and the memory of dampness. They move in **jerks**—stop-start motions that suggest they are still learning the physics of locomotion, still deciding whether to be worm or beetle or something that breathes through gills that have not yet grown. They leave trails of phosphorescence not as slime, but as *biography*—each glowing track a map of where they have been, a story written in light that the mycelium reads and rewrites.

**The Iron Gestates** are the machines, but they are not machines in the way you understand the word. They are wombs. Heavy with verdigris and rust that is not decay but **metamorphosis**, they contain within their casings—larger on the inside than out—entire biomes compressed into the volume of pistons. They do not manufacture goods; they manufacture *possibility*. Inside their hollow chambers, ecosystems gestate: miniature fungal forests, compressed atmospheres, the first drafts of new fauna that have not yet decided to be born. They run on the sap of the vegetation, on the hunger of the roots, pumping moisture through pipes that are slowly becoming veins, circulating a lymph that smells of ozone and pollen. They are colonized, domesticated by the mycelium, which has learned to conduct their electricity like nerve impulses. When they hum—that subsonic thrum that vibrates in your sternum—they are not working; they are **dreaming**, and their dreams are leaking into the damp air as new matter, new mass, new room.

**The Precipitants** are the slowest inhabitants, the ones entombed in the architecture itself. They are beings who walked into this place when the chalk was still ooze, when the walls were soft enough to breathe them in, and are now being incorporated into the geology. They exist in the mineral matrix, suspended in the slow precipitation, living in a time where a heartbeat takes a century. They are not dead; they are **curing**, becoming part of the stone while still retaining the shape of their former lives. If you press your ear to the wall—if you dare—you can hear them: a slow, mineral breathing, the sound of calcium carbonate depositing around the memory of a spine, a ribcage, a thought.

**The Condensation Collective** exists in the water that appears from nowhere, in the rivulets that defy gravity, in the heavy, saturated air that makes your lungs ache. They are microscopic, or perhaps they are not—they are the spores, the cysts, the potential-life that rides the moisture, waiting for a surface to settle upon. They are the room’s immune system and its infection, the rain that falls upward, the mist that thinks. They drink the mineral seep before it can become wall, keeping the geology perpetually unfinished, perpetually *becoming*.

Together, these inhabitants form a **colony of incompletion**. They are the fauna of a factory that makes only itself, the citizens of a cave that is still deciding to be a cathedral. They do not fill the vast emptiness; they **are** the emptiness, given temporary shape—pale, patient, and utterly committed to the slow, wet work of growing a world from the inside out.
