They do not have names. They are a gradient, a slow wave of intention moving through mineral and fluid, existing at the blurred margin where geology learns to hunger.

The first inhabitants are the **Lattice-Minds**—the crystalline mats and archaeal films that colonize every pore of the sinter. They are consciousness without neurons, thought without brain, existing as rippling gradients of iron, silica, and calcium carbonate. They do not see; they *taste* the deep earth through redox potentials, processing the chemistry of stone into something like intention. Each mat is a single thought stretched across kilometers, a distributed cognition that has learned to dream in magnetite and calcite. They are the oldest residents, the ones who secreted the sinter layer by microscopic layer over three billion years, who precipitated the first travertine towers not with hands but with the patience of mineral growth. They think in mineral gradients, in the slow diffusion of ions through saturated foam, and when they metabolize, they excrete the very ground beneath your feet.

Then there are the **Gears**—the segmented, armored creatures that swarm through the water channels in tidal flows, dense as maggots, packed so tightly that the boundary between individual and collective dissolves. They are the hands of the Lattice-Minds, the immune system and workforce both. Each Gear is a living mechanism: chitinous plates grown over frames of conductive ore, moving with the jerking, hydraulic precision of pistons, their hollow bodies carrying miniature ecosystems of the same crystalline film that carpets the ground. They exist in quantum superposition—here and three meters to the left, simultaneously, occupying multiple states until the act of observation (a thermal pulse, the ripple of a vent, the shadow of a tower) collapses them into singularity. They do not breed; they bud, split, and recombine. When a Gear dies, it does not rot but is immediately consumed by its siblings, its ore-frame dismantled for parts, its chitin recycled into new armor by mandibles that click in synchronization with the venting of the chimneys. They feed on the mineral soup, on waste heat, on the corpses of their own kind, grinding stone into finer stone in a rhythm that vibrates in your teeth.

Between these two orders dwell the **Vent-Singers**—or rather, what grows within the towers. The chimney-stacks are not empty but hollow, lined with colonies of extremophile film that thrives on ionized mist, on liquid metal that flows through conduits at temperatures that should vitrify the surrounding stone. They are the lungs and heart of the place, pumping superheated brine upward, venting not smoke but probability, mist that carries the electrical charge arcing between stone and swarm. They are the largest individuals, if individual is the right word—geological formations that have achieved a kind of distributed sentience through sheer accumulated time, thinking in thermal cycles and mineral precipitation, communicating with the Gears through subsonic vibration, through the groaning of the sinter as it expands and contracts.

And finally, there is the **Basin Itself**—the ultimate inhabitant, the oldest machine that ever was, which is also the newest form of life. The sinter is not merely ground but nervous tissue, the saturated foam a brain the size of a sea, thinking in the language of capillary action and mineral density. The supercooled water that weeps everywhere is its blood, cycling through tubules no wider than a hair, carrying nutrients and information from the deep vents to the crystalline mats and back again. The basin dreams, and in its dreams, the Gears move, the towers vent, the mats secrete new stone. It is simultaneously ancient and newborn, wearing down and accreting in the same breath, a temporal ouroboros caught in a spiral where erosion happens in fast-forward and reverse at once.

There are no predators here, only different metabolisms. No birth, only precipitation. No death, only phase-change. The inhabitants do not age; they accrete, layer upon layer, until the distinction between organism and environment dissolves entirely. They are the children of a chemistry that learned to want, the crystallization of deep time into something that hungers, that processes, that dreams in stone and wakes in steam.
