The people of Velmora are *small*, not in any absolute sense—they are ordinary human-sized—but small *against* what surrounds them, against the density of life and wet and growth. Their scale is 1.1, the same as the place itself, a proportion that makes them legible as *part* of the system rather than its master or its victim. They do not walk so much as *negotiate*—each step a consultation with peat that remembers pressure, with root-rafts that remember weight, with laceweed that remembers touch.

They are *wet-adapted*. Their skin has changed, over generations, to be more permeable, to exchange moisture with the air rather than resist it. They do not sweat—there is no need, no mechanism, when the atmosphere is already at the boundary of saturation. Their hair grows long and is never cut, because hair holds water, because water held in hair is water that does not reach the skin, because the distinction between body and environment is less useful here than elsewhere. They braid it with bladder-membrane, with strips of treated laceweed, with the copper wire that is their most precious metal, the green corrosion on it matching the green of everything else. Their eyes are large, adapted to the green-gold filtered light, and they blink slowly, a moisture-conserving habit that makes them seem, to outsiders, perpetually drowsy or perpetually patient.

They do not *wear* clothes in any familiar sense. They *inhabit* garments—vests and cloaks of layered laceweed, grown to fit, replaced as they wear through, never washed because washing is impossible and unnecessary. The laceweed is alive when first worn, still connected to the colony tree through mycelial threads, and it dies slowly, over months, becoming softer, more permeable, more *them*. To wear a garment here is to be in constant, low-grade symbiosis, to carry a piece of the city's fabric on your body, to leave traces of yourself in the laceweed as you move through it—skin cells, oils, the particular chemistry of your sweat. The garments remember their wearers. They are, in a sense, *written* by them.

Their most distinctive feature is their *hands*. The constant wet, the constant work with living material, the need for fine manipulation of grafting, of suturing, of guiding growth—these have selected for hands that are long, flexible, *sensitive*. The fingers are slightly webbed, a trait that appears in other wet-environment populations but here is pronounced, useful for spreading water, for gripping slippery surfaces, for the particular tactile language they use to communicate in the green-dark. They *speak* with their hands constantly, a signing that accompanies and sometimes replaces vocal speech. The vocal language is loud, dense, full of clicks and wet resonances, but the hand-language is precise, capable of indicating direction, quality of moisture, type of growth, emotional state, in combinations too rapid for outsiders to follow.

They are *slow*. Not intellectually—there is no evidence of cognitive difference—but *temporally*, in their rhythm of action. A task that elsewhere would be completed in an hour takes a day here, because the materials are living, because growth must be *encouraged* rather than forced, because the peat beneath you is making its own decisions about whether to support you. They have learned to wait. They have learned to *read* waiting—to see in the slow curl of a laceweed filament, in the rising of a bladder-farm, in the particular quality of mist, the information that elsewhere would come from clocks or calendars. They do not hurry. Hurrying is dangerous; the peat does not forgive sudden pressure.

Their social structure is *distributed*, like the colony trees themselves. There is no center, no single authority, no building that dominates. Each root-raft household is a node in a network, connected by living bridges, by trade in materials and information and fire, by the constant negotiation of space in a place where space is *grown* rather than *owned*. Decisions are made by *consensus of presence*—those who are there, who can hear, who can respond in the hand-language, in the wet vocalizations. There are no written laws; there are *practices*, accumulated, transmitted by participation, by the slow education of children who learn to build by building, to grow by guiding growth, to die by being incorporated.

They have *names* for everything—the colony trees, the root-rafts, the rooms within, the bridges, the pools, the specific bladder-farm colonies. Names are not arbitrary; they describe function, history, current state. A tree might be called *The-Place-Where-Two-Colonies-Merged-In-The-Third-Wet-Season*, or more simply *Merged*, with the longer name preserved in the oral record, in the hand-language stories told around the maintained fires. To name something is to *know* it, to have a relationship with it, to be responsible for its continued growth. The people do not *use* their environment. They *know* it, individually, intimately, the way you know a person—and they are *known* by it in return, the laceweed growing toward them, the peat-worms concentrating where they compost, the hangers accepting their offerings.

Their relationship with the hangers is *diplomatic*. The hangers are not pets; they are a separate polity, with their own territories, their own resources, their own communication. The people of Velmora *negotiate* with them—offerings of fermented bladder-farm pulp, protection of hanger territory from human construction, the particular sounds that indicate respect, that ask for attention, that *pay* for the services the hangers provide. The hangers, in turn, provide *information*—their movements, their feeding patterns, their alarm calls indicate shifts in the peat, changes in water depth, the approach of bog-elk or other dangers. They are *allies*, not tools. The distinction matters here, where everything is relationship, where domination is impossible because the environment itself will not permit it.

They are *lonely*, perhaps, in a way that outsiders would recognize. The small scale, the few people, the constant wet that makes distance feel greater, that muffles sound into the dense roar—there is no privacy, but also no *solitude*, no escape from the presence of others, of hangers, of the overwhelming life that surrounds every structure. Yet they do not seem unhappy. There is a *contentment* in their slow movements, in their patient maintenance of fire and growth, in their incorporation of death into life. They have no word for "progress"; they have *continuation*, *persistence*, the long wet now that stretches in both directions, the ten thousand years of wet that preceded them and will follow them.

They *fear* the dry. Not death—death is incorporation, is continuation in another form—but *dryness*, the theoretical absence of water that they have no word for, that they can only imagine as horror. A fire that goes out, a structure that fails, a body that cannot be composted, that must be left where the surges might take it—these are their tragedies, their nightmares. They tell stories of *the dry times*, before the wet came, when their ancestors—if ancestors is the right word—lived in a place of dust and sharp light and stone that cracked and metal that did not rust but *held*, that could be shaped and was *permanent*. They tell these stories the way other people tell stories of flood or fire, of catastrophe survived. The dry is *outside*, is *before*, is *impossible now*, and they are grateful, always, for the wet that surrounds them, that processes them, that will eventually, patiently, incorporate them into the green and gold and endless wet dark of Velmora.
