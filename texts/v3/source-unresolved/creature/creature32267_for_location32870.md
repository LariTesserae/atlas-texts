They are the throat-singers of the high place, and you know them first by sound—not the individual voices, which merge into a continuous, resonant wash, but by the *texture* of their presence. When you stand at the rim and look down into the grey-green gloom, you do not see them as distinct figures at first. You see a movement, a shiver in the mist, a thousand ripples in the air like heat rising from summer asphalt, except the air is cool and heavy with moisture. Then the mist parts, or your eyes adjust, and you realize the bowl is not empty at all but *saturated*—a press of bodies shoulder-to-shoulder, fur brushing fur, the entire depression floor alive with a churning, patient mass of grey.

They are small, these inhabitants—no taller than a child, perhaps, with the compact, thick-limbed build of creatures who conserve heat in a world that should be freezing but is instead a sauna of stone. Their fur is dense and silvery-grey, not the color of rock but of the mist itself, so that when they move through the steam, they seem to dissolve and reform, to pass through phases of existence. Their eyes are enormous, black and luminous, catching what little light filters down through the permanent haze and reflecting it back with a wet, intelligent gleam. They have no hair on their faces, only smooth, pale skin that glistens with condensation, and their hands—dexterous, five-fingered, with opposable thumbs—are perpetually stained with mineral dust and the dark residue of the flint they work.

They do not walk so much as *flow*. Their movement is liquid, a slow migration of bodies through the narrow passages between the steaming vents. A band of them will emerge from a cavity in the basalt wall, a overhang that drips with mineral-laden water, and drift across the mud flats toward a new seep, their passage marked by a low, rumbling vocalization that vibrates in the chest rather than the ear. They are talking constantly, these creatures, but their language is not words so much as rhythm and pitch—a complex grammar of clicks, hums, and resonant barks that carries across the bowl and bounces off the curved walls in overlapping echoes. To listen to them is to hear the place itself speaking, the stone translating their voices into something geological, something that sounds like the mountain remembering.

The drumming is the heartbeat of their society. All day, in shifts that never seem to cease, they strike stone against stone—not the sharp crack of knapping, but a deep, hollow *thunk* that resonates through the bowl with a frequency you feel in your teeth. They have preferred stones, particular nodules of flint and chert that they carry with them, wrapped in strips of lichen-dyed hide, and they use these as both tools and instruments. A group will gather in a hollow where the warm water pools, and they will drum in patterns—complex, mathematical, passed down through generations of slow-moving time. The rhythm syncs, somehow, with the pulse of the vents, the hiss of the steam, the slow drip of water from the cliff faces. It is a music of containment, of life adapted to a boundary, celebrating the walls that hold them.

They are makers, but not builders. They chip stone with a deliberation that borders on meditation, striking flakes from cores with a precision that suggests not just utility but artistry. Each tool is shaped, used, perhaps discarded, left to join the mineral mosaic of the floor where it will eventually be buried by centuries of sediment. They do not accumulate. They do not hoard. A scraper used to process the tough, fibrous moss that grows in the warm seeps will be abandoned when it dulls, left on a flat stone where the steam will slowly etch it, where it will become part of the landscape rather than an intrusion upon it. Their technology is ephemeral, geological, a conversation with the rock rather than a conquest of it.

They live in the architecture of erosion. The basalt walls, worn smooth by freeze and thaw into organic, rounded forms, provide countless niches—caves no larger than a curled body, overhangs that shelter from the occasional rain, hollows where the warm springs bubble up through the mineral crust. Families or small bands claim these spaces, marking them not with walls but with scent, with the particular resonance of their drumming, with the arrangement of their tools and their sleeping furs. The boundaries between dwellings are fluid, negotiated through the constant vocal exchanges that fill the air, through the subtle postures of submission and dominance that pass between them in the dim light.

Their relationship with the heat is intimate, almost parasitic. They gather around the vents, not to worship them, but to *use* them—to warm their bodies after the nights when the temperature at the rim drops toward freezing, to soften the plant matter they eat, to steam the mineral crusts that they scrape and consume for salts. The steam is their blanket, their kitchen, their cathedral. They move through it with a comfort that suggests they cannot imagine a world without it, their fur perpetually damp, their skin always glistening, their breath visible in small clouds that join the greater mist. They are creatures of the threshold, of the warm pocket in the cold world, and they have no interest in the rim above, in the thin air and the blade-like wind that scours the plateau outside their bowl.

What lives here is a civilization of density and duration. They are not numerous in the way of ants—each individual is distinct, known, remembered—but they are *close*, pressed together by the geometry of their world, by the warmth that draws them to the center of the bowl, by the walls that make escape unthinkable. They have no agriculture, no fire, no metal. They have stone and steam and each other, and a sound that never stops, a drumming that turns their small, enclosed world into an instrument, playing a song that has been slowing down, deepening, resonating in the same hollow for centuries, for millennia, since the mountain collapsed inward and trapped them in the warmth, and they began, slowly, to make it theirs.
