The inhabitants are those who have stopped trying to speak.

They are the ones who press their palms flat against the warm copper rails and feel the heartbeat of the pumps conducted straight through bone. They dwell in the thickets where conductive vines drape the gantries like lianas, in the dry ridges between the basins where the slag pebbles have been worn concave by generations of tread, in the shallows where blind fish turn in circles too tight to be called swimming. They are not numerous. The Hollow’s carrying capacity is measured in ledges and niches; perhaps a dozen souls, perhaps two dozen, scattered through the crowded margins like seeds in a metal pod. They have no need for privacy in the auditory sense—the roar has long since taken that away—but they have claimed territories: a corner where the mist gathers thickest, a gantry that crosses at the hour, the warm shallows of Basin Seven where the water holds the heat of the exhaust vents.

Their bodies have been adapted, or have adapted. Their ears are vestigial, crowded shut by years of pressure, or perhaps sealed from birth. They do not miss airborne sound. Instead, they feel. The floor speaks to them in a vocabulary of tremors—the intake shudder that ripples through the ceramic, the long sigh of the exhaust, the hammer of pumps translated into Morse against the sternum. They wear resonators at their wrists, in the heels of their boots, small devices that translate the machine’s slow pulse into patterns readable against the skin. When they wish to communicate, they do not raise voices. They press a palm to a conduit and let it sing back. They tap rhythms on the gantry rails—three quick, a pause, two slow—and the message travels through the composite walls to the next niche, the next ledge, the next pair of eyes (or rather, hands) waiting in the damp. They use light: bioluminescent patches sewn into their clothing that pulse in the dim, chemical flares that bloom green in the humid dark, the slow blink of lamps modulated by breath and gesture.

They are gardeners, but the garden is a machine. They tend the aeroponic lattices where roots drink from mist rather than soil, pruning the vines when they grow too greedy toward the center void. They harvest the flowers whose stamens spark with captured current, collecting pollen that is also electricity. They are mechanics, but the mechanics is organic. They clear biofilms when they clog the narrow channels, replace worn panels with pre-weathered twins so the room continues to look like a place that has always been itself, scrape the limescale from the chilled ceiling plates before it blocks the condensation that feeds the hanging planters. They are priests of a liturgy measured in pressure and patience.

Their time is not the sun’s time. They sleep when the great ventilation lungs draw down, curled in the warm pockets near the exhaust vents, lulled by the subsonic thrum that vibrates in the marrow. They wake when the intake begins, the whole chamber shuddering with the inrush of thin high-altitude air being seized, warmed, humidified, forced to become breathable. They feed during the intake, when nutrient solution pulses through the conductive vines and the air smells of ozone and breaking green. They mate during the exhaust, when the room breathes out, when condensation beads on every surface and the mist rises from the basins—a time of release, of letting go, of moisture climbing the rails to feed the hanging gardens from below. They move with glacial deliberation. A journey from the eastern wall to the western takes an hour, and they do not hurry. The machines will still be breathing when they arrive. A repair might take a season. Moss thickens by millimeters. Rust blooms, stabilizes, is matched by a pre-weathered twin.

They have forgotten the cold outside, or rather, they carry it as a memory—a faint blue at the edge of vision, a phantom bite that never quite arrives because the Hollow is sealed. They are warm-blooded creatures in a warm room, surrounded by the waste heat of engines, the respiration of compost heaps, the sheer thermal mass of a factory-biome, and they are content. They have become native to the contradiction. They know the pebbles are slag, rounded in tumblers; they know the cliffs are stacked prefabricated modules; they know the rain is manufactured, following its slow ellipse beneath the ceiling plates, shifting across the court over days. Yet when the warm drizzle falls in the central court—when moisture-laden air rises from the basins and strikes the chilled grid overhead—they do not call it a simulation of weather. They call it rain. They stand beneath it, or walk through it slowly, and feel it on their skin: warm, mineral, faintly metallic, alive.

They know the moths are pale and numerous and bred for pollination, that the beetles with iridescent wing-cases are vibration-oriented, that the geckos sun themselves on heat grilles because they are cold-blooded and the grilles are warm. But when a moth lands on a shoulder, soft as ash, its wings beating a rhythm too fast to hear but felt as a flutter against the collarbone, they do not think of its engineering. They think of weight. They think of warmth. They think of the slight tremor of its thorax, a tiny drum announcing *here, here, I am here*.

They are the ones who keep the breath going. They check the valves that change state once a day. They listen—not with ears, but with fingertips—to the groan of thermal expansion, the shriek of water through narrow passages, the pneumatic punctuation of seals. They know the difference between a healthy roar and a sick one, between the steady-state ocean and the approaching shore of failure. When a machine begins to fail, they do not panic. Failure here is not an event; it is a weather pattern. They wait. They watch the moss thicken, the rust bloom, the biofilm reach its equilibrium. They intervene only when the equilibrium tips, and then they intervene with the patience of geology.

They are neither fully human nor fully machine, but something that has grown in the niche between—the Hollow’s true natives. They have no name for themselves, or perhaps they have many names, tapped out in vibration against the walls, passed from palm to palm through the humid dark. They live in the lush margins while the center remains a void, hung with chains and a lone maintenance platform and the pale motes of spores drifting in the flat light. They are the caretakers of the almost-empty, the tenders of the roaring, the quiet heartbeat inside the cathedral-scale silence.

Stand with them at the edge of the central court. Feel the floor thrum beneath your feet. Watch the condensation climb the copper rails, defying gravity, feeding the hanging planters from below. Listen—not with ears, but with the sternum, the fingertips, the soles of the feet—to the great slow breath of the room. The rain is preparing overhead, somewhere in the grid, to fall very slowly. The intake is beginning. The Hollow is waking. And they are home.
