The primary inhabitant is not singular. It is the *Weave*—the mycelial continent that dreams beneath the frost. It has no face, no center, only a distributed sentience that thinks in decades and millennia. When a Vitri tower finally succumbs after ten thousand years, the Weave does not mourn; it *remembers*. It pumps the dead tower’s nutrients through its hyphal highways—arteries of continent-spanning scale—and the other pillars brighten, their hourly pulse quickening, as if the network is digesting a memory. They sing to each other in infrasound, frequencies felt in the sternum rather than heard, a geological lullaby that vibrates through the polygonal stone plates and up into the thin, cool air.

Then there are the *Vitri* themselves—two-hundred-meter exclamation points of amber suspended in the white-gold glare. They are not trees but *ascensions*: fungal architecture mineralized into permanence, grown rather than built, rising from the plateau like frozen lightning. Their “bark” is a laminate of silica and chitin, porous and strangely warm to the touch despite the ambient 0.8-degree cold, housing within its layers a vertical city of slow-life. If you press your palm against the translucent wall, you feel not wood but *process*: a faint thrumming as the ammonia-based polymer sap—viscous, golden, never freezing—moves through capillary channels in a glacial pulse. Once each hour, a wave of bioluminescence travels upward, a heartbeat of pale green-gold light that illuminates the internal chambers. Through the amber, you can see the *lushness*: terraced gardens of lichen in russet and grey, bacterial mats glowing faintly in the dim interior, spores suspended in crystalline dormancy. These are worlds within worlds, ecosystems stacked vertically like geological strata, living on chemistry rather than water, condensing the trace 0.01% humidity of dawn mist into their structure, then respiring it back as silver vapor at dusk. They are dense, layered, teeming—a coral reef of the sky.

Within these towers lives a *microcosm*—communities of extremophiles that have never known rain, no rivers, no dew of any significance. They metabolize the viscous ammonia sap, breaking it down and building it up in cycles so slow they mirror the erosion outside. They are the Vitri’s immune system, its digestive tract, its memory banks. When the outer laminate cracks from thermal fracturing, these inner inhabitants seal the wound, precipitating minerals to heal the breach, working at a pace where a millimeter of growth is a triumph of centuries.

But the Vitri are not alone. Between them, across the frozen waves of silicate and salt, move the *Lithograzers*—tortoise-giants the size of buses, their shells laminated from the same white mineral dust that fills the fissures in the ground. They are ambulatory geology, slow enough to be mistaken for outcroppings until they shift. A crust of lichen grows on their carapaces between feedings, turning them into mobile gardens, drifting ecosystems that carry spores and nutrients from one dying tower to the next. It takes them a month to walk the kilometer between Vitri. When they feed, they graze on the fallen amber fragments of dead towers, grinding the mineralized chitin and recycling the fallen giant back into the plateau. When they step, the ground cracks with a deep, resonant *thrum*—the loudest sound in a world of 0.5 silence—a percussive note that travels through the stone and up into the thin air, announcing their passage to the Weave below.

Beneath everything, the stone itself breathes. The polygonal plates, cracked and filled with dust that shifts like crushed glass, expand and contract with the thermal cycle, creating the subsonic vibration that seems to emanate from the earth. This is the respiration of the world: a daily sigh as the silver mist rolls in at night and burns away under the bleaching light. In this breathing, in the cracks and the fine white dust, dwell lithophiles—chemical-eating microbes living on trace minerals and the faintest whispers of moisture, completing the cycle by breaking down the Lithograzers’ waste and the fallen Vitri fragments into the salts that will, over epochs, form the next generation of stone.

Together, they form a *choreography of stillness*. The Vitri pulse with light; the network dreams in infrasound; the Lithograzers migrate across the ancient, weathered plain; and the stone exhales. They are bound by non-aqueous chemistry, by the slow exchange of nutrients through fungal highways, by the recycling of death into growth. They do not hurry. They do not speak. They exist at the speed of erosion, thinking thoughts that take millennia to form, connected by a mind that spans the continent, singing in a silence so complete that you can hear the slow, geological thought of the world turning beneath your feet.
