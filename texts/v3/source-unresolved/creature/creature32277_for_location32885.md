The lithotrophs do not merely cover the floor; they have become the architecture. What began as a spilled-ink stain on rusted steel has, over decades of patient accretion, grown into a three-dimensional forest of chemosynthetic tissue. These are not single organisms but supercolonies—dense mats of bacteria and archaea interwoven with fungal hyphae that have learned to respire iron instead of oxygen. They are thick as a man's thumb in places, spongy and yielding, black as wet coal when saturated, turning to charcoal grey and cracking like dried mud when the air dries momentarily between the pump cycles. They exude a sweetish, metallic tang, the smell of geology metabolizing.

They grow upward now, defying the original horizontality of the factory floor. They climb the girders in slow, creeping waves, building stalactites of ochre and white where mineral-laden water drips and evaporates, leaving behind efflorescences of calcium carbonate and iron oxide that resemble cave formations. These are slow-growing chimneys and draperies of biological-mineral composite. The colonies pulse, not with a heart, but with the rhythm of the pumps that still cycle somewhere in the dark infrastructure. When the water rises, the mats expand, their surface pores opening like the gills of some vast, subterranean fish, filtering nutrients from the tea-colored flood. When the water recedes, they contract, trapping moisture in their gelatinous matrix, entering a state of suspended animation that can last for days.

Within these mats lives a second economy. The grazers.

They are the motile aspect of this place, the pale, translucent things that move across the gantries and conveyor belts. To call them "isopods" or "anemones" is to impose categories that dissolve upon closer inspection. They are colonial, each apparent individual being a cluster of hundreds of smaller units—zooids—bound together in a gelatinous matrix of shared cytoplasm. They are segmented, yes, but the segments are not rigid; they flow and reconfigure. A grazer might stretch to half a meter in length when moving, then contract into a fist-sized, pulsating bulb when feeding. Their bodies are the color of old bruises—grey-violet, translucent enough to show the dark smear of fungal matter moving through their digestive tracts, the slow peristalsis of nutrients being absorbed.

They move by hydraulic pressure. Water is pumped through channels in their bodies, extending pseudopodia-like feet that grip the wet rust with thousands of microscopic cilia. They travel in synchronized waves, a behavior that suggests a distributed nervous system, a chemical consensus reached through the slime trails they leave behind. These trails are not merely waste; they are maps, chemical signatures that tell others where the fungal mats are thickest, where the mineral crusts are freshest. The mandibles that scrape the black mats are not metal but chitin reinforced with silica, glinting like mica in the dim light, capable of grinding the toughest chemosynthetic biofilms.

They are cool to the touch—almost cold—and when handled, they leave a residue of slime that feels like cold egg white, drying to a film that cracks and flakes off. They reproduce during the high tide of the pump cycle, when the water rises to its maximum. Then, the colonies release buds—small, drifting spheres of gelatinous tissue that float in the current, carried by the slow inhalation of the machinery until they settle on new metal, new rust, and begin to grow. It is a dispersal strategy timed to the mechanical breathing of the place.

But the grazers are not alone. In the water column itself, in the tea-colored depths that pool in the hollows of collapsed machinery, there are other lives. The water is not sterile; it is a soup of dissolved minerals and suspended organic matter, and it carries its own fauna. Microscopic, certainly, but also visible to the patient eye: translucent worms that undulate through the murk, feeding on the detritus of dead grazers that sink to the bottom. They are the recyclers, the sediment-builders, turning the fallen into the foundation for new crusts.

And in the condensation, in those trembling curtains of droplets that hang from pipes and girders, there is an aerial ecosystem. The moisture that refuses to settle is not merely water; it is a medium. Here, in the boundary layer between air and surface, live the larvae of the grazers—or perhaps a different order entirely. They are thread-like, descending on mucus lines from the ceiling, swaying in the perpetual draft of cool air. They are pale as spider silk, feeding on the bacteria that colonize the droplets, the biofilm that forms on every surface where water meets air. When they are ready, they drop, falling through the humid air to land in the pools below, where they metamorphose into their benthic, grazing forms.

Deeper still, in the shadows where the walls meet the floor, in the stagnant corners where the water is darkest and the mineral sediment is thickest, there are the Deep Ones. The description speaks of "floating debris" and the "deeper water," but it does not name what waits there. They are not numerous. They are large, perhaps the size of a cat, though their shape defies description—something between a crustacean and a mollusk, armored with plates of precipitated mineral that they secrete themselves, a living scale. They are the predators, the apex of this strange food web. They do not graze; they hunt. They move through the deep water with a slow, sinuous grace, sensing the vibrations of the grazers above, the shift of water when the pumps cycle. They are rare, seen only as shadows, or as the disturbance in the sediment when something large passes through the murk. They are the reason the grazers move in synchronized waves—not just for feeding efficiency, but for safety, the many confusing the few.

The machinery itself has not been abandoned; it has been adopted. The pipes that vent steam are not merely leaking; they are incubators. Inside the hot, mineral-rich flow, extremophiles thrive—thermophilic lithotrophs that live in the walls of the pipes, invisible but essential, processing the iron at temperatures that would kill the surface colonies. They are the deep metabolism of the place, the hidden engine that keeps the chemical gradients steep, the food chain fed. When a pump kicks on, it is not just moving water; it is circulating life, a circulatory system for a body that is part fungus, part machine, part animal.

And the red light? In the half-submerged control booth, that single blinking light is the last remnant of human intention, but it has been repurposed. The grazers orient toward it, or away from it—no one knows which. It may be a territory marker, a mating beacon, or simply a point of reference in a world without sun. It blinks with an arrhythmia that suggests a dying battery, yet it has blinked for years, perhaps decades, a mechanical heartbeat in the cathedral of rust.

To live here is to live in slow time. The grazers may take weeks to cross the forty-meter basin. The fungal mats grow millimeters per year. The mineral crusts accrete like glaciers. Yet everything is in motion—the water, the air, the creatures, the slow weathering of steel into soil. It is a closed loop, yes, but it is not static. It is a breathing, a pulse, a metabolism spread across metal and water and gelatinous flesh. The factory did not die; it was transformed, its purpose changed from making things to being a thing—a living thing, patient and mineral and utterly strange.
