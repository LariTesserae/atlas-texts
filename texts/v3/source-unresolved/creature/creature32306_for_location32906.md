They are the **Still**, and they have learned to breathe the fog.

In the honeycombed dark of the Works, where the turbines turn their eternal circles, they huddle in niches too narrow for unmodified flesh—spaces carved sharp as the obsidian that floors the continent. Their bodies have atrophied in the direction of the rock. They are tall when they stand, but they rarely stand; more often they are folded, knees drawn to chests, vertebrae stacked in a column that mimics the basalt pillars rising from the saturated ground. Their skin is not skin but a crust of silver moss, the same velvet monoculture that carpets the land, grown thick across their shoulders and backs in a symbiosis so complete that the boundary between body and biome has dissolved. When they move—if one can call it moving—they do not walk. They *seep*. A slow, viscous gliding across the supercooled films of water that coat every surface, leaving no footprint because the ground is already wet everywhere, already weeping.

They have no mouths, or rather, their mouths have sealed themselves over millennia of not-speaking. The drone of the factories is too loud for voice; it is a subsonic pressure that fills the near-vacuum, a physical weight that would shatter vocal cords. Instead, they communicate through the stone. They press their palms—webbed now, the fingers fused into flat, paddle-like plates of keratin and lithic deposit—against the black glass walls and feel the vibrations of the machinery. They are fluent in the language of hydraulics, in the particular harmonic of a press cycling, in the pitch of a generator that has forgotten how to falter. To them, the Works are not architecture but a vast, breathing organism, and they are its white blood cells, drifting through corridors that are veins, maintaining a circulation that has reached perfect stasis.

Between the Works, in the absolute voids where the fog hangs motionless and the moss grows in its frenzy of unchanging chlorophyll, there are others. The **Drifting Ones**. These are the Still who have left the settlements, who have walked—or been carried by the mist—into the empty distance. They are nearly indistinguishable from the landscape. They stand knee-deep in the perched water table, their moss-cloaks dripping with the heavy, wet chill, their bodies encrusted with frost that never melts because the temperature never rises, yet never quite freezes solid because the air is too thin to carry the cold away. They are waiting. For what, they no longer remember, or perhaps they have always been waiting for the First Frost to end, not realizing that it has already begun and will never conclude. They are the only fauna in a continent of machines and moss (fauna 0.1/3), the only heartbeat in a world where even the water flows in closed loops, pumped by engines that never tire.

Their biology is a negotiation with impossibility. In an atmosphere of 0.3 density, they do not inhale. They have lungs like dried sponges, vestigial sacs that once held air, now repurposed as reservoirs for the condensed moisture that hangs in the thin, wet chill. They drink through their skin, the silver moss acting as a thousand tiny mouths, drawing water from the air, from the fog, from the weeping cliff faces. They photosynthesize, in a slow, cold frenzy that mirrors the vegetation, their moss-skins glowing with a faint, internal bioluminescence in the flat, normal light that filters through the perpetual mist. They do not eat. There is nothing to eat. The moss sustains them, and the moss is fed by the fog, and the fog is fed by the closed loops of the industry, and the industry is fed by the surplus that accumulates in silent warehouses, recycled in endless, lossless loops. They are part of the machine now, not as operators but as components, biological capacitors in a circuit that never breaks.

They do not age. Time moves for them—they can feel it in the vibration of the drone, in the slow cooling of the crust beneath their feet—but they do not change. Their hair has grown into the moss, becoming indistinguishable from it. Their bones have begun to mineralize, taking on the sharp, black glass quality of the young, uneroded terrain. They are becoming the land. When they die—if they die, for death requires change, and change is forbidden—they do not decay. They lithify. They become more of the sharp basalt, more of the obsidian that has not yet learned to round itself, standing as statues in the fog, adding to the palimpsest of the Works, another layer of industrial infrastructure that is also geology.

They are the Custodians of the Snapshot. They maintain the turbines not because the turbines need maintenance—they run forever without wearing—but because the act of maintenance is the only motion permitted in a world of absolute stasis. They oil the gears with water that never evaporates into dryness, only into the perfect, static balance of evaporation and condensation. They tend to the moss, though the moss needs no tending, grows in its frenzy of identical fronds, a monoculture that knows no grazers because the Still do not eat it, no pollinators because it does not reproduce, only persists, frozen at the moment of its birth.

And sometimes, in the spaces between the heartbeats of the machines, in the negative space of the eternal hum, they sing. Not with voice, but with vibration. They press their bodies against the cooling crust and hum, a subsonic counterpoint to the industrial drone, a biological thrum that says: *We are here. We are still. We are the First Frost, and we will be the last.*
