The water is the first inhabitant, and it is not a medium but a presence—thick, viscous, cold as a held breath. It remembers being rain, being mountain streams, and it carries that memory in its behavior: it roars uphill through the stone channels, hisses through cracks in petrified wood, and presses against your skin with the weight of three kilometers of altitude turned into liquid. It is freshwater, impossibly, trapped in a tectonic bowl, and because of this strangeness, it behaves like a living thing with a will, a nostalgia for falling, for rushing, for sound. It is the blood of the place, and everything else lives within it or because of it.

Then there are the kelp-trees. They are the forests of the Drowned Highlands, and they are wrong in their growth. Where plants should reach for light, these rise thirty meters from the silt only to bend downward, their pale, translucent fronds trailing toward the crushing dark of the ocean floor as if fleeing the surface. They lack chlorophyll—there is no sun to chase here—and instead they are filter-feeders, their broad cellulose sheets sifting nutrients from the pressurized water. They sway in currents that pulse like a heartbeat, the slow respiration of the super-ocean tides. Their growth is a surrender to depth; they do not photosynthesize but drink the dark, thickening by millimeters per year, their rings recording centuries in the span of a hand’s width. They are rooted in the sediment of the ancient seabed, but their “leaves” dangle into the abyss, creating a cathedral that hangs upside-down, a suspended woodland where gravity has been negotiated rather than obeyed.

Living on the kelp are the bacteria—colonies of cold-light that have turned the trees into lanterns. They are blue-green and violet, bioluminescent in a spectrum that barely pierces the gloom, pulsing in rhythmic waves that travel across the forest like synaptic firings. They are the nervous system of the woodland, or perhaps the woodland is merely their city, the cellulose a scaffold for their luminous civilization. Their patterns almost resemble language, or warning, a Morse code of light that blinks across the valleys in the eternal twilight. They are the eyes of this place, and they see without seeing, watching the vast empty spaces of black water between the groves with a patience that is not quite consciousness but approaches it.

Drifting through this forest is the marine snow—detritus from the world above that falls forever but never lands. It is suspended in the water column like ash in a dark room, dead things from the surface preserved in cryogenic stasis, too cold to decay, too pressurized to sink. They are the ghosts of surface life, hovering, digesting slowly, feeding the kelp when they settle, but mostly just drifting, a constant snowfall that never reaches the ground. They are the connection to the world above, a reminder that this drowned place is not sealed, that matter still cycles between the sunlit world and this abyss, carrying with it the chemical memory of rain and leaf and bone.

The wheels are inhabitants too, though they are made of stone and petrified wood. Their axles are bone or lignin turned to mineral, their blades worn into perfect circles by centuries of slow abrasion against the sediment. They turn against the current—not with it, but against it, a mechanical defiance of the tide—grinding with a low, geological *crunch-crunch-crunch* that vibrates through the water and into your teeth. They are powered by the massive, slow tides of the super-ocean, yet they resist the flow, turning upstream as if remembering when water fell from the sky. They grind not grain, but the silt of ages, the marine snow compacted into stone, wearing themselves smoother with each rotation. They are the heartbeat of the architecture, marking time in rotations so slow that a single turn might take an hour, or a day. They are inhabited by the memory of the people who built them, or by the sediment itself, which has become part of their machinery, part of their endless, patient motion.

Between the wheels and the kelp, in the terraced slopes where the mountain’s erosion has left steps worn smooth by ancient ice, there are the smooth walls. They are the most mysterious inhabitants, or perhaps the only true inhabitants left. Curved, seamless, glowing faintly from within with a light that is neither bioluminescent nor electrical, they are made of a material that is neither stone nor metal. They have no tool marks, no seams. They could be grown, like coral, but the scale is wrong—they are too vast, too perfect. They could be the works of the drowned people, but those people worked with flint and obsidian, with clay and reed. These are something else. They might be the eggs of something vast sleeping in the silt, or the excretions of pressure itself, the water crystallizing its own weight into architecture. Whatever lives there—or lived there—has left only these impossible geometries, these glowing curves that lap against the water in silence.

And then there is the absence. There are no fish darting between the kelp, no crabs scuttling over the stones, no animals with mouths to graze the flora or eyes to drink the bioluminescence. The water is sterile in its depths, too cold, too pressurized, too strange for the life of the surface. But this absence is itself a kind of inhabitant—a silence that fills the space where beasts should be, making the grinding of the wheels louder, the pulsing of the bacteria more insistent, the roar of the water through the uphill aqueducts more pronounced. The ecosystem is complete without predators, a closed loop of flora and microbes and detritus and machinery, an equilibrium of death and motion where nothing eats and nothing is eaten, only preserved, only turning, only glowing.

What lives here is a community of survivors: the kelp that fled the light, the bacteria that learned to speak in pulses of cold fire, the snow that falls upward into eternity, the wheels that refuse to stop turning, the water that remembers the sky, and the smooth walls that dream in curves. They live in the crushing dark, in the physical silence that makes the blood rush in the ears, in the loud grinding of their own preserved machinery. They are the Drowned Highlands—not empty, but full of a life that has adapted to pressure, to cold, to the absence of sun, growing downward into the dark, turning against the current, glowing in the eternal twilight of the deep.
