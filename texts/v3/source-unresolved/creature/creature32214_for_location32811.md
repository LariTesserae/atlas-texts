What lives here is first the crust, and last the void.

The crustose colonies are the only biological presence, though “biological” feels too active a word for them. They exist in the cooler fissures of the ceramic floor, those dark cracks that divide the polygonal plates like the glaze lines on a cooled vessel. They are gray and bone-white, fungal or lichenous, feeding on the silicates of the fired clay with a metabolism slowed to a rate indistinguishable from the stone itself. They do not grow; they persist. They are time-invariant, existing at the speed of geology, scattered across the terracotta expanses like pale thoughts in a vast, cooling mind. They are the only soft things in this place, the only things that were ever alive in the conventional sense, now balanced with the emptiness around them, neither spreading nor retreating, simply being, in their low-saturation way, the sole tenants of the cracks.

But the silence is older than the crust. It lives here as a presence, a thick, warm medium that presses against the skin and the ears with the weight of the sealed chamber. It is not the absence of sound but a positive quantity, mineral-heavy and dense, filling the space where light dies at the rim. It is the silence of a place that has reached its final temperature and will not change. Within this silence, there is only the infinitesimal creak of stone contracting as it holds its ancient heat—a snap so slow, so rare, it might be imagined to be the heartbeat of something vast and dreaming. The silence lives in the intervals between these creaks, in the pause between thermal pulses, in the absolute dark that tastes of hot dust and the faint, metallic tang of heated clay.

The heat itself is an inhabitant. A constant 2.3, radiating from the stone in invisible waves, it is the slow metabolism of the hollow. It does not fluctuate; it is the warm blood of the geological process, locked in stasis, a sealed system holding its warmth in perfect equilibrium. This warmth is alive in the way that a kiln is alive before it cools—except this kiln has gone cold in the sense that it has reached its maximum and will not rise further, yet remains warm, a paradoxical life of constant temperature. It presses against the skin, palpable and infrared, the only touch in a place where nothing moves and no wind enters to disturb the sealed air.

Then there is the ceramic itself. The floor is not merely ground; it is a vast, self-manufacturing organism that has completed its firing. Over millennia, the geothermal heat rising from below has baked the clay pan into this single, slow-cooked slab—terracotta and ochre, crazed with a network of fine, dark fissures that divide it into polygonal plates. This is the primary inhabitant, the room-sized void where primitive technology manufactured itself in the dark and then rested, untouched and undeveloped. The plates are its skin, settled and weathered, their edges worn smooth by a dry wind that scours but cannot alter the fundamental equilibrium. The walls are its shell, scorched and vitrified into black, glassy slag—natural obsidian melted and re-cooled into a sheen that catches no light, rounded by erosion into gentle curves where no sharp edge remains. This stone-creature lives in the state of completion, a single moment of geological time held forever, the pot and the workshop simultaneously, the product and the process indistinguishable.

The darkness is a substance here. It is absolute, thick, and warm, a mineral-heavy void that fills the hollows between the plates and the space beneath the vaulted ceiling. It is not empty; it is full of the pressure of infrared heat and the weight of the silence. It lives in the cracks where the lichens feed, in the vitrified patches of the walls, in the non-aqueous chemistry locked in salts and fired silicates. It is the medium in which the crust exists, the fluid in which the heat swims, the canvas upon which the ceramic rests, the palpable pressure that is the only illumination.

What lives here, finally, is the equilibrium itself. The frozen moment. The pause between firing and cooling, stretched into eternity. There is no fauna, no animal stirring, no insect, no burrowing thing, no echo of movement. But there is life in the mineral sense—the self-manufacturing process that prefigures pottery without ever being touched by a hand, the lichens that feed on fired clay at the rate of stone, the silence that presses like a living thing, the heat that radiates without end, the darkness that holds all in its warm, sealed embrace. It is a null ecology in biological terms, but in the terms of this place, it is full: full of stasis, full of completion, full of the warm, dark, silent presence of a world that has finished becoming and simply remains, forever, in the hollow.
