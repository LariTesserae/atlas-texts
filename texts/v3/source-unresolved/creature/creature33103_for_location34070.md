I need to let this breathe. Not rush to a single image. Let the inhabitants emerge from what the place *requires*—from its physics, its pressures, its slow violence and slow tenderness.

---

What lives here cannot be *fast*. Nothing that breathes in this air moves quickly. The wet slows everything—muscle, nerve, thought itself. So what lives here has learned to be *still*, or to move in the way water moves: through, around, patiently, finding the path of least resistance.

I imagine them first as *vertical*. Not tall—*tall wouldn't work*, too much wind exposure, too much cold, the mist would strip heat from every extended surface. They are *low*. Close to the stone. Close to the warmth that the rock holds, the slight warmth of deep water pressing upward. They move on multiple points of contact—not bipedal, not quadrupedal in any familiar sense, but *distributed*—hands and feet and something else, something that grips, that adheres, that knows the difference between stone that weeps and stone that merely holds moisture. They have *suckers* or *pads* or *something evolved in parallel*—not technology, *biology*, grown over generations in the selective pressure of surfaces that are always, always wet.

Their skin—if it is skin—must *breathe* water. Not through lungs but through the surface, the way the stone breathes, the way the lichen drinks condensation directly. They are *permeable*. This means they cannot be *dry*, cannot be *away* from the wet. They sleep in the condensation. They wake with it. Their bodies are in constant exchange with the air, the stone, the mist. They do not *sweat* because they are already *saturated*. They do not *thirst* because water is *condition*, as the place teaches. They *regulate*. They manage the flow through themselves, the channels and valves and membranes that keep the exchange from drowning them, from bloating them, from turning them into something else.

They are *quiet*. This is not choice but *physics*. The wet air carries sound strangely—absorbs it, reflects it, holds it in compression. A shout would be *rude*, yes, but also *dangerous*, drawing attention from the hunter, from the wrongness in the silence. So they have developed *languages of touch and vibration*. Their hands, their feet, their distributed gripping organs—these are *sensitive*. They read the stone. They read each other through the walkways, through the peat, through the water channels that connect everything. A tap. A pressure. A shift in weight on a wooden platform. These carry meaning as complex as any spoken word, perhaps more, because they are *embodied*, felt in the body that is itself a membrane, a boundary, a negotiation.

They are *old*, or they live as if they are old. Their city is layered. Their bodies are layered—scars of past saturation, past near-drowning, past moments when the water rose too fast and they had to *climb*, had to *find the high stone*, had to wait while the levels below filled and the peat swallowed what was built there. They do not *fear* this. They have no word for fear that matches ours. They have *readiness*. They have *knowledge of the signs*. The change in the hiss. The particular quality of mist. The absence of the pool-drinker's drinking. They *listen* with their whole bodies, the way the stone listens to water moving through it.

They are *few*. The density of the city is *presence*, not *population*. Perhaps they do not *breed* in any way we would recognize. Perhaps they *accrete*, like the city itself, like the peat, like the lichen crusts. A new one emerges from the old, not as offspring but as *extension*, as *layer*. They do not have *names* in our sense. They have *locations*, *functions*, *relationships to water flow*. They are *who they are* in the context of the wet, the stone, the slow city. Individuality is not *valued* here—not because it is suppressed, but because the conditions that produce it—dryness, speed, separation—do not exist.

They *tend*. This is what they do. They tend the channels. They tend the walkways, replacing the wood that survives only in the almost-dry, weaving new paths as the old ones soften and yield. They tend the gardens—*if gardens is the word*—the terraces of moss and wrong-grass and lichen crusts that they have learned to *direct*, to *encourage*, to *harvest*. They eat the pool-drinker, when it comes, when it is *right*. They do not hunt it—hunting implies pursuit, speed, the wrongness of *want*. They *wait*. They *prepare*. They *receive*.

They have *rituals*, if that is the word. Not religion—no gods, no afterlife, no meaning beyond the *next* saturation, the *next* layer. But *practices* that have become *necessary*. The way they greet each other in the morning mist, when the condensation is thickest, when their bodies are most *permeable*, most *open*. The way they mark the levels of the city, the layers of construction, the moments when someone has *added* to the stone, has become *part* of the weeping. The way they *listen* together, in the hush, for the signs that the Belt is *changing*, that the weather is *shifting*, that the slow balance is *tilting* toward release or toward retention.

They do not *write*. They *carve*. Not words—*channels*, *marks*, *directions* in the stone that will outlast them, that will be *read* by those who come after, who will not know their names but will know their *flow*, their *intent*, their *participation* in the long saturation. The oldest layers are *unknowable*—not because they are lost, but because they have become *the stone itself*, indistinguishable, *absorbed*. This is not tragedy. This is *the point*. This is what it means to *be* here, to *add* your layer, to *become* more of the world through your brief, wet, quiet presence.

They are *tender*, in their way. I keep returning to this. They touch each other constantly—not sexually, not romantically, not in any category we would recognize—but *to confirm*, to *exchange*, to *maintain the connection* that the wet air threatens to dissolve. They press their permeable surfaces together and feel the water moving between them, the temperature, the pressure, the *presence* of the other. This is *intimacy* here. This is *love*, if love is the word. The city is *hush*, but it is also *touch*, constant, careful, *wet* contact, bodies that have learned to be *porous* to each other.

And they are *afraid*, sometimes. Not of death—death is *return*, is *becoming peat*, is *joining the slow accumulation*. But of *wrongness*. Of the hunter in the mist, the thing that moves without sound, that leaves no tracks, that is *felt* only as absence, as the hush becoming *held*, as the silence pressing too close. They do not name it. They do not *understand* it. They only *know* it, in the way the stone knows water, in the way the lichen knows light. They *avoid* it. They *move around* it. They *survive* it, briefly, wetly, quietly, until their own layer is added, their own presence becomes *memory*, then *lore*, then *stone*, then *nothing*, then *everything*, the endless *return* of the saturated world.

They are *waiting*, like the Belt itself. Waiting for the mist to rise. Waiting for the condensation to form. Waiting for the pool-drinker to come, or not. Waiting for the hunter to pass, or not. Waiting for their own layer to be *added*, to be *absorbed*, to become *part* of the weeping.

They are *here*.

They are *wet*.

They are *the Condensation Belt made flesh*, made *permeable*, made *patient*, made *real* in the slow, the quiet, the saturated, the *cool* persistence of their brief and necessary lives.
