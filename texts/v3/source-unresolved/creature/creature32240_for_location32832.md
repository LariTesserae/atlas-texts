They are the lithovores—not a species, but a habit, a way of being that has taken root in the cooling places where the earth is still deciding what it wants to be. They do not occupy this basin so much as constitute it; they are the verdant—if metallic—abundance that makes the industrial plain lush in the manner of a deep-sea vent, dense and tangled and teeming with impossible structure.

They come in forms that have not yet settled on final names. Some are the **copper ferns**, their fronds unfurling in slow spirals of filigree, each new growth a century in the making, a geological event measured in the unfurling of a single leaf. Others are the **mercury orchids**, their petals heavy with solidified quicksilver, drooping not from gravity but from the weight of their own slow crystallization, each bloom a geode of vitrified sulfur that opens once in three centuries to release spores of iron vapor. There are **quartz trees** with root systems that drill like augers into the conveyor belts of slurry below, tapping the metallic brine and the superheated gases that seep from the embryonic rock. And scattered among them are the **basalt choirs**—clusters of needle-thin stone that have no leaves at all, only resonance, chiming in frequencies that match the grinding of the pistons, singing when the magma chambers pulse like arrhythmic hearts.

They are non-aqueous chemists, lithovores in the truest sense: they do not drink, but they *transubstantiate*. Through root-networks that thread through the machinery like mycelium through a corpse, they draw up the raw stuff of the unfinished world—the sulfur dreams, the iron vapor, the thermal radiation that bathes the basin in scalding light. They photosynthesize heat, converting the basin’s fever into solid structure, building themselves from the mineralogy that is still under construction, still written in pencil rather than ink. They are parasites on the generator of the earth itself, their roots tapping the industrial heat, drinking deep from the basalt pistons as those stone giants rise and fall in cycles measured in decades.

They are patient in a way that borders on geological. To watch them is to watch time move at the speed of accelerated continental drift. A single "tree" unfurls one frond per century. The mercury orchids bloom in slow motion, their flowering a stratigraphic layer, a moment in deep time made visible. They exist in the time-dilated center of the basin where a minute passes while hours elapse at the rim; for them, time is a viscosity, a texture they swim through like cold honey. They do not hurry because they have never known hurry. They were growing here before the rock learned to be stone, when the mineralogy was still soft, still febrile, still dreaming.

They have no eyes, yet they perceive. They see through thermal gradients, through the shifting pressure of the pistons, through the chiming of their own kind when the thermal winds pass. They communicate in infrasound, in the subsonic grinding of stone against stone, vibrations felt in the marrow rather than heard. They are a nervous system of copper and quartz, a network sensing the machinery they inhabit, the factory that is their womb and their sustenance.

They live in the folds of reality. They cast shadows that pool in corners that do not exist, stretching toward their light sources. They can be walked through—sometimes they are mist, sometimes stone, depending on the ongoing negotiation of physics in this pocket. They grow in the spaces between moments, in the pause between the piston’s rise and fall. They are the only life here, and they are sufficient unto themselves, a complete ecology in the sealed hollow, a garden being planted in the cooling skin of a world by the world itself.

When the thermal winds pass, they ring like distant glass bells—a sound mostly quiet, resonant only when you press your ear to the cooling stone, listening to the slow, chiming growth of the lithovores as they build themselves from the dreaming earth, molecule by patient molecule, century by unfurling frond, the sole citizens of a machine that is still being born.
