They are a sparse congregation, these residents, spread thin through the sixty-meter length of the nave like seeds scattered in a vast field. They do not gather; they drift apart, maintaining the distances that the emptiness demands, each occupying its own volume of dark water.

At the lowest level, where the hexagonal tiles meet the mineral bloom, the **Armored Ones** move. They are crabs, but not the crabs of shore or reef—these are dinner-plate vast, their carapaces no longer merely shell but accretion, a landscape of barnacles and tube worms that have made their backs into mobile reefs. They scuttle with a sound like gravel sliding over stone, leaving trails in the silt that never settles, furrows that persist for hours before the current slowly rewrites them. They are patient, these crabs, each carrying on its back a small ecosystem of filter-feeders and commensals, a garden that walks. They know the floor intimately, reading the satin sheen of the worn concrete with their many legs, finding purchase in the topographic swirls of exposed aggregate where the erosion has carved shallow valleys.

Above them, suspended in the middle water, the **Glass Ribbons** pass. These are fish, but fish that have forgotten the sun. Pale, eyeless, translucent as strips of old window glass, they move through the turbine housings in loose, drifting formations—never schooling, never touching, each maintaining its own orbit in the three-dimensional void. They have no use for eyes in this permanent twilight; instead, they navigate by the subsonic roar, by the pressure waves of the pumps, by the subtle vibrations of the contracting metal that travel through the water and into their lateral lines. They are perhaps four inches long, and they leave no wake, so perfectly do they match the density of the medium around them. When they turn, they do not flash; they simply become visible or invisible as the angle of the amber light changes, ghosts haunting the ribs of the vault.

In the arteries, the **Coiled Ones** dwell. Eels, thick as a man's wrist, have claimed the intake pipes as their homes, their bodies undulating with the artificial current that never ceases. They are the color of wet bark, of old bone, blending seamlessly with the manganese patina of the steel. They do not hunt so much as wait, mouths open to the flow, filtering the microorganisms that drift past in invisible clouds. They are the living cables, the organic wiring of the nave, and when they move through the pipes, they create eddies that disturb the silt, sending small brown clouds upward to join the slow snow of the deep.

But the true percussion section, the voice of the nave, belongs to the **Clicking Thousands**. Pistol shrimp, no larger than a thumb, colonize every grate and crevice in numbers that approach the geological. They sound like rain on a tin roof, like the snapping of dry twigs in a vast, invisible forest, sharp and percussive against the mechanical bass. Each click is a cavitation bubble collapsing, a tiny implosion that stuns their prey—the young of other species, small crustaceans, the unwary. They are the metronome of the place, their staccato rhythm overlaying the industrial drone, a ceaseless conversation in the dark that has continued for millennia. They do not sleep; they click in shifts, in waves, their chorus rising and falling with a logic no longer human.

Higher still, in the dim amber glow of the emergency fixtures that have burned down to embers, the **Film-Keepers** cling. Cyanobacteria, thin and photosynthetic, form a greenish patina on the walls where the light reaches—hardly plants, hardly animals, but the ancient lineage that first made oxygen, now surviving on the faintest photons of the dimmed sodium glow. They are the only photosynthesizers here, the sole converters of light to life in this sunken world, and they grow in slow, spreading mats that resemble spilled velvet, drinking from the water's dissolved nutrients, anchoring themselves to the calcified I-beams.

And where the water moves fastest, near the drainage grates where the valves hiss, the **Wave-Touched** anchor themselves. Strands of kelp—or something kelp-like, adapted to the pressure and the cold—wave in the artificial current. They are the verticals in a horizontal world, the only things that reach upward, grasping at the catwalks with holdfasts that have bored into the mineralized steel over centuries. They sway with a hypnotic rhythm, the only motion that is neither mechanical nor animal, a vegetal breathing that punctuates the stillness.

Drifting through all of this, invisible to the naked eye but present in the billions, are the **Drifting Sparks**—the bioluminescent creatures that turn the empty volume into a slow galaxy. They are small, perhaps colonial, perhaps single cells, glowing with blue-green light that punctuates the darkness like distant stars. They do not swim so much as float, carried by the currents, lighting up when disturbed by the passage of a fish or the thunk of a crab, creating trails of phosphorescence that fade like dying embers. They are the nave's own night sky, reflected downward into the water column.

And in the apse, turning with geological patience, is the **Breathing Machine**—the turbine itself. Though it is not alive in the way the fish are alive, it has become an organism of sorts, a mechanical metabolism driven by the pressure differential between the Nave and the deeper reservoir below. Its blades churn the water in a slow rotation that has continued unbroken for millennia, a heartbeat measured in minutes rather than seconds. The valves cycle like a respiration, pneumatic hisses releasing plumes of silt and microorganisms into the dark. The whole system breathes, and the inhabitants have learned to live within that breath, to time their movements to the drone, to nest in its pipes, to graze on the films that grow in its amber light, to thrive in its cold, constant embrace.

They are all of them strangers to the concept of "shore," of "bank," of "dry." They know only this: the six-degree water that fills the lungs of the world, the constant roar that vibrates in the sternum, the company of others who keep their distance, and the slow, patient turning of the machinery that has outlived its builders, now merely a feature of the landscape, as natural and as ancient as the silt that never settles.
