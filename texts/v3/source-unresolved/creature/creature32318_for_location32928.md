They are not colonizers. They did not arrive with tools or blueprints, did not raise the walls or string the bridges. They are the settlement's own dreaming, the accumulated consciousness of biological time pressing lignin against lignin until thought became architecture.

Begin with the **Drip-Lit**, those translucent colonies that hang in the cathedral voids like living chandeliers of gelatinous intelligence. Each droplet is a cell, each cell a blind eye that watches by sensing pressure and chemical signature. They do not breathe; they absorb—the condensation that never quite dries, the exudate weeping from the damp bark, the slow percussion of water gathering in basement pools. In feeding, they sing: a high, crystalline tone that harmonizes with the low, shuddering crackle of cellular expansion, creating chords that resonate in the sternum of any intruder, that make the teeth ache not from volume but from sympathetic vibration. They are the moisture given awareness, the seeping given voice, and they cluster thickest in the spaces where the light is filtered through membranes into something pale and diffuse.

Between the terraced balconies and the braided vine bridges move the **Resonance-Born**. They have no stable anatomy because they exist primarily in the vibration, in the wall of sound that drowns out thought. To perceive one clearly is impossible; they appear as heat-shimmers where the noise is thickest, as distortions in the warm air, as the sensation of pressure behind the eyes that makes vision blur. They are warm-blooded in a sunless place, their heat generated by friction against the biological thunder itself—the audible tearing of cellulose as the stalks expand. They feed on the growth, on the moment when lignin stretches and cracks. When they pass through the wide, empty streets—the metropolis of negative space, those twenty-pace corridors of packed earth—they leave trails of temporary silence, pockets where the growth-song drops away and the world seems to hold its breath, making the return of the roar feel like a physical blow against the chest.

In the cambium layer, where bark meets living wood, dwell the **Engravers**. They are flat, radial creatures, their bodies spreading like mycelial lace across the inner walls of the towers. They carve the doorways, the knothole exits three levels up, the impossible staircases that spiral upward into ceilings. They do not use tools; they secrete acids that soften the lignin, then reshape it with patient pressure, growing the architecture as they grow themselves. They are cold-blooded, slow, eternal in their patience, and when a tower reconfigures itself over the course of hours, pulled by thickening vines into new geometries, it is the Engravers who dream the new floor plans into being, their neural networks spread through the root systems like a second nervous system, weaving the city's recursive patterns.

Deeper still, in the hollows where gravity becomes negotiable and space is measurably larger than the exterior suggests, live the **Chamber-Saints**. These are the largest inhabitants, soft and massive, their bodies filling the voids—folding through geometries that would tear lesser flesh, conforming to interiors that are cathedral-scale and empty to the point of vertigo. They are part animal, part fungal mat, part architectural support, pressing their flanks against the spongy carpet of shed cellulose, secreting enzymes that digest the wood without consuming it. They sleep in the damp dark, digesting and maintaining the hollow chambers as living organs. When you step from a branch and fall *upward* into a hollow, landing in moss that cushions you with a wet, sucking sound, you are landing upon the back of a Chamber-Saint, or perhaps in the lap of one, and it does not wake, or perhaps it dreams you into being, its slow breath moving the condensation in rhythm with your own.

The streets belong to the **Limb-Wrong**. They are the predator and prey, the shadows with too many limbs, or too few, or limbs in the wrong places—jointed at angles that suggest broken Euclidean sense. Some move on too many legs, a centipedean blur of chitin and warm blood; others drag themselves on a single muscular stalk, peristaltic and inevitable, their bodies folding through the recursive patterns of the undergrowth. They chase each other through the empty plazas with a biological pace that is neither hurried nor slow, but inevitable. Their screaming, buzzing, drumming, and clicking layers upon the growth-roar until it becomes a weather system, a physical presence that makes your teeth ache and your vision blur. They are not cruel. They are simply hungry, and their hunger is part of the erosion, part of the settling, part of the equilibrium that is itself a kind of settled madness.

But the most numerous, the most present, are the **Echoes**. They exist in the folds where the sound layers upon itself, folding back on time as well as space. They are not quite solid, not quite memory, not quite fauna. They appear in the corners of the hollow rooms, half-seen, moving through doorways that open into impossible distances, traveling spans of time that feel wrong—too short or too long. They are the previous residents, or the future ones, or the residents of the space between heartbeats. They are the city's reverberation, the sound made flesh, or the flesh made resonant, living in the wet, resonant space between the growing and the breaking.

And beneath all of them, or perhaps encompassing all of them, is the **Colony**—the single organism dreaming itself into urban form. It is warm, low, damp, and loud beyond endurance. It breathes through the pools, exhales mist that condenses on high bark and drips down to feed the roots, which drink and expand and scream their silent, deafening song. The inhabitants are its blood, its dream, its immune response, its memory. They live in the cathedral of hollow wood and harder silence, growing, settling, and breaking reality in the space where the world ends at a rim of packed clay and packed root, and the interior begins.
