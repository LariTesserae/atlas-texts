The air is never silent; it is a resonant chamber, a vast amphitheater of stone where sound does not dissipate but accumulates. The primary voices belong to the frogs—dozens of species, each occupying a specific niche in the acoustic spectrum, each adapted to a life of perpetual damp. There are the *Puddle-Collectors*, small and stone-colored, who sit upon the warm bedrock sheets and call in high, bell-like notes that strike the flat stone and ricochet in perfect parabolas, creating standing waves of sound that make the humidity itself seem to vibrate. Their cousins, the *Pool-Dwellers*, are larger, with vocal sacs that inflate like pale balloons against their throats, bellowing in bass frequencies that travel for kilometers across the level plain, warning rivals and beckoning mates in a language older than the erosion that flattened the mountains. Between them, the *Film-Riders*—minute, iridescent tree frogs no bigger than a thumbnail—cling to the algae-slick surfaces, their calls so high-pitched they register as a continuous, shimmering hiss, a white noise that underlies the deeper croaks and bellows. They breed in the temporary pools, laying eggs in gelatinous rafts that drift in the mineral bloom, hatching into tadpoles that graze on the bacterial mats coating the stone, their mouths rasping the rock clean in tiny, patient circles.

Above this chorus, the dragonflies rule the humid air layers. They are not the delicate, ephemeral creatures of temperate meadows, but heavy-bodied, ancient-looking things with wings like hammered copper and stained glass, iridescent with structural colors that shift as they hover in the thermals rising from the warm stone. They hunt by sight in the three-dimensional grid of the plain—darting between the widely spaced trees, patrolling the edges of the pools where mosquito larvae hatch. Their nymphs live in the water column, armored and predatory, clinging to submerged roots and stones, waiting to emerge and split their skins, leaving behind exuviae that cling to the ferns like ghostly, translucent armor.

The mosquitoes exist as weather. They do not swarm so much as *become*—thick, drifting veils that shift with the slightest breeze, a living mist that rises from the shallows at dusk and settles again as the stone cools. They are not merely a nuisance but a foundational species, the blood-to-nutrient pipeline of the ecosystem, their larvae filtering microbes from the pools, their adults feeding the dragonflies, the birds, and the bats that wheel silently at the edges of the canopy layer. When they rise, the air tastes of copper and life, a humid soup that coats the skin and the wings of everything that flies.

The trees are the architecture of this world, though they are few. They stand kilometers apart, solitary monarchs of a forest that eroded into memory. They are figs, perhaps, or cypresses—gnarled, ancient, their bark mapped with lichen and their canopies flag-shaped by the constant, unobstructed wind that sweeps across the flatness. Their roots do not dig deep but spread wide, gripping the thin peat skin that barely veils the stone, drinking from the slow seepage of the weeping bedrock. They grow at the speed of geology, adding rings so thin they are nearly invisible, their wood dense and dark with centuries of slow accumulation. They are the perches, the nesting sites, the landmarks in a featureless expanse. Beneath their canopies, the temperature drops by degrees, creating microclimates of shade where moisture lingers longer, where ferns unfurl in the dim light and small mammals—if any venture here—might find refuge from the endless afternoon.

But the mammals are absent, or nearly so. The large vertebrates belong to older lineages. The wading birds—herons the color of storm clouds, egrets with plumes that tremble in the humid air, ibises with curved bills like scythes—stalk the pool edges with a patience that mirrors the land itself. They stand motionless for hours, one-legged, reflections doubled in the breathing mirrors of the water, waiting for the silver flash of fish or the stir of a turtle. Their cries are sharp, metallic, like stones striking stone, cutting through the insect drone and frog chorus to mark territory in a landscape where distance is measured in sound. They nest in the trees, building platforms of sticks that accumulate over generations, becoming part of the arboreal structure, slowly sinking into the branches as the trees grow around them.

In the deeper channels—sluggish, ancient veins of water that thread between the rock sheets—life moves with a different rhythm. Here the fish are small, silvery, schooling in the murk, flashing like dropped coins when light penetrates the canopy. Turtles slide from half-submerged logs, their shells encrusted with algae and generations of mineral deposits, making them look like moving stones, living fossils in a landscape that has forgotten it was ever young. And there, in the deepest pools where the water is thick with tannins and the warmth of the stone radiates upward, the saurians wait. A caiman, perhaps, or a monitor lizard the length of a man—its scales the color of wet slate, its eyes golden and lidless, watching with a stillness that predates the plain itself. It is a relic of the mountain era, a cold-blooded monument to the time when this land was high and dry, now adapted to the eternal wet, floating in the warm water like a log until the moment it strikes, its jaws closing with the patience of the erosion that shaped its home.

Beneath the visible world, in the thin skin of peat and the pores of the weeping stone, another community thrives in darkness. Fungal networks—mycelial threads finer than spider silk—stitch together the root systems of the distant trees, forming a wood-wide-web that allows them to share nutrients and warnings across kilometers of stone. Lichens crust the bedrock in patches of sulfur-yellow, rust-red, and pale grey, their bodies flat against the stone, extracting minerals from the rock itself, slowly etching their presence into the geological record. Bacteria live in the water film, in the condensation that gathers on the stone each morning, metabolizing the minerals exhaled by the earth, turning rock into soil at a rate of millimeters per century.

The polygonal plates of cracked algae and mineral bloom host their own microcosm: water striders skating across the surface tension of the pools, beetles that dive and carry bubbles of air beneath their wing cases, protozoa drifting in the water column like living dust. Everything is in a state of suspended animation, neither thriving nor dying, simply persisting in the endless, humid afternoon.

Together, they form a society stacked in vertical layers—the lithic base of lichen and microbe, the aqueous middle of frog and fish and reptile, the aerial superstructure of insect and bird, and the sparse arboreal canopy of the ancient trees. They are the inheritors of a mountain's surrender, the beneficiaries of its flattening, living in the thin, teeming margin between the stone that weeps and the sky that condenses. They do not know they are at the end of geological time; they simply continue, patient and loud, breeding in the shallows, calling across the flatness, hunting in the humid air, existing in the eternal, slow-motion present of a world that has reached its oldest stable state and simply breathes, drips, and teems.
