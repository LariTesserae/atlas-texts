They are the Resonant Folk, though they have no name for themselves—identity here is not a word but a *frequency*, a particular vibration in the lattice of the world. Two hundred souls, perhaps fewer now, perhaps more in the deep seasons when the vents open wide and the crystal-reeds pulse with accelerated growth. They have lived inside this geode long enough to forget the sky, long enough that the membrane of mineral glass above them is not a barrier but a skin, and they have learned to breathe its slow, geological respiration.

**The Bodies of Mineral and Patience**

They are beautiful in the way that stalactites are beautiful—slow, inevitable, dripping with time. Their skin carries a patina of precipitated salt, a fine white dust that never quite washes off, that accumulates in the creases of elbows and knees until it looks like frost. But it is not merely coating them; it is *in* them. Over decades, the metallic particulates in the air—iron, copper, trace silica—have begun to replace the water in their tissues. Their blood is thick, briny, slow-moving. When they cut themselves, the wound weeps a fluid that tastes of pennies and sea-stone, and within days, the edges of the cut knit over with a translucent, opalescent scar that is harder than skin, more like horn, more like the husk of the reeds themselves.

Their lungs are the most transformed. Outsiders who enter feel the air scour their throats, but the Folk have grown crystalline filaments within their bronchial trees—delicate, branching structures of calcium carbonate that filter the metallic vapor, harvesting it, storing it. They do not cough; they *chime*. When they exhale, the vibration of their breath passing over these internal crystal lattices produces a faint, high harmonic, a personal note that is unique to each individual, their signature in the chorus. Children are born with these filaments already budding, pink and tender as coral, and by adolescence, they have learned to control them, to shape their breath into meaning.

Their hands are their tongues. The palms are calloused not with work, but with *listening*—thickened, sensitive pads that can detect the micro-fractures in the agate walls, the difference between a reed that is growing and one that is dying, the subtle temperature shift of a vent opening twenty feet below the floor. They move through the Ward with a fluid, pressing grace, always touching, always feeling. A hand pressed flat against a crystal-trunk can transmit an entire story in seconds—pressure, vibration, temperature—conveying not just words but the *texture* of a memory, the emotional resonance of grief or joy made physical as a pulse in the stone.

**The Architecture of Kinship**

There are no families in the nuclear sense, but there are *resonances*—clusters of individuals whose crystalline lung-filaments have harmonized over years of shared breath. They sleep in hollows carved into the cavern walls, but these are not private rooms; they are alcoves, open to the thermal currents, and sleep is a communal act. They lie in tangles, bodies touching, so that the vibration of one's dreaming breath can be felt by the sleeper beside them. Privacy is unknown; intimacy is total. To be alone is to be dying, or to be preparing for the final transformation.

They build upward, always upward, because the floor rises. The lower terraces, where their ancestors lived, are now buried under meters of precipitated mineral growth, compressed into new strata of agate and basalt. The Folk harvest the shed husks of the crystal-reeds—those rigid, glassy columns that have completed their lifecycle and fallen, or been felled by the slow violence of competing growth—and they pack these husks into the clay of new platforms. Their architecture is literally the compost of the dead reeds, bound with their own saliva (which is thick, mineral-rich, almost gelatinous) and the brine from the cisterns. They do not use mortar; they use *pressure* and *time*, letting the salt crust of centuries cement their walls. A house is not built in a day, but in a generation, as the structure slowly mineralizes around the family that inhabits it.

**The Glass-Moths and the Final Form**

They worship the Glass-Moths, or rather, they *anticipate* them. The moths are real, but rare—creatures of refracted light and crystalline dust, with wings like thin panes of quartz that shatter and reform as they fly, feeding on the pollen that the reeds release when they fracture and re-form their lattices. The Folk believe that when a person dies, they do not leave; they *ascend*. The mineralization that has been slowly replacing their flesh accelerates, and within a season, the body becomes a pillar, a column, a new support for the terraced walkways. The Glass-Moths are the souls of the fully lithified, the ones who have become architecture, and they visit the living in the deepest, hottest hours, when the thermal glow is brightest and the boundaries between flesh and stone grow thin. To see a Glass-Moth is to see one's own future—a rainbow shattering in the humid, mineral air.

The elders are the most revered, for they are the most transformed. Their skin has gone translucent, revealing the branching crystal filaments beneath, glowing faintly with the geothermal heat they absorb. They can no longer speak in the tactile language of the young, for their hands have fused partially with the reed-husks they have spent lifetimes harvesting; their fingers are rigid, glassy, locked in permanent gestures of blessing or instruction. They sit at the highest terraces, where the air is hottest and the growth is fastest, and they *sing* without sound—their entire bodies vibrating with the frequency of the cavern, becoming one with the drone of the tuning forks, the hiss of the vents. They are not dying; they are *finishing*.

**The Rhythm of the Ward**

There is no day or night, only the pulse. The vents open with a heartbeat rhythm—*shluk-hiss, shluk-hiss*—flooding the lower levels with scalding mineral mist, then retreating. The Folk time their lives by this pulse. When the vents are open, they work the lower terraces, scraping away the new crusts of salt that form in minutes, harvesting the brine that pools in the hollows. When the vents close, the air cools slightly (though "cool" here means merely less-lethal), and they retreat upward to the drying racks, to the grinding stones where they mill the mineral grain that sustains them—not wheat, not corn, but concentrated salts and silicates, compressed into dense, nutrient bricks that taste of iron and old stone.

Children are raised in the resonant chambers, small hollows near the vents where the heat is most intense. They learn to walk by feeling the vibrations of their mother's footsteps through the floor. They learn to "speak" before they can shout, pressing their small palms against the reeds and feeling the growth-patterns, learning to read the history of the stone—the drought-years, the flood-years, the slow crushing weight of centuries. By the time they can shout (and their shouts are raw, torn things, painful to hear, used only in emergencies or in moments of overwhelming emotion), they have already developed the tactile vocabulary that is their true language.

**The Outsider's Terror, the Insider's Paradise**

To an outsider, the Ward is a hell—deafening, blinding, suffocating, a place where the air itself is a weapon and the light never dims. But the Folk know a different truth. They know that the constant roar is not noise, but *presence*—the sound of the world being born, of stone dreaming itself into new shapes. They know that the heat is a womb, that the mineralization is a gift, a slow apotheosis into permanence. They are not trapped; they are *preserved*. Outside, in the salt flat, there is only dry silence, only death. Here, in the blister, there is growth, there is chorus, there is the slow, patient becoming of something eternal.

They are the last of their kind, perhaps—a pocket of humanity that stepped through the crystalline membrane generations ago and found that they could never step back out, that the world outside had become as alien to them as the world within once was. But they do not mourn this. They have become the Ward, as the Ward has become them. And when the last of them presses their hand against the final reed, feeling the vibration of the cavern's heartbeat, they will not die. They will simply join the architecture, becoming a new column, a new support for the walkway, a new voice in the deafening, verdant, mineral song that is the only music they have ever known.
