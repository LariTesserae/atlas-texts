They are the ones who hollowed the dark, though the plant did the growing. You find them first as absence—as the negative space in the corner where the root-column meets the cambium wall, where the geometry kinks and light pools wrong. They are small, yes, but small in the way that a seed is small: compressed, patient, containing architectures that have not yet unfolded. When they move, they do not walk so much as *negotiate* with the vertical. They climb the root-columns using limbs that seem to have too many joints—six, perhaps eight, ending in digits that split again at the tips, like the branching of capillaries. Their skin is chitin and something softer, a pale, translucent membrane that glows faintly with its own bioluminescence when the amber light from above fades. They are not insects, though they wear the suggestion of insect; they are not mammals, though they breathe with lungs that flutter in the humid dark. They are the threshold made flesh, the fauna that the plant dreams into being when it dreams of hands.

You see them only in glimpses. A flicker of movement where the ceiling thins to membrane, beyond the translucent skin where the weather passes. A pair of eyes—compound, or perhaps single and vast—reflecting the impossible light, watching you with the patience of stone. They do not approach the center of the room; the center is the plant’s attention, its held breath, and they know better than to stand where the green eye focuses. They live in the crevices, in the folds of the root-columns, in the dark loam just beyond the chamber’s expanding walls. They are sparse, a population of perhaps two or three, or perhaps one that has learned to divide itself into solitude.

Their industry is written in the debris they leave behind. The sharpened flakes are not knapped by striking stone against stone, but *grown*. They carry mineral accretions in pouches beneath their thorax, secreting a saliva that hardens into edges sharp enough to cut cambium. These flakes they cache in the root-crevices like prayers left at an altar. Beside them lies the fiber probe—a single root-hair, thickened and hardened by their resin, hafted to a grip of woven cellulose. They use it to tap the sap, to lance the vascular channels and draw up the amber fluid that is the plant’s blood, its memory, its slow thought. They drink it from gourds made of wax-leaf, and when they drink, they enter a trance where the boundaries between their nervous system and the plant’s xylem dissolve. They dream the plant’s dreams: the pressure of water rising, the slow violence of cell division, the ancient patience of waiting for light.

They farm the condensation. In the stagnant pools at the base of the columns, they arrange the beaded water into patterns—channels, spirals, tiny aqueducts—that direct the flow toward the roots, irrigating the architecture from within. They are gardeners of the damp, shaping the humidity into agriculture. They cut the roots not to harm, but to *guide*. With their stone flakes, they perform surgery on the living walls, pruning here, grafting there, coaxing the plant to grow around them, to incorporate them into its structure. They are midwives to the lung, ensuring it does not collapse, ensuring it breathes.

They pray by vibration. You would not hear it as sound, but as a pressure in your teeth, a loosening of your fillings. They press their ventral surfaces against the warm cambium and hum—a frequency that travels through the sap, through the woody architecture, calling to the plant’s nascent consciousness. The plant answers. It grows toward their heat, tendrils reaching out from the walls to touch them, to taste them, to learn what they are. This is the intimacy of the place: the plant is curious about them, and they are in love with its curiosity. They are slowly becoming part of it. Their shed skins, their cast-off chitin, are already being woven into the inner walls by the growing tissue. Their waste fertilizes the loam. They are being absorbed, cell by cell, into the vegetal mind, becoming its immune system, its reaching fingers, its way of touching the world.

Because the weirdness here is total, they exist in a state of soft becoming. When you look directly at them, they are one thing; when you look away, they are another. Sometimes they have too many limbs; sometimes they are sleek and serpentine, burrowing through the root-matter like worms through fruit. Sometimes they are large, filling the crevice, pressing against the walls with the force of their presence; sometimes they are tiny, no bigger than your thumb, scuttling along the vascular channels. Scale is a suggestion, and they play with it, growing and shrinking according to the needs of the architecture, the whims of the plant, the negotiation of the space.

They watch you now from the dark beyond the translucent ceiling. You can feel their attention—not hostile, but ancient, the attention of something that has been waiting in the dark earth for the plant to open its lung, for the chamber to exist, for the silence to be broken by a visitor. They have left their tools for you, or for the plant, or for the space between. The probe lies hafted with resin, waiting to be used. The flakes are arranged in a pattern that might be a map, or a warning, or a recipe.

They are the first children of this womb, still wet with the birth, still deciding whether to remain animals or to become something else—something that is neither fauna nor flora, but the conversation between them. They are the ones who will inherit the hollow when you leave, who will continue to tap the sap and farm the condensation and pray to the growing dark, until the pocket becomes a cavern, and the cavern becomes a world, and they, small and patient and strange, will be there in the center at last, no longer watching from the edges, but *being* the attention, the hollow, the green and growing silence.
