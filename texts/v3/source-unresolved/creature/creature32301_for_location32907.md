What lives here begins with the thinnest skin. The chemolithotrophs do not colonize so much as *inhabit* the boundaries—those narrow thermal margins where the stone's residual warmth bleeds into the four-degree flood. They are not green; they are the color of old bone, of pearl left too long in vinegar, of the ghost-film that forms on forgotten jars. They do not photosynthesize. They eat iron. They breathe the slow oxidation of the gear-teeth, consuming the rust that has reached its terminal state not as decay but as saturation, a final meal. Under the diffuse green-blue light, these films appear as topographical maps drawn in pale ink, contour lines of metabolism etched onto steel. They are the only vegetation, yes, but they are more accurately the place's slow dermis, a living patina that has learned to feed on permanence itself. Where they touch the metal, the boundary softens; the bacteria do not distinguish between machine and mountain, seeing both simply as mineral, as sustenance.

But life here is not confined to the surfaces. It hangs suspended in the water column. The particulate constellations—those mineral precipitates drifting in the light—are not dead dust but the shed thoughts of the bacterial films, calcium carbonate and silica particles moving in their own slow, staticky orbits, motionless yet never still in their potential. They are the snow of this place, falling upward in reverse, accumulating not on the floor but in the luminous column, a blizzard of geology that never settles. To be among them is to drift through the exhalations of the stone, through the dandruff of centuries suspended in amber light.

Then there is the resident that has no body but occupies every cubic centimeter: the hum. It is not sound, exactly, but pressure—a subsonic chord that vibrates in the sternum, in the marrow, in the fluid of the inner ear. It is the harmonic of mass under load, the gears locked in permanent mesh singing their inability to turn, the residual vibration of iron remembering its purpose. This is the voice of the factory, and it has not stopped speaking; it has simply lowered its register below the threshold of hearing, becoming a constant, a drone that underlies all other silence. To live here is to be acoustically haunted, to feel the subvocal murmur of industry in a state of dreamless sleep.

And the droplet. The droplet is not an inhabitant but a visitor, yet it is the only thing that moves, the only heartbeat in the stopped clock. It releases from the stalactite—a formation that is itself a slow animal, growing by accretion, by the mineral patience of dripping time—and falls for seconds through the viscous coolness. Its impact is a *tick*, a punctuation mark in the sentence of silence. It is the messenger between the ceiling and the floor, between the unseen blue distance above and the debris-strewn geology below. In its descent, it carries the chemistry of elsewhere down to the rust, a communion of elements, a brief, liquid life that ends in dissolution against a gear-tooth.

There are also the currents that are not currents: the faint, phantom trickle of electricity in the conduits, a current so minimal it generates no heat, a ghost of power still haunting the wires, feeding perhaps the narrowest band of microbial life where metal meets water. It is a nervous system without a brain, a synaptic flicker in the dark, the last thought of a machine that once ran on fire and steam.

But perhaps the truest resident is the stasis itself. The chamber is described as the held breath of something vast. If that is so, then the occupant is the breath-holder, the vast thing that has paused, that has stopped time to examine its own machinery. The thermodynamic equilibrium is not death but a kind of meditation, a samadhi of iron and water. What lives here is a state of being rather than a creature. It is the life of the equilibrium point, where erosion and accretion have balanced, where rust and calcite have married, where the factory has achieved its dream not of perpetual motion but of perpetual *presence*.

There is no hunger here. No growth, save the microscopic and the mineral. No death, save the slow completion of chemical reactions. What lives here is a form of consciousness without neurons, an awareness distributed through the limestone bowl, the gear-trains, the bacterial films, and the hanging water. It is a mind that thinks in millennia, that dreams in subsonic frequencies, that tastes the iron and limestone on a tongue of absolute stillness.

To descend into this place is to enter the body of a thought that has not yet finished forming. The machinery is its skeleton, the water its blood (stagnant, cool, nearly anoxic), the bacteria its diffuse nervous system—slow, chemical, lithic. The droplets are its synapses firing, the hum its subvocal murmur, the mineral snow its memories drifting in the green-blue dark.

Nothing swims here because swimming is a violence against this place. The only movement is the fall, the accretion, the chemical exchange at the boundaries. The inhabitants are patient. They have all the time that has ever been. They are the Anthropocene's afterlife, the slow digestion of industry by geology, a metabolism so vast and slow that it has become indistinguishable from the rock itself. They live in the pause between the tick and the tock, in the held breath, in the perfect, terrible stillness where nothing changes and everything, finally, is complete.
