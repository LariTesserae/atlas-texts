The primary inhabitant is the mass itself—the peat, that compressed archive of dead sphagnum and centuries of withered leaf, which has achieved a kind of sentience through sheer density. It is not soil but body: a spongy, breathing torso that occupies the hollow completely. You could say it lives here the way a lung lives in a chest, expanding and contracting with the diurnal shift of temperature, drawing in the cold air and exhaling moisture. It is warm, paradoxically, at its core—a geothermal whisper beneath the feet—while its surface chills. The peat is the memory of the place, the sediment of time that refuses to decay because it is too saturated, too acidic, too cold. It is a being that grows upward, millimeter by millimeter, filling the cirque, raising its floor, consuming the wound from within. It is the landlord and the architecture, the breathing substrate upon which everything else must perch.

Then there are the stunted willows and dwarf birch—the elders, though they are no taller than a kneeling person. They have been twisted by wind into horizontal sculptures, lying parallel to the ground like prayers that have given up on heaven. They do not stand; they sprawl. Their roots knit into the peat to form load-bearing walls, meaning these trees are literally the structure of the room, the studs and beams of this organic cathedral. They are ancient in their slowness, having grown for decades to achieve a height that elsewhere would be achieved in a season. They live in a state of suspended animation, their leaves small and waxy to hold the moisture that is their constant companion.

Above them hang the curtains of fruticose lichen and reindeer moss—gray-green, pendant, filtering the light. They are the atmosphere made fabric. They do not root in soil but drink directly from the saturated air, extracting nitrogen and moisture from the fog that perpetually shrouds the hollow. They live as a collective, a single organism draped in tatters, swaying in a wind that never quite arrives, only presses against the dense walls and dies. They are the skin of the place, the permeable membrane between the stone and the interior, and they are perpetually damp, dripping with the condensation they harvest.

Dotting the surface of the peat, like pustules or jewels, live the sundews and butterworts—the carnivores. They are the only violence here, but it is a violence of patience. Their leaves are sticky with mucilage, glistening with trapped moisture and the rare, unfortunate insect that blundered into this green twilight. They digest slowly, over days, in the cold. They are the hunters of the megalopolis, the ones who supplement the meager nutrients of the peat with the flesh of the airborne. They live in the light, such as it is, small and red and waiting.

Jutting from the compressed walls like balconies or shelves live the fungal brackets—the decomposers, the recyclers, the undertakers of the plant world. They are the ones who turn the dead back into peat, who add their own bodies to the accumulation. But they are also the lights. In the darkest corners, where the submarine gloom deepens to absolute black-green, certain fungi pulse with a faint bioluminescence—a greenish heartbeat, a slow throb of phosphorescence that suggests this place has a pulse, a nervous system, a dream. They live in the rot, in the interface between the living moss and the dead stone, and they glow like embers in the dark.

The fauna is sparse, almost a rumor. There is the spider, suspended in a web not of silk but of condensation—a lattice of water droplets strung between two peat towers, trembling with each exhalation of the place. It lives in an architecture of dew, a cathedral of droplets, waiting for the mechanical buzz of the cold-adapted fly that occasionally navigates the corridors. The fly is a mechanical thing, a clockwork insect that has evolved to function in the thin, bitter air, its wings beating a staccato rhythm against the silence. There are voles, perhaps, moving through the green dark in the narrow channels worn smooth by their passage—small, furtive mammals living in a world of moss and lichen, their hearts beating fast against the cold. And once, rarely, a ptarmigan might descend, a white ghost leaving prints in the peat that will fill with water and become black mirrors by morning.

But the water itself is an inhabitant—a constant, moving presence. It does not rain here so much as materialize, condensing from the saturated air onto every surface, glazing the rock in rime, hanging in heavy drops from the fungal balconies, threading through the root-masses in silent rivulets. It is the blood of the place, the tears of the wound, the sweat of the breathing peat. It lives in a cycle of evaporation and condensation, rising from the warm depths of the peat, cooling against the stone, falling back down to be drunk by the roots. At night it freezes, expanding in the crevices, cracking the sharp rock a fraction more, feeding the young erosion, ensuring the place remains a wound that is constantly re-opening, constantly being filled by the green life that rushes in to heal it.

And then there is the silence—a heavy, subsonic presence that occupies the space between the ticks of freezing water and the snaps of frost-laden twigs. It is not empty; it is full of pressure, the sound of the place holding its breath. It lives in the gaps between the heartbeat of the fungi and the drip of the condensation. It is the silence of a room that is also a wound, of a throat that is choked with life, of a decision that has not yet been made.

What lives here, ultimately, is a single organism that is also a community—a megalopolis of photosynthesis packed into a space no larger than a cathedral nave. It is the accumulated dead that is still breathing, the living moss that is still growing, the stunted trees that have become walls, the lichens that are the curtains, the carnivores that are the hunger, the fungi that are the pulse, the rare animals that are the thoughts, the water that is the blood, and the silence that is the dream. It is a place that is still deciding what it is, and in the meantime, it is filling itself with cold, wet, green life, growing tighter and tighter, a fist clenching around a handful of moss, a wound knitting itself shut with green thread, a room building itself from the inside out, breath by subsonic breath.
