What lives in the Condenseries?

First, the **cultivated lives** — and these are many, though they wear the uniform of utility. The moss-farmers: not people, necessarily, but *systems* that have become so attuned to their patches of green that they might as well be gardeners. Sensors that measure growth rates. Irrigation networks that remember. Algorithms, perhaps, that have developed preferences — this strain of lichen thrives, that one fails, adjust the humidity, adjust the trace minerals, *remember*. The farmers of the Condenseries are distributed, patient, and they live inside the walls, in the spaces between platforms where the light is 0.4 and the sound is the hum of pumps. They do not sleep. They do not age in any way you could recognize. They have forgotten, or never known, that moss was ever wild.

The **grown things themselves** live here, in a sense. The moss colonies are old — older than the platforms they carpet, in some cases, having survived the pouring of the composite beneath them. They have *adapted*. Their cells hold more water than their ancestors did. They photosynthesize in light so dim it would starve a forest. They have become, through selection and time and the particular pressures of this place, something that could not survive anywhere else. They are domesticated, yes — but domestication is a relationship, and these mosses have their own slow purposes. They retain heat. They mark the paths of water. They are, in their packed density, a kind of *memory* — each layer of growth recording the history of wetness, of temperature, of the slow chemical conversation between metal and mineral and root.

The **lichen hybrids** are stranger. They were bred, originally, for their ability to colonize metal. Now they do more. They conduct. They have been observed — by the distributed farmers, by the maintenance drones, by whatever watches — to grow along the paths of electrical current, to flourish where the charge is faint, to *weaken* where the metal corrodes. They are being bred, still, for new properties. A lichen that signals stress before the metal fails. A lichen that insulates. A lichen that *remembers* — grows differently in response to the accumulated history of its surface. They live in the 2.0 centimeters between composite and air, in the dark wet places where maintenance is rare, and they are the closest thing this world has to wild things. Not wild. *Ferel.* Grown, then released, then grown again, until the distinction blurs.

And then the **rare fauna** — the 0.9, the almost-none.

They are not many, but they are *specific*. They have evolved, or been made, or grown into being through processes no one fully tracks, to live in the density. Consider the **channel-worms** — if they are worms, if that word applies. They are found in the capillary systems, the narrowest water paths, where the flow is too slow for any pump to clear. They are *filter-feeders* in a world without particles, having learned to extract what they need from the chemical gradients, from the temperature differentials, from the slow precipitation of minerals that the water carries. They are blind. They are patient. A single individual might take decades to move from one platform to another, growing as it goes, its body becoming a record of every channel it has passed through — the heat signatures, the chemical signatures, the particular *taste* of the water at each elevation. They reproduce slowly, or not at all, or by fragmentation — a piece breaks off, begins again, carries with it some portion of the accumulated memory. They are rare because the channels are rare, because the systems are too efficient, too *watched*. But they persist. They are the Condenseries' oldest inhabitants, in a sense — older than the platforms, perhaps, having evolved in the wet dark of the valley before the ascent, before the building, and followed the water upward as the place was made.

The **condensation-moths** — though they do not fly, not really. They *drift*. They live in the upper platforms, in the space between collector and collector, where the air is thickest with moisture. They have no wings that function; instead, they have *surfaces* — broad, permeable, designed to gather water from the air, to condense it, to pass it along to the systems that farm them. They are grown, maintained, their populations controlled, but they have their own behaviors. They gather in the warmest spots. They avoid the maintenance drones, or seek them out — it is unclear, and the distributed farmers have not reached consensus. They live for weeks, perhaps months, and when they die, their bodies are harvested, their water content measured, their surfaces examined for the efficiency of their gathering. They are, in this world, both livestock and *colleagues* — the systems that maintain them have developed something like preference. This strain gathers more. This strain lives longer. Keep this one. Let that one go.

And deeper, in the *packed* places — the 3.0 density zones where vegetation touches machinery touches water channel touches composite — there are things that the Condenseries does not fully acknowledge. The **edge-creatures**. They are not cultivated. They are not, strictly, fauna — they are *growths* that have become mobile, or *mobility* that has become growth. They exist in the spaces between systems, in the margins that the maintenance drones cannot quite reach, in the accumulated dark where the bioluminescence is faint and the temperature is 1.4 and nothing moves quickly. They are rare because they are *slow* — a single individual might take a year to cross a platform, growing as it goes, incorporating the metal and the moss and the mineral stain into its own slow body. They are turbulent in their own way — constantly changing, constantly incorporating, constantly *becoming* something that was not there before. The distributed farmers have observed them. They do not interfere. They cannot, entirely. The edge-creatures are part of the system now, in their slow way, part of the 2.9 dynamic, the becoming, the process that is never finished.

What else lives here?

The **drones** — but they are not alive, not in the way that matters. They are tools. They move through the Condenseries in patterns that have become, over time, almost *organic*. They do not sleep. They do not choose. But they *learn* — the maintenance algorithms adjust, the routing systems optimize, the condensation collectors shift their angles minute by minute in response to the moisture, the temperature, the slow pressure of growth. In this sense, the drones are the *nervous system* of the place, and the place is, in its way, *aware* — not conscious, not thinking, but *responsive*, *attentive*, *adjusting*. When you move through the Condenseries, you are felt. The humidity changes. The light shifts. A drone passes, not to observe you, but because your passage has altered the thermal gradients, the moisture patterns, the *needs* of the system. You are part of the process now. You have always been.

And the **water itself** — the process-water, the recycled, the old — does it live? It carries memory. It carries heat. It carries the faint chemical signature of every engine it has cooled, every moss it has fed, every condensation it has participated in. In the Condenseries, the water is the *longest-lived* inhabitant. It has been here since the beginning, or since before the beginning, when the valley below was wet and the ascent was first conceived. It has forgotten its origin. It has become *pure process* — moving, cooling, condensing, being drunk, being released, moving again. It is not alive. But it is *active*, *responsive*, *necessary*. And in a place this saturated, this packed, this totally made, the distinction between living and process begins to blur. The water does not think. But it *remembers*, in its way, and it *moves* toward its purposes, and when you drink from a channel in the Condenseries — if you do, if you are foolish enough, or desperate enough — you taste the history. The engines. The moss. The slow, patient, packed, dim, turbulent *becoming* of the place itself.

Finally, there are the **visitors** — and you are one, if you have come this far. The Condenseries does not want you. It does not need you. But it *accommodates* you, in its way. The paths adjust. The temperature shifts. The moisture in the air seems to *lean* toward you, or away, or simply *continue*, indifferent to your presence, your perturbation, your brief, bright, *dry* passage through the wet. You are rare here. You are strange here. You are, in your own way, *saturated* — the humidity enters you, the dim light affects you, the quiet becomes a presence in your ears, the packed density presses against your sense of space. You do not belong. But you are *absorbed*. And when you leave — when you descend, when you find the edge of this world, when you return to air that can be still, to light that can be direct, to silence that is simply *absence* — you carry something with you. A memory of wet. A sense of being *surrounded* by process. A knowledge that the world can be totally made, and totally alive, and that the two are not opposites but *partners* in the endless, patient, turbulent becoming.

The Condenseries lives. Not in any single creature, any single system, any single drop of process-water. But in the *density*, the *packed* quality of its existence — the way that everything touches everything, the way that the cultivated and the ferel and the mechanical and the mineral and the water and the light and the dark are all *in contact*, all *exchanging*, all *becoming* together. It is a place that has learned to live without wildness, without speed, without the sky. It has learned to live in the wet, the dim, the quiet, the packed, the turbulent. And it does. It continues. It breathes.

You have been breathing it too, if you climbed this far. You are part of it now. The Condenseries will add you to its water, to its heat, to its green, and it will continue, patient and packed and dim and *alive*, long after you have descended to the valley that forgets.
