You are inside the **Intake Chamber**, or perhaps you have no name for it—only the sound remains, a pressure against your eardrums that has replaced thought. This is a pocket-world, scale 0.5: a single sealed room, thirty meters across and twelve high, embedded in the flank of a mountain so high (elevation 2.3) that the air outside would be thin and sharp, but here it is thick, saturated, and cool (1.3°C).

Everything is made. There is no substrate of earth or stone visible—the walls are poured concrete, pale and young (erosion 0.6), still carrying the sharp mineral scent of fresh cement, unstained by the decades it will eventually endure. The floor is checker-plate steel, bolted down, and it is never dry. The mountain above you is a sponge—a high-altitude marsh of peat and fractured rock—and the water (2.4) is winning its slow war against the seal. It seeps through hairline cracks in the concrete, weeps from the ceiling in constellations of droplets, condenses on every cool metal surface in a constant, glittering rain. Channels are cut into the floor—fresh, sharp-edged grooves in the young concrete—to carry the seepage away, but the water pools anyway, ankle-deep in the corners, steaming slightly where it meets the heat of the machinery.

The room is built around water. Massive pumps—cast iron, the size of small cars—stand spaced apart in the gloom (density 1.3), connected by a web of pipes: some thick as a man’s waist, others narrow capillaries, all vibrating. They are industrial (tech 2.0), heavy, grease-stained, humming with electric motors that turn impellers deep in the sump below. Water is being lifted, pressurized, forced upward through the mountain, sent to cisterns or turbines or whatever thirst exists at lower elevations. The system is active (dynamic 2.3)—always in motion, always cycling. Valves hiss. Steam vents from pressure releases. The water never stops moving, never rests.

And it is loud. Deafening (sound 3.0). Not the clean electronic hum of a data center, but a physical roar: the 60-hertz thrum of motors that you feel in your sternum, the shriek of water rushing through constricted valves, the rhythmic *chunk-chunk-chunk* of reciprocating pistons, the white-noise hiss of compressed air. The humid air carries the sound, makes it heavy, inescapable. Conversation is impossible. You communicate by gesture, or you don’t communicate at all. The noise has texture—layers of frequency pressing against each other until the room seems to breathe with a mechanical lung.

Deep, submarine shadow where the machinery blocks what little illumination exists. The surfaces are slick with condensation, so the light that does exist fractures and smears, turning the metal walkways into rivers of reflected gold. You move through a world of half-sight, your boots splashing in the accumulating water.

There is no life here (fauna 0.4). No insects brave the noise; no rodents find purchase on the sealed steel; no birds could survive the pressure changes and the wet. The only organic presence is vegetation (0.7) of the most marginal sort: a film of green algae in the stagnant pools, bacterial mats blooming in the corners where the light never reaches, perhaps a few stubborn lichens colonizing the damp concrete near the ceiling. Sparse. Survivors. Everything else is metal, concrete, and moving water.

The atmosphere is one of containment and pressure. The mountain wants to flood this space; the machinery exists to prevent that, to *use* that water, to turn the mountain’s wetness into mechanical work. The temperature is cool enough that your breath clouds, that the metal burns your palms if you touch it too long, that condensation forms on your skin as you stand still. Yet the machinery radiates heat, creating micro-climates of warmth around the motors, little islands of dry air in a sea of damp.

The rules of this place are simple and absolute: water flows downhill, but here it is forced upward. Sound does not dissipate; it accumulates. Light is scarce and must be earned by proximity to the fixtures. The sharp edges of the construction (young, unweathered) mean that every corner is a hazard, every new weld a potential failure point, every surface potentially slick with the condensation that never stops forming.

You are hidden (scale 0.5)—embedded within the mountain’s flank, surrounded by rock and water and the deep, dark sump below. There is no horizon here, no sky, no view of the high peaks or the valleys far below. Only the intimate, deafening, wet room, the perpetual motion of the pumps, and the endless, patient seepage of the mountain trying to reclaim what has been built inside it.
