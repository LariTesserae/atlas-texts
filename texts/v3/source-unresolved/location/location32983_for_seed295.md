You stand at the rim of a valley that should not exist—at least, not with these properties. The elevation is severe: the air carries the thin, sharp quality of high altitude, the kind that makes lungs work and thoughts slow. Yet the temperature is wrong. It is warm here, oppressively so, a wet heat that rises in visible shimmers from the ground itself. You are high in the mountains, yet you are in a steam bath.

The valley floor is not floor at all. It is a metropolis.

Everything you see has been made. There is no exposed bedrock, no natural soil, no untouched substrate. The entire landscape is constructed from travertine—that porous, cream-colored stone deposited by mineral springs over uncountable millennia—but this is travertine that has been *worked*. It has been chewed, secreted, excreted, and calcified by colonies of macroscopic organisms over epochs so long that their architecture has become geology. The structures are organic in origin but mineral in substance: towers of layered calcium carbonate honeycombed with galleries, walls of fused chrysalis-casings and regurgitated stone, terraces that step down like the seating of an amphitheater built for an audience of millions. No tool has ever touched this place. There are no devices, no mechanisms, no artifacts of intelligence as you would recognize them. Yet nothing here is wild. The erosion is ancient and settled—the edges are softened, the surfaces weathered to a velvet roughness, the whole vast complex in a state of patient, geological equilibrium with the life that maintains it.

The light is wrong for the height. At this elevation, the sun should be a bleaching, ultraviolet violence. Instead, the valley exists in perpetual twilight. The travertine towers are so dense, so closely packed, that they interlock overhead, forming a canopy of stone that filters the sky into a uniform, dim gray-green gloom. Only where the structures have collapsed, or where geothermal vents punch through the roof of the world, do shafts of pale light fall—dusty, underwater-looking beams that illuminate clouds of spores and insects.

Moisture is everywhere, but never as rain. Water here moves as condensation, as seepage, as the slow weeping of the stone itself. The hot springs that built this place still simmer in its depths, and the heat they generate rises through the porous architecture, meeting the cool mountain air in the upper galleries to create a perpetual interior drizzle. Every surface is damp. The travertine sweats. Water runs in rivulets down the calcified walls, collecting in stepped pools that overflow into the next tier, a gravity-fed plumbing system of stone and patience. The air is thick with it—warm, mineral-scented, tasting of iron and wet chalk.

Vegetation is lush in the extreme, but it is a vegetation of shade and damp. Ferns unfurl from cracks in the mineral walls in fronds the size of your arm. Moss carpets every horizontal surface in spongy, emerald layers. Fungi—pale, fleshy, and vast—fruit from the dark recesses of the lower galleries, their mycelium threading through the travertine like neural tissue. Trees grow here too, but they are stunted, ancient things, their roots gripping the constructed stone, their canopies contributing to the darkness below. The greenery is layered, dense, high-saturation—a vertical jungle climbing the architecture, turning the built environment into a hanging garden that has forgotten it was ever made.

But the vegetation is not the point. The fauna is overwhelming.

You cannot move without disturbing it. The valley is saturated with animal presence. Swarms of midges and thermophilic flies rise in tornadoes from the warm pools, their wings creating a constant, granular hum. Bats—millions of them—hang in clusters from the ceiling of every cavern, their bodies a writhing, furry darkness, and at the dimming of the light (which is always dimming, always near the threshold of night), they erupt in clouds that blot out the sky. On the terraces, herds of shaggy, deer-like creatures graze on the moss, their hooves clicking on stone, their breathing a wet, heavy chorus. Smaller things—lizards, beetles, burrowing mammals—scuttle through the undergrowth in a constant, rustling traffic. The density of life is such that the air itself seems to thicken with wings and fur and the shed skins of insects.

And the sound. The sound is deafening.

It is not the silence of high places, nor the wind-song of open ridges. This is a roar. The geothermal vents scream—a constant, subterranean hiss and gurgle of boiling water venting through fissures in the travertine. Waterfalls, fed by the condensation and the springs, pour down the stepped terraces in a thousand thin, white threads, creating a white noise so dense it fills the skull. The insects add their layer: the buzz of a million wings, the drone of hive-structures vibrating in the warm stone, the clicking of mandibles. The animals contribute: the bellowing of the herd animals, the shrieks of birds nesting in the mineral spires, the chittering of the bats as they navigate the dark. It is a sound without silence, a wall of noise that has no variation, only volume. You feel it in your teeth.

Time moves slowly here. The travertine accretes at the rate of millimeters per century. The erosion that softens the edges of the towers takes geological ages. The colonies that built this place work on timescales of centuries, their secretions hardening into permanence long after the individuals have died. The ecosystem is in a state of deep, established balance—the lush vegetation feeds the grazing herds, whose dung fertilizes the moss, which traps the moisture, which accelerates the mineral deposition, which expands the architecture. It is a closed loop of biological construction, a city that grows itself, that repairs itself, that is simultaneously artifact and organism. There is no flux here, no rapid change, only the patient, grinding cycles of accretion and weathering, growth and slow collapse.

You are in a place that is entirely made, yet contains no technology. It is a world built by biology and patience, high and warm and dark, drowning in life and drowned in sound.
