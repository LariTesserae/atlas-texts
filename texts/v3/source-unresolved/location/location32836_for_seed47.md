You stand on a surface that has forgotten what it means to be uneven.

The ground is a **peneplain of fused silica and devitrified glass**, a single continuous sheet of pale, iridescent stone worn smooth by ten billion years of mineral wind. It stretches to every horizon—continental in scale, vast enough to hold its own weather—yet it is perfectly, mathematically flat. The only relief is the gentle curvature of the world itself, the ground falling away in a vast, unbroken arc. It is ancient in the way that bedrock is ancient: settled into its final form, the end-state of erosion, a landscape that has reached the bottom of its own history and stopped changing. There is no water here—none has touched this surface in eons. The chemistry is anhydrous, built from silicates, iron oxides, and trace metals suspended in a thin, cool atmosphere that tastes of ozone and copper.

The temperature holds at a bracing coolness, a constant 1.4 on your scale, maintained by the extreme elevation—twenty kilometers above the world’s former sea level, though no seas remain. The air is thin, sparse, almost a vacuum; density hangs at 0.3, meaning that between every breath of nitrogen and argon there is a vast emptiness. Sound struggles to exist here. Your footsteps make no noise. The wind, if it can be called that, is a whisper of dust so fine it behaves like a fluid, yet it carries no voice.

But above this dead, flat silence rises a **maelstrom**.

The sky is not sky. It is a permanent, turbulent ocean of **ionized crystal vapor and metallic plasma**, a storm-state that has raged without pause since the planet’s crust cooled. Filaments of gold, violet, and white light writhe and knot and tear themselves apart in endless, soundless cataclysm. This is the world’s weather—dynamic, hyperactive, a turbulence of electromagnetic fury that never dissipates, never rains, never ceases. Yet it never touches the ground. There is a shear layer, a boundary of near-vacuum, where the storm ends and the silence begins. The plasma burns at the edge of space, churning in patterns that suggest intelligence, that defy the planet’s rotation, casting a diffuse, shifting illumination across the plain. The light is normal—2.0, visible, steady—but it comes from everywhere and nowhere, filtering down through the storm like sunlight through deep water. Shadows do not exist here, only a general, trembling brightness.

Scattered across this emptiness are the **Monoliths**.

They are sparse—so sparse that you might walk for days across the glass before encountering one, or see only a single thin line on the horizon, a black sliver against the storm-light. They are not buildings. They are **instruments**, vast and smooth and featureless, rising from the bedrock like obsidian needles. They are built from a material that absorbs light and emits a subsonic hum, a vibration felt in the teeth and sternum rather than heard. They predate the erosion, predating even the flatness of the plain; they stand as if the ancient crust was grown around them, or as if they extruded from the planet’s mantle in some forgotten age.

Their technology is beyond description—2.8 on your scale. They exhibit impossible geometry: angles that ache to look at directly, surfaces that recede into dimensions not quite perpendicular to our own, interiors that are larger than their exteriors. They are active. You can feel it—a pressure behind the eyes, a sense that the plasma storms above are being **steered**, **harvested**, or **contained** by these scattered sentinels. They hum at frequencies that make the vitreous lichen chime like distant bells in a vacuum.

Life persists, but barely.

In the micro-fissures of the glass, where ancient tectonic stresses cracked the surface eons ago, grows **crystalline lichen**—thin, photosynthetic sheets of silica and carbon that drink the storm-light and grow at a pace measured in millimeters per century. They are sparse, the color of sea-glass, pale green and blue, spreading across the plain in slow, branching fractals. They are the only vegetation.

Fauna is rarer still. You might see, once in a long expedition, a **Drift-Stalker**—a creature the size of a whale, translucent as jellyfish flesh, with a skeleton of flexible carbon-fiber and a body full of lighter-than-air gas sacs. It moves across the plain on hundreds of jointed, hair-thin legs, or perhaps it floats just above the surface on thermal updrafts rising from the Monoliths. It is silent. It does not call out. It moves between the towers across the vast, empty distances, a relic of an ecology that has nearly ended, adapted to the thin air and the subsonic song of the machines.

The rules of this world are strict and strange. Water does not exist. There is no cycle of evaporation or rain. The erosion that created this plain was not hydrological but **aeolian**—wind carrying dust so fine it acts like a grinding fluid, wearing mountains into this perfect, primordial flatness over geological ages. Yet the world is not dead; it is **settled**—at the end of its life, having reached its oldest-stable-state—but kept in a state of **permanent agitation** by the plasma storms above, a turbulence that will outlast the star.

Time moves strangely here. The Monoliths seem to be **unfolding** rather than decaying, revealing deeper layers of complexity as you watch, or perhaps they are growing, very slowly, into the storm. The plasma above pulses with a rhythm that matches no known celestial cycle. The silence is absolute—0.1 on your scale—broken only by the subsonic resonance of the machines and the faint, crystalline chiming of the lichen when the vibrations pass through the stone.

You walk across the glass. The air is too thin to carry your footsteps. You are alone, except for the hum, except for the light, except for the vast, silent, turbulent machinery of the sky. It is a place of ultimate erosion and eternal storm—the world’s crust worn to a nub, its atmosphere reduced to a screaming, beautiful, soundless fire that never touches the ground it illuminates.
