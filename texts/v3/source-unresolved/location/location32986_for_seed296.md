You stand on a surface that should not exist: a plateau so high the air tastes of metal and distance, yet so thoroughly worn by deep time that the stone beneath your boots is smooth as a riverbed, polished by eons of wind and ice into a near-flattened plain. This is the Table of Final Forms—a high-altitude plain sitting at the roof of the world, where the mountains once were. They have been eroded into their oldest-stable-state, reduced to a primordial shelf of bedrock that extends in every direction, a regional expanse of ancient, settled stone that feels less like geology and more like a memory of height.

The air is cool, carrying a dampness that never quite leaves. It is not the wetness of rain, though storms occasionally sweep across the plateau in slow, horizontal sheets. Rather, it is condensation made manifest—the atmosphere itself weeping. Moisture beads on every surface, runs in rivulets down the flanks of structures, pools in the shallow depressions of the worn rock. The temperature hovers in that narrow band where water exists in all its shy phases: mist clinging to stone, vapor rising from vents, the occasional pocket of dryness inside sealed chambers where the damp cannot reach.

Light here is scarce—dim, filtered through a perpetual overcast of high-altitude cloud that never breaks. The sun, when it comes, is a pale coin seen through frosted glass, casting no sharp shadows. Instead, the world glows with a diffuse, underwater luminescence, as if the plateau sits at the bottom of a shallow sea of air. In the deepest hours, bioluminescent fungi provide the only illumination, pulsing with soft blue-green light from the crevices of the ruins.

And there are ruins—or rather, *works*. A city, dense and layered, clings to the flattened stone. It is urban in the geological sense: settlement-scale, organized, infrastructure-dense. But it is built not upon the land, but *into* it, following the contours of the erosion-smoothed rock. The construction is stratified, stacked like sedimentary layers—older industrial foundations buried beneath newer accretions of pipe and conduit, all of it now equally weathered. Factories hum with engines that have run for millennia, their turbines turning in the thin air, belching steam that immediately condenses into the surrounding damp. The density is high; structures press near-touching against one another, creating canyons of metal and mineral where the wind sings low, and the saturation of presence is such that every cubic meter contains machinery, moisture, and organic growth in equal measure.

Vegetation is lush here in a way that defies the altitude. Moss carpets the stone in thick, emerald drifts. Lichens the size of dinner plates crust over the factory walls, their patterns indistinguishable from circuitry. Strange, primordial flora—club mosses and horsetails that should have gone extinct eons ago—sprout from the seams between industrial plates, their fronds waving in the damp breeze. The green is everywhere, colonizing the built environment until the boundary between machine and organism dissolves. A cooling tower is now a trellis for fungal vines; a smokestack is a hollow trunk for a colony of pale, leafless plants that photosynthesize using geothermal heat.

Fauna is rare. You might see a pale moth drifting between the pipes, or hear the skitter of something insectoid in the moss, but birds are absent, and mammals have never been recorded here. The ecosystem is active, however, in its motion: spores drifting on the mist, the slow creep of lichen across a decade, the cycling of industrial processes that keep the plateau warm in its core, the weather systems that form and dissipate in visible waves across the flat expanse, the condensation dripping from every eave in a rhythm that sounds almost like breathing.

The sound is mostly quiet. There are no voices. The industrial hum is a constant, subsonic drone, felt in the teeth rather than heard. Beyond that, the drip of water, the creak of ancient metal settling into the stone, the sigh of wind through the dense architecture. It is a silence saturated with presence, broken only by the mechanical pulse of the city’s heart.

What is strange here is temporal. The erosion has reached its final form—the stone is smooth, settled, primordial—yet the city upon it is industrial, mechanized, producing. The two states, the ancient and the manufactured, exist in a kind of equilibrium, as if the factories were grown from the rock itself, or as if the rock were remembering being a factory. Gravity feels slightly wrong, or perhaps the mist carries a weight that presses down. Time moves in layers; you can stand in a square where a steam engine from the age of coal stands next to a conduit that seems to predate the erosion itself, both equally encrusted with the same verdigris moss. The weirdness is geological: the sense that you are walking through a moment that has already ended, preserved in the damp, cool dimness of a world that has settled into its oldest, most stable shape, yet still hums with motion, still breathes steam, still grows.
