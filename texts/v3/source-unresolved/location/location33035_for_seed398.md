You approach across a field of obsidian that has not yet learned to be soil. The glass is sharp enough to cut the thin air, which at this elevation—extreme, the roof of the world—should be freezing, yet the temperature presses against your skin like a fever, 2.6 on any scale that measures heat. This is a young landscape; erosion has barely begun its work. The caldera rim is fresh, the pumice still warm from its extrusion, the geology speaking in the present tense. You feel the heat rising from the crust itself, a thermal breath that has nowhere to go but up into the stratosphere, except it doesn't. It stops. It pools.

You find the gap—a fissure in the black glass that opens not onto a view but into a pocket. The scale here collapses. You step through and the world inverts. You are inside.

The air changes immediately. It is thick now, damp, heavy with a moisture that shouldn't exist at this altitude, in this heat. Water is present but sparse, existing in a state of intermittent grace: condensation gathering on surfaces that are somehow colder than the ambient inferno, rivulets tracing paths through mineral veins, pools collecting in bowls of volcanic glass that serve as... something. Fountains? Basins? The dampness is localized, intimate, clinging to the architecture rather than the ground.

Because here is the city.

It is urban—organized, infrastructural, layered with the logic of settlement—but it is empty. Density is near-void; you walk across plazas the size of valleys that contain only a single monolith, or nothing at all, the negative space itself seeming to be the point. The built environment is tech beyond modern, 2.7, engineering that current physics can only approximate as "resonance-stabilized probability lattices" or "grown singularities." The structures do not sit on the ground; they phase through it, half-present, humming at frequencies you feel in your molars. Walls of crystalline matrix that are solid when you lean against them and translucent when you look through them. Staircases that ascend into volumes of air that are somehow compressed, denser, pressing back against your palm. The infrastructure is dense in intention but scattered in form—transit conduits that spiral through the hollow dark, utility arteries that pulse with contained light, districts that exist as zones of altered gravity where your weight shifts by fractions.

There is no vegetation. None has colonized this place; the chemistry is wrong, or the time too short, or the radiation too strange. Instead, mineral life grows—fungal-mineral hybrids that are simpler than plants, threads of silver and quartz threading through the architecture like synaptic patterns, calcite blooming on the surfaces of the impossible walls. The world is post-life or pre-life, a mineral ecology that has never known chlorophyll, only heat and pressure and the slow accretion of salts.

The light is dim. At this elevation, you would expect a clarity that burns, but the pocket is enclosed—perhaps by a membrane of the same beyond-modern material, perhaps by atmospheric pressure differentials, perhaps by the sheer weirdness of local physics. The illumination is perpetual twilight, amber and violet, filtering through the translucent structures and casting no hard shadows. The heat is visible as shimmer, waves rising from the ground, the air itself seeming to sweat.

Sound is mostly quiet. There is no fauna—no birds, no insects, no wind through leaves (there are no leaves). The silence is vast, a hollow that fills the empty spaces between structures. But the place is dynamic, active in its processes. You hear the subsonic thrum of the infrastructure, a city-wide heartbeat that vibrates in your sternum. You hear the drip of condensation—a slow, mineral percussion, water finding its way down the crystalline lattices to gather again in the glass basins below. You hear the creak of young stone as it cools and contracts, sharp reports like distant gunshots that startle the silence. Thermal vents exhale steam that vanishes before it can rise, swallowed by the dry heat above. The entire caldera breathes in a slow cycle: heat up, condense, drip, evaporate, repeat.

This is a strange place. The geometry refuses to hold. Corridors that should be parallel intersect at angles that hurt to look at directly. Gravity varies by district; in some plazas you float slightly, in others you are pressed to the floor. Water flows upward along certain conduits, drawn by forces that have nothing to do with capillary action. Time moves unevenly—the young erosion of the exterior world is matched here by a temporal dilation, minutes inside stretching into hours outside, or perhaps the reverse. The city is a settlement without settlers, a metropolis of function without inhabitants, perhaps a machine that dreams it is a city, or a city that is slowly remembering it was once a volcano.

And the dampness—those pockets of water—they are the softest thing here, the only mercy in a world of sharp glass and hard heat. They gather in the hollows of the architecture, reflecting the dim light, holding the day's warmth in their shallow depths. In the vast empty spaces between structures, where the density approaches void, you can stand and feel the scale of absence—the city as a question, the mountain as a vessel, the heat as a presence that fills the hollow, waiting.

You are inside a throat. Inside a weather system. Inside a city that is also a geological event, a technological singularity, a pocket of impossible physics where the rules of elevation and temperature have been suspended by the sheer strangeness of the built. The dampness clings to your skin. The hum resonates in your bones. The silence is not empty; it is full of the city's slow, mineral, impossible breathing.
