You stand in a valley that has not finished being born. The rock is still screaming.

The sound hits you first—not a single noise but a roar composed of ten thousand frequencies. It is the sound of water, yes, but water in a state of perpetual argument with the stone: waterfalls sheeting down faces of obsidian that fractured only yesterday, water boiling in geothermal vents that punch through the permafrost, water freezing and shattering in the upper air where the temperature hovers at the knife-edge of 0.3. The land groans. Plates grind. The young earth—sharp-angled, unweathered, its scree slopes still raw—shifts beneath your feet with a sickening lurch, and you realize the ground is not solid but *negotiating*. A mud volcano three hundred meters to your left erupts with a wet slap, hurling steaming black fluid into the perpetual twilight.

The air is thick. Water at 1.6 does not mean puddles; it means saturation. Your lungs fill with mist that tastes of iron and ozone. Every surface weeps. The cliffs—recently emerged from tectonic upheaval, their strata still vertical and un-eroded into soil—sweat condensation that runs in rivulets down faces of basalt and granite. The atmosphere condenses against your skin, cold and clammy, while geothermal breath rises from below in ghostly columns that never quite reach the low, bruised sky. Visibility is limited to perhaps fifty meters; beyond that, the valley dissolves into grey.

But the valley is not empty. It is *dense*.

Vegetation at 2.4 in this cold, this dark, this wet, is an obscenity of green. Not the sparse tundra you’d expect, but jungles of a kind—layered, near-touching, a stratified mat of biology that carpets every available surface. Mosses grow in velvet slabs three centimeters thick. Fungal shelves, pale as bone, jut from rock faces. Lichen—no, something lichen-like but more vigorous—forms curtains that sway in the steam-vents’ updrafts. And everywhere, bioluminescence: soft blue-green radiance pooling in the depressions, lighting the fog from below so that the valley floor glows with the dim, subaqueous light of a deep ocean trench. The plants do not merely grow here; they *pile*, creating a spongy, breathing super-organism of kelp-like fronds and cold-adapted ferns that drink the condensing moisture directly from the air.

The fauna is abundant in this saturation. Small things, mostly—amphibious, many-legged, with translucent skin that shows the dark water they’ve just left. They skitter across the fungal shelves, dive into the warm pools, hatch in clouds from the steam. Their sounds add to the deafening chorus: a chittering, a croaking, the splash of bodies moving through the dense undergrowth. They are not afraid of you. The world is too loud, too busy, too *young* for caution.

And then the technology.

It is everywhere and nowhere. The water itself is the medium—a smart fluid, carrying nanoscale constructs that are beyond modern physics, post-industrial, operating on principles of condensed matter that your instruments cannot parse. You see it in the way the mist forms into transient geometric shapes before dissipating, in the way the waterfall’s roar has an undertone of harmonic resonance, a sub-bass that vibrates in your sternum. The water is *processing*. It erodes the young rock with chemical precision, depositing minerals in patterns that look almost like circuitry, almost like language.

They hum. Not loudly—the water is louder—but with a steady, subsonic thrum that you feel in your teeth. They are few, perhaps a dozen visible through the fog, standing like sentinels in a landscape that is too turbulent to guard. Around their bases, the water flows upward, defying gravity in thin, shimmering columns that freeze into lattice structures as they reach the cold air, creating temporary bridges of ice that shatter and reform in cycles of destruction and creation.

The land rises in some places, sinks in others. Pools become islands; islands become pools. The scattered monoliths tilt, then right themselves, or sink slowly into the saturated earth, being digested.

Light is scarce—0.4, a perpetual dusk under a sky choked with condensing vapor and volcanic ash. The sun, if it exists beyond the cloud ceiling, is a rumor. What illumination there is comes from the bioluminescent flora, from the faint phosphorescence of the smart-water as it churns, and from the occasional flash of lightning in the upper clouds, distant and purple. Shadows are absolute, pools of darkness between the glowing vegetation.

The young land is not strange; it is a construction site. The inhabitants—fauna and flora alike—have adapted to the turbulence, the noise, the wet. They thrive in the density, the near-touching layers of life, the high-saturation existence where every breath is wet and every surface is alive.

You cannot stay long. The cold seeps into your bones despite the steam. The sound—deafening, tectonic, hydraulic, machinic—begins to feel like it is rearranging your thoughts. The ground shifts again, and you step back as a new fissure opens, hissing, venting a jet of mineral-rich water that arcs into the dark and freezes mid-air, a temporary sculpture of ice that will melt by morning.

The valley exhales. You leave, or you become part of the lush, the wet, the deafening, the new.
