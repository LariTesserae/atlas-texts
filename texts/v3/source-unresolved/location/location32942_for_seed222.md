The air tastes of copper and ozone. You stand on a plateau so high the horizon curves, yet the warmth here is wrong—thick and damp against your skin, as if the atmosphere itself is a greenhouse membrane stretched over the world. This is the **Table of Exposed Circuitry**, though no map names it that; the maps are older than the stone, and the stone is older than the silence.

**The Ground**
You are walking on a machine that has forgotten it was built. The surface is not soil but *lithified infrastructure*—a lattice of fused silica and weathered alloy, cracked into vast hexagonal plates that shift almost imperceptibly beneath your boots, settling into new configurations as the temperature drops. Between the plates, the cracks reveal strata that should not exist: traces of gold and copper oxidized to malachite and azurite, crystalline conduits the size of tree trunks, now dry but still humming with residual charge. The rock rings when struck—not a dull thud, but a clear, high tone that hangs in the thin air. It is warm to the touch, heated from below by geothermal vents the ancient engineers tapped and never capped. Steam rises in thin, patient columns from fissures in the stone, carrying moisture into an atmosphere that is otherwise thirsty.

**The Water**
It does not rain here. Rain is a memory this place has shed. Instead, water exists as *condensation*—a daily cycle of betrayal and gift. Each dawn, as the temperature differential between the warm stone and the cold upper atmosphere peaks, dew beads across the metal-rock in impossible abundance, gathering in the shallow basins where the plates have warped, pooling in hollows no deeper than a hand’s span. By noon, the sun—intense, bright, almost tactile at this altitude—burns it away. The landscape is damp, intermittent, a place of pockets and thresholds. You can drink from the north-facing crevices where lichens hold the moisture longest, but you must be quick. The water tastes of iron and something else, something faintly electric.

**The Light**
The illumination is *wrong-bright*. At 2.3 elevation, the sun should be harsh, but here it is filtered through a high, thin veil of particulates—metallic dust shed by the eroding tech-rock—that scatters the light into a prismatic haze. Shadows are razor-sharp, black as ink, and they move with a sluggishness that suggests the light itself has mass. Occasionally, the distant spire catches the sun and throws back a beam so intense it is painful to look at, a searchlight sweeping across the plateau in slow, deliberate arcs. The sky is a deep, bruised violet at the zenith, fading to a bleached, burning white at the horizon.

**The Fauna**
They are everywhere, yet you hear nothing. This is the silence of 0.4—not the absence of life, but the absence of *noise*. The creatures move on padded feet, their steps absorbed by the resonant stone. They communicate in subsonics—pulses felt in the sternum, pressure changes that make your ears pop. You see them first as movement in the peripheral vision: pale, translucent things with too many jointed legs, skittering across the plates in synchronized herds, their bodies harvesting the dawn dew through specialized mandibles. They are crab-like, but wrong—their carapaces are segmented like armor, and they leave trails of microscopic frost where their bodies cool the stone beneath them.

Higher up the food chain are the **Stilt-Predators**: tall, thin, bipedal, with forelimbs like mantis blades folded against their chests. They stand motionless for hours on the warm rock, waiting, their bodies the color of the weathered alloy so they are nearly invisible. When they strike, they do not make a sound. The kill is a vibration, a sudden stillness in the herd. They drink rarely, obtaining moisture from their prey, and they mark territory not with scent but by striking the stone—a single, clear *ping* that rings across the plateau and is answered, sometimes minutes later, by a distant tone from another predator.

**The Vegetation**
Sparse. What grows here has learned to live on condensation and stubbornness. Lichens in colonies the size of dinner plates cling to the shaded undersides of the plates—*sonorus* lichens, they glow faintly at dusk with bioluminescence, a soft blue-green that makes the stone look like a circuit board powering down. Between the plates grow **dew-stalks**, succulents no taller than a finger, their leaves translucent and veined, storing water in gelatinous tissues. They are grazed by the herds, but they grow back, patient and slow, rooted in the mineral dust that accumulates in the cracks. There are no trees. There is no shade but the shadow of the stone itself.

**The Built**
It is scattered, faint, half-reclaimed. You pass a **ring of standing stones**, each exactly three meters high, arranged in a circle fifty meters across. They are antennae, though they no longer transmit—only receive, their surfaces etched with erosion patterns that look almost like script, or perhaps circuitry worn smooth. Nearby, a **dome** has collapsed, half-submerged in the regolith, its interior a dry, warm chamber where condensation drips from the ceiling into a basin that never quite empties. Inside, the air is still and smells of dust and old warmth.

Cairns mark the way—or marked it once. They are not graves, but waypoints, built by the fauna themselves, perhaps, or by the last people who walked here, stacking stones to say *I was here, the stone remembers*. In the far distance, visible from everywhere on the plateau, stands the **Spire**: a single, intact structure of gleaming, unweathered material, obviously artificial, obviously active. It pulses once each afternoon, sending a wave of warmth across the land that you feel as a pressure behind the eyes. At night, it glows with a soft, internal light, blue-white, indifferent.

**The Rules**
The world here operates on principles that are strange but consistent. Sound is dangerous—not because predators hunt by noise (they do not), but because the landscape itself *listens*. Disturb the stone too loudly, and the resonance changes. The tech beneath your feet is recording, archiving, perhaps waiting. The fauna know this. They move with a reverence, a quiet.

Water is never wasted. To spill it is to invite the cold, the dryness, the long night. The dew must be harvested before the sun claims it.

Elevation means exposure. There is no shelter from the light, no hiding from the wind that scours the plateau, carrying metallic dust that glitters like snow. The erosion is patient—2.3 on the scale of established wearing—the wind sandblasting the ancient machine, revealing more circuitry, more conduits, slowly returning the built world to mineral form while the Spire continues its vigil.

**The Dynamic**
Despite the silence, the plateau is in motion. The temperature cycles: hot days that make the stone radiate heat waves visible as shimmering air, cool nights when frost forms on the metal and the fauna huddle in the geothermal hollows. The condensation breathes—forming, evaporating, forming again. The herds migrate in slow circuits, following the dew, pursued by the Stilt-Predators who know their paths. The stone settles, plates shifting, cracks widening, new surfaces exposed to the light. The Spire pulses. The wind erodes. The lichen glows and fades.

It is a place of balance—sparse, open, warm, high, bright, and quiet—where the boundary between the natural and the built has dissolved into something weathered, established, and waiting. The fauna are abundant, but they are silent. The technology is beyond modern, but it is tired, integrated into the rock, becoming geology. The water is scarce, but sufficient. The light is everywhere, pressing down, making everything sharp and clear and slightly wrong.

You walk here, and the stone rings once beneath your boot—a single, clear note—and then the silence returns, vast and watchful, as the sun begins its slow descent toward the violet horizon.
