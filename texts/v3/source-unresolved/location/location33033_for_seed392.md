The light arrives first—not as sun, but as a condition, a pressure. It is the light of 3.0, total and shadowless, pouring down from a sky that has forgotten to be blue and instead holds the white-gold of molten noon. It beats upon a floor that extends to every horizon, a low world (1.0) that has no rise, no ridge, no geological memory of upheaval. This is a place that lies flat as a held breath.

Beneath your feet, there is no earth. The substrate is *made*—every molecule of it constructed, fired, fitted, or grown according to a pattern too regular to be natural. It is a surface of terracotta and vitrified clay, perhaps, or stone so finely worked it has become ceramic. The material is young (0.7): the edges where slabs meet are knife-sharp, the surfaces unweathered, bearing the fresh chips of primitive tools—stone flakes, obsidian scrapers, bone awls. You can see the marks of hands that worked with simple fabrication, pre-metal or barely-metal, yet the scale of the work is regional (1.5), a valley-sized mosaic of human (or not-quite-human) intention. And yet it never changes (0.4). The sharpness does not dull. The freshness does not fade. The tool marks remain as crisp as the moment they were struck, locked in a state of perpetual genesis.

Water is everywhere but scarce in volume (1.9 against a density of 1.3). The air is thick, hot (2.6), saturated to the point of condensation. Moisture gathers on the cool ceramic surfaces—great beads that slide down the vertical faces of constructed walls, drip from the undersides of overhanging platforms, and pool in the shallow, deliberate depressions of the paving. These pools are warm, stagnant, mirror-bright. They reflect the white sky, but the reflections are wrong: they show a green world, a forested world, a world of water and root that does not exist above. The water weeps from the rocks themselves, though the rocks are all made, all built, all artificial down to their crystalline structure.

Between the scattered constructions—pillars, low walls, stepped platforms spaced far apart across the emptiness—vegetation grows with a lushness that borders on the aggressive (2.5). Ferns uncurl from the mortar lines in perfect logarithmic spirals. Vines of impossible thickness drape over the stone, their leaves broad, waxy, and motionless. Moss carpets the north faces of the structures in patterns that resemble circuitry or neural tissue. The plants do not sway. They do not grow, not visibly, nor do they decay. They exist in a state of eternal, frozen verdance, as if the world has paused at the exact moment of their flowering. Because everything is built (3.0), even the soil is suspect—perhaps it is crushed vegetation, compressed and fired into brick, or perhaps the plants grow without soil at all, drawing moisture directly from the condensing air, rooted in the geometry of the cracks.

Life is abundant (2.2), yet it is still. Dragonflies the size of your hand hover in the hot air, their wings beating in a rhythm that does not match the silence. Lizards bask on the terracotta, their scales gleaming, but they cast no shadows, or they cast three shadows that point in different directions. Birds—herons, ibises, creatures with too many joints in their wings—perch on the constructed pillars, and they do not sing. Instead, they breathe, a sound like wet cloth being pulled apart, or they emit clicks that seem to come from inside your own skull. The fauna moves in loops: a lizard will traverse a circuit of exactly twelve tiles, then reverse and retrace its path, then reverse again, forever, without hunger, without purpose, without progression. They are abundant, yes, but they are sparse in *presence*—each creature is isolated in its own bubble of motion, separated by vast empty spaces (1.3) of gleaming, unchanging tile.

The constructions themselves are primitive in technology (0.9) yet total in coverage (3.0). They are built of the same material as the ground—fired earth, fitted stone, perhaps wood that has been mineralized into something harder than iron. The techniques are those of early tool-use: pecking, grinding, fitting without mortar or with a mortar that has hardened into something glassy and eternal. Yet the result is a complete environment, a made-world with no natural substrate showing through. The structures are young—freshly emerged, sharp-featured—but they will never erode, never settle, never crack. They exist in a state of permanent newness, as if the world was finished yesterday and time has refused to pass since.

And here is where reality begins to fray (2.6). The geometry is wrong. You walk toward a pillar and the distance does not diminish correctly; you arrive at its base having taken seven steps, or seventy, and you cannot reconcile the count. The pillars repeat fractally—each one contains a smaller version of itself at its center, and that one contains another, ad infinitum, though they were built with stone tools that could not have carved such recursion. The water in the pools shows reflections of the pillars as they were *before* they were built, or as they will be when they have eroded—except they will never erode. The sky above is bright and empty, but sometimes, if you look at the corner of your eye, you see a second sky, darker, with different stars, pressing against the first like a bruise.

Sound is mostly absent (0.6). There is a low, constant hum, felt in the teeth rather than heard, emanating from the ground itself—the resonance of a world held in perfect, frozen equilibrium. Drip. Drip. The condensation falling from the constructed eaves. The occasional scrape of a primitive tool against stone, though no hand holds it, no arm moves it. The buzz of the dragonflies, which is too loud for their size, and occurs in a frequency that makes your vision strobe slightly at the edges.

Time does not pass here (0.4). The world is stable, invariant, a photograph of a moment that never ends. The heat does not rise. The water does not evaporate, only condenses. The fauna do not age. The lush vegetation does not photosynthesize, or if it does, it does so in a closed loop that returns the same oxygen it consumed. It is a place of zero entropy, a garden built by hands that worked with obsidian and bone, yet somehow finished a task that should have taken millennia in the span of a single, unchanging afternoon.

You stand in the open space between two distant platforms. The light is total. The air is wet and hot and still. Around you, the world is made, fresh, and eternal, and it is coming apart at the seams—not with violence, but with the quiet, patient unraveling of a dream that has forgotten it is dreaming.
