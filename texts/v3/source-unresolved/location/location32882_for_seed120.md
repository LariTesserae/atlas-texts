You step into a pocket that should not exist—a chamber no wider than a lungful of held breath, wedged at the extreme tip of a mountain that is still learning how to be a mountain. The elevation is wrong here. You are high enough that the air should be thin and freezing, yet the heat presses against your skin like a fever, thick and wet, carrying the mineral tang of newborn stone and the green rot of rapid growth.

The walls are embryonic geology. They are not rock so much as *becoming*—obsidian still cooling into glass, pumice weeping its own trapped steam, mineral deposits crystallizing in real-time, growing layer upon layer while you watch. Erosion has not touched this place; there are no scratches of wind or water, only the smooth, bulging urgency of matter still arranging itself. The ceiling drips, but not with rain—with condensation so dense it forms curtains of water that hang suspended in the hot, heavy air, trembling with every vibration.

Everything is built. The floor is not earth but a lattice of woven reeds and packed clay, lashed together with fibers that steam where they touch the heated stone beneath. There is no natural substrate visible—only construction, primitive and total, packed so densely that there is no room to turn around. Stone tools lie half-buried in the press of fungal growth; unfired clay vessels are stacked against walls that are simultaneously the chamber’s skin and its architecture. The builders—if they are builders—worked with hands and simple stone, yet their work is everywhere, saturating the space until the distinction between the constructed and the growing dissolves.

Lushness thrives in the impossible conditions. Bioluminescent moss carpets every surface, glowing with a dim, greenish light that filters through the steam to cast the chamber in perpetual twilight. It grows over the tools, over the clay, over the new rock, feeding on the condensation and the heat. Fungal shelves jut from the walls, pulsing slowly. The vegetation is so dense, so packed into the available space, that it forms a second skin—a breathing, photosynthesizing layer that is gradually digesting the architecture even as the architecture supports it.

The sound here is overwhelming. Water hisses from fissures in the newborn stone. Steam vents shriek. The mountain itself groans as it rises, a tectonic sigh that vibrates through the floor. Somewhere—everywhere—there is the rhythmic, wet *thock* of stone striking clay, the scrape of fiber against fiber, the wet slap of moss expanding. The acoustics are strange; sounds seem to pool in the corners, growing louder as they decay, or echo backward from surfaces that haven’t finished forming yet.

Reality is unstable. Gravity is a suggestion. Small objects—a dropped pebble, a shed flake of clay—sometimes drift upward, caught in thermal currents that flow toward the ceiling where the mountain is still stretching toward the sky. The walls breathe; you can watch the obsidian expand and contract with the heat, trapping air bubbles that pop with gunshot cracks. Time moves in pulses—sometimes the condensation freezes mid-drip, hanging as glass, before shattering and resuming its fall. The builders’ work seems to happen in all directions at once: a wall is simultaneously unbuilt, half-finished, and ancient, covered in moss that has not yet grown but already glows.

Fauna is rare, but present in the margins. A single creature—perhaps a salamander made of steam and mineral, or a thing that might be a builder left behind—moves through the reed-lattice, leaving trails of phosphorescent slime that evaporate before they hit the floor. Otherwise, the place is silent of animal sound, filled instead with the wet, clicking, growing noise of vegetation and geology and construction.

This is a room-sized wound in the world, a geode at the summit of a fever-dream mountain, where the earth is still being made and unmade around a primitive, packed, luminous civilization that exists in the brief, hot moment before the stone hardens completely and the steam clears.
