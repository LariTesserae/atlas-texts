You descend into a bowl of warm air, a depression in the lowlands where the earth has settled into itself over millennia of slow, patient erosion. The light here is unremarkable—pale, diffuse, filtered through membranes of living tissue that press in from all sides rather than hovering above. This is a place of strict boundaries, a hamlet-sized territory where the world ends at a rim of packed clay and packed root, and the interior begins.

The settlement is grown, not built. There are no tools here, no devices, only the architecture of biological time and the slow pressure of lignin against lignin. Enormous, hollow stalks rise from the loam—each one a tenement, a tower, a cathedral. They are vegetation total, plant-as-substrate, yet they bear the organized density of urban infrastructure: terraced with balconies of woody growth, honeycombed with internal chambers, connected by bridges of woven root and braided vine. The city clusters and layers upon itself, creating streets and plazas and intersections between these living edifices. Yet step inside any structure and you find not rooms but *void*—hollow chambers of cathedral scale, empty to the point of vertigo, where the walls are thick, damp bark and the floor is a spongy carpet of shed cellulose, and the air hangs heavy with condensation that never quite dries.

The moisture is intermittent but insistent. It does not rain so much as *seep*. Water gathers in the basements of these organic towers, in the hollows of fallen giants, forming shallow pools that reflect the dim light in fractured, shifting patterns. The air is warm, thick, tasting of chlorophyll and slow decay. Every surface is slick with the residue of transpiration, with dew that condenses from the breath of the place itself.

But the space between presences is vast. This is the paradox that defines the location: urban in its organization, yet empty in its density. The "streets" are wide, echoing corridors of packed earth, twenty paces across, containing nothing but the occasional fallen branch and the slow, heavy drip of water. The buildings stand far apart relative to their immense girth, creating a metropolis of negative space, a city of hollow rooms where the silence should be absolute but is violently, physically not.

Because the sound is deafening.

It begins as a roar. Not wind, though warm currents circulate through the hollows like breath moving through lungs. The noise comes from the growth itself—the cellular expansion of these massive plants occurring at a rate that is somehow audible, a constant, shuddering crackle of cellulose tearing and stretching, magnified and distorted by the resonant chambers of the hollow stalks. Superimposed on this biological thunder is the chorus of the fauna—abundant, teeming, warm-blooded and cold—screaming, buzzing, drumming, clicking. But reality here is breaking down, thinning at the edges, and the sounds do not behave. They layer upon one another, folding back on themselves, until they become a physical pressure, a wall of vibration that you feel in your sternum, that makes your teeth ache and your vision blur. It is not merely loud; it is a presence, a weather system of noise that drowns out thought, that makes the hollow spaces feel less like architecture and more like the inside of a screaming mouth.

The weirdness is structural. The geometry refuses Euclidean sense. Stairs spiral upward into floors that are actually ceilings. You enter a doorway carved into the base of a tower and emerge from a knothole three levels up, having traveled an impossible distance in a span of time that feels wrong, too short or too long. The plants grow in recursive patterns, fractal branches that fold back on themselves, creating interiors that are measurably larger than their exteriors. The erosion here is established, ancient, in-balance—the place has weathered into a state of equilibrium that is not natural, not stable, but is itself a kind of settled madness. Gravity feels negotiable; you step from a branch and fall *upward* into a hollow, landing softly in moss that cushions you with a wet, sucking sound.

The dynamics are constant and multi-layered. Vines thicken visibly, wrapping around the "buildings," pulling them into new configurations over the course of hours. Condensation drips in slow, heavy percussion, each drop a tiny gong in the resonant dark. Predator and prey move through the undergrowth—shadows with too many limbs, or too few, or limbs in the wrong places—chasing each other through the empty plazas in a biological pace that is neither hurried nor slow, but inevitable. The weather cycles internally: mist rises from the pools, condenses on the high bark, drips down to feed the roots, which drink and expand and scream their silent, deafening song of growth.

This is a city that is a single organism, or a colony of them, dreaming itself into urban form. It is warm, low, damp, and loud beyond endurance. It is a cathedral of hollow wood and harder silence, where the only infrastructure is the body of the world itself, growing, settling, and breaking reality in the wet, resonant space between heartbeats.
