You stand where the world has exhaled all its water and forgotten to inhale it back. This is the **High Sinter**, a continental plateau locked at an extreme elevation—2.8 on whatever scale measures the distance between a world and its sky—where the air is warm (1.8) but thin, pressing against your skin with the dry insistence of a memory. The atmosphere is heavy with nitrogen and argon, laced with metallic vapors that condense not into rain but into a perpetual, drifting haze of copper and iron dust. There is no blue to the sky; instead, it bruises from a deep, twilight violet at the zenith to a hazy, glowing amber at the horizon, a color that suggests sunset held in stasis for millennia.

The terrain is **established** (erosion 2.3), ancient and in its own state of balance. It is a vast (scale 2.6) ocean of stone frozen mid-wave. The ground is a mosaic of **hexagonal basalt columns**, each pillar as wide as a house, cracked and weathered into smooth, undulating plates that fit together like the dried skin of some titanic beast. Between these plates lie drifts of **metallic loam**—not soil, but pulverized ore: magnetite, hematite, flecks of native gold that glitter dully in the normal light (1.7). Wind, slow and patient, has carved **yardangs** into the softer stone—ridges sharp as knife-edges, oriented to the prevailing breeze, creating a landscape of frozen dunes that have not moved in ten thousand years. There are no rivers. Water (0.3) exists only as a trace, a chemical ghost in the crystal lattices of the rock, insufficient to carve canyons or sustain thirst. Instead, the land is scored by **dry washes**, channels worn not by water but by the slow, grinding passage of life itself, now filled with black sand that shifts once a century and chimes like distant bells when it avalanches.

Life here does not drink. It **eats stone**.

The fauna (1.9) are abundant, yet utterly alien (weirdness 2.2). They are the **lithophages**, and the dominant species are the **Striders**—vast, warm-blooded grazers that stand twenty meters at the shoulder, their bodies a chimeric fusion of calcified chitin and native metal. They move on thousands of jointed legs, each foot a grinding plate of tungsten carbide that clicks softly against the basalt, a sound below the threshold of hearing, felt instead as a vibration in the sternum. A herd of Striders advances across the plateau at a pace that makes glaciers seem hasty (dynamic 1.0); it takes them a year to cross a single valley. They graze by pressing their ventral surfaces against the stone, pulverizing the crust, extracting silicates and minerals through a biochemistry that runs on hydrogen sulfide and thermal vents, not on water or chlorophyll. There is no vegetation (0.2)—no plants, no photosynthesis, no green. The Striders *are* the primary producers, converting rock into biomass, and what they excrete is not waste but **refined metal**—ingots of pure iron, copper, and aluminum left in gleaming trails behind them, harvested by the cities.

Around the Striders swarm smaller creatures: **dust-mites** the size of dogs, their bodies like living gears of brass and crystal, scavenging the filings; **aerial filterers** that drift on thermal currents, sieving metallic dust from the air with comb-like wings, their bodies swollen with accumulated ore; and **burrow-worms** that tunnel through the basalt plates, their bodies secreting acid that slowly dissolves the stone, widening the cracks between the hexagonal columns.

The built environment (built 1.8) is sparse (density 1.4) and strange. The cities do not sit upon the ground; they **inhabit death**. When a Strider dies—and they live for millennia, but death is patient too—its massive shell, twenty meters thick of layered metal and keratin, collapses into a cathedral of hollow bone and curved plate. These **ossuary-cities** dot the vast plateau like scattered jewels. The industrial infrastructure (tech 2.3) is housed within the ribcages of these dead titans. Factories line the hollow bones, their machinery grinding and clanking in cycles that take days to complete a single operation. Ventilation shafts rise from the eye sockets, exhaling superheated gas and mineral vapor in slow, rhythmic breaths. The cities are **layered**: the living Striders crawl through the lower chambers, their massive bodies filling the thoracic cavities, while the human-equivalent inhabitants dwell in the cranial domes, looking out through the translucent, membranous hides that serve as windows.

Infrastructure connects these sparse settlements across the continental scale: **cable-cars** and **monorails** suspended on pylons of sintered metal, crossing the empty distances between cities, carrying ore and goods in slow procession. The industry is **electrified** (tech 2.3), powered by vast arrays of solar collectors that gleam like dark mirrors across the plateau, and by taps into the planet’s geothermal heart—vents of superheated gas that the Striders’ biology also depends upon.

Sound (1.2) is mostly quiet. The thin, warm air carries little noise. The wind is a dry whisper through the yardangs, a susurrus of stone against stone. The factories hum at infrasonic frequencies—a constant, low thrum that you feel in your teeth and bones rather than hear. The Striders communicate through subsonic rumbles that travel for miles through the bedrock, a language of tremors. When a herd passes, the ground trembles gently, a slow percussion that matches the pace of their breathing. The only sharp sounds are the **chiming** of the metallic dunes when they avalanche, a sound like distant cathedral bells, each note hanging in the dry air for minutes, decaying slowly into silence.

The **rules** of this world are those of a closed, mineral loop. Nothing is wasted. The cities do not produce refuse; the slag and tailings of industry are consumed by the Striders, who excrete fresh, pure ore. The stone is eaten, turned to metal, turned to city, and when the city falls or the titan dies, it becomes stone again under the slow teeth of erosion and the patient jaws of the next generation of grazers. Time is measured not in days but in **grazing cycles**—the decades it takes a herd to strip a valley of its mineral skin, forcing the city to migrate to a new ossuary or to wait millennia for the Striders to return and replenish the land.

It is a world of **patience** (dynamic 1.0). Nothing rushes. The Striders blink once an hour. The factories operate on weekly cycles. The wind wears away a millimeter of stone per year. The light (1.7) is normal but strange—filtered through the metallic haze, it casts long, sharp shadows at noon and a diffuse, glowing twilight that lasts for hours, pooling in the metallic dust to create patches of brightness that move across the ground like slow tides.

Here, life is a geological process, and geology is alive. The plateau breathes with the herds. The cities dream within the skulls of the dead. And the wind, dry and warm, carries the taste of copper on the tongue, whispering that water was never necessary—only time, stone, and the slow, grinding hunger of things that eat the world and shit metal.
