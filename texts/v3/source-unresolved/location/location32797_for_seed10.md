You stand on a surface that has forgotten how to be a mountain. The stone here is **peneplain-old**—worn by three billion years of patient, pre-oxygen rain and wind until it is flat as a tabletop, extending to every horizon in a shallow, concave dish. It is high—thin air, the kind that makes your lungs burn—and it is cold, a persistent 0.5-degree chill that should have frozen this place solid eons ago. Yet the ground is wet. Not damp, but **saturated**: a film of liquid water coats every grain of the ancient, rust-red grit, and in the shallows between stone-ripples, it pools in mirrors that reflect a sky the color of old bruises.

The water is wrong. It does not freeze. It flows in spirals that tighten as they approach the towering structures dotting the plain, and in places it climbs upward, defying gravity in thin, shivering rivulets that evaporate before they reach the cloud-mist above. This is the **condensing atmosphere**—a perpetual, dim twilight where moisture hangs in the air like breath in a crypt, so thick that sound behaves strangely, traveling in knots and eddies rather than straight lines.

Between the pools, the terrain is **sparse**—vast emptiness, low-saturation grey and ochre, broken only by the presences that matter. And they are vast. Every five kilometers, or ten, or twenty, a **megathallus** rises.

These are the vegetation—**total**, in the way that a forest is total, except these are not trees but something older and stranger: fungal architecture grown to the scale of skyscrapers, or perhaps the fossilized remains of a civilization that was always biological. Each thallus is a hollow tower, ribbed like a termite mound but smooth as bone, kilometers tall, tapering into the mist where their crowns dissolve into clouds of spores. They are **sparse** in their distribution but **total** in their dominance—the substrate itself, the ground you walk on being their fallen, composted ancestors, compressed over millennia into the flat, peaty stone beneath your boots.

They are also **industrial**. There are no buildings here—no constructed artifacts, no human geometry—but the thalli are engines. You can hear them before you see their bases: a **loud**, subsonic thrumming that vibrates in your sternum, the sound of massive pumps drawing water from the supercooled aquifers below, of vast digestive chambers breaking down the ancient rock into nutrients, of bioluminescent pulses racing through vascular channels like electricity through copper. They are factories that grow, that respire, that manufacture the very atmosphere you breathe. The "tech" is **organic-industrial**, a scaled production system that has achieved mechanization without ever building a single wall—every pipe is a vein, every engine a heart, every smokestack a sporocarp venting chemical exhaust into the freezing air.

The **light** is dim—1.4 on any scale—filtered through the permanent mist and the grey overcast, but punctuated by the towers’ own bioluminescence: slow, arrhythmic pulses of blue-green and amber that travel up their flanks in waves, signaling the health of their internal processes, or perhaps communicating with each other across the empty plain in a language of light.

And the **fauna** are abundant. They are everywhere but sparse in any given cubic meter—**aeroplankton**, translucent and gas-bagged, drifting in the mist in schools that darken the sky like smoke; **megafauna** the size of whales that graze on the spore-clouds, their bodies barely visible in the fog, their calls the low, foghorn moans that add to the industrial din; swarms of insectoid things, no bigger than your thumb, that ride the thermal vents rising from the towers’ bases, their wings beating in a **turbulent**, hyperactive storm of motion that makes the air itself shimmer with life.

The **dynamic** here is **chaotic**. Nothing is stable. The towers are not fixed; they sway, growing new chambers while old ones collapse inward, a constant storm-state of creation and destruction happening in fast-time atop the slow-time geology. The water churns, boiling in spots where the industrial heat vents from the stone and flash-freezing in others, creating a landscape of constant phase-change, of steam and ice and liquid existing in impossible proximity. The **weirdness** is a reality breakdown—the towers exist in multiple temporal states simultaneously (you see them as saplings and as ruins, growing and decaying at once), the shadows fall at angles that disagree with each other, and the water remembers things: it carries impressions of past rains, past floods, replaying them in miniature eddies at your feet.

There are no structures built by hands. No ruins of empires. Only the **untouched** wilderness of a place that has been **industrialized by evolution itself**, an ancient, flattened world where the end of geological time has been colonized by a hyperactive, reality-warping biology that turns the entire regional plateau into a single, breathing, roaring, **turbulent** engine—cold, wet, loud, and alive in a way that makes your own heartbeat feel like a hesitation, a stutter in the machine.
