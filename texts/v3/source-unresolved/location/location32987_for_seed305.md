The light arrives first—not warm, but *violent*. It falls from a sky too thin to blue, a raw, ultraviolet glare that bleaches the world to bone-white. You are high here, on a continental shelf thrust upward so recently that the mountains still remember being molten. The peaks are young, fractured, knife-sharp; erosion has not yet had time to soften their edges. They rise in dense, packed ridges, a jagged sea of stone frozen mid-swell, spanning horizons vast enough to generate their own weather systems—slow, patient circulations of cold air that condense moisture from the thin atmosphere into a perpetual damp.

It is cold. The temperature sits near the edge of freezing, a deep, structural cold that seeps into the young rock. Yet the place is damp—not with rain, exactly, but with a constant, fine precipitation of condensation. The thin air holds moisture it cannot warm, and so it weeps. Every surface weeps. The sharp stone sweats. The air itself feels wet, heavy with microscopic droplets that gather in pockets within the fractured geology—small, dark pools trapped in the new stone, supercooled and still. The dampness is intermittent, patient, a slow accumulation that feeds what grows here.

What grows here is lush to the point of saturation. The ground is a packed carpet of **vitreous moss**—a cold-adapted vegetation that does not resemble leaves so much as glass. It forms thick, translucent mats over the sharp rock, growing in fractal geometries that catch the violent light and refract it into shifting, painful rainbows. The moss is packed so densely that no stone shows through; the entire high plateau is a continuous, breathing skin of chlorophyll and crystal, pulsing slowly with moisture. It is young growth, aggressive and total, occupying every crevice in the young earth.

And beneath this glassy lawn moves the sound.

It is loud—a constant, industrial drone that seems to come from everywhere at once. A grinding. A hissing. A clicking, chitinous percussion that never pauses. The thin air carries it strangely, making the noise feel both distant and immediate, as if the landscape itself is clearing its throat. The sound is overwhelming, a wall of mechanical rhythm that drowns out thought.

Then you see them: the fauna.

They are packed. Saturated. There is no room between them. The vitreous moss is not merely carpet—it is *substrate*, a living floor crawling with **assemblages**. They are small, no larger than your hand, but infinite in number. Biomechanical. Cold-adapted exoskeletons of brass and chitin and translucent polymer, jointed with impossible precision. They have too many legs, or the legs bend in directions that suggest other geometries. They move across the moss in dense, slow waves, a tide of industry that flows at a glacial pace—dynamic, patient, taking centuries to cross a single valley.

They are the technology here. Engines. Factories. Mechanized production on a scale that has no architecture. You will find no buildings, no constructed structures, no artifacts of manufacture littering the young stone. The built environment is nearly zero. Instead, the creatures *are* the industry. They process the vitreous moss as they move, grinding it through internal furnaces, extracting minerals from the supercooled pockets of water, generating power through thermoelectric gradients between the freezing air and their own warm cores. Steam vents hiss from their dorsal plates. Gears turn, visible through translucent panels of chitin. They refine, they smelt, they manufacture—but they do not build. When they need a structure, a bridge or a processing node, they simply *climb atop one another*, forming temporary architectures of living brass that dissolve when the work is done, the creatures dispersing to continue their slow, loud migration.

The world is strange in its rules. The condensation is not merely moisture; it is *fuel*. The damp cold creates a perpetual thermal gradient that the assemblages harvest, a slow-burn energy that allows their industry to function without fire, without smoke, without the need for shelter. They grow, in a sense—new assemblages bud from mineral deposits in the rock, crystallizing into gear and piston, emerging fully formed from the young, sharp earth. They do not reproduce so much as *accrete*.

Time moves slowly here. The assemblages drift across the continental plateau with the patience of glaciers. Their processing takes decades. The weather systems turn over centuries. The erosion, though young, proceeds with a glacial inevitability. But the sound never stops. The grinding, the hissing, the eternal clicking of packed, biomechanical life fills the bright, cold air—a loud, slow, overwhelming industry written in brass and moss across the roof of the world.
