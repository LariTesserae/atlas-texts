The light arrives first, unfiltered and surgical. At this elevation—well above the tree line, perhaps three thousand meters into a thin, bright sky—the sun strikes with the flat, white intensity of a hospital corridor or a welding arc. It is cool here (the air carries the chill of altitude, a persistent 8 degrees Celsius even in full exposure), yet the illumination is absolute: 2.9 on the brightness scale, a glare that bleaches color to near-monochrome and casts shadows that are black, sharp, and immediate. You can see for kilometers across a landscape that refuses to hide.

The ground is young. This is a plateau of emerging geology, still under construction—raw obsidian flows that have not yet weathered to soil, pumice fields that crunch like meringue underfoot, and steaming fumaroles that vent sulfurous breath from the cooling crust below. Erosion is minimal; the mountains are embryonic, sharp-angled, not yet rounded by time. Water is everywhere, perched in the porous volcanic rock, seeping from the weeping cliff faces, pooling in the hollows between the new ridges. The water table sits at the surface. Every step sinks into saturation.

But the ground is not mineral. It is *vegetal substrate*. A living mat covers the raw geology—a continuous, spongy carpet of extremophile growth: pale lichens, silvery mosses, and dense colonies of something like kelp or horsetail that have adapted to the altitude and the cold. This is total vegetation, an all-living mass that has colonized the emerging rock within decades of its cooling. The bog breathes. It is thick, springy, and smells of green sap and wet stone. You cannot walk on rock here; you walk on the compressed bodies of ten thousand generations of these plants, compressed into peat that is still forming, still being constructed by the slow, patient accumulation of organic matter.

Rising from this carpet, spaced with brutal regularity across the valley—hundreds of meters apart, sparse as fence posts in an empty field—are the Giants. They dominate the scale. These are not trees but *architectures of plant*: massive, isolated towers of lignified tissue, perhaps twenty to thirty meters tall, resembling clubmosses the size of redwoods or stromatolitic structures grown from stacked layers of vegetal secretion. They are hollow, or honeycombed, their surfaces weeping condensation in the cool air. They stand alone in the bright, wet expanse, each one a vertical island in a sea of low green. Between them: open water, black volcanic gravel, and the distant, scattered hulks of industry.

The industry is mechanized but scattered. Tech level is high—engines, electrified pumps, pneumatic drills—but the built environment is sparse, a few structures per square kilometer. You see concrete pump houses the size of garden sheds, painted in faded industrial yellow, their exhausts venting steam into the thin air. Metal pipelines snake across the peat, elevated on stilts to avoid the water table, carrying fluids from the depths of the young crust. There are drilling rigs, their bits turning with agonizing slowness—the dynamic here is 1.3, a landscape of gradual cycles—boring into the emerging geology to tap geothermal heat or extract minerals that have not yet fully formed. The machinery is massive but moves sluggishly: pistons rise and fall with the patience of geological time, yet they generate a constant, overwhelming drone.

And the sound is deafening. It is not the chaos of a city, but a monolithic, low-frequency roar that never ceases. The wind, rushing across the high plateau, finds the hollow plant-towers and turns them into organ pipes, moaning through their internal chambers in a bass note that vibrates in your sternum. The water contributes its own percussion—dripping, gurgling, rushing through the industrial sluices. The pumps thrum. The drills grind. Because the air is thin and the light is so bright, the sound seems to come from everywhere and nowhere, a pressure against the eardrums that makes conversation impossible. You feel it more than hear it, a physical weight of noise.

There are no animals. Fauna is rare—0.9—absent almost entirely. No birds nest in the Giants; no insects buzz in the peat (or if they do, their sound is lost in the roar). The ecosystem is a duet of plants and machines. The Giants photosynthesize furiously in the intense light, growing slowly, adding millimeters per year to their towers. They are patient. The machines extract, slowly, from the patient rock. The water moves through the system in slow, condensing cycles, evaporating in the bright sun and returning as mist that clings to the plant surfaces.

This is a place of recent violence and slow industry. The geology is still emerging—the ground occasionally shifts, small steam vents opening with a hiss, new fissures appearing in the peat. The vegetation colonizes these wounds immediately, the mat sealing over cracks with new growth. The industry follows, moving its scattered structures to exploit the new fissures, the new heat. Everything here is under construction: the land, the ecology, the relationship between the organic and the mechanical.

The scale is regional, a valley that extends past the immediate horizon, a town-sized expanse of wet, bright, roaring wilderness. You could walk for hours between the Giants, across the spongy, waterlogged substrate, past the scattered pump houses and drilling platforms, never encountering another living creature, never leaving the sound, never escaping the light. The world feels new, raw, and loudly, slowly being made.
