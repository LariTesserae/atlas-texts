The light arrives first, and it is wrong. Not the warm gold of low places, but a flattened, merciless white that presses against the eyeballs with physical weight. You are high—so high the air tastes of metal and old snow—and the sun here does not warm; it only illuminates, revealing a world suspended in moisture.

This is the Condensation, or the Weeping, or the Vertical Marsh—names shift depending on which tongue speaks them, for the place has no fixed geography. You stand on a platform of woven reeds and bone, lashed with gut and caulked with a resin that weeps amber tears when the temperature shifts. Around you, the structure rises in tiers, a cathedral of organic architecture packed so densely that walls touch walls, floor meets ceiling in the next breath, and the corridors are barely shoulder-width. It is a hamlet compressed into the volume of a single house, or a city the size of a grove, layered upon itself until the distinction between building and terrain dissolves.

The rock beneath is ancient—erosion has smoothed it over epochs into a surface like wet soap, pocked with hollows that never dry. Water is everywhere, but it behaves strangely. It condenses from the thin, cold air directly onto your skin, your clothes, the stone, so that everything is perpetually damp, beaded with droplets that never quite fall. The air itself is thick, saturated, a soup you breathe. At this altitude, in this cold, water should freeze, but here it remains liquid, supercooled or cursed, seeping from the stone in rivulets that flow upward, defying gravity, feeding pools that hang from ceilings like inverted lakes.

The cold is absolute. Your breath crystallizes instantly, hanging in the air as a small, bright cloud that dissipates into the general mist. Yet the damp does not chill you to the bone; instead, it clings, a second skin of humidity that makes the cold feel intimate, pervasive, inevitable. Ice forms on the leeward sides of structures, lace that grows and melts in cycles too rapid to track, part of the turbulence that defines this place.

For all its antiquity—the stone worn smooth, the patterns of settlement established long before memory—the settlement is in a state of storm. It is turbulent, hyperactive, a place of constant creation and unmaking. Walls of woven birch and pale, resonant bone are erected in the morning and sag into ruin by dusk, consumed by the very moisture they are meant to shed. The inhabitants—small, wiry figures in furs of a blue-gray hue, wielding tools of knapped flint and antler—move through this chaos with the calm of those who have never known permanence. They are builders of the ephemeral, carpenters of a city that is always half-finished, half-collapsed, a frozen moment of construction caught in amber humidity.

The density is oppressive. Every cubic meter contains presences: the builders, the tools, the dripping water, the mist, the structures pressing in. It is a high-saturation environment where near-touching is the only possible state. You cannot swing a staff without striking a wall, a rope, a neighbor, or a condensation tendril thick as a finger.

And through it all move the fauna. They are abundant, saturating the volume like the water itself. They are translucent, gelatinous creatures—some no larger than a fist, others drifting like living curtains between the levels of the settlement. They make no sound. They move through the saturated air with a silence that is absolute, muffling the world. The thick, humid atmosphere absorbs all noise; you hear only the drip of water, a slow, meditative percussion, and the creak of wet wood, and the blood in your own ears. The silence is so complete that the turbulence of the place—the constant shifting, the creaking, the collapse of a distant wall—seems to happen in a vacuum, a visual storm without acoustic signature.

These creatures are the gardeners here. In a place where vegetation is sparse—only a sparse scattering of pale lichens and a few stubborn fungi clinging to the north faces of the stone, and perhaps some algae that stains the water green in patches—the fauna are the primary life. They do not eat the plants; they are the plants, in a way, or they are something else entirely. They secrete the calcium that reinforces the walls, they filter the air, they die and become part of the mortar. They are silent partners in the construction, drifting through the corridors like thoughts made manifest.

The weirdness settles over everything like frost. Reality is thin here. The water that flows upward does not merely defy gravity; it carries memories. If you drink from the upward-streams, you taste the future rain. The structures, when they collapse, do not fall; they unravel, becoming mist that reforms into new shapes an hour later. Time moves in eddies. The bright light, so intense, casts shadows that move independently of their sources, sketching architectures that do not yet exist.

This is a place of boundaries—small in scale, bounded by the sheer cliff face on one side and the void of the high plateau on the other. It is a single feature, a single area, a hamlet-sized cathedral of condensation and bone. It has existed long enough to be worn smooth, to be in balance with its own destruction, yet it changes so rapidly that no two visits find it the same.

You stand in the central chamber, where the air is thickest, where the light is brightest, where the silence is most profound. Around you, the city breathes. Water weeps. The cold holds. And the translucent things drift past, touching your face with a cold that is not unkind, only absolute, leading you deeper into the wet, bright, silent heart of the place.
