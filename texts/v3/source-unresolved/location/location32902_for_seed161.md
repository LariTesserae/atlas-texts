High above the haze-line, where the air grows thin and warm, there sits a tableland no larger than a city district, yet old enough to have forgotten the mountains that birthed it. This is a mesa of ultimate flatness—extreme elevation worn smooth by three billion years of patient wind. The rock here is not jagged or dramatic; it has been planed to a stone floor, a primordial plain suspended in the sky, its edges crumbling slowly into dust that drifts down to the world below. The surface is warm to the touch, heated by geothermal breath rising through fissures in the ancient crust, keeping the high-altitude air at a perpetual mild spring.

Upon this stone stage stands a settlement that is dense as a knot of roots. The architecture is urban in its organization—narrow lanes web between multi-story structures, courtyards open to shared spaces, drainage channels carved into the living rock—but the technology is hand-wrought: dry-stone masonry without mortar, timber frames lashed with fiber, roofs of layered thatch and bark. The buildings press close, three and four stories high, their walls softened by centuries of encroaching growth. This is a primitive city in its final, settled form, neither expanding nor decaying, merely persisting in the equilibrium of deep time.

The vegetation is paradoxically lush. With no rain, no rivers, no standing water to drink of, the flora has evolved to drink the sky itself. A persistent cap of high-altitude fog rolls over the mesa each dawn, and the plants—thick mosses, lichens, ferns with fronds like lace, and low, gnarled shrubs with waxy leaves—capture the moisture directly from the air. They grow in profusion, draping every wall, filling every crevice, carpeting the streets in spongy green. The canopy is dense and layered, filtering the already dim light into a perpetual twilight. The world here is green and grey, stone and chlorophyll, a vertical garden packed into a room-sized world.

Because the air is thin and the vegetation thick, sound behaves strangely—it carries far but feels muffled, as if the world is heard through water. And it is loud. The fauna is abundant: colonies of small, quick creatures—scaled and furred, winged and many-legged—thrive in the vertical forests and the crumbling eaves. They chatter, buzz, trill, and rustle from dawn to dusk, a constant, layered chorus that never fully ceases. The wind itself adds its voice, whistling through the stone canyons of the streets, a low, patient drone that has worn the rock smooth over eons.

Everything moves slowly. The fog comes and goes in gradual cycles. The moss grows a millimeter per decade. The buildings settle into the stone as the mesa sinks microscopically into the mantle. This is a place at the end of its erosion, at the end of its growth, settled into a final, stable form. It is warm, wet with condensation but dry of liquid, dimly lit, deafening with life, and utterly still in its ancient, primitive, urban solitude.
