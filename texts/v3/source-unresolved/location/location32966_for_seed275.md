The Undergrowth of Keth

You descend not into darkness but into a cathedral of slow birth. The elevation is zero — you are beneath the skin of the world, inside a cavern system so vast it has its own weather, its own climate, its own time. The scale is regional: you could walk for days through this place, moving from one industrial district to the next across a valley of growing stone, never seeing the same wall twice.

The air is cool — a steady, penetrating chill that carries no humidity, because there is no water here. Not a drop. The chemistry of this place runs on silicon and carbon, on hydrogen and sulfur, on mineral acids that would burn water-based life. Instead of rain, there is a fine, crystalline dust that drifts down from the ceiling like snow made of salt and glass. Instead of rivers, there are channels of slow-moving, viscous sap — amber-thick and ancient — that ooze from the walls and pool in the low places, never evaporating, never flowing fast, solidifying into ambergris-like resin that the city mines for fuel.

The geology is embryonic. The walls are not stone yet, but something *becoming* stone. You can press your palm against the cavern walls and feel them yield slightly, warm and pulsing, as minerals precipitate from the air and accrete in real-time. Stalactites grow not over millennia but over months, dripping not water but a milky, mineral-rich fluid that hardens into new rock the moment it touches the floor. The erosion here is negative — the landscape is accumulating, building itself up, layer by layer, while the city tries to keep pace. The raw geology is undeveloped, pre-formation: jagged, sharp, still deciding what it wants to be.

And the vegetation is total.

Not the green of leaves and chlorophyll, but the green of new copper, of oxidized mineral, of life that has learned to eat rock. The flora here are the **Lithophytes** — vast, sparse organisms that rise from the cavern floor like the pillars of a ruined temple, but still growing. They are spaced far apart — you can see across the void between them, forty meters of empty air and dust-mist — yet their canopies interlock above, forming a continuous ceiling of living architecture. They are the substrate. They are the buildings. They are the factories.

Each Lithophyte is a tower of accreted mineral and organic matter, perhaps fifty meters tall, with a trunk of compressed silica and cellulose, branching into limbs that support a canopy of translucent, glass-like structures. They are not photosynthetic; they are chemosynthetic, harvesting energy from the mineral dust, from sulfur vents in the deep rock, from the slow decay of the stone itself. Their "leaves" are crystalline membranes that chime softly in the thermal currents, producing a high, glassy music that is almost but not quite audible.

Between these giants, the ground is carpeted in a low, dense mat of fungal moss — total coverage in the sense that no raw rock shows, but it is thin, only centimeters high, a living skin over the embryonic geology. It is this moss that makes the primary sound of the place: a soft, dry rustling, like paper shifting in a wind that doesn't exist, as the mycelium network shifts, grows, and digests the mineral substrate beneath it.

The city — for it is a city, settlement-scale, urban in its organization — sprawls across this forest floor. But it is a sparse city. The density is low, balanced with emptiness. The industrial complexes are vast, rambling structures of iron and ceramic and polymer, spaced kilometers apart across the moss-carpet, connected by elevated walkways and pneumatic tubes that run through the hollow trunks of the Lithophytes. These are the factories: engines that run on mineral combustion, on the burning of sulfur and hydrogen, on the slow, grinding digestion of stone by massive bio-reactors. They are electrified, humming with a low-frequency thrum that you feel in your teeth more than hear, a vibration that travels through the moss and up through your boots. The light is normal — a diffuse, overcast glow that comes from bioluminescent patches on the Lithophyte canopies, and from the arc-lamps of the factories, casting long shadows across the crystalline dust that settles on every surface.

The architecture is layered and under construction. The oldest factories are being swallowed. The Lithophytes grow around them, incorporating the iron and ceramic into their mineral trunks, so that a smokestack might emerge from what looks like a natural rock formation, or a warehouse might be revealed to be the hollowed-out interior of a massive fungal growth. The city is not built *on* the land; it is growing *with* the land, a symbiosis that is slightly unsettling. The weirdness lies in the pace and the direction: you can watch the walls move. A corridor that was straight yesterday is slightly curved today, bowed by the expanding girth of the Lithophyte that surrounds it. The infrastructure is constantly being rerouted, pipes bursting as roots expand, foundations sinking as new mineral layers push up from below.

Fauna are abundant here, though they are strange. They have no need for water, having evolved in this sealed, non-aqueous environment. The **Silicate Mites** are everywhere — swarms of them, hard-shelled, crystalline creatures no larger than your thumb that skitter across the factory floors, cleaning the mineral deposits from the machinery with rasping mandibles, their bodies clicking softly against the metal in a sound like hail on a tin roof, but quieter. They are the maintenance crews, the janitors of this waterless world, breeding in the warm vents of the engines. Larger creatures — the **Grazers** — wander between the industrial districts, six-legged, slow-moving, with hides like polished slate, feeding on the fungal moss and excreting the crystalline dust that feeds the Lithophytes. They are docile, almost peaceful, but their eyes are wrong — too many, arranged in spirals, or set on stalks that wave independently — a reminder that this is an ecosystem still finding its shape, still coming into being.

The sound is mostly quiet. There is no water to make noise — no dripping, no rushing, no rain, no waves. The factories hum at frequencies just below the threshold of hearing. The Lithophytes creak as they grow, a sound like ice forming on a lake, but slower, deeper, more viscous. The Silicate Mites click in chorus. The dust falls with a sound like distant applause, or breathing. It is a hushed, reverent silence, broken only by the occasional low blast of a factory horn, muffled by the thick, mineral-heavy air, or the groan of geology as a new chamber opens up somewhere in the dark, and the thermal currents shift to fill it.

This is a place in motion. The dynamic is active, multiple processes overlapping. The city expands outward, new factories rising on the edges of the cavern where the moss is still young, while the old ones in the center are slowly digested by the Lithophytes, their metal becoming nutrients, their concrete becoming sediment, their machines becoming fossils-in-waiting. The ecosystem is predator and prey, but the predators are the plants, growing over the machines, and the prey are the minerals, being consumed and reconstituted into new growth. Weather cycles exist here — thermal currents that shift with the heat of the factories, creating slow winds that carry the crystalline dust from one district to the next, depositing it in drifts against the walls, feeding the next generation of Lithophytes. The whole place is a machine that is also a garden, a factory that is also a forest, a city that is being born even as it is being used.

You stand on a gantry between two Lithophytes, looking out over the sprawl. Below, the fungal moss glows faintly in the factory light, pulsing with slow bioluminescence. Above, the canopy interlocks, a ceiling of glass-leaves filtering the light into a soft, greenish day. Somewhere, a Silicate Mite colony is stripping a conveyor belt clean, their crystalline shells flashing in the beam of a work-lamp. Somewhere else, a new stalactite is forming, dripping its milky fluid onto a patch of moss that will, in a few years, swell into a new pillar, a new home, a new part of the city.

It is cool. It is dry. It is strange. It is alive.

And it is still under construction.
