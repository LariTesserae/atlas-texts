The space is a thorax. Specifically, it is the hollow chest-cavity of a creature that fell, recently—geologically speaking—and has not yet finished dying. You are inside a single, vast room, a pocket of the world embedded within the sharp, white architecture of ribs that curve overhead like the vault of a cathedral built from ivory and grief. The scale is intimate despite the height; from any point on the mossy floor, you can see the far wall, the opposite curve of bone, the dark aperture where the spine once connected. It is a contained microcosm, a settlement-scale chamber hidden within the corpse of something continent-sized.

The air is saturated. Water is the dominant element, a 1.9 on any scale of wetness. The ribs weep. They are porous, young bone—erosion has not yet had time to smooth their surfaces—and they sweat a constant, mineral condensation that gathers in the dark channels of the marrow and slides down in thin, patient streams. These streams do not fall so much as *ooze*, finding the low points between the ribs, pooling on the floor in shallow, black mirrors that reflect the faint, bioluminescent gloom. The temperature is cold, a damp chill hovering just above freezing, so the breath of the place—if the place could breathe—would mist. The water in the pools is strangely warm to the touch, a lingering heat from the creature's final metabolism, creating a microclimate of rising vapor that fills the chamber with a slow, drifting fog.

Vegetation is total here. The floor is not earth; it is a raft of sphagnum and mycelium three meters thick, a spongy peat that has swallowed the sternum, the keel, and the jagged shards of fractured bone. This is plant-as-substrate, plant-as-architecture. The moss does not merely grow on the creature; it *is* the ground, humming with cold-adapted life, pale green-grey and silver, forming a living carpet that shifts subtly underfoot. Shelf fungi bracket the ribs in tiers, pale and woody, creating natural platforms and walls. Lichen crusts the bone in patterns that resemble maps of forgotten cities. The erosion is young—these features are sharp, fresh, the bone still yellow-white and splintered where it cracked on impact, not yet worn smooth by centuries of rain. The moss grows *into* the cracks, prying them wider, feeding on the calcium, a slow, vegetative reclamation that is visibly active, the peat visibly thickening, new fungal shelves budding overnight.

Within this vegetal cathedral exists a settlement. It is urban in its organization—there is infrastructure, a layout, a sense of civic space—but sparse in its density, a town of scattered islands in a sea of mist. Wooden walkways, lashed together with fiber rope and no metal, span the gaps between the ribs at various heights, creating a vertical infrastructure. They creak as they settle, a low, groaning sigh that is one of the few sounds here. Platforms jut from the bone like balconies, supporting huts woven from reeds and lined with insulating moss. At the center, where the ribs converge toward the fossilized spine, there is a plaza: a circle of packed peat where low, smokeless fires burn, fueled by dried puffballs and compressed peat. The technology is primitive, pre-metal, manual—tools are carved from the creature’s smaller bones, needles and awls and scrapers, hand-crafted and passed down. There are no machines, only the creak of wood and the lash of rope.

But the settlement is not dense. Between the walkways, there is only emptiness—wide swathes of mist-filled space, the dark pools, the slow drift of pale shapes. The huts are separated by meters of empty, weeping peat. It is a sparse urbanism, balanced with the wilderness, a city that has learned to leave room for the overwhelming.

For the fauna is overwhelming. The moss is not still; it moves. It is carpeted, layered, *infested* with bone-worms—translucent, segmented, silent creatures that pulse across the peat in slow, synchronized waves, a tide of pale life that makes no sound but the softest whisper of membrane against moss. They are everywhere, on the floor, on the walls, climbing the ribs in dense, glistening columns. Higher up, between the walkways, drift the rib-moths—creatures the size of a hand, with wings like rice-paper, eyeless and pale, navigating the dark by scent and vibration. They are a blizzard, a silent snowfall of wings, overwhelming in their numbers, yet they produce no buzz, no flutter, only the softest brush against bone. When they land, they do not stay; they dissolve, melting into the moss to become part of the substrate, a strange metamorphosis that suggests they are not separate from the plants, but a mobile phase of them. The ecology is a closed loop: the vegetation feeds the fauna, the fauna die and feed the vegetation, the water cycles through all of them in a constant, condensing rain.

The light is darkness. The chamber is roofed by the creature’s own mass—mummified tissue that has turned to something like leather, overhanging flaps of desiccated hide that block the sky. No sun penetrates. The only illumination is bioluminescent: the faint blue-green pulse of the shelf fungi, the glow of the moss-pots cultivated in the huts, the occasional flash of a spore-sac releasing its dust into the still, cold air. It is a velvety dark, thick and pressing, in which the drifting moths appear as pale, ghostly interruptions.

Sound is mostly quiet. The dominant acoustics are the sparse, irregular percussion of water dripping—*plink, plink*—into the black pools, and the creak of the wooden infrastructure as the corpse settles, millimeter by millimeter, under the weight of the growing peat. The fauna are silent. The mist muffles. The silence is heavy, expectant, broken only by the occasional splash of something moving through the warm pools, or the soft, wet sound of the moss growing.

The place is strange. The water that drips from the ribs is warm, though the air is glacial. The bone-worms have no mouths, yet they eat, processing the calcium through their skin. The layout of the walkways follows the grooves of the creature’s ancient nervous system, the paths of nerves now fossilized into the bone, so the "streets" of the city trace the ghost of a mind. The creature is still dying, still collapsing inward, and the city adjusts to the shifting architecture, re-lashing walkways, moving platforms, a settlement in constant, slow negotiation with the decay of its host.

It is dynamic. Multiple processes run simultaneously: the condensation cycle—evaporation from the warm pools, rising to the cold bone, precipitating back down in a closed, indoor weather; the biological pace of the worm-tides pulsing across the floor; the fungal growth extending new shelves; the slow, grinding tectonics of the corpse settling. Predator and prey exist here in the slowest possible tempo—the worms consuming the dead moss, the moths being consumed by the carnivorous pitcher-plants that grow in the damp corners, their traps silent and patient.

To stand in the plaza, surrounded by the sparse glow of moss-lanterns, looking up at the curving ribs that vanish into the dark, listening to the dripping water and the creak of wood, watching the silent, overwhelming drift of pale wings through the mist—this is to be inside a pocket of the world, a single room where the boundary between the built and the grown has dissolved, where a primitive, sparse city clings to the sharp, fresh bones of a god, cold and wet and dark, alive with a quiet, teeming, overwhelming stillness.
