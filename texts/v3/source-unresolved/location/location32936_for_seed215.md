You stand on a continent that is still deciding what it wants to be.

The air is thin here—three thousand meters up, perhaps four—carrying the weight of its own weather systems, vast enough to generate fronts that roll across horizons measured in hundreds of kilometers. It is cold in the way of places that hold moisture close to the freezing point but never quite surrender to it: a chill that soaks through rather than bites, that keeps everything in a state of perpetual damp. The light is ordinary, the pale gold of midday diffused through a sky so saturated with condensation that shadows barely exist. Everything glows with equal intensity, as if the world were wrapped in rice paper.

The ground beneath you is wrong for a city. It is too new. The rock—basaltic, maybe, or some metamorphic precursor still sorting itself into definable strata—has not yet learned the softness of age. Erosion here is a process that has barely begun; the stone is sharp, fractured, full of angles that catch the mist and hold it. Water does not flow across this surface so much as emerge from it. The stone weeps. Every cliff face, every exposed outcrop, every fractured plane in the raw geology seeps with moisture drawn from the condensing atmosphere, droplets gathering in the mineral veins until they run in silver threads down to the saturated earth below. There are no rivers yet, not really—only a landscape so wet that the distinction between ground and water has not been fully negotiated. Pockets of standing water sit in the hollows, black and still, reflecting the uniform sky.

And yet there is a city here. Or rather, the city is the landscape, spread across it like a nervous system.

Because this is a place of sparse density but high construction, the infrastructure does not cluster. It extends. Vast platforms of some material that seems grown rather than built—bone-white, slightly translucent, resonant when struck—perch on stilts above the seeping ground, connected by bridges that span valleys still too young to have names. The structures are few and far between, separated by kilometers of empty, weeping rock, but they are everywhere in the sense that they form a continuous network. A road here is not a road but a ribbon of compressed earth and smart-material that adjusts its elevation as the geology beneath it shifts, which it does constantly, the continent still settling into its uplift. The technology is beyond the modern, yes, but it wears normalcy like a well-worn coat. Gravity is locally negotiated in the foundations of the platforms. The bridges are grown from engineered coral-like substances that strengthen under the pressure of the cold, wet wind. Atmosphere processors—silent, vast, shaped like termite mounds or standing stones—maintain the precise humidity and temperature that keep this high place from freezing solid or drying out. They are the reason the rocks weep; they pull moisture from the thin air and distribute it across the emerging terrain, accelerating the weathering, encouraging the slow birth of soil.

Between these nodes of construction, the land belongs to movement. The vegetation is sparse—tufts of hardy grass, mats of lichen, the occasional stunted shrub clinging to a crevice—but the fauna are abundant, and they have made the wet cold their kingdom. Herds of something like musk-ox, but larger, with coats that shed the constant mist, move across the plateau in columns that stretch to the horizon. They are quiet animals, their hooves making no sound on the saturated earth, their breath rising in small clouds that dissipate into the general damp. Smaller creatures—swarms of insects adapted to the cold, birds that nest in the hollows of the infrastructure—fill the spaces between the great beasts. The ecosystem is active, in motion, a pulse of life moving through the vast spaces, following the water, following the sparse grass, migrating with the weather fronts that roll across the continental shelf.

Sound here is a question of distance. Close up, there is the drip of water from stone, the whisper of wind through the sparse grasses, the lowing of the herds—a sound so low it is felt in the chest rather than heard. The infrastructure is silent, or nearly so; its technology operates on principles that do not require combustion or friction, producing only a subsonic hum that the mist absorbs. From the platforms, you can watch weather systems the size of countries form and dissolve, rain falling in curtains that never quite reach the ground, evaporating in the dry updrafts that the atmosphere processors generate. The world is dynamic—shifting, settling, growing—but it moves at the pace of geology and climate, vast and slow and quiet.

This is a place under construction by its own inhabitants, a continental project of habitation where the city does not impose itself on the land but collaborates with its emergence, guiding the erosion, harvesting the condensation, building bridges across valleys that have not yet finished collapsing. The fauna graze beneath the silent bridges. The rocks continue to weep. And the mist rolls on, normal, cold, and endless, keeping everything soft, new, and waiting.
