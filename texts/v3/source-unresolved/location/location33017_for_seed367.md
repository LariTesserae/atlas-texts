You descend into a bowl that shouldn’t exist this high. The elevation presses down—thin, sharp air at first—but then the walls close in, ancient and curved, and the world narrows to a pocket. This is the **Resin Warren**: a cirque no larger than a city block, suspended at altitude where the stone has forgotten how to be jagged. Erosion, that patient sculptor of 2.9 eons, has worn every surface into undulating waves of mineral, a desert of smooth stone that ripples like frozen water. The ground underfoot is warm—geothermal breath seeping up through cracks in the crust—dry as bone, coated in a crust of salt and gypsum that crunches softly beneath leather boots. There are no streams. No puddles. No liquid water to speak of; the parameter of wetness here is a memory, a rumor.

Yet the air is thick.

The vegetation defies the dryness. A forest grows here, but it is not made of wood and leaf. Towering fungal columns rise twenty meters into the dimness—pale stalks the width of a cottage, crowned with broad caps that overlap like shingles, creating a canopy so dense that the sky above is a rumor of pale light. Between them grow shelf fungi in terraced curtains, lichens in velvet drifts of ochre and rust, and creeping mats of mycelium that bind the mineral dust into a spongy, living carpet. The biomass is overwhelming, lush in the way a jungle is lush—layers upon layers of growth, near-touching, saturated with color and texture—but every surface is leathery, waxy, desiccated. These are not thirsty plants; they are succulent extremophiles, drawing moisture from the warm, saturated air, condensing it onto their gills and pores, storing it in pulpy, fibrous flesh. They do not drip. They sweat, microscopically, into the humid dark.

Light filters down in a dim, amber-green gloom. The canopy is so complete that noon here feels like dusk. Shadows are soft, diffuse, everywhere. The illumination comes from within as much as above—bioluminescent patches on the undersides of bracket fungi pulse with a faint, breathing glow, and the spores themselves seem to catch the light, drifting in visible clouds that turn the air into a slow-moving fog of gold and grey.

And the settlement is built into this.

It is a town, but a pocket-town, a settlement-scale intrusion into the fungal architecture. Stone and timber and woven mycelial rope. Tech is primitive—flaked stone, fire-hardened wood, bone needles, copper only in the form of rare, hammered sheets traded from elsewhere. The structures nestle into the erosion-hollows of the bedrock, half-carved, half-built, terraced up the flanks of the fungal towers. Wooden walkways span between stalks, sagging slightly under the weight of traffic. Gardens are cultivated on the flat caps of giant mushrooms, where edible fungi are encouraged to grow in dense, managed rows. Storage pits are dug into the warm stone, lined with mineral crust to seal them. There is no mortar but packed dust and resin; the buildings seem grown as much as constructed, organic and angular in equal measure, a primitive urban fabric woven into the biology.

But the town is not alone.

Fauna is overwhelming. The air is alive with it. Swarms of chitinous insects—beetle-things the size of a thumb, winged creatures like dragonflies made of parchment, clouds of spore-mites that hang in the air like pollen—fill every cubic meter. They are not a background hum; they are a presence, a density that brushes against skin, that fills the mouth when you breathe, that darkens the light when they move in coordinated waves. They pollinate the fungal forest, they graze on the mycelial mats, they are the livestock and the workers and the weather of this place. Beneath them, in the dry leaf-litter of shed spores and dead fungal tissue, crawling things skitter—reptilian, armored, six-legged—grazing, hunting, breeding in cycles that never pause. The ecology is a frenzy, a high-saturation churn of predator and prey, of birth and consumption, all happening at a biological pace that feels almost too fast, too loud.

Sound is constant and loud. The geothermal vents hiss and rumble from fissures in the valley floor—a bass note of escaping heat. Above that, the insect swarms create a roar, a thrumming static that vibrates in the chest. The fungal stalks creak as they grow, expanding in the warmth, a slow groaning of biology. Wooden structures settle and shift. Voices carry strangely in the humid, spore-thick air, but they are often drowned out by the wings, the clicking of mandibles, the skittering of a thousand feet on stone. It is a place of noise, of active motion, of multiple processes overlapping: the weather cycles of geothermal steam, the blooming cycles of the fungi releasing their spore clouds, the predatory cycles of the swarms.

The dynamics are relentless. Warm air rises from the vents, carrying spores upward in visible columns. The insects respond to temperature gradients, swarming toward the heat, retreating to the cooler caps. The fungal forest is slowly expanding, the mycelium knitting the mineral dust into soil, the stone slowly being digested and replaced. Erosion has stopped here—or rather, it has reached its final form; the stone is so smooth, so settled, that it no longer wears, only waits, while the biology above it churns and renews.

The rules of this place are simple and strict. Water is not drunk from streams; it is squeezed from fungal pulp, or collected from condensation on cool stone in the deep hours. Food is cultivated on the caps, or harvested from the swarms—insects roasted, fungal fronds dried. The settlement is dense, near-touching, layered like the forest itself: ground level for the heavy stone-work, mid-levels for living on platforms, upper reaches for the spore-harvesters who climb into the canopy to collect the reproductive dust that is both food and medicine and currency. The scale is intimate—you can see from one wall of the cirque to the other, can shout across the whole settlement and be heard—but the density of life makes it feel vast, endless, saturated.

It is warm, always warm, despite the altitude. The geothermal heat keeps the valley in a perpetual, humid spring. The light is dim, the air is thick, the sound is loud, and the place teems with a life that has learned to thrive without the liquid that most worlds require. It is ancient, settled, primordial in its stone but young and furious in its biology, a pocket of overwhelming, active, lush desolation hidden high in the world.
