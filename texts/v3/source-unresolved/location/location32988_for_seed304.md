The hill rises from the flat wetland like a half-submerged breath—low, broad, and perpetually damp. It is geologically infant: a mound of silt and blue clay deposited in the last few centuries, still soft enough to retain the impression of a boot, still weeping the water that saturated it at birth. The sediment is young, sharp-edged, unweathered; where the surface has been cut or scraped, it gleams with moisture and crumbles at the touch. Around its base, the land is marsh—cool, still water holding the sky in fragments, reeds too sparse and wiry to form a proper thicket, just scattered tufts of vegetation that struggle against the saturation. The air here carries the chill of 0.6 degrees—not freezing, but a persistent, damp cool that seeps into stone and bone alike, condensing on every surface in a fine, glittering film.

But the hill is not empty. It is packed—density 2.7—hollowed out into a warren of such intricacy that the remaining earth resembles a sponge, or perhaps a lung. The settlement within is urban in its organization though primitive in its means: a network of chambers, galleries, and vertical shafts carved by hand with stone adzes and antler picks, supported by timber props lashed with fiber cord, lined with clay plaster that the inhabitants renew weekly as the walls slump. There is no wasted volume. Living spaces press against storage pits, which press against drainage channels, which press against ventilation chimneys that pierce the surface like snorkels. The scale is intimate—the entire city perhaps the size of a large cathedral, or a single vast room subdivided into countless niches—but within that boundary, existence is saturated. One can stand in a chamber and hear the breathing of three households through the shared walls.

Water is the architect here. The water table sits at the surface; dig down a hand's breadth and water seeps in, cool and mineral-heavy. Inside the hill, it is everywhere: dripping from ceilings in steady percussion, pooling in clay-lined sumps, running through carved channels that gurgle softly into the marsh below. The atmosphere inside is a condensing one—walls sweat, ceilings weep, and in the cool temperature, the moisture hangs in the air as a perpetual mist that catches the dim light (1.5, normal but filtered, gray and diffuse) into a luminous gloom. The inhabitants have learned to build with this wetness: floors are raised on stone slabs, sleeping platforms are woven from the tough reeds that grow on the surface, and every chamber has a designated “weeping corner” where water is allowed to gather and drain.

Technology is of the hand and the hafted stone. There is no metal here, or so little that it is legend; instead, there is obsidian-edged flint, bone needles, wooden shovels, and an intimate knowledge of clay behavior. The people plaster their walls with a mixture of clay and crushed shell to make them harder, to slow the slump. They weave baskets to carry away spoil. They have organized labor on an urban scale—gangs of diggers, teams of plasterers, keepers of the water-channels—yet their tools remain simple, their construction manual and layered, built up generation by generation as the hill slowly exhales moisture and contracts.

Above, the surface is nearly bare—vegetation sparse at 0.7. Only the toughest grasses and a few stunted, salt-tolerant shrubs take root in the thin, soggy soil, their roots shallow because the water is too near. The hill looks, from a distance, like a bald, wet, gray knuckle pushing up through the marsh grass. But inside, in the packed dark, life is dense and warm-blooded. Fauna is rare (1.2): one might find pale, eyeless fish in the deepest sump-pools, or cave-adapted insects that skitter over the damp clay, but the press of human presence—of bodies, of stored grain, of smoke from clay-lined hearths—has driven most wildlife away. What animals remain are those that live in the walls themselves, or in the few deep, unexcavated pockets where the water runs black and still.

The sound of the place is mostly quiet (1.1). The water drips. The clay hums—a low, subsonic resonance that seems to come from the hill itself as it expands and contracts with moisture. Voices are muffled by earth; footsteps are soft; the scrape of a stone tool against wet clay is a intimate, private sound. Occasionally, a distant chunk as a wall slumps, followed by the sound of repair—the slap of clay, the tamping of earth. But mostly, it is the sound of water moving through earth, and breathing.

And the hill is alive—dynamic at 1.7. It is never finished. The young erosion means the walls are constantly shifting; passages close overnight and must be reopened by dawn. The moisture content fluctuates with the weather cycles outside; after a rain, the hill swells, and doors stick, and new cracks appear in the plaster. The inhabitants are in a constant state of geological maintenance, digging deeper as the upper levels become too unstable, carving new sumps as old ones silt up, redirecting the flow of the interior rivers. The weirdness (1.4) lies in this instability: the city is not built upon the land but is the land, reshaped daily. In the deepest chambers, where the air is thick with condensation and the temperature drops to the cool extreme, the walls seem to pulse, to breathe. Water sometimes flows upward in narrow capillary channels, defying gravity, pooling on ceilings before dripping down. The geometry of the tunnels shifts subtly from week to week; a passage that was straight becomes curved, a chamber that was round becomes ovoid, as if the hill is dreaming and rearranging itself in its sleep.

Light comes only from above—ventilation shafts angled to catch the overcast sky, or from the lamps of rendered fat and fiber wicks that burn with a smoky, yellow flame. In the deeper levels, it is always twilight, the normal light of the surface world reduced to a memory. Here, in this packed, wet, breathing dark, the people live close together, shoulder to shoulder in the press of the chambers, listening to the water, repairing the walls, and waiting for the hill to exhale again.
