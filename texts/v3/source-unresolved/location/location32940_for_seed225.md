It is a room without walls, a hollow carved from the living rock and opened to the sky. You stand at the lip of a doline—a sunken court where the ceiling has fallen away centuries ago, leaving a shaft of light to fall thirty meters down into a chamber that is, despite its depth, blindingly bright. The light does not behave like ordinary sun; it is diffuse, bounced, and multiplied by walls sheathed in moonmilk, a creamy calcite secretion that glows with its own soft luminescence, so that even the shadows here are only pale grey, never black. The space is small in the way a cathedral nave is small—bounded, intimate, a single feature—but within those boundaries it is nearly void. There is almost nothing per volume: just air, thick with moisture and the slow drift of dust motes, and the vast emptiness between presences.

The stone is warm. You can feel the heat radiating from the south-facing walls, stored from the day’s sun and released slowly, keeping the temperature at a perpetual, humid mildness. The surfaces are established, in their own state, weathered smooth by water that no longer flows but has, over long ages, planed the rock to a satin finish. It is damp everywhere—not wet, but beaded with condensation, with a constant, patient drip from the ceiling that has worn shallow basins into the floor, now holding shallow pools of milky, mineral-heavy water. The pools are warm, stagnant, reflecting the strange light upward.

Vegetation is sparse, a matter of margins and cracks. Ferns—pale, etiolated fronds—cling to ledges where dust has collected, their roots drinking from the condensation. Moss carpets the lower walls in patches of deep green, and lichens crust the higher stone in maps of grey and orange. There is no canopy, no tangle; just these isolated presences holding on at the edges of the void.

Yet the place is abundant with life, though it is a silence-haunted abundance. Thousands of bats hang in clusters from the ceiling, their wings folded like dark umbrellas, utterly still, their breathing too quiet to hear. They fill the upper air with their presence, a living ceiling of fur and membrane, but they make no sound—only the occasional, soft *tick* of a claw adjusting its grip, or the dry whisper of a wing unfolding as one drops to hunt the insects rising from the pools below. In the water, pale, blind things move—cave fish or salamanders, eyeless and pink, drifting in slow, patient loops. On the walls, isopods and springtails crawl in teeming, silent multitudes, grazing the mineral film. The fauna is everywhere, but it is a fauna of stillness, of slow metabolisms and patient waiting, so the place remains mostly quiet, broken only by the plink of water, the sigh of air moving through fissures, and the distant, muffled chorus of the outside world, filtered through tons of earth.

Scattered across the floor, half-buried in the dust and flowstone, are the tools. Not built—nothing is constructed here, no wall raised, no shelter framed—but made. Handaxes of basalt and chert, their edges still sharp, flakes of knapping debris, a core stone pocked with the scars of percussion. They lie in a rough crescent near the driest part of the floor, as if someone sat here long ago and worked the stone, flaking blades and scrapers, before moving on, leaving the debris of their manufacture. A dark patch of charcoal and ash marks where a small fire burned, the wood long since consumed, leaving only a stain in the mineral dust. These are primitive technologies, pre-metal, simple fabrication, but they are not ancient in the archaeological sense—they feel recent, as if the maker stepped away yesterday, yet the erosion around them is established, in balance, suggesting they have rested here through seasons of slow drip and patient weathering.

And the place is strange. The geometry is too perfect, the curve of the walls a near-flawless ellipse that no natural collapse should produce. The moonmilk glows with a faint phosphorescence that competes with the daylight, so that at the edges of vision, the walls seem to shift, to breathe. The bats, when they do stir, fly in patterns that suggest navigation by something other than echolocation—by the magnetic pull of the stone, perhaps, or by the quality of the light itself. The pools reflect not the sky above, but a deeper darkness, as if the water is a window into somewhere else. Most peculiar is the silence: with such abundance of fauna, one expects sound, yet the chamber swallows noise, the moonmilk absorbing it, so that a shout dies immediately, and even the dripping water seems to fall without echo.

Time moves slowly here. The drips are patient, one every few seconds, each drop wearing the stone a fraction of a millimeter. The tools sink imperceptibly into the dust. The bats hang in torpor, their hearts beating at a crawl. The light shifts across the floor over hours, tracing a path that is almost, but not quite, the sun’s true arc. It is a place of gradual cycles, of change across long durations, where the boundaries between the constructed and the natural have dissolved—not because humans built here, but because the stone itself seems to have been worked, shaped, into a vessel for light and silence, waiting, in its warm, damp, empty vastness, for whatever comes next.
