You descend not by stairs but by the slow tilt of the world itself. The Glass Expanse—Keth-Vorath to those who remember its tongue—lies at the bottom of a crater so vast that its far wall dissolves into heat-shimmer before you reach it. You are below the surface of the world, though the sky blazes overhead with a bright, pitiless clarity (light 2.6). The rim of the volcano is a distant diadem, and you stand in the bowl, surrounded by a silence so complete it feels like pressure (sound 0.2).

The ground is not earth. It is **fulgurite and obsidian**, fused by an ancient heat into a near-flat plain of black glass that stretches for miles in every direction (scale 1.9, erosion 2.6). The erosion here is not the violence of water or wind, though both have passed this way. It is the erosion of *time*—millennia of thermal cycling, of microscopic expansion and contraction, that has worn the architecture down until the distinction between building and geology has nearly vanished. What were once walls, towers, and terraced streets are now smooth, abstract **monoliths** that rise from the glass like the fins of some buried leviathan. They are still recognizably constructed—you can see the hand-stacked courses, the deliberate angles—but they have been sanded by centuries into shapes that resemble natural formations: hoodoos, or the melted wax of candles left burning too long.

The city is sparse (density 0.7), its structures scattered across the plain with miles of empty glass between them. A settlement of thousands once, now a handful of domes and ziggurats standing in the vast silence. The builders worked with **primitive tech** (tech 0.7): flint and copper, bone and cord, hand-chipped obsidian blades that they used to carve channels into the living rock. There is no metal smelted here, no kiln-fired brick—only the glass itself, which they learned to work when it was still warm, shaping it with wooden paddles and leather thongs into vaults and habitations that have since cooled into permanence.

But the place is not dead. It is **turbulent** (dynamic 2.8), though the turbulence is geological, slow, and utterly silent. The caldera breathes. You can see it if you watch long enough: a section of glass floor buckles upward, a new fumarole opens with a hiss that the damp air (water 0.8) immediately swallows. Steam rises in pale columns from vents that were not there yesterday. The ground subsides in one quadrant while swelling in another, tilting a nearby structure—a once-vertical wall now leaning at a drunken angle—so that it will collapse in perhaps a decade, or a century, the timeframe irrelevant in this place of ancient, ongoing change. Pockets of water collect in the low points: shallow, brackish pools that steam in the cool morning air (temperature 1.3), fed by condensation that drips from the glass overhangs where the warm stone meets the cooler sky. The water is never still for long; it seeps into cracks, travels through the porous pumice bedrock, and reappears elsewhere, dissolving silica, carrying minerals, reshaping the foundations of the city from within.

There is no vegetation (vegetation 0.3). The glass is sterile. The only "plants" are **chemosynthetic crusts**—pale, leathery mats of bacteria and archaea that colonize the damp cracks, feeding on the sulfur and iron leaching from the cooling stone. They are not green; they are the color of old bone and rust, and they spread in slow, branching patterns across the surfaces of the ruins.

Yet the fauna is abundant (fauna 1.5). The glass-eaters. They have evolved—or been bred, or simply *found*—in the thermal vents and condensation pools, a food web built entirely on geothermal energy, bypassing the sun entirely. They are **silica-metabolizers**: translucent, segmented creatures like living glass shards, with too many jointed legs and mouths that rasp against the stone. They swarm in the warm places, in the steam-vents, in the shallow pools where the water is thick with mineral bloom. They are eyeless, navigating by vibration and chemical signature, and they make no sound as they move—a silent, clicking, scraping tide of life that flows across the black plain, consuming the city grain by grain, molecule by molecule. When they feed, they leave the glass etched, frosted, slowly erasing the last tool-marks of the builders.

The atmosphere is one of **exposed vertigo**. You are deep below the surrounding world, yet the light is too bright, reflecting off the glass floor with a glare that makes it difficult to look down for long. The silence is broken only by the occasional *groan* of settling stone, a sound like a glacier calving, felt in the teeth rather than heard. The air tastes of ozone and damp mineral. The city is being unmade—not by flood or fire, but by the slow, turbulent, silent metabolism of the earth and the patient hunger of its creatures.

It is a place where construction and collapse are the same process, viewed from different timescales. The primitive hands that raised those glass walls are dust, their tools long since eroded into the plain, but their geometry persists, softened by age, being actively dismantled by heat and chemistry and the endless, quiet swarming of the glass-eaters. A town-sized necropolis (built 2.2, scale 1.9) that is also a living, shifting body, cool and bright and strangely, terribly silent.
