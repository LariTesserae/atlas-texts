You enter a basin that was once a factory floor, now a shallow, indoor sea. The space is small—perhaps forty meters across—bounded by the skeletal remains of walls, their upper reaches lost in shadow. It is cool here, the air heavy with moisture that refuses to settle. Every surface weeps.

The water is everywhere. It pools in the rusted depressions of the concrete, ankle-deep, tea-colored with tannins and iron oxide. It rises in the hollows of collapsed machinery, forming still mirrors that reflect the dim, diffuse light filtering from high, grimy windows or gaps in the roof. The atmosphere condenses on every cool metal surface—pipes, girders, the ribcages of abandoned vehicles—hanging in thick, trembling curtains of droplets that fall with soft, irregular ticks. The slag and concrete weep, sweating mineral-laden water that carries the orange of rust and the white of calcium carbonate.

There is no green. Vegetation died here long ago, or never grew. Instead, the floor is a mineral garden of fungal complexity—thick, black mats of chemosynthetic colonies that spread across the metal like spilled ink, pulsing slowly with the rhythm of pumps that still cycle somewhere in the dark. These are not plants; they are lithotrophs, feeding on the slow oxidation of the iron beneath them, excreting crusts of white and ochre that build tiny, cave-like formations on the steel. The erosion is established, ancient—decades, perhaps centuries—and the place has settled into its own state of decay, a balance between the water's appetite and the metal's resistance, weathered and in-its-own-state.

The density is overwhelming. In this small, cathedral-like volume, presences press against one another. The air is saturated with the creatures. They are abundant, strange, and utterly without chlorophyll. Pale, translucent things—segmented, perhaps, or colonial—cling to every vertical surface. They resemble nothing so much as wet, grey sea-anemones that have learned to walk, or perhaps giant, aquatic isopods the size of a hand, their many legs gripping the wet rust. They graze. You can see them moving in synchronized waves across a gantry crane's crossbeam, scraping the fungal mats with mandibles that glint like mica. They are cool to the touch, gelatinous, and they leave trails of slime that catch the dim light before fading.

Scattered through this living clutter are the remnants of industry. A control booth sits half-submerged, its windows fogged with condensation, a single red light blinking inside—slow, arrhythmic. Pipes traverse the space at waist-height, some intact, others ruptured, venting thin jets of steam that hiss against the cool air and immediately bloom into clouds of condensation. A collapsed conveyor belt forms a ramp into the deeper water, its rollers encrusted with white mineral blooms and crawling with the pale fauna. Occasionally, a pump somewhere in the infrastructure kicks on—a low, mechanical groan that vibrates through the floor—and the water level shifts, a slow inhalation and exhalation that moves floating debris in lazy circles.

The light is dim, a perpetual twilight. It comes from above, filtered through layers of grime and moisture, casting everything in shades of grey, rust-orange, and the sickly white of the fungal crusts. Shadows are dense, pooling in the corners where the walls meet the floor.

The sounds are mostly quiet—the tick of dripping water, the soft, rasping scrape of mandibles on metal, the distant, subsonic hum of active machinery. But there is a biological pace here, a movement. The creatures pulse in their colonies. The steam vents hiss and subside. The water circulates, carrying dissolved minerals from the decaying metal to the fungal mats, which feed the grazers, which die and sink to become sediment for new crusts. It is an ecosystem in motion, a closed loop of rust and water and strange, patient life.

The rules of this place are mineral. Gravity pulls the condensation down. The cool preserves. The industrial past provides the chemistry—iron, water, oxygen—and the fauna have learned to eat rust. Time moves in the slow weathering of steel, the patient accretion of limescale, the endless, dripping present.
