You descend through a throat of black glass. The walls are obsidian, fractured in conchoidal patterns, sharp enough to draw blood from the dry air itself. This is young rock—barely a million years old, thrust up from the mantle in jagged, unweathered spires that have not yet learned the softness of erosion. The elevation is extreme; the air here is cool and thin, carrying the faint taste of ozone and hot metal. There is no water. Not a drop, not a memory of rain. The stone is desiccated pumice and crystalline salt, baked dry by geothermal heat and the vacuum of altitude. Any moisture clings to the undersides of mineral overhangs as frost that sublimates before it can fall, leaving only a white efflorescence that dusts the dark glass.

Yet this is not empty. It is hidden—a pocket-scale world, a single feature contained within the mountain's stone heart, invisible from the peaks above. And it is dense. The interior of this peak is not a cavern but a city: a vertical metropolis of industrial saturation, layered construction packed into the negative space of the geology. Factories climb the walls in terraced rings, their conduits, smokestacks, and catwalks forming a lattice so tight that daylight—what little filters through the narrow entrance high above—never reaches the lower levels. The structures are near-touching, stacked and interpenetrating, creating a high-saturation environment where every cubic meter holds a presence: machinery, piping, waste-heat radiators, and the press of living things. The light is dim, a perpetual industrial twilight of sodium vapor and blue-white arc-welding, filtered through perpetual dust and chemical haze.

Here, life abounds in strange profusion. The fauna is everywhere—thousands upon thousands of creatures that have no need for water or photosynthesis. They are lithovores, or perhaps chemo-synthesizers, their biology based on non-aqueous chemistry: they process sulfur, iron, and the heavy metals exhaled by the factories, their bodies translucent with mineral deposits, their segmented forms clicking silently against the metal catwalks. They swarm through the infrastructure like blood through veins, cleaning, recycling, consuming the waste heat and mineral detritus in an endless, silent metabolism. They fill every gap between the near-touching structures, abundant and omnipresent, the city's immune system and its tireless workforce, communicating only through vibration and chemical signals that leave no audible trace.

The place is turbulent. The mountain is alive with micro-tremors; the young geology shifts and settles in its sleep. Yesterday's smelting plant may be crushed by a slow rockfall by morning, only to be replaced by a new forge by evening as the population surges to fill the gap. The city is in constant, chaotic reconfiguration—a storm-state of creation and destruction, hyperactive, an anthill-energy of industry and biology intertwined. Vents open and seal with explosive gas. Walkways extend and retract. The fauna surge in roiling, silent clouds through the shifting passages, adapting to the new architecture as quickly as it appears.

But it is quiet. Despite the turmoil, there is little sound. The creatures do not vocalize; they move with the softness of dust settling on stone. The engines of the factories hum at frequencies below the threshold of hearing, their thunder absorbed by the dense, layered mass of the structures that press against one another. Even the collapses are muffled—thuds swallowed by the press of packed industry and the cushion of swarming fauna. You hear only your own breathing, dry and ragged in the thin, cool air, and the low, constant, subsonic groan of the mountain as it remakes itself around you, a sealed world of mineral and machine where strange life thrives without water, without plants, without sun—only the heat of the young earth and the endless, quiet, turbulent churn of a hidden industrial ecosystem.
