The stone is warm.

Not the aggressive heat of a desert sun, but a deep, mineral warmth, like the inside of a baked potato, radiating up from the planet’s slow heartbeat. You stand on the Mesa—and it is a mesa in the old sense, a tableland lifted high above the surrounding darkness, though here the “surrounding” is only more of the same flattened antiquity stretching to a horizon that curves away in a subtle, disorienting arc.

The elevation is real. The air is thin enough to make your lungs work, yet thick enough to carry heat and pressure in a way that feels underwater. It is a high-altitude twilight, the sky a dome of bruised violet so dense it seems to press down, filtering what little light exists into a uniform, directionless gloom. There is no sun here. There has never been a sun, or if there was, it died before the stone finished cooling. The darkness is absolute but not cold; it is the darkness of a closed oven, a womb of rock and heavy gas.

This is the Peneplain—the end-state of erosion, achieved billions of years ago and held in perfect stasis ever since. The mountains that once stood here have been unmade. Wind, when it blew, wore them down to this: a surface of fused glass and metamorphic schist, polished by time into a dull, oily sheen. The ground is not flat, exactly. It ripples in waves of negative space, shallow depressions where ancient rivers once flowed—rivers of something that was not water, perhaps, or water that has long since sublimated into the dry chemistry of the deep stone. The erosion has stopped. The atmosphere is still. The dynamic forces have reached equilibrium, and so the landscape is frozen mid-sigh, a topology of exhaustion.

Scattered across this vast, dark plain—for it is vast, regional in scale, a city-sized network spread thin as a film—are the Works. They are urban, but sparse, separated by kilometers of empty stone. There is no center, no heart, only a constellation of structures that seem to have *grown* rather than been built, their foundations fused to the bedrock in convolutions that suggest organic accretion. They are industrial: you can see the gantries, the retorts, the great circular kilns that turn with a slow, eternal rotation, grinding the stone into powder, extracting trace minerals, running on geothermal taps that draw heat from the mantle below.

The architecture is strange. The buildings have too many curves, or perhaps no curves at all—angles that meet in ways that hurt the eye, like looking at a tessellation that refuses to resolve. They are made of a material that is neither metal nor ceramic nor concrete, but something in between—a vitrified composite, perhaps, or a metal-organic framework that the industry cultivates in vats of non-aqueous solvent. There is no water here, not to speak of (that 0.2 is a generous trace, found only in the deepest fissures as a memory of humidity). The chemistry is dry, mineral, based on silicates and heavy metals and perhaps exotic ices that sublimate at this warmth. The machines do not rust; they do not corrode. They have reached a steady state where oxidation is balanced by reduction, where every piston drives against an equal and opposite force, creating motion without progress.

The light is nearly absent. What illumination exists comes from within the Works—a faint, amber glow that seeps from fissures in the structures, from the mouths of the kilns, from the vents of the geothermal exchangers. It is enough to see the immediate ground, the texture of the stone, the edges of the paths that connect the buildings. Beyond that, the world dissolves into dark. The glow does not flicker. It is constant, like the breathing of something vast and sleeping.

There is no vegetation. None. The concept of green does not exist here. There are no plants, no moss, no lichen in the Earth sense. If there is life, it is of a different order—perhaps a crystalline fungus that grows in the deep heat vents, or a chemosynthetic film that coats the undersides of the stone, feeding on minerals and thermal gradients. It is not green; it is pale, or dark, or translucent. It does not photosynthesize; there is no light to use. The absence of plants means the absence of decay in the usual sense. Things do not rot here; they simply persist, or they are processed by the machines into something else, which is then processed again, in an eternal loop.

Fauna are rare. You might walk for days and see nothing. Then, in the flicker of a maintenance drone’s lamp, you might glimpse a scuttler—a crab-like thing made of ceramic and wire, or a soft-bodied drifter that floats on thermal currents, feeding on the mineral dust. They are not native, perhaps, but remnants, or constructs, or the last refugees of an ecology that died when the water left. They move through the Works with a hesitation, as if unsure of their place in a world that has forgotten biology.

The sound is mostly quiet. There is no wind—the atmosphere is becalmed, a stagnant soup of nitrogen and heavy gases. There is no water to lap or drip. The only sound is the low, subterranean hum of the Works, a frequency felt in the teeth rather than heard, the harmonic resonance of massive engines turning in perfect, unchanging rhythm. It is the sound of equilibrium. If you press your ear to the ground, you can feel the vibration of the planet’s slow geothermal pulse, a heartbeat so slow it might be mistaken for silence.

And time... time does not pass here. Or rather, it passes without consequence. The dynamic of this place is zero, or near to it. The erosion stopped when the atmosphere stilled. The machines reached their steady state millions of years ago and have not deviated since. The city does not grow; it does not crumble. It is a settlement that is also a closed thermodynamic system. To walk here is to walk through a photograph that has been exposed for eons, every detail sharp and unchanging.

This is the strangeness: not that it is alien in the way of jungles or oceans, but that it is the *end of the story*. It is post-life, or pre-life, or a life so different we do not recognize it. It is a place where industry has outlived its purpose and continues anyway, because stopping would require an input of energy, a gradient, a change—and there are none. It is a city that dreams of being a mountain, and has succeeded.

The warmth envelops you. The darkness presses close. The hum vibrates in your bones. And the stone, ancient beyond measure, waits—not for anything to happen, but for the long, slow heat-death of the universe to finally cool its bones.
