You stand at the edge of a drowned continent.

This is the **Rooftop Sea**, a high-altitude cataclysm where the world’s oldest mountains have been swallowed by water. The elevation is extreme (2.9): you are kilometers above the nearest lowland, yet you are surrounded by an inland ocean that fills every valley, every basin, every scar in the primordial rock. The peaks here are not jagged or young; they are ancient, worn down by three billion years of rain, ice, and wind to a state of near-flattened permanence (erosion 3.0). They rise from the water as smooth, dark domes—metamorphic stone polished to a glassy sheen by eons of abrasion, the oldest stable state of the crust, settled into final form. Between them, the water plunges into trenches of staggering depth, creating a landscape of vertical extremes: a maze of submerged canyons and emergent islands that extends past the horizon in every direction (scale 2.5).

The water is everywhere (2.8). It is not merely present but dominant—a cold, heavy medium (0.8) that fills the air with mist, that crashes between the ridges in perpetual whitewater, that hangs suspended in the atmosphere as a wall of rain and spray. The temperature is cool, carrying the chill of altitude and glacial melt. This is a post-flood world, or perhaps a pre-dry world, where the boundary between land and sea has dissolved entirely. The water is dense (2.0)—high-saturation, thick with sediment, plankton, and organic matter, so thick with life and particulate that visibility rarely exceeds a few meters. It is a near-touching world, where kelp fronds brush against stone, where fish schools move as single organisms, where the water itself feels like a substance, not a void.

And it is turbulent (2.9). This is not a calm lake but a storm-state, a place of hyperactive creation and destruction. Currents roar through the submerged passes at tremendous velocity, generating standing waves that never break, whirlpools the size of city blocks, and geyser-like upwellings that boil the surface where geothermal vents heat the deep basins. The water is in constant, chaotic motion—Solaris-like in its restless energy, though governed by entirely normal physics (weirdness 0.3). Massive volumes of water are pumped upward by thermal convection, spilling over ancient sills, carving new channels through the soft sediment while the hard rock remains unyielding. The sound is overwhelming (2.0): a continuous, grinding roar of moving water that vibrates in your chest, punctuated by the thunder of mini-waterfalls cascading between islands, the groan of stressed metal, and the cacophony of animal calls.

Beneath the surface, the world is lush (2.2). Cool-water kelp forests rise from the deepest trenches like submerged redwoods, their rubbery fronds reaching twenty meters toward the light, forming dense, swaying jungles that filter the gray radiance. Mosses and aquatic ferns carpet every surface—boulders, cliff faces, the rusted hulks of human industry—clinging to life in the cold, rushing dark. Floating mats of algae drift in the turbulence, thick as meadows, hosting entire micro-ecosystems. The ecology is abundant (1.9): silver schools of cold-water fish flash in the gloom, their bodies dense with the effort of swimming against the current; massive filter-feeders strain the plankton-rich water, their gills fanning in the flow; along the exposed ridges, colonies of waterbirds nest in the spray, their cries adding to the aquatic roar. Great, slow-moving creatures—whale-like in their bulk—drift through the deep channels, stirring up clouds of silt as they pass.

Scattered across this chaos are the faint marks of industry (built 0.6, tech 1.7). They are not cities but artifacts—the scattered, partial remains of a civilization that once tried to tame these waters. Rusted skeletons of hydroelectric stations cling to the cliff faces, their concrete foundations gripping the ancient rock while the water laps at their doors. You can see the remains of turbines, massive iron gears half-submerged, still turning slowly in the current, grinding with a mechanical rhythm that competes with the water’s roar. Pipes and conduits snake down into the depths, some still venting steam or compressed air, bubbles rising through the kelp. It was an industrial age—engines, mechanized production, electrified infrastructure—but now it is merely scrap. A few small structures perch on the highest ridges, abandoned, their windows shattered, their roofs colonized by moss. The industry is dying, or perhaps already dead, being slowly digested by the very water it once sought to control.

The light is normal (1.7), diffused through the overcast sky and the churning water, casting everything in a soft, gray radiance. There are no harsh shadows, only the muted glow of a world where the sun is filtered through vapor and depth.

The rules of this place are governed by violent equilibrium. The ancient rock (3.0) resists the hyperactive water (2.9), but the water is winning, slowly, imperceptibly. The turbulence constantly reshapes the shoreline, tearing at the industrial ruins, carrying sediment from the abyssal depths to build new islands of silt and kelp, while simultaneously scouring the primordial stone back toward flatness. The lush vegetation grows faster than the erosion can strip it, creating a dense, layered biosphere that thrives on the chaos. The fauna migrates through the channels in great, noisy herds, following the currents and the food they bring. It is a place of high saturation and high energy—normal in its physics, but extreme in its expression, where the oldest stone on earth meets the most restless water, and where the ghosts of industry grind on in the roar of the flood.
