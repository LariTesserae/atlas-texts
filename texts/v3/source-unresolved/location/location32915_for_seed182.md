You descend through a throat of stone that still remembers being magma. The rock here is young—unweathered, jagged, its surfaces knapped and fractured rather than smoothed by centuries of water. Sharp fins of basalt and obsidian jut from the walls at angles that violate comfort, catching the dim amber light and throwing shadows that seem to reach for you with too many joints. This is a place that has not yet learned to be old. Every edge is a fresh cut.

The air is a wet heat, 2.7 degrees of it, thick enough to swim through. It carries the mineral tang of new stone and the sweet rot of accelerated growth. Condensation beads on every surface—metal, mineral, leaf—hanging in heavy drops that do not fall so much as *negotiate* their descent, sliding down pipes and dripping from the tips of stalactites that are still growing, still sharp, still deciding their shapes. Pools collect in the low places, black and still, reflecting the dim light with a murky fidelity.

This is a city, but compressed. Scale has been hoarded here; the entire settlement fits within a single vast chamber, a cathedral of industry that has been allowed to fill up completely. Density is absolute—there is no empty space, no breathing room. Everything presses against everything else. Brickwork abuts machinery abuts root systems abuts more brickwork. The architecture is layered and vertical, a palimpsest of construction built atop construction, pipes woven through walls like veins, conduits burrowing through floors. It is urban in the way a coral head is urban—packed, efficient, saturated with function.

And it is lush. Vegetation thrives in this hot, damp dark, 1.8 parts of it, a tropical profusion that has colonized every niche the industry has left unclaimed. Thick vines, the width of a man's thigh, drape from gantries and coil around pistons. Ferns unfurl from the cracks between bricks. Bioluminescent fungi—pale blue and sickly green—pulse softly from the shadowed corners, providing what little light there is. The plants grow *on* the machines, not merely around them; moss carpets the housings of engines, roots pry into gearboxes, leaves filter the condensation and drink it. The boundary between the built and the grown has dissolved into a single, humid, breathing fabric.

The industry here is mechanical, industrial, electrified—but it moves with the patience of geology. Engines turn at the pace of continental drift. Gears mesh with a sound like distant thunder, muffled by the saturated air. Belts creak. Steam rises from vents in slow, white columns that dissipate into the general dampness. The machinery is not abandoned, but it is *slow*—a vast, intricate clockwork designed to operate over centuries. It hums at a frequency felt in the sternum rather than heard.

Light is scarce, dim, filtered. It comes from the fungal glow and from narrow shafts of amber radiance—sodium lamps? furnace doors?—that barely penetrate the moisture-laden atmosphere. The illumination is submarine, pressure-heavy, casting everything in sepia and shadow. You can see the condensation in the air as a faint mist, a luminous haze that catches the light and holds it.

Fauna is almost absent. You might see a pale, eyeless thing scuttling between pipes—a cave-adapted insect, or something bred in the wet dark—but movement is rare. The place is too full of industry, too hot, too *occupied* by the vegetable and the mechanical to support animal life in any abundance. What lives here is adapted to the silence and the slowness.

The strangeness is in the integration, the *wrongness* of the fusion. The plants grow in directions they should not, following the heat gradients of machinery rather than any sun. The architecture seems to be exhaling—the walls breathe with the thermal expansion of the deep rock, and the buildings, packed so tight, shift and groan against each other in a slow tectonic waltz. Time moves differently here. A machine that should rust in months is preserved by the mineral-saturated water; a vine that should wither in dark grows fat and pale, feeding on the waste heat of engines and the carbon dioxide of combustion. The erosion is young, but the city feels ancient in its stillness, as if it has been operating since before the stone finished cooling.

Sound is mostly absent. The humidity swallows noise. You hear the drip of water—plink, plink, plink—into metal basins. The slow grind of stone against stone as the chamber breathes. Your own breathing, too loud, too wet. Occasionally, a deep *thrum* from the machinery, felt in the floor, a reminder that something vast is turning somewhere in the packed dark.

It is a fermentation vat. A greenhouse. A factory. A tomb that is not yet full. Everything is packed tight—stone, steam, steel, and green growing things—waiting, sweating, turning slowly in the hot dark below the world.
