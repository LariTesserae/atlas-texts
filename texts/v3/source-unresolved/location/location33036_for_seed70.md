You wake to the taste of wet stone and the weight of warm air pressing against your skin—not heavy with humidity alone, but saturated, as if the atmosphere itself has reached its holding capacity and is sweating. The light is ordinary, a pale, diffuse gold that filters through a sky you cannot see, only infer from the evenness of the glow. You are high. The altitude asserts itself in the thinness of the air, a subtle lightness in the chest, a clarity in the distance that makes the horizon feel close enough to touch, yet the world around you is drowning.

Water is everywhere. Not as rain, not yet, though the air is so thick with moisture that condensation beads on every surface, running in slow, patient rivulets down your arms. No—this is a landscape submerged. You stand on a narrow ridge of black rock that juts from a warm, motionless sea, and around you, in every direction, the water stretches to a hazy rim where it meets the sky. It is a freshwater sea, or perhaps a lake of impossible size, sitting at the roof of the world. The water is the color of weak tea, stained with tannins from the vegetation that grows in it, warm as a bath, utterly still. There are no waves. The surface is a membrane, unbroken except by the islands.

The islands are young. Their youth is written in the rock: sharp, unweathered, volcanic glass and basalt that have not yet learned the softness of age. They rise from the water in jagged spires and serrated cliffs, black and gleaming, catching the light in fractured edges that could slice skin. The erosion here is minimal, barely begun—these peaks emerged recently, violently, thrust up through the crust and left standing in the flood, their flanks too steep for soil to accumulate, too fresh for the rounding touch of centuries.

But soil is not needed. The vegetation has found a way. It is lush to the point of obscenity, a dense, layered saturation of green that climbs the sharp rock faces in defiance of gravity. Mosses carpet every surface, thick and velvet, in shades of jade and emerald and a strange, bruised purple that suggests mineral stains or perhaps bioluminescence waiting for dark. Ferns unfurl from cracks, their fronds dripping constantly, feeding the water below. Vines hang in curtains, heavy with moisture, trailing into the warm sea where they root in floating mats of peat and pumice. The density is overwhelming—layer upon layer of growth, near-touching, competing for the normal light that penetrates the mist. High-saturation, almost hallucinatory; the greens are too green, the shadows too deep, the air between the leaves thick with spores and pollen and the faint, sweet rot of perpetual damp.

And yet, for all this vegetal exuberance, the place is nearly silent. The sound here is mostly quiet—a hush so complete it feels borrowed. You hear the soft, liquid percussion of water lapping against the rock bases, a sound without echo, swallowed by the humidity. You hear the drip of condensation from leaf to leaf, a slow, metronomic patter. But there are no birds. No insects buzz. No frogs. The fauna is rare to the point of absence, as if the islands emerged too recently for colonization, or as if the water itself—warm, still, perhaps slightly anoxic in its depths—repels the smaller lives. Occasionally, a dragonfly the size of a hand drifts past on a current of heated air, silent as a ghost, its wings beating without sound, before vanishing into the mist. That is all.

The strangeness of the place settles into the bones. It is not malevolent, but it is wrong. The islands drift. You can feel it if you stand still long enough—a slow, imperceptible sway, a rotation, a migration across the warm water on currents that move at the speed of growing things. The archipelago is not fixed; it is a fleet, a congregation of floating monoliths, each carrying its cargo of sharp rock and lush green, moving toward some distant convergence or apart, driven by forces too slow to witness in a single sitting. The water between them is warm, yes, but it is also ancient in its stillness, a heat bath that has been sitting at this altitude, defying the expectation that high places should be cold.

There are no structures here. No made artifacts. The black rock has never known a tool. The vegetation grows without cultivation, dies without harvest, and decays with a patience that spans decades. The rules of this world are biological and geological only. Time moves slowly here—dynamic processes are patient, gradual. A cliff face erodes by millimeters per century. A vine thickens by a centimeter per year. The islands drift by meters per decade. The water evaporates and condenses in cycles that blur the line between weather and climate. It is a place of suspension, of being caught between emergence and dissolution, high above the world yet submerged in its own warm, silent flood.

If you wade into the water—warm, knee-deep, then waist-deep—you find the bottom is not solid. It is a ooze of organic matter, a sinking carpet of roots and peat and the compressed bodies of dead plants, holding the islands aloft like pontoons. The water is clear near the surface, murky below, and down in the depths, things move that you cannot see, shadows that might be fish or might be the slow turn of submerged vegetation. But they make no sound. They rise to the surface only as bubbles, silent and odorless, popping without a whisper.

Above, the sky is hidden by a ceiling of mist, a wall of atmospheric water so dense it feels like swimming, like being submerged in a liquid that is not quite water, that presses against your eardrums with a pressure that is almost, but not quite, painful. The light is normal, but it comes from everywhere and nowhere, a flat, featureless illumination that erases shadows and makes the sharp islands appear to float in a void of pale gold.

This is the place: a high, warm, young archipelago drowning in its own flood, drifting slowly toward an unknown shore, loud with the silence of a world that has not yet learned to speak with animals, only with water, rock, and the slow, insistent green of things that grow.
