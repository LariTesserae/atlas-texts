You enter through a seam—a vertical crack no wider than a held breath, smelling of wet stone and something older, like cold metal. The moment your shoulders pass the threshold, the air runs out. Not gradually. All at once. You are submerged.

This is a pocket. It exists inside the larger world the way a thought exists inside a skull: compressed, self-contained, a room that is only the size of a large closet but feels, in the dark, as though it might be endless. The scale is intimate, hidden, a secret kept by the rock. The ceiling is close enough to touch if you rise, but you do not rise. The water holds you at a neutral depth, neither sinking nor floating, suspended in a medium that is too thick to be ordinary H₂O.

The water is everywhere—2.9 parts of a whole that is almost entirely liquid. But it is not uniform. It is stratified by a logic that has nothing to do with temperature or salt. The water at the bottom of this small chamber has been here longest, and it has grown heavy. Not heavy with sediment, but heavy with *duration*. It is a gelatinous mass, translucent and pale, settling at the lowest point of the fissure like a sediment of time itself. It behaves as both floor and fluid: you can stand upon it, and your boots—if you wear boots, though the place has stripped you of most things—sink in only to the ankle, while the surface ripples outward in slow, concentric rings that take minutes to dissipate.

Above this dense stratum, the water is lighter, less patient, still dark but mobile. And above that, near the ceiling, there is a pocket of air so saturated with moisture that it is indistinguishable from mist, from breath, from the exhalation of something sleeping.

The walls are young. The erosion here is fresh, a violence that has not yet been smoothed by centuries. The stone is sharp, angular, fractured in planes that catch phantom light and throw it back as absence. The rock face is a mosaic of fresh breaks—white where the mineral is exposed, dark where the water has already begun its slow, chemical conversation with the silica. There are no stalactites yet, no softening. Only edges. If you brush the wall, you will cut yourself on the youth of the place.

And yet, packed into every cubic inch of this water—this heavy water, this light water, this suspended middle—there is vegetation. Lush, impossible, etiolated. It does not need light; it feeds on the minerals leaching from the young stone, on the slow chemistry of the deep dark. It is a forest of filaments: pale, hair-thin strands of algae or fungus or something that has no name in the taxonomy of the surface world. They grow from the "floor" of dense water, reaching upward in a thicket so thick that the water itself seems to be merely the spaces between the growth. The density is absolute—2.6 on the scale of packed saturation. There is no empty water here. Every gap holds a spore, a drifting seed, a fragment of shed membrane. To move through it is to push against a living curtain, to part strands that cling to your skin with a viscosity that is almost affectionate.

Scattered through this submerged garden are the built things. Not many—0.8, a scattering, faint marks of intention. On the heavy-water floor, arranged in a triangle that makes no geometric sense because the floor itself is undulating, sit three stones. They are not local stone. They are water-worn, river-smooth, placed here by hands that existed before the crack opened, or perhaps by hands that will exist after it closes. Nearby, a single post rises from the dense layer, carved with spirals that seem to rotate when viewed peripherally, though the water around it is perfectly still. It leans at an angle that suggests it is falling upward, or sideways, or into a direction that does not exist in the three dimensions you remember.

There is also a doorframe. Just the frame, no door, standing upright in the lightest water near the ceiling, leading nowhere. Or leading into a space that is also filled with water, a recursion of rooms within rooms, each darker than the last.

The darkness is total—0.2, a light level that is not merely dim but *subtractive*. It does not merely lack photons; it seems to absorb the concept of illumination. You cannot see your own hands. You know where they are by the pressure of the water, by the way the vegetation brushes against your knuckles, by the cold that pools in your palms. The vegetation glows, but wrongly. It is phosphorescent in reverse—instead of emitting light, it emits *dark*, a bioluminescence of shadow that makes the surrounding water seem, by contrast, almost bright. You can feel these cold spots against your skin, drifting past you like the touch of a finger in a dream.

Sound is mostly quiet. The heavy water at the bottom acts as an acoustic tomb, absorbing the drips, the sighs, the settling of the young stone. But if you press your ear—or your skull—against the dense layer, you hear a low, grinding hum. It is the sound of the rock below compressing, of the water slowly, over hours and days, increasing its own density, becoming more solid, more *real*. It is a patient process, a dynamic of almost geological slowness. The vegetation sways in a current that completes one oscillation every twenty minutes. A single bubble, if one forms, takes an hour to rise three inches.

Fauna is rare. You will swim for a long time—hours, subjective—before you see it. It lives in the dense layer, gliding along the surface of the heavy water as if it were a meadow. It is flat, pale, eyeless, the size of a dinner plate but thin as a leaf. It has too many joints, arranged in a symmetry that suggests it was designed by a geometry that excludes mercy. It grazes on the filaments, moving at the speed of a growing icicle, leaving a trail of disturbed spores that takes a day to settle. It does not react to you. It has never known anything but this dark, this pressure, this slow time.

And the weirdness—the reality breakdown—is in the water itself. The heavy stratum is not merely dense; it is *authoritative*. It defines the local gravity. Stand on it, and down is down. Swim above it, and the rules shift. In one corner of the chamber, the heavy water climbs the wall, defying the lighter water above it, creating an overhang of liquid stone that you must swim beneath. In another corner, the heavy water recedes, leaving a pocket of air that is not at the ceiling but at mid-height, a bubble of breathable nothing suspended in the middle of the flooded room. The vegetation grows toward this pocket, not upward, but *toward*, as if pulled by a magnet of dryness.

The scattered artifacts—the stones, the post, the doorframe—do not sink into the heavy water. They rest upon it, or within it, caught in the density gradient. They are from different times. A flint blade, sharp as the day it was struck. A potsherd, if pottery counts as too much technology, then a worked bone, white and carved. They are arranged in a pattern that suggests a ritual, but the ritual is for something that has not yet arrived, or that arrived long before the stone cracked open to let the water in.

Time here is slow, but it is also *layered*. The water at the bottom is older than the water at the top. You can taste the difference if you let the water touch your lips—a metallic tang of centuries, a sweetness of recent rain that has found its way down through the crack. The creature glides through the old water. The vegetation roots in the new. You are suspended between them, in the eternal present of a place that has only just been born and is already ancient.

It is cool here. 0.8 degrees of warmth, a temperature that draws heat from your body in a slow, steady siphon. The water is 4 degrees above freezing, perhaps, but the heavy layer at the bottom is colder still, near the temperature of deep ocean trenches, preserved by its own density.

You are in a room that is a womb, a tomb, a throat. The water presses against your eardrums. The vegetation combs through your hair. The sharp stone waits at the edges, ready to cut. And somewhere, in the absolute dark, the heavy water continues its slow compression, becoming more floor, more ground, more *real*, while the world outside forgets that this pocket ever existed.
