The basin lies low, a vast depression barely above the water table. The earth here is newborn — not the ancient, worn stone of stable continents, but raw, recent material: volcanic ash still sharp-edged, marine clays soft and slick, minerals precipitating fresh from the saturated ground in crusts of ochre and white. It weeps. Water seeps from every pore of the young rock, from the undersides of leaves, from the saturated air itself, condensing in a perpetual, warm drizzle that never quite becomes rain. The water table is not below the ground; it *is* the ground, a viscous, mineral-charged slurry that oozes upward and pools in shallow, steaming pans across the flat expanse.

Above this, the light is wrong. The temperature is absolute — a wet, tropical heat that presses against the skin like a living weight, oppressive and close — yet the illumination is dim, a diffuse, greenish-amber twilight that seems to emanate from the vegetation itself. A permanent canopy of steam, spore-cloud, and particulate life filters the sun, or perhaps the density of the flora is so complete that only fragments of light penetrate the lower layers. The world is lit from within, by the faint bioluminescence of fungi rotting in the duff and the shimmer of wet surfaces reflecting what little radiance reaches them.

Vegetation erupts in layers of impossible saturation. There is no bare earth visible, only a living mat meters thick. Giant ferns unfurl with fronds heavy and wet, dripping. Mosses form cushions the size of boulders, sponges of moisture. Vines drape between the trunks of strange, squat trees whose bark is already entirely obscured by lichens, orchids, and fungal brackets. The lushness is total — a press of green and umber, of growing and decaying, each stratum packed against the next with near-touching density. Roots grip the young, unstable earth in a lattice so complete that erosion is arrested. The land is technically "emerging" — still accreting layers of peat and biological matter, still rising from the depths in a slow, inexorable upwelling — but the process has reached a perfect equilibrium. It builds itself upward at exactly the rate it would sink or wash away, a frozen moment of geological birth that never progresses.

And life is overwhelming. The air is thick with fauna. Swarms of insects — iridescent beetles, clouds of midges, dragonflies the size of small birds — fill every volume of space, a constant, loud thrumming and buzzing that vibrates in the teeth. The sound is a roar: the deafening, polyphonic chorus of frogs in the warm pools, the bellowing of large, unseen herbivores moving through the undergrowth with a sound like wet thunder, the rustle of a thousand small things in the leaf litter. Lizards bask on every stone, their bodies pressed flat to absorb the heat. Amphibians of strange, fleshy morphology slide through the muck. The density of animals is such that you are never more than an arm's length from some form of life — a press of bodies, a high-saturation of presence that leaves no silence.

Yet nothing changes. There is no wind — the air is perfectly still, heavy and motionless, carrying the scents of sulfur, rot, and green growth without stirring them. The temperature does not fluctuate between day and night. The water level does not rise or fall. The young geology, though raw and unformed — lacking the valleys and ridges of erosion — shows no sign of wearing down; the biological crust protects it too perfectly, rebuilding any loss instantly. The ecosystem is in a state of absolute climax, a dynamic equilibrium so complete it appears frozen in time. The landscape is eternally nascent — it looks as though it has just emerged from the sea or the mantle, still wet with its creation, and it will remain in this state of emerging forever, never aging, never eroding, never becoming anything other than this hot, wet, dense, loud, dim place.

The scale is open — this basin extends for miles, a regional expanse of steaming flats and impenetrable thickets, bounded only by low, mist-shrouded rises of the same young, weeping earth that fade into the green gloom. There are no structures, no artifacts, no sign of intelligence or tool-use. It is a world of pure biology, a crucible of life that reached its final form the instant it was born, and holds that form with the stillness of stone.
