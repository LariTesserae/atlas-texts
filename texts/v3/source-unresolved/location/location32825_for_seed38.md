The light here does not fall so much as seep. It is the color of old jade, filtered through a sky that is less a vault than a lid—a permanent, high-altitude overcast, kilometers thick, that presses down with the weight of its own moisture. You are high, two thousand meters or more above any sea, yet the air is warm, thick, and wet, a greenhouse held aloft by the sheer mass of living things. The temperature never drops to freezing; decay and growth are both rapid, yet they cancel each other out in a slow, fermenting equilibrium.

You stand—or rather, you float—on a surface that is not ground. It is a mat. A continent-sized raft of compressed time and cellulose, ten meters thick in places, buoyant on a water table that lies barely a handspan below your boots. The bedrock, if you could drill through the peat, would be a flattened plain, a peneplain worn smooth by three hundred million years of rain and patience, now buried beneath this soft, dark skin. The erosion is complete; the original mountains are gone, reduced to a tabletop that has been colonized by a single, patient ambition: to become plant.

The vegetation is total. There is no soil here, only substrate. Sphagnum moss does not grow *in* the earth; it *is* the earth, a spongy, russet carpet that drinks the rain and holds it in its cellular structure. Over this grows an architecture of living peat: hummocks and hollows, waves of cotton grass that ripple in a wind you cannot feel, domes of dwarf shrubbery that have grown into the shape of whale backs. The plants are not rooted in mineral soil but in the compressed bodies of their ancestors, a layering of dead and living that settles slowly into the dark, oily peat that forms the true ground. It is a landscape built from the bottom up, a cathedral of accumulation where the floor is also the walls, the walls are also the ceiling, and everything is slightly yielding underfoot.

The water is everywhere, but it is still. It sits in the hollows between the hummocks, black and mirror-dark, reflecting the jade sky. It weeps from the sides of the peat mounds in thin, constant trickles, so that the ground is always damp, always dripping. The air condenses on every surface—on the leaves, on your skin, on the scattered stones that protrude like the backs of drowning things. It is a post-rain world that has forgotten how to dry, a place of weeping rocks and condensing atmosphere where the boundary between water and air is a suggestion rather than a rule.

And yet, for all this wetness and verdure, the place is silent. The sound is mostly quiet, broken only by the *plop* of a methane bubble rising from the deep peat and bursting at the surface of a pool with a sound like a cork pulled from a bottle, or the soft, fibrous *shush* of cotton grass bending, or the subsonic *thrum* of the ground itself as it breathes. The fauna are abundant, layered through the volume of the place like strata—canopy, understory, root-zone, depths—but they are creatures of the hush. Bog-titans, massive herbivores with hides like translucent parchment, graze the sphagnum in herds that stretch to the horizon, their footsteps making no sound in the yielding peat. Between them crawl amphibians the size of your hand, insects that hum below the threshold of hearing, and small, bright birds that nest in the grass and sing in frequencies just at the edge of perception. Life is dense here, near-touching, high-saturation, but it moves through the mist like thought, silent and slow.

Scattered across this vastness are the only hard things: the Built. They are faint marks, easily missed in the undulating green. Stone circles, half-swallowed by the peat, their tops barely protruding. Cairns that have sunk three-quarters into the mat, so that only their crowns show, weeping condensation like everything else. They are not numerous, and they contain no tech, no devices, no tools—only stone, placed by hands that are not here now, or perhaps by hands that were never human. They are being digested. The peat grows over them at a rate of millimeters per year, and in a few thousand years, they will be gone, incorporated into the substrate, leaving only a slight rise in the ground, a memory of geometry in a world of organic chaos.

The strangeness is the time, and the breath. Here, the dynamic is slow, patient, geological—but it is alive in a way that defies the scale. The entire mat, this continent of peat, rises and falls. It breathes. Gas builds beneath the dense, impermeable layer of living moss, and the whole surface, kilometers wide, lifts by a centimeter, then settles, over the course of a day. The vegetation moves with it, the domes flexing, the pools sloshing in their basins. The stones sink. The titans adjust their stance. And the plant—the great, clonal organism that may be a single individual spanning this entire highland, connected by a rhizomatic network of fungal and root tissue that spans hundreds of kilometers—it grows. It has been growing for ten thousand years, perhaps a hundred thousand, and it will grow until the weight of its own accumulation crushes the water table below, or until the climate shifts, or until it simply reaches the end of its long, slow life, settling into its final, near-flattened form.

You walk here, and you feel the give of the peat beneath your feet, the slight suction as you lift your boot. The air is green and thick and quiet. Somewhere, a bubble pops. A titan shifts its weight, and the ground trembles, just slightly, as the world inhales. The stones wait, sinking. The light remains jade, eternal, dim. And the landscape grows, upward, outward, minute by minute, into its primordial, settled shape—a high, wet, green, breathing plain, where the plants have won, and the mountains are forgotten, and time moves like the ooze of water through a sponge.
