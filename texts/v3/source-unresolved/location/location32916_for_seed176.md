Imagine a mountain the size of a cathedral nave.

It rises not from a plain but from a sea of cloud, a single jagged tooth of basalt thrust upward so recently that its flanks are still glass-sharp, unweathered by the patient sanding of centuries. This is **young rock**—erosion has barely begun its work, so every edge is a knife, every face a fresh wound in the earth. Yet it is extreme in its elevation, a needle of stone piercing the upper atmosphere where the air should be thin and freezing, but here it is thick and warm, humid as a lung.

The spire **weeps**.

Water is not merely present here; it is the fundamental medium. The stone itself sweats—geothermal warmth from the planet’s blood pumping through the young, fractured rock meets the cold upper air, and the mountain condenses its own weather. Every surface gleams with a film of moisture. Water runs down the black glassy faces in rivulets that never dry, pooling in the hollows of the sharp terrain, dripping from every projection like a slow, endless rain from within. The atmosphere is saturated, a condensing soup where visibility shifts from ten meters to three in the space of a breath. It is warm—tropical warmth at an altitude that should kill—creating a greenhouse of perpetual mist.

And in this wet heat, **life has erupted with furious density**.

Vegetation does not merely grow here; it *inflates* the space. Ferns unfurl to the size of small trees, their fronds dripping with captured condensation. Moss carpets every horizontal surface in spongy, electric-green layers three feet thick, so dense that it has begun to swallow the lower reaches of the stone. Vines—thick as a man’s wrist, veined with bioluminescent sap—cable up the cliffs, seeking purchase on anything solid. The flora is layered in a near-touching profusion: canopy of fronds, understory of fungi, ground cover of wet moss, each stratum hosting the next in a vertical ecology that has had no time to stabilize, only to accumulate. The green is oversaturated, almost hallucinatory against the black stone, fed by the constant drip and the mineral-rich sweat of the rock.

But this is not a wilderness. Scattered through the green, like bones picked clean by time, are the **remains of industry**.

The structures are not a city—they are *artifacts*, partial and half-reclaimed. A rusted boiler half-buried in moss, its stack still venting steam in rhythmic, patient pulses. A water-wheel at the base of a weeping cliff, turning with glacial slowness—one revolution every twelve minutes—driven by the constant runoff of the mountain’s tears. Pipes, thick as tree trunks, snake up the flanks, some ruptured, belching white vapor into the already-steamy air, others intact but groaning under the pressure of geothermal taps drilled into the young rock. There are sheds—corrugated metal huts—clinging to ledges, their roofs collapsed under the weight of wet vegetation, their doors hanging open to reveal interiors rusted orange and green. Electrical cables drape between stone spires like lianas, sparking occasionally in the conductive mist, humming with a current harvested from the static of the condensing atmosphere. It is industrial technology at a scattered, almost archaeological scale—mechanized, steam-powered, electrified, but abandoned to the wet.

The **sound** is overwhelming, a constant roar that has no silence in it. The mountain’s weeping is not quiet; it is the sound of a thousand drips amplified by stone chambers, a susurrus that becomes a roar where the water finds a channel. Superimposed on this is the hiss of steam from a hundred fissures, the groan of metal expanding and contracting in the thermal cycling of day and night, the shriek of wind forcing itself through the lattice of pipes and cables. It is loud enough to feel in the teeth, a white-noise cathedral where the liturgy is machinery and water.

Yet the **dynamic** is agonizingly slow. Despite the violence of the sound, nothing rushes. The water wheel turns with the patience of geology. The steam vents pulse on cycles measured in hours, not seconds. The erosion that will eventually round these sharp edges operates on millennia. The vegetation grows, yes, but in the time it takes to watch, a fern might unfurl by a centimeter. The industrial decay is similarly patient—a rivet rusts through over decades, a moss carpet claims a floor plate over years. There are weak gradients, minimal flux; the place is in a state of suspended animation, loud but frozen in its processes.

**Fauna** are rare here. The constant steam, the deafening noise, and the mineral-heavy water have driven away most creatures. Those that remain are strange—**unusual** in their adaptation. Massive snails with shells of magnetite and rust, moving at a pace that makes the water wheel seem frantic, grazing on the conductive moss. Clockwork beetles, perhaps escaped from the machinery or evolved to mimic it, their brass carapaces green with verdigris, clicking through the undergrowth in sparse, lonely processions. They are few, and they move through the dense green like thoughts in a fog, easily missed, easily crushed underfoot by a careless step.

The **rules of this world** are written in the relationship between the young stone and the wet heat. The mountain is still rising, pushed up by forces that have not yet spent themselves, but it is being consumed simultaneously by the very moisture it generates. The industrial scars are attempts to tap the geothermal heart of the new rock—drilling into the heat, channeling the water, harnessing the steam—but the technology is failing, being digested by the rapid vegetation and the corrosive wet. There are no seasons here, only the slow turning of the wheel and the pulse of the vents. Time moves differently; a human might live and die here and see only the moss thicken by a hand’s breadth, the rust advance a millimeter, the steam hiss its eternal song.

It is a room-sized world at the top of the sky, a cathedral of steam and sharp stone, where industry and jungle have entered a slow, wet stalemate, and the mountain weeps its warm, mineral tears into the clouds.
