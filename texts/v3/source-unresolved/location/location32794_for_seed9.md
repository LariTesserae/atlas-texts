You arrive not by climbing but by descending—though the distinction blurs here, at **2.5 elevation**, where the mountain’s throat opens like a mouth into a chamber that feels less like geology and more like a held breath. The air is **warm** (2.2) despite the altitude, thick with the wet heat of a place that sweats. It is **water** made manifest: 2.2 on the scale, meaning condensation hangs in every breath, films the surfaces, drips in slow percussion from unseen heights. The atmosphere is a cloud trapped indoors, a permanent post-rain mist that softens edges and makes the **light** (2.1) diffuse, milk-pale, filtering through steam as if through frosted glass.

The **scale** is intimate (0.7)—a cathedral, perhaps, or a single vast room the size of a hamlet compressed into one volume. You can almost see across it, but **density** (1.7) intervenes: layer upon layer of presence packed near-touching. The walls are **emerging** geology (erosion 0.3), raw and unweathered—obsidian glass still sharp enough to cut, basalt columns that look as though they were extruded yesterday, mineral deposits crusting surfaces in newborn white and ochre. This is **pre-formation** rock, embryonic, under construction by heat and pressure that haven’t yet learned to smooth their work. Yet **everything is made** (built 2.7): the stone has been carved, lined, and buttressed; there is no natural substrate left unmediated. The volcanic glass is bolted. The basalt is welded. Even the dripping water is channeled, collected, recycled through the machine.

This is an **industrial** heart (tech 1.6), but it beats slowly. Copper pipes the girth of tree trunks run alongside conduits thick as pythons, all of it humming with **electrified** purpose—geothermal turbines turning in the dark, heat exchangers breathing steam, a scaled production of warmth and mineral extraction. But the **sound** (1.4) is mostly quiet: the machinery is muffled by the wet, the steam absorbs the higher frequencies, leaving only a subsonic thrum that you feel in your sternum, and the liquid percussion of water finding its level—drip, drip, splash—into collection basins lined with ceramic.

And then the **vegetation** (2.4): **lush**, impossible, verdant. In this high, constructed, steaming dark, life has seized every niche. Ferns unfurl from the joints of pipe flanges. Moss carpets the catwalks, turning metal into something organic, something soft. Orchids—pale, waxy, strange—bloom in the condensation, their roots drinking from the steam vents. Vines thicker than a man’s wrist drape from the ceiling, dripping with moisture, creating a second canopy beneath the rock. The green is saturated, dense, layered over and under and between the industrial elements until the machinery looks like it is being digested, absorbed into a slow photosynthesis.

**Fauna** are rare (1.1). You might see a moth, pale and bewildered, circling a lamp. Perhaps a spider in a corner, fat with condensation. No birds—the altitude and the enclosure keep them out, or the steam has driven them away. The place is too new, too constructed, too full of human industry for the wild to have colonized it properly. The only "animals" are the maintenance drones, silent and spider-like, crawling the catwalks, or the occasional glimpse of something in the mist—a shape that might be a salamander, might be a leak, that vanishes before you can be sure.

The **weirdness** (1.5) settles in the juxtaposition: the cathedral scale of the space, yet the grotto intimacy of the plant growth; the industrial hum beneath the water’s silence; the raw, **emerging** rock that looks as though the mountain is still being born around the machines, while the machines look as though they are fossilizing into the stone. There is a **dynamic** pulse (2.4) to it all—weather cycles internal to the chamber, steam rising in columns that cool and fall as rain inside the roof, biological pace racing against mechanical rhythm. The moss grows visibly, overnight. The mineral deposits accrete, layer by layer, slowly burying the bolts. The turbines turn, and the earth provides, and the water cycles, and the plants drink, and everything is in motion, but it is a motion of **quiet**—a hush like the inside of a shell, warm and wet and waiting for the mountain to finish forming around it.

You stand on a grating, your boots sinking slightly into the moss that has claimed the metal, and watch a droplet of condensation form on a pipe, grow heavy, and fall—joining the slow, ancient, industrial rain.
