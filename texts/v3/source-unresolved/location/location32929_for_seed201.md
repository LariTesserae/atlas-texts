You stand at the lip of a wound in the plateau.

The air here is not merely thin; it is *aggressive*, a metallic tang that coats the tongue and makes every breath a conscious act. Above you, the sky is the color of bleached bone—so bright it hurts (light: 2.9), the sun hammering down through an atmosphere too sparse to soften it. This is extreme elevation (2.6), the roof of the world, where the cold (0.2) is a physical weight, pressing frost into every crevice. You are looking down into a cylindrical void, perhaps sixty meters across and twice as deep—a cathedral nave turned vertical, a single room open to the heavens (scale: 1.3). The limestone walls are young (erosion: 1.2), sharp-edged and unweathered, as if the mountain had been gouged out yesterday. The rock is pale, almost luminous, reflecting the harsh light back up in blinding shards. There are no gentle slopes here, only sheer faces and sudden ledges, the geometry of a recent collapse.

And it is mostly empty (density: 0.4). The volume of this place is air. Vast, hollow, resonant in a way that feels less like echo and more like absence. The walls rise sheer on all sides, enclosing a space that is intimate in its boundaries yet infinite in its verticality. Sound dies here (sound: 0.4). A stone dropped from your hand would fall in perfect, terrible silence, the moss at the bottom waiting to swallow it without a whisper.

But at the base—because there is a base, a floor of talus and soil caught in the bowl of the collapse—there is a carpet of life. Lush (vegetation: 1.7), impossibly dense, a sponge of emerald and jade and rust that has colonized every horizontal surface. It is cold-loving lushness: mosses packed thick as velvet, ferns uncurling in the dimness, lichens crusting the stones in neon oranges and acid greens. The vegetation is fed not by rain, for the sky above sheds only occasional, hesitant moisture (water: 1.0), but by the breath of the mountain itself. The walls weep. Condensation gathers in the pores of the limestone, beading on the sharp stone, and here is the first strangeness (weirdness: 0.7): the water does not always fall. In the upper reaches, where the rock is most porous, thin rivulets climb *upward*, drawn by capillary hunger, feeding colonies of moss that cling to the ceiling like inverted forests. The dampness is constant, a perpetual mist that hangs in the lower third of the shaft, turning the light into something milky and diffuse even as the sun above remains surgical and white.

The cold is severe, yet the green thrives. It is a paradox of the place—life persisting in a freezer, sustained by the damp and the stone’s slow respiration. And the life is abundant (fauna: 2.4), though you would not know it from listening. Millions of creatures move through the moss: pale, eyeless insects, larvae fat with stored warmth, small birds with muted plumage that nest in the crevices of the high walls. Swarms of midges dance in the column of light, their wings silent. A fox, white as the limestone, pads across the talus at the bottom, its paws making no sound on the absorbent green. The ecosystem is a hive of motion—predator and prey, the slow turning of decay, the constant dripping and climbing of moisture—yet the silence is absolute. The moss drinks the sound. The shape of the pit traps it. You can hear your own heartbeat, the rush of blood in your ears, the creak of your primitive leather garments (tech: 0.7) as you shift your weight.

There are marks of those who have come before (built: 0.9). Scattered, faint, respectful of the emptiness. On the rim, three cairns of white stone, each exactly the height of a man’s reach. Halfway down the north wall, a platform of lashed driftwood and bone, weathered gray, jutting from a ledge like a bird’s nest. At the bottom, near the densest part of the moss, a single lean-to constructed of hides stretched over a frame of antler and bent saplings. No metal glints anywhere; the tools are flint and obsidian, cordage twisted from sinew and fiber. The builders were careful, or afraid, leaving only these sparse gestures in the vast hollow.

The place is active (dynamic: 2.5), but its motions are slow and internal. A chimney effect draws cold air down from the rim, where it pools at the bottom like water, displacing warmer, moister air that rises in a ghostly current you can see only in the drift of spores and the bending of the moss. The mist breathes in and out. The condensation climbs and falls in its impossible cycle. The fauna moves in patterns too small to track—a beetle tunneling, a moth emerging, a predator waiting. Weather happens here in miniature: a frost forming on a ledge at dawn, melting by noon, reforming as the sun swings behind the rim.

It is bright at the bottom, brighter than it should be. The white walls act as mirrors, bouncing the high-altitude light into a pool at the base of the shaft where the moss grows thickest, glowing almost phosphorescent in the intensity. But the light is wrong somehow—it pools, but it does not spread. It stays in a perfect circle, a spotlight of the sky, while the corners of the pit remain in shadow despite the glare above.

You are in a room the size of a cathedral, suspended at the top of the world, kept alive by the mountain’s own damp exhalation, haunted by silent creatures and the ghost of primitive hands that once sought shelter in its hollow heart.
