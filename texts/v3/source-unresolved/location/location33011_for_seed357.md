You stand on a platform of compressed peat and root-matter that extends to a horizon trembling with heat-shimmer. The elevation is real—you feel it in the thin, hot pressure of the air, in the way sound carries too far, too clear. This is a young world, geologically speaking. The bedrock here was thrust upward so recently that its edges are still sharp, unweathered, visible only where the vegetative skin has torn away in fresh scars of pale limestone and volcanic glass.

But you rarely see the stone. The ground is not ground; it is **substrate**—a continuous, undulating carpet of vegetation two meters thick, a sponge of moss, fern, and fungal mycelium that drinks from the saturated air and exudes it again in a constant, silent perspiration. Between your boots, the mat pulses with hydraulic pressure, water forced up from depths where the young, fractured rock holds aquifers close to the surface.

And then, rising from this green sea, the **presences**.

They are sparse—kilometers apart, perhaps—each one a continent of architecture grown, not built. No tool has ever touched them. They are **total construction** in the only way a tool-less world allows: cellulose and lignin woven into load-bearing geometries, calcified sap forming window-panes that weep condensation, chitin-bark shingling roofs that expand and contract with the heat. A tower here might resemble a brutalist cathedral, all flying buttresses and ribbed vaults, but its walls are translucent with living chlorophyll, pulsing with the slow systole of sap. Another, in the next valley, suggests a honeycomb of hexagonal cells, each chamber a room hollowed out by the plant’s own growth patterns, the “doors” being membranes of elastic tissue that seal and unseal with wet, sucking sounds.

The **density** is deceptive. The vegetation is total—it fills every cubic meter of the valley, climbs the structures, drapes between them in curtains of hanging liana—but the architectural presences are scattered like islands in a storm-tossed ocean of green. You can see three of them from where you stand: one to the east, crackling with new growth; one to the west, already shedding a skin of dead bark into the churning mat below; and one directly ahead, its upper spires lost in a perpetual, churning bank of mist.

That mist is the **water**, present at 2.5—ubiquitous, condensing, weeping. The air is hot (2.6), heavy with moisture drawn up from the high water table and the constant precipitation that falls as a warm, vertical drizzle. Every surface sweats. The structures drip from a thousand pores; the ground steams where the sun (normal light, 1.8, but filtered through vapor) strikes the wet mat. It is a post-rain landscape that never ends, a marsh-edge that has climbed the mountains.

And the **sound** is overwhelming—1.6, loud, but wrong. There are no animals here (0.1). No birds, no insects, no mammals. The fauna is zero. Instead, the noise is the architecture itself **breathing**. A groan like a glacier calving as a support beam of petrified wood, ten meters in diameter, splits under its own rapid growth. A sharp *crack* like a pistol shot as a new shoot bursts through a wall. The wet *slurp* of a membrane sealing. The constant, rushing white noise of water cascading down the exterior channels of the towers, finding the ground, rejoining the mat. And beneath it all, the wind—turbulent, chaotic—tearing through the sparse canopy, making the structures sway and knock against each other across the valleys in subsonic collisions.

This is the **dynamic** heart of the place: 2.8, turbulent, a storm-state of permanent construction and collapse. A wall that was solid yesterday is today a lattice of new, pale green shoots. A tower that stood for a decade is now a ruin of snapped, bleaching trunks, being devoured from within by its own faster-growing offspring, which are already knitting a new spire beside the corpse. The landscape is an anthill-energy of creation and destruction, a bazaar of biomass where nothing is permanent except the churn.

**Weirdness** settles in at 2.0—strange but not surreal. The structures have **rooms**. You can see them through the translucent walls: chambers lined with photosynthetic moss, furnished with shelves of bracket fungi that glow faintly with bioluminescence when the mist thickens. Doorways arch at human scale, though no human has ever walked through them. The plants have grown architecture because the architecture is the plant—the distinction has dissolved. They do not know they are buildings; they simply grow toward light, toward water, toward the structural logic of their own turgor pressure, and the result is a city.

There is no technology here (0.1). No devices, no tools, no fire. The “construction” is purely biological, pre-tool, a cathedral raised by the patient, violent urgency of cell division. The rules of this world are written in xylem and phloem: grow fast, collapse faster, feed on your own dead, drink the sky. The young, sharp mountains beneath provide the minerals; the hot, wet air provides the medium; the sparse, open scale provides the room for these massive, solitary minds of vegetation to gestate and scream their wooden screams into the steam.

As you walk—if you can call it walking, on a surface that shifts and sighs beneath your weight—you pass under the overhang of a structure’s lower branches. Water pours from a spout-like flower, a meter wide, drumming a tattoo on a leaf below that sounds like a gong. The air tastes of green and copper and ozone. Somewhere, a kilometer distant, a tower groans and begins to fall, not crashing, but folding, collapsing inward with the slow, terrible inevitability of a dying star, while around its base, the mat of vegetation surges upward to claim the gap, already building, already beginning the next structure in the endless, loud, wet, turbulent churn.
