The ground is a crust of pale, fractured gypsum and halite, unweathered and sharp underfoot—a playa that emerged from the mantle only recently, still holding the memory of pressure in its crystalline lattice. It is a low basin, barely above the surrounding mineral void, ringed by a rim of the same material that has not yet eroded into soil. The surface is not sand but *precipitate*: the desiccated husks of chemolithotrophic colonies that once bloomed here when trace moisture seeped from below. Now they are brittle, paper-thin scales that flake away in silent, electrostatic drifts, accumulating in the hollows between structures like ash.

Rising from this crust are the Structures. There are eleven of them within the visible horizon—a hamlet, not a metropolis, but dense enough to constitute a settlement. They are grown, not built. Each is a hollow tower of calcium carbonate and silica, secreted by colonial organisms that metabolize the bedrock itself, excreting mineralized biofilm that hardens into architecture. They stand between ten and thirty meters tall, shaped like inverted roots or the fossilized ribcages of leviathans, all curves and sharp internal angles. Because the erosion is young, their surfaces are unsoftened—jagged, faceted, glittering with fresh crystal faces that have never known wind abrasion. They look like cathedrals, like termite mounds scaled to building size, like coral transplanted to a desert.

The density is profound emptiness. Between the towers lies nothing but the white crust, stretching for hundreds of meters in every direction, a vast space that emphasizes the isolation of each structure. Yet each tower is itself hollow—a cathedral of vacant chambers, the interior space vastly exceeding the footprint. You could stand inside the largest and not see the opposing walls in the dim light. The settlement is urban in its organization: paths of compressed mineral dust connect the structures, worn smooth by the passage of the growers themselves, and within the towers, you can discern floor levels, archways, and what might be habitation niches—alcoves lined with nacre, platforms of compressed salt. But it is built without tools, without fire, without technology. The inhabitants are the architects, lithovores that move through the architecture like slow weather, their bodies soft and pale against the mineral walls.

The light is dim, diffuse, coming from everywhere and nowhere—a mineral twilight, perhaps the world has a thick atmosphere of sulfur dioxide, or the place sits beneath a crust of ice, or the sun is a distant ember. It casts no sharp shadows, only a uniform gray-blue luminescence that makes the crystal towers glow from within, as if lit by internal phosphorescence. The temperature is cool, a constant chill that preserves everything. There is no liquid water; chemistry here is non-aqueous, driven by sublimation and solid-state diffusion. Trace acids, perhaps hydrochloric or sulfuric, etch patterns into the tower walls, creating dendritic gardens of corrosion that the lithovores then graze upon. The air is still, thin, or perhaps nonexistent. Sound is absolute zero—no wind, no fauna calls, no geological rumbling, no creaking of thermal expansion. The silence is total, a vacuum of acoustic pressure that makes your own heartbeat feel like a shout.

Yet the place is violently active. This is the paradox: a landscape in motion that makes no noise. The towers grow—visible only over months, new layers of calcite accreting like tree rings, but inorganic, precipitating from the breath of the organisms within. Thermal cycles cause them to expand and contract with glacial slowness, generating stresses that fracture the crystal in silent, hairline cracks that seal themselves with new mineral growth within days. Below the surface, brines of heavy metals—mercury, perhaps, or liquid sulfur at high pressure—flow through capillary channels in the rock, feeding the lithovores, causing the towers to pulse with chemical activity, their walls slowly migrating outward like a heartbeat too slow to hear. The crust itself subsides, millimeters per year, as the bedrock dissolves from beneath and reprecipitates above, a conveyor belt of mineral moving through the settlement. Dust devils of fine particles spin in the hollows, invisible and silent, driven by electrostatic differentials between the towers, lifting the dead husks of microbes into slow, spiraling columns that dissipate without a sound.

Vegetation is sparse but present: crusts of extremophile lichen, gray-green and dormant, clinging to the north faces of the towers where condensation—rare, precious, non-aqueous frost—accumulates in the cool mornings. They are not plants in the aqueous sense but symbiotic mineral-fungal colonies, photosynthesizing dim red light, fixing carbon from carbon dioxide and mineral substrates.

Fauna are rare. You might see, once in a long while, a *lithodrone*—a slow, many-legged creature with a shell of nacre and opal, grazing on the lichen crusts. It moves across the plain like a geological event, taking a day to cross the space between two towers. It makes no sound. It leaves a trail of dissolved mineral that sublimates instantly into the dry air, a temporary etching that fades within hours.

The weirdness is architectural and ontological. The towers are not stationary. Over the course of a season, they rotate—slowly, imperceptibly, each turning a few degrees on its axis, so that the doorways face different directions than they did last year, as if the settlement is slowly rearranging itself according to a logic not yet complete. The interiors contain geometries that defy the exteriors: chambers that are larger inside than the tower's footprint should allow, connected by passages that seem to fold space, staircases that ascend into ceilings. The light inside the towers is slightly different—warmer, or perhaps just the absence of the blue cast—and time feels compressed. Stand inside for an hour, and outside, a day may have passed, or perhaps only a minute. The lithovores do not seem to age; they grow, metamorphose, and dissolve back into the mineral, a cycle of reincarnation that has no beginning or end.

The settlement has no name, or its name is a vibration below the threshold of hearing, felt in the teeth rather than heard. It is a city of the lithic and the biological, a place where architecture is metabolism, where the distinction between building and organism has dissolved into something older and stranger. It is young, sharp, growing, and utterly silent—a place where the rocks dream, and the dreams are made of calcium, silica, and time.
