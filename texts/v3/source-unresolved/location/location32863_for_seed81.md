The heat arrives first as a pressure behind the eyes, a wet weight that has nothing to do with the thin, high air. You are standing on a plateau so elevated that the horizon curves slightly, yet the atmosphere here is thicker than it should be—saturated with mineral steam that has nowhere to rise and everywhere to condense. The light is wrong: dim, amber, filtered through a perpetual ceiling of cloud that is less sky than suspended weather, a luminous gloom that makes midday indistinguishable from dusk.

This is the Condensery, or what remains of it.

The terrain is not earth but accretion. Over millennia, the high-altitude basin has been weathered smooth by a peculiar erosion—one that dissolves rather than grinds. The ground is a crust of travertine and silica, pocked with shallow pools of superheated water that steam gently in the dry high wind. Between the pools, the mineral crust ripples in frozen waves, as if the land itself were a liquid captured mid-pour. There is no soil, only precipitate. No plants grow here—vegetation died out or never evolved—but the heat supports stranger architectures: forests of crystalline silica that rise like frozen lightning from the warm mud, salt flowers that bloom in geometric profusion along the waterlines, and fungal mats of impossible iridescence that coat the undersides of rock overhangs in oil-slick rainbows.

The city is vast but hollow. You see it as a scattering of megalithic structures across the plateau—geothermal collectors with throats like volcanic vents, condensation towers that rise two hundred meters into the steam, processing spires connected by elevated aqueducts that hiss with pressurized vapor. They are spaced kilometers apart, each one a self-contained industrial heart floating in a sea of mineral emptiness. The density of the place is near-void; you can walk for hours between structures, wading through warm shallows, crossing bridges that span chasms of steaming mist, never encountering another soul or even a sign of recent passage. The infrastructure is dense where it exists—layers of pipes, conduits, and catwalks wrapping around the towers like barnacles—but the overall impression is of a settlement built for a population that never arrived, or for machines that require miles of solitude to operate.

The water behaves strangely here. It is everywhere—condensing on every surface in a perpetual drizzle that never quite falls, forming curtains of droplets on the sides of the towers. But due to the superheated mineral content and the peculiar capillary action of the silica crust, the water climbs. It defies gravity, wicking upward along the concrete and iron, feeding inverted waterfalls that rise from the pools to the clouds. The air itself is a sponge; it squeezes moisture from your lungs and deposits it on your skin in a constant, lukewarm glaze. The streets, where they exist, are channels—concrete gutters designed to carry the runoff of the condensation farms, now filled with slow-moving, steaming currents that reflect the dim light like liquid mercury.

Sound is scarce. The steam hisses from vents in a white noise that numbs the ears. Water drips from the eaves of the towers in a rhythm so slow it becomes a form of silence. Somewhere, deep in the industrial core of one of the distant structures, a turbine turns—a low, subsonic thrum that you feel in your teeth more than hear. The wind is rare at this elevation, but when it comes, it carries the groaning of cooling metal, the singing of high-tension cables, the hollow boom of a condensation chamber releasing pressure. Otherwise, there is only the wet sound of your own breathing, amplified by the humid air.

Fauna is rare, almost mythical. You might, if you watch the steam long enough, see a drift-whale: a translucent, gas-buoyant creature the size of a bus, filled with luminescent fluid, grazing on the mineral mist in the upper atmosphere, its body pulsing with bioluminescent organs that cast brief, blue shadows across the plateau. Closer to the ground, heat-resistant lithotrophs skitter across the hot crust—segmented, eyeless things that feed on the chemical energy of the vents, leaving trails in the condensation that evaporate behind them like smoke. They are solitary, ancient, and indifferent to the ruins of the city.

The weirdness of the place settles in your bones. Time moves differently in the steam pockets; step into a dense cloud and your watch might stutter, or the dripping water might seem to hang suspended, neither falling nor rising. The mineral growths sing—a high, crystalline chiming when the wind passes through the silica forests, a sound like breaking glass heard from very far away. The architecture is half-dissolved and half-grown; the towers are being eaten by the very moisture they harvest, their concrete surfaces soft as clay, yet simultaneously encrusted with new mineral deposits that add layers, slowly turning the industrial machines into geological features, fossils of the present tense.

It is a post-rain landscape that never dries, a high place that is unbearably hot, a city that is empty yet humming with industry, a world without plants but rich with stone and steam and the rare, drifting life that has adapted to breathe mist instead of air.
