You descend through water that is not cold so much as *old*—a coolness that carries the weight of millennia, pressing evenly against your skin from every direction. This is no ocean. The horizon, such as it is, curves too tightly; the pressure is wrong for any sea that touches a sky. You are submerged in an **inland deep**, a lake so vast it has forgotten it is not a sea, perched at an elevation so extreme that the air above it would thin your breath to nothing, yet here, below, the water holds you in a dense, viscous embrace.

The light is **darkness**. Not the black of a trench at the bottom of the world, but a high-altitude gloom, a perpetual twilight filtered through leagues of water thick with ancient particulate, with the shed cells of a billion billion organisms, with the mineral dust of a continent grinding itself to silt. Visibility extends only to the length of your arm, perhaps two. Beyond that, the world dissolves into a featureless grey-brown, a void that is nonetheless full.

And it is **loud**.

Not with the crash of surf or the hiss of shallows, but with a subsonic roar—a constant, overwhelming *biophony*. The water itself seems to sing. It is the resonance of **abundant fauna** that has colonized this stable world: great, slow filter-feeders the size of cathedrals drifting past, their baleen plates humming as they strain the water, their calls vibrating through your bones at frequencies you feel in your teeth. Schools of translucent fish, like living glass, flash in the gloom, their passage a susurrus of a thousand tiny wings. Crustaceans the size of hounds scuttle across the seafloor, their carapaces clicking against stone—or against what was once wood.

For the seafloor is **built**. Everything here is made. There is no natural substrate, no naked rock, no sediment that was not placed, grown, or secreted by intention. The ground is a vast, **total construction** of biological origin: a petrified forest of impossible scale, a city of lignin and calcium carbonate, grown rather than assembled. But it is made with **no technology**—no tools, no fire, no metal. It is architecture of pure cultivation, the patient work of organisms that shaped their own bodies into habitations, spires, and foundations over geological time, a civilization of growth and secretion and slow, mineral accretion.

Yet it is **ancient**. The **erosion** has reached its final, primordial state. What were once towers and arches and habitations—clearly artificial, clearly built—have been worn by eons of water, by the patient chemistry of cool, high-altitude depths, into near-flattened mesas. The structures are **sparse**, scattered across the seafloor like the worn teeth of some great beast half-buried in silt. Between them stretches an empty plain of compressed, fossilized vegetation—a substrate of peat and lignite and secreted shell, smooth and featureless, worn flat by time. The ruins rise only a few meters from this plain, their edges rounded, their surfaces polished to a dull sheen by the ceaseless, slow pressure of water. They are settled into their final form, a landscape at the end of its erosion, a topography that has forgotten it was ever anything but geology.

The **vegetation** is total. The substrate itself is plant-matter—ancient, compressed, fossilized, yet still retaining the cellular structure of wood, of kelp, of fungal mycelium. The ruins are not stone; they are **grown**. You can see it in the grain, in the way the "rock" fractures along lines that remember cellulose, in the occasional pocket of amber-like resin that weeps slowly into the water, still preserving the scent of a forest that died millions of years ago. The boundary between the built structure and the living world has blurred. Some of the flattened ruins are still, impossibly, **growing**—a slime-mold intelligence of geological slowness, maintaining the architecture just enough to prevent total collapse, secreting new mineral layers at a rate of millimeters per century.

The world is **never changing**. There are no currents here, no tides, no wind to stir the surface far above. The water is still, a perfect **equilibrium**. The temperature holds at a constant cool, a thermal stasis where decay and growth have reached a balance so complete that time seems to pass at the rate of sediment settling. The fauna moves through this stasis like dreams—abundant, teeming, yet locked in patterns that have not varied in ten thousand years. It is a frozen moment, a snapshot of a climax ecosystem suspended in deep time.

You drift above the flattened ruin of what was once a spire, now a smooth, oblong hill of petrified wood. It is **unusual**—not alien, exactly, but wrong in its perfection. The right angles are still there, buried under the erosion, detectable only by the way the water flows around them, by the schools of fish that navigate the invisible geometry of ancient intention. The fauna is weird too: creatures with too many joints, with eyes that reflect no light, with bodies that seem to be made of the same translucent material as the fish, as if they have evolved to become part of the water itself, to become part of the architecture.

The sound swells—a whale-song, or the song of something like a whale, a vibration that makes your vision blur at the edges. It is loud, this place, roaring with the sheer biomass of life that thrives in the dark, in the cold, in the high-altitude deep where the world was built by plants and time, and has been left to settle into its final, silent, roaring form.
