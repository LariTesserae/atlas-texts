The land has forgotten how to be a mountain. It is the End-State Plain, a vast, near-flattened expanse where the violence of uplift has been erased by three billion years of patient water, wind, and the slow grinding of ice that no longer exists. The bedrock is a dark, dense stone—basaltic, perhaps, or something older and stranger—worn not to sand but to a slick, glassy pavement that stretches to every horizon without a hill to break the view. It sits barely above the level of the distant, invisible sea, a low bowl of the world where water does not run off; it lingers, pooling in the absolute flatness, rising to the surface through the porous, ancient rock until the stone itself seems to sweat. The air is saturated, a condensing atmosphere where moisture hangs in suspension, coating every surface in a film of cool, mineral-rich water that beads like mercury on the polished stone. The temperature is cool—never freezing, never warm—just the perpetual damp chill of a world that is always wet, always near the dew point.

There are no plants. Not a blade of grass, not a lichen in the green sense, not a tree or a fern. The photosynthetic world ended here long ago, or perhaps never colonized this particular chemistry. Instead, the stone is dressed in a living patina—a crust of simple, tenacious life: chemosynthetic bacteria and archaeal mats that draw their energy from the minerals in the rock and the water, not from the dim, diffuse light. It is a mineral ecology, a post-botanical world where the ground itself is barely alive, a dark, rubbery skin that pulses with moisture and secretes the acids that continue to wear the stone smooth. This is the only "vegetation," and it is barely that—just a scum of existence, simpler than ferns, older than moss.

Yet the fauna are abundant. The plain teems with them, though they are scattered across the vastness, sparse in any given acre but numbering in the uncountable millions across the region. They are the Grazers—creatures of cool, damp stone that have evolved in the absence of plants to fill every niche. Imagine something between a crab, a slug, and a centipede, armored with plates of chitin that have become mineralized, almost fossilized, while the creature within remains soft and wet. They stand on dozens of jointed legs, moving in herds that flow across the glassy pavement like dark, rippling water themselves. They scrape the bacterial crust from the rock with radula-like tongues, filtering the minerals and microbes, growing fat on a diet of stone and chemistry. They are strange—*unusual*—in ways that defy easy categorization: perhaps they are colonial organisms, thousands of individual zooids sharing a single armored shell, moving as one mindless herd-animal; perhaps their undersides glow with a faint, bioluminescent blue-green light, pulsing in the dimness like a slow heartbeat, making the plain at twilight resemble a starfield reflected in a shallow, black pool; perhaps they communicate through vibrations that travel through the stone itself, a subsonic language that shakes the ground beneath your feet. They are the dominant life here, and they are everywhere, yet never dense—always separated by meters of empty, wet stone, scattered across the open scale of the plain.

The sound is deafening. It is not merely the howl of wind across the unbroken flatness, though the wind does scream here, finding no tree, no ridge, no obstacle to break its fury. It is the constant, rushing *roar* of the sheet-water moving across the glassy stone—a sound like the world’s own blood in its veins, a perpetual, low thunder of friction between water and ancient rock. But beneath that, or woven into it, is the clicking, the scraping, the resonant chorus of the Grazers. Millions of armored feet striking stone. Millions of mandibles scraping crust. And their voices—if voices they are—low, booming, amphibious sounds that carry for miles across the flat, a biological thunder that never ceases, day or night. The dynamic of the place is one of perpetual, roaring motion: the water flows in thin, endless sheets toward some distant sink; the herds migrate in great, slow cycles, following the moisture and the bacterial blooms; predators—strange, sinuous things with too many eyes and translucent skin—stalk through the shallows, hunting the Grazers; and the entire system pulses with a wet, clicking, deafening vitality. It is an ecosystem in motion, predator and prey locked in a dance as old as the stone, cycling nutrients through water and shell and acid.

Scattered across this plain are the marks of the Builders. Primitive, but unmistakably intelligent. Small structures of stacked stone, half-submerged in the perpetual damp, their walls worn smooth by the same erosion that flattened the mountains—not ruins, exactly, but *settled*, as if they have always been there, slowly sinking into the wet earth. They are single huts, or the partial remains of huts: faint marks, occasional artifacts left half-buried in the sheet-water. A copper blade, dulled and green with verdigris, primitive and hand-forged. A carved bone flute, its holes crusted with mineral deposits. A circle of standing stones, each worn to a smooth nub by the patient water. The tech is stone, bone, and early metal—no iron, no machines, only hand-tools of flint and obsidian, simple fabrication, manual craft. These people—amphibious, perhaps, or at least adapted to the wet—live between the water and the stone. They do not farm plants, for there are none; instead, they *herd* the Grazers, driving them with sticks and low calls into stone pens, harvesting their mineralized shells when they die, using their hides to patch their huts, their meat for food. They are part of the ancient ecology, a late and strange addition to a world that had already reached its final form.

The light is dim. A perpetual, luminous twilight. The sky is a uniform, bruised grey, thick with moisture and the dust of ancient volcanic winters, or perhaps the world orbits a dim, reddened star. Whatever the source, the illumination is diffuse, cool, and shadowless—a flat, even grey that makes everything soft-edged and strange, erasing depth and distance. In this light, the bioluminescence of the Grazers, if that is what it is, becomes visible—a faint, moving constellation on the dark plain, blinking and pulsing in the wet dark.

The rules of this world are written in water and wear. Gravity holds, but the stone is treacherously slick; travel is slow, a slipping, sliding progress across the glassy pavement. Sound carries with terrible clarity in the dense, moist air—whispers become shouts, and the chorus of the Grazers becomes a physical pressure against the eardrums. The water is everywhere and nowhere: you cannot dig a well, for the water is already at your feet, but you cannot drink it directly, for it is thick with minerals and microbes that would sicken a dry-throat. Time moves differently here—the erosion has stopped, the land has settled into its oldest-stable-state, yet the life and the water refuse to be still. The Grazers breed in the shallows, their eggs attaching to the stone in gelatinous masses, hatching into larvae that drift in the sheet-flow, growing slowly into the armored adults. The Builders move their huts when the water rises, or when the Grazers have overgrazed a patch, leaving behind only the worn stones and the scattered artifacts, sinking back into the wet dark.

This is the Wet Flats of the Final Erosion: a cool, ancient, deafening world of stone and water and clicking, glowing life, where the absence of green has not meant the absence of abundance, only a different, stranger, louder kind of being—a place where the earth has stopped changing, and the water, and the creatures, roar on forever in the dim, saturated dark.
