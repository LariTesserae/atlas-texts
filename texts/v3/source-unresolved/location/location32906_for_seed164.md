The **Shelf of First Frost** is a continent-sized plateau thrust upward from the mantle less than a million years ago, its crust still cooling, still *sharp*. The elevation is extreme (2.5/3), placing the land above the weather of the world below, yet the air is thin to the point of absence (density 0.3/3)—a near-vacuum that nonetheless carries a heavy, wet chill (temperature 0.5/3, water 1.6/3). Moisture hangs in the rarefied atmosphere like a held breath, condensing on every surface, weeping from cliff faces, pooling in the hollows of obsidian that has not yet learned to round itself. The ground is saturated: a perched water table sits mere inches beneath the crust everywhere, turning the young, uneroded terrain (erosion 0.8/3) into a vast, high-altitude bog of black glass and basalt. Sharp-edged shards of rock jut through carpets of silver moss, and rivulets of supercooled water trace paths that change nothing, for the land is too new to have memory, and too still to carve canyons.

Across this empty immensity (scale 3.0/3), separated by hundreds of miles of silent, mist-shrouded wilderness, sprawl the **Works**—settlements that are urban in logic but sparse in footprint (built 1.5/3). They are honeycombed into the cliff faces, terraced into the escarpments, a layered palimpsest of industrial infrastructure (tech 2.5/3): massive turbine halls, smokestacks that vent steam which hangs motionless in the windless air, hydraulic presses the size of cathedrals, and electrical grids that pulse with a steady, unblinking current. The architecture is functional, brutal, *dense* where it exists, but the spaces between these nodes of civilization are absolute voids—empty distance, empty sky, empty time.

The sound here is overwhelming (2.4/3) yet perfectly monotonous. There is no wind to modulate it, no bird or insect to interrupt it (fauna 0.1/3), no creak of wood or rush of water over stone. Instead, a single, eternal drone fills the thin air: the roar of engines running at perfect, unvarying efficiency, the hiss of hydraulics, the subsonic thrum of generators. It is a sound without rhythm, a pure tone of industry that has reached equilibrium and cannot deviate (dynamic 0.4/3). It presses against the eardrums like a physical weight, the acoustic signature of a machine that has forgotten how to stop.

Yet between the factories, the land is impossibly lush (vegetation 1.6/3). A dense, velvety carpet of vegetation blankets the young rock—not grass, but something thicker, almost fungal, perhaps bioluminescent in the flat, normal light (1.8/3) that filters through the perpetual fog. It grows over the roads, up the gantries, through the cracks in the concrete, a silent, prolific monoculture that knows no grazers, no pollinators, no decay. The plants photosynthesize in the cold, wet gloom, drawing moisture from the condensing air, growing in a frenzy of chlorophyll that never ceases but never progresses—each frond identical to the one beside it, the ecosystem frozen at the moment of its birth.

The strangeness (weirdness 2.2/3) lies in the **rules of the world**. Here, time moves but nothing changes. The young mountains do not erode because erosion requires friction, and friction requires variation, and variation is forbidden in this place. The water flows in closed loops, pumped by the industry, evaporating and condensing in perfect, static balance. The machines run forever without wearing, producing a surplus that accumulates in vast, silent warehouses or is recycled in endless, lossless loops. The vegetation grows but does not die, or dies and instantly lithifies into more of the sharp, black stone. It is a landscape of perfect stasis, a snapshot of industrial might superimposed upon a newborn continent, where the roar of civilization echoes across an empty, unchanging void, and the only motion is the vibration of the eternal hum.
