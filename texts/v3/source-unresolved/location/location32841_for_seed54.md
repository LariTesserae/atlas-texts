The air arrives before you do, thick and cold, carrying the metallic tang of ozone and the wet mineral scent of stone that has never known sun. You are high—atmospheric pressure sits lightly on your ears, a constant, subtle ache—and yet the atmosphere is saturated, a paradox of altitude and humidity that coats every surface in a film of supercooled water. This is not rain; it is condensation incarnate. The darkness here is absolute but not empty. It is a polar dark pressed down by a ceiling of geothermal steam, a twilight that never resolves, where the only illumination comes from the faint, bioluminescent pulses of the city itself and the dim, bruised glow of vents far below.

The ground is raw. Erosion has not yet finished its argument with the bedrock; the terrain feels embryonic, under construction, as if the mountains are still deciding their final shape. Jagged scree and unweathered volcanic glass slope upward into the murk, but they are not natural. They are the foundations of a metropolis that grows rather than rises. The architecture is dense—layered, vertical, and near-touching—filling the valley from floor to precipice with structures that resemble calcified coral or frozen lightning. These are not built from steel or concrete but from programmable mineral matrices, smart-matter that responds to pressure and chemistry, weeping water from a thousand pores. The buildings sweat. Rivulets run down their flanks in constant, patient streams, pooling in the streets, feeding channels that spiral into the earth. The structures are warm to the touch in places, heated by conduits that carry not electricity but something stranger—zero-point fluctuations, perhaps, or the slow metabolism of folded-space reservoirs that make the water flow uphill, defying the weak gradients of this place.

There is no green here. No chlorophyll, no photosynthesis, no patient turning of light into leaf. Vegetation is a memory this world does not possess. Instead, the ecology is lithotrophic and chemosynthetic, a mineral world populated by an overwhelming fauna. They are everywhere—2.7 on the scale of presence, a saturation of life that presses against the skin of the city. Pale, translucent, and segmented, they move through the wet dark in densities that near-touch, a tide of bodies filling the narrow canyons between towers. They are not insects, exactly, though they chitter and click in a wet, resonant chorus that never ceases. They are slow—glacially, geologically slow—drifting through the mist in synchronized waves, their movement so gradual that you might mistake them for sediment until you see the deliberate flex of a million legs. They feed on the minerals leaching from the raw geology, on the bacterial mats that coat the condensation pipes, on the waste heat and the exotic chemistry of the city's beyond-modern infrastructure. They are the city's immune system, its maintenance crew, its citizens. They have no plants to eat, yet they thrive, a post-botanical ecology built on stone and steam and the slow, patient release of geothermal energy.

The sound is a constant, wet roar—a white noise of dripping water, hissing vents, and the rustling, clicking, scraping of the fauna as they navigate the dense urban lattice. It is loud, a pressure against the eardrums, yet the dynamic of the place is profoundly still. Nothing rushes. The steam drifts in slow eddies. The creatures crawl at the pace of growing crystal. The city itself expands with the patience of erosion reversed, new layers of smart-mineral extruding from the raw rock at the periphery, unfinished, embryonic, perpetually under construction. Time moves differently here, or perhaps it is simply observed from a different scale—centuries pass in the blink of a geothermal pulse, and the metropolis, for all its density and noise, is only now emerging from the mountain, half-formed, dreaming in the dark.
