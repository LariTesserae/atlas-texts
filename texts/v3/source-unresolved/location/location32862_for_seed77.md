The heat arrives in waves that bend the horizon. You stand on a plain that extends to the curve of the world—elevation barely above the thermal floor, baked under a normal sky that nonetheless feels close, pressing, because the scale is continental. The ground is not soil but architecture: a sparse scattering of vast, pale structures rising from the flatness like the frozen crests of some mineral tide. They are spaced widely—miles between presences—so the emptiness between them has weight, a low-saturation silence that makes each formation feel like an island in a sea of baked air.

These structures are total construction without tools. They are grown, not built—secreted layer by mineral layer by colonies of lithotrophic organisms that bloomed here in a geologically recent past and lithified themselves into permanence. The result is geometry masquerading as geology: spirals that tighten according to Fibonacci ratios, walls of hexagonal basalt-like prisms, chambers with ceilings of interlocking crystal. Because erosion is embryonic—only just beginning to soften the edges—the surfaces remain raw and sharp. Facets catch the light at impossible angles, unweathered, as if the landscape were still under construction, the mortar still wet. There is no rounding, no patina of time; only the pristine, angular assertion of biological algorithm made stone.

The temperature is severe, yet the air carries a dampness that contradicts the dryness. Water exists here as condensation. At night, or in the deep shadows of the structures’ northern faces, the mineral surfaces cool below the dew point, and moisture gathers—beading, then dripping in slow, deliberate drops into hollows at the bases. These are the only pools: shallow, intermittent, brackish with dissolved calcium and silica. During the day, the heat drives the moisture back into vapor, and you can see it shimmer, a ghost rising from the crevices. Occasionally, a weather system born of the plain’s own vastness—thermal columns rising from the heated stone—gathers enough moisture to release a brief, localized rain that evaporates before it touches the ground, leaving only a darkening of the pale surfaces where it struck.

Motion here is subsonic and patient. The structures are active, though no animal walks among them. They expand and contract with the thermal cycle—a daily respiration so slow it is felt rather than seen, a groaning of crystal lattices shifting against one another. The sound is near-silence, broken only by the creak of mineral under stress, the plink of a condensation drop falling into a stone basin, and the hollow whisper of wind threading through the vast, empty chambers. There are no birds, no insects, no footsteps. Fauna is absent; the ecology is botanical and sparse. Hardy, succulent organisms—lichenous or bryophytic—cling to the dampest crevices, the north-facing shadows, the places where condensation lingers longest. They are few, scattered like punctuation marks across the blank pages of the architecture.

The light is ordinary, but the scale distorts it. Because the plain is so vast, weather moves across it in visible systems—clouds that form, travel for hours, and dissipate, casting shadows that crawl across the geometric structures like the hands of a clock too large to read. The structures themselves create their own microclimates: oases of cool shadow at their bases, ovens of reflected heat on their sun-facing flanks.

The rules of this place are those of accretion and thermal stress. Nothing here was quarried or carved; everything precipitated. The landscape is still coming into being, even as it stands complete—new layers of mineral are deposited each night as the damp air surrenders its moisture to the cooling stone, adding microns to the spirals, thickening the walls. It is a world where construction is indistinguishable from growth, where the built environment is a biological process fossilized mid-breath, and where the silence is not empty but full of the slow, grinding music of stone expanding in the heat, waiting for erosion to finally begin its work.
