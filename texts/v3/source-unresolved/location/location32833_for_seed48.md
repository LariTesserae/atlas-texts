The basin breathes steam. You stand on a concrete apron that ends abruptly at a shoreline of no shore—only a gradual drowning, where the mineral ground sinks beneath water so shallow it cannot decide whether to be land or lagoon. The water is warm, held near the surface by a geothermal pulse from deep below, and it weeps upward through every crack in the weathered infrastructure. Condensation beads on every pipe, every girder, every salt-stained wall, dripping in a slow, patient rain that never reaches the ground because the ground is already saturated.

There are no plants. The ground where it rises above the water is a crust of evaporated minerals—white, pink, ochre—baked into a龟裂lichen-thin patina that is not lichen, not life as you would recognize it, only a bacterial film, a slime of extremophiles painting the mud in faint, oily colors. The earth here is post-vegetative or pre-vegetative or simply indifferent to the vegetable kingdom; it is a mineral world, a chemistry set left out in the heat.

But the water is overwhelmingly full of animal life.

They are small, pale, shrimp-like things—translucent, many-jointed, eyeless or nearly so—drifting in the warm shallows by the billion. They coat the stilted legs of the factories in a writhing, silent carpet. They swirl in the discharge channels where the industrial runoff pours back into the lagoons, filtering the mineral-rich broth, breeding in the heat, living fast and dying faster in a cycle so dense it blurs into a single, twitching organism. They do not buzz or chirp or croak. They move with a soft, clicking percussion that is almost below hearing, a susurrus of chitin against chitin, drowned out by the thicker silence of the humid air.

The light is dim. The sky is a vault of permanent twilight, a ceiling of steam and industrial haze that diffuses the sun into a uniform, pale glow. Shadows are soft, edges blurred. It is hot—oppressively, wetly hot—but the light never burns. It is the light of a Turkish bath, of a greenhouse at noon, of a world where the sun is always setting but never arrives.

The city is old. The erosion here is not violent but patient, a settled state. Concrete has gone porous, leaching lime into the water. Steel has rusted to a deep, flaking orange, then to a powder that mixes with the silt. The structures are vast and sparse—factory shells, condenser towers, pump houses, elevated walkways that span the lagoons on stilts driven deep into the warm mud. They are separated by wide, empty expanses of water and mineral flat, so the settlement feels hollow, a skeleton picked clean by time. Density is a memory here; what remains is infrastructure without mass, pipes and channels and empty warehouses where the machines have long since been dismantled or simply rusted into the landscape.

The sound is a low, continuous thrum—distant turbines, the hiss of steam through pressure valves, the groan of thermal expansion in metal. It is not loud. The humidity absorbs it, turns it into a felt vibration in the sternum rather than an audible roar. Above it, the drip of condensation. Below it, the soft, endless movement of the swarms.

This is a world in balance, though the balance is strange. The heat of the earth feeds the water; the water feeds the microbial films; the films feed the swarms; the swarms die and feed the films again. The industry draws off the heat, the minerals, the water itself, processing it through its weathered works and returning it warmer, richer, more saturated with the nutrients that the swarms require. It is an ecology built on heat and flow, on the absence of green things and the overwhelming presence of small, pale, patient animals. The dynamic is constant: steam rises, condenses, falls as warm rain into the lagoons, is pumped through the factories, heated again, discharged, evaporated. The fauna breed in this cycle, a pulse of life in a mineral world, overwhelming in their numbers, silent in their multitudes, a tide of translucent bodies moving through the dim, wet, empty spaces between the bones of the city.
