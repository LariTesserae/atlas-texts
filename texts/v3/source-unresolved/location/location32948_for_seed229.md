The air is thick with warmth, pressing against the skin like a wet cloth that never dries—except there is nothing wet here. Not a drop of water has fallen on this high basin in ten thousand years, yet the valley thrums with a lushness that defies thirst. You stand in the Singing Grove, a depression no larger than a city block, ringed by serrated peaks of gray stone that pierce a bruised, lavender sky. The elevation is high—your ears pop with the thinness of the upper atmosphere—but geothermal vents lace the bedrock, and the dense, mineral-heavy air traps heat like a lid, keeping the temperature balmy, almost feverish.

The terrain is a study in established erosion: ancient seabeds uplifted and desiccated, now sculpted by centuries of abrasive wind into cathedral spires and hoodoos of salt and silica. The ground crunches underfoot—not soil, but a crust of gypsum and fossilized ripples, weathered to a smooth, bone-white patina that stretches between the giants. It is a landscape settled into its own state, in balance with its dryness, yet never still.

The vegetation is the contradiction that makes this place. Twelve massive specimens stand scattered across the grove—sparse, yes, with vast empty spaces between them, but each one is an explosion of verdant strangeness. They do not drink. Instead, they are lithovores, towering fungal-crystalline structures that rise thirty feet into the dim air, their "trunks" spiraling columns of compressed mineral salts, their "leaves" vast, translucent membranes filled not with water but with pressurized sulfur gas. They photosynthesize through chemistry, harvesting energy from the steam vents below and minerals from the dust. In the low light—thick with atmospheric haze that filters the sun into a perpetual, dim twilight—these membranes glow with an inner, bioluminescent pulse, shifting from deep amber to sickly violet. They are lush in their way, dense with impossible fronds that flutter and chime, but they are also alien, growing in geometries that hurt the eyes, spiraling counter-clockwise against the wind, their surfaces slick with a resin that smells of copper and ozone.

And the sound is overwhelming. The wind never stops. It screams through the gas-filled fronds, creating a chord that discordantly resonates—part organ pipe, part didgeridoo, a roar that vibrates in your teeth. But the wind is only the bass line. The fauna is abundant, hyperactive, a turbulence of life. Swarms of hexapods, their carapaces made of iridescent chitin that refracts the dim light into rainbows, cloud the air between the giants. They are loud—clicking, buzzing, a deafening chorus of stridulation that rises and falls in waves. Larger creatures, perhaps reptilian or avian, with too many joints or wings that beat in syncopated rhythms, stalk the salt flats, their movements chaotic and urgent. They feed on the aerial plankton that drifts on thermal currents, on the spores released by the crystalline trees, on each other. Nothing here sleeps; the ecosystem is in a storm-state, a Solaris-like flux of creation and destruction, of constant swarming and feeding and fleeing.

Scattered among the giants are the marks of the inhabitants—built structures that are primitive, hand-crafted, scattered like bones across the white ground. Platforms of woven mineral-fiber, elevated on stilts of knapped flint to escape the drifting dust. Huts of stacked salt-blocks, sealed with a primitive mortar of volcanic ash and tree-resin. Stone circles where the ground has been smoothed by generations of use. The technology is early-tool-use, pre-metal: sharp stones, fiber cordage, containers carved from the hollowed stalks of dead lithovores. The people—if they are people, or something similar—move through the grove with the agility of the fauna, climbing the spiraling trunks with hand-tools of bone and flint, harvesting the gas-membranes for their flammable, hallucinogenic vapor, which they store in dried bladders.

The world here operates on rules that feel wrong. Gravity seems slightly lighter, or the thick air cushions every fall, allowing the fauna to leap impossibly high, bounding from frond to frond in arcs that seem to defy physics. The colors are shifted—what should be green appears as a bruised blue, what should be red is infrared, visible only as a heat-shimmer. The air itself is alive with static electricity; your hair stands on end, and sparks jump between the crystalline trees in silent, branching forks of light.

Most strangely, the grove is unstable. The dynamic is hyperactive, turbulent. Dust devils spin constantly, picking up the fine salt and carrying it in screaming columns that obscure the sky. The ground sighs and groans as geothermal vents open and close, the earth expanding and contracting with thermal pulses. The crystalline trees themselves grow visibly, visibly fast—you can hear them creak as they add new layers of mineral, and occasionally, with a sound like thunder, a frond will burst, releasing a cloud of spores that immediately begin to germinate in the air, floating upward to seed new growths before falling back to the dust. The erosion that has shaped the stone is slow, but the biology is frantic, a bazaar of exchange and consumption, an anthill-energy of industry and instinct.

It is a sealed environment, non-aqueous, a pocket of high-altitude warmth where water is a myth, yet life has found a way to be lush, to be loud, to be abundant in its sparse, scattered, small-scale intensity. It is a place of balance and storm, of ancient weathered stone and frantic new growth, of dim light and deafening sound, where the rules of water and carbon have been rewritten in sulfur and silicon, and the world breathes, turbulent and strange, without ever taking a drink.
