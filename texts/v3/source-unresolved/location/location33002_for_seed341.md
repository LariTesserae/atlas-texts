The heat arrives first as a pressure, a wet weight behind the eyes. Then the dimness—not quite shadow, but a bruised, green-gray luminescence that seems to emanate from the air itself rather than from any sky. You are standing in a place where the sun has been remembered but not seen for a long time, filtered through a permanent canopy of steam that hangs three meters above the waterline and never lifts.

This is a hamlet that has forgotten it was ever dry. The scale is intimate, a cathedral of industry shrunk to the size of a village square: perhaps twenty structures clustered within a hundred meters of each other, surrounded by an endless flatness of warm, shallow water. The buildings are five stories high, brick and riveted steel, their lower floors submerged in water that reaches your thighs and carries the temperature of a fever—thirty-eight degrees, maybe forty, the exact warmth of a living mammal. The water is black-brown, viscous with tannins and mineral runoff, and it moves in slow, tidal pulses that have nothing to do with any moon. It breathes.

The architecture is layered, established, in a state of elegant rot. Rust blooms across the corrugated roofs in fractal patterns. Concrete balconies sag under the weight of vegetation that has no business growing there—massive, prehistoric ferns with fronds like ship sails, and vines thick as a man’s waist that drape from every gutter and window ledge. The greenery is lush, overwhelming, a verdant sludge that consumes mortar and glass. Yet it is utterly silent. No insects drone in the fronds. No birds nest in the eaves. The leaves do not tremble when the steam-shift wind passes through, or if they do, it is a motion too slow to register, a vegetal respiration synchronized to something deep in the machinery below.

The sound is a wall. It is loud—2.3 on whatever scale measures the drone of existence—a constant, low-frequency roar that seems to come from the ground itself. It is the sound of massive pumps cycling water through subterranean pipes, of turbines spinning in windowless halls, of steam being forced through relief valves in rhythmic, percussive hisses. The noise is so complete that it has become a kind of weather. You feel it in your sternum. It makes conversation impossible. It has, presumably, driven every animal from this place—the frogs, the fish, the mosquitoes, the rats. The fauna level is effectively zero. The ecosystem has been reduced to a dialogue between water, metal, and plant, a triangle of non-living or barely-living things.

The weirdness settles in like humidity. The water flows uphill. Not dramatically, not in defiance of physics, but with a subtle wrongness—capillary action through the porous brick, condensation that rises from the warm pools to gather on the undersides of leaves three meters up, where it hangs in pendant gardens of droplets that never fall. The steam from the factory stacks does not dissipate into the low, gray sky; instead, it condenses into solid-seeming pillars that support the weight of walkways and pipe-bridges, translucent columns of moving water that you can touch and that feel warm and slightly electric against the skin.

The vegetation is the strangest element. It grows in perfect rows. Not the chaotic profusion of a jungle, but a cultivated geometry—hedges of *Phragmites* reeds planted at exact intervals, climbing vines trained to follow the paths of cable conduits, moss that carpets the north faces of buildings in identical, repeating patterns. It is as if the plants are being farmed by the factory, or the factory is being farmed by the plants. They photosynthesize not from the dim, diffuse light of the sky, but from the sodium glare of the floodlights that burn perpetually within the complex, casting a sickly amber glow through the steam. In the deepest shadows, beneath the waterline, things glow with a faint, bioluminescent pulse—fungal colonies or algae mats that throb in time with the machinery, a heartbeat of green light visible through the murk.

The terrain is low, barely above sea level, a subsiding delta or a drained marsh that has been reclaimed by its own water. The ground beneath the warm flood is soft, silty, composed of decades of industrial sediment and decaying organic matter. The buildings stand on stilts and pilings that have been eaten halfway through by rot and by something else—some chemical process that makes the wood porous and sponge-like, weeping rust-colored sap when it rains, which it does frequently, a warm, oily drizzle that smells of copper and ozone.

The dynamic is one of cycles. The water level rises and falls by half a meter every six hours, not with any tide, but with the factory’s production schedule. When the great presses stamp and the smelters fire, the water rises, flooding the lower streets, and the pumps engage with a roar that shakes the windowpanes. When the shift changes and the fires are banked, the water recedes, leaving stranded puddles that steam in the residual heat, and the pumps fall silent to a mere gurgle. The vegetation responds—the fronds unfurl when the water rises, drinking deeply; they curl and droop when it falls, conserving. It is an ecosystem in motion, but the motion is mechanical, industrial, a biological pace dictated by gears and pistons.

There are rules here. The water is not drinkable, but it is not immediately lethal; it leaves a metallic film on the skin and a taste of pennies on the tongue. The steam will burn you if you stand too close to a vent, but the ambient air, though thick, is survivable—a sauna without the health benefits. The light is constant, a perpetual twilight of 1.0, neither day nor night, because the steam clouds are permanent, fed by the cooling towers and the hot springs that vent from fissures in the bedrock below. The absence of animals is absolute; the loudness of the place is a force field, a sonic barrier that has scrubbed the landscape clean of anything with a nervous system more complex than a plant.

And in the center of it all, where the water is deepest and warmest, where the steam is thickest and the light most dim, there is a structure that might be a cooling tower, or might be a tree—a thing of concrete and chlorophyll, of riveted steel and bark, humming with a frequency that makes your teeth ache. It is the heart of the place, and it is neither fully built nor fully grown, but something that has settled into a balance between the two, weathered, established, existing in its own state of humid, roaring, vegetal stagnation.
