The air arrives first—thin, scalding, and strangely light. You stand on a plateau so high that the sky above is not blue but a deep, bruised violet, the color of old bruises pressed against thin skin. The sun here is normal, ungodly, a white disk that bleeds glare across black glass and ochre stone, but the atmosphere is too sparse to soften it. Breathing is a deliberate act; each inhalation pulls in air that tastes of metal and ozone, heated from below by a geothermal pulse that keeps this extreme elevation at a simmer. It is hot—2.7 on the scale of warmth, a dry, pressurized heat that makes the throat crack and the eyes water, yet it does not feel like desert heat. It feels like the inside of a kiln that has just been opened, a heat that rises from the bones of the earth rather than from the sky.

The ground is raw. Erosion here is embryonic, barely begun. You walk on basalt that cooled perhaps a century ago, still sharp enough to shred boot leather, its surface a mosaic of volcanic glass so black it seems to drink the light. Between the glass plates, sinter deposits bloom in crusts of white and orange—silica and sulfur precipitated from ancient steam, forming terraces that look like frozen waterfalls. Water is present but reluctant (1.2). It does not flow in rivers. Instead, it exists as condensation, gathering in beads on the undersides of metal overhangs each dawn, or as superheated brine trapped in pockets beneath the crust, venting only as mist through fumaroles that hiss and sigh on cycles of hours. There are pools, but they are mineral baths—saturated, steaming, ringed with salt. No plants grow here (0.1). The only suggestion of life is a faint, bacterial film staining the hottest rocks in greens and purples, chemosynthetic mats that metabolize the sulfur and heat, nothing more. The world is post-botanical, or perhaps pre-botanical, a mineral kingdom awaiting a biology that never arrived.

And yet, built upon this raw geology is a city (2.3). It is not a metropolis of sprawl but of density (2.4)—a settlement that compresses itself into vertical canyons and stacked terraces, a place where structures touch, near-touching, layered upon one another like sedimentary plates forced upward by some internal pressure. The architecture is industrial (2.5): vast cooling towers that do not emit smoke but rather draw moisture from the air, their surfaces beaded with condensation that drips into collection troughs; conduits of fused glass and heat-resistant alloy snaking between buildings; massive engines that process the basalt itself, grinding the volcanic rock into silica and rare minerals. But the machinery is strangely silent (0.9). The engines turn on magnetic bearings, or they are thermal differentials so vast and slow that their motion is imperceptible, a rotation measured in degrees per day. The only sounds are the low, moaning wind through the glass spires, the occasional *tink* of cooling metal, and the distant, geological groan of the plateau settling.

The scale (2.2) is regional—a valley-sized complex, or perhaps a series of mesas connected by bridges of braided cable and glass. From a high vantage, the city appears as a dense knot of black and silver against the ochre plain, but up close, it reveals itself as a labyrinth of narrow passages where the walls radiate heat and the shadows are cool and rare. The density is oppressive in the core, where buildings lean together, sharing walls, creating microclimates where the temperature can swing from scorching to merely hot within a few meters. The erosion (0.5) is still shaping the place—the city is under construction in a literal sense, with new layers being added to the structures as the mineral accretion grows, the buildings slowly becoming part of the geology, half-machine, half-rock.

Fauna (1.4) is rare and strange. You might see, if you wait—and everything here requires waiting, for the dynamic is slow (0.6)—a glass-skinned grazer, something like a tortoise but with a carapace of translucent silica, moving across the basalt at a pace that suggests it measures time in geological epochs. It feeds on the bacterial films, scraping them with a beak of obsidian. Or you might see thermal worms, pale and segmented, burrowing into the sinter deposits near the vents, their bodies pulsing with bioluminescent heat. They are rare, few in number, adapted to an ecology that barely exists. They move slowly, everything moves slowly here. A construction crane might take a year to lift a single beam. A geyser might erupt once a decade. The city grows by accretion, not by speed.

The light (1.5) is normal but harsh, casting knife-edge shadows that do not soften at the edges. The weirdness (2.4) of the place lies in its contradictions: the hot thin air, the silent industry, the raw new rock supporting ancient-looking machinery, the vertical density against the horizontal openness of the plateau. It is a place where physics feels negotiated rather than fixed. Sound travels strangely in this hot, thin atmosphere—distant noises are muffled, while close sounds seem to resonate with a crystalline clarity. Time feels thickened. The city is a slow machine, a patient engine turning the heat of the earth into structure, into order, into something that resembles civilization but is built from glass and steam and the slow violence of geology.

At night—if night comes, for the days are long and the twilight lingers—the condensation freezes instantly on the metal surfaces, forming rime that glows in the moonlight. The fumaroles continue to vent, their steam catching the light like ghostly breath. The rare creatures retreat into the warm rock. The city hums, inaudibly, a vibration felt in the teeth rather than heard, as the engines continue their glacial work, and the plateau, still emerging, still raw, dreams of becoming something else.
