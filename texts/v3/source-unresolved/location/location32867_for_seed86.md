The space is a cathedral that forgot it was a grave. You stand on a floor of fractured limestone that shatters underfoot with a sound like breaking porcelain—sharp, angular, *young*. The rock here has not known the patience of water wearing it smooth; instead, it is a landscape of fresh violence, of recent collapse and sudden exposure. Jagged fins of stone rise from the ground at impossible angles, and the ceiling, where you can see it, is a saw-tooth sky of newly exposed mineral, still wet with the birth-tears of the mountain. This is a karst system that opened yesterday, geologically speaking, and yesterday it filled with life.

The air is cool to the point of numbness, carrying a damp that is not wet so much as *insistent*—a condensation that gathers on every surface, turning the stone slick and dark, pooling in the hollows of the fractured floor. The moisture is intermittent, capricious: here a steady drip from a stalactite, there a patch of mist that drifts through a passage like breath held too long. Pockets of standing water gleam in the dimness, black and still, reflecting nothing.

The light is meager, a perpetual twilight. It does not come from above—this is too deep, too far below the surface world—but from within. The walls are dusted with a sparse vegetation of pale fungi and crustose lichen that emit a faint, phosphorescent glow, a cool blue-white luminescence that barely reaches the ground. It is enough to see the shapes of things, to make out the gleam of wet stone and the movement of bodies, but not enough to dispel the shadows that pool in the higher vaults. The light is constant, unwavering, part of the stone itself.

And the sound. The sound is overwhelming. It is not the silence one expects of caves, but a roar—a constant, layered clamour of life at 2.8 on whatever scale measures presence. The acoustics here are perfect for amplification; every voice, every footstep, every drop of water is magnified and thrown back from the high ceiling. You hear the fauna before you see it: a vast, scattered population of *them*—the Hollow Ones, or whatever name they bear—moving through the chambers in their sparse, nomadic bands. They are numerous, overwhelming in their collective presence, yet spread across this regional expanse with a density that keeps the caverns from feeling crowded. They are spaced like stars, each group occupying its own territory of stone, connected by the echo of their calls.

They are a primitive people, their technology visible only in the glint of stone tools—flakes of chert, hafted scrapers, the occasional fire-drill smoking in a corner of a chamber. They build nothing. There are no walls raised by hands, no permanent structures, no artifacts left to mark their passage beyond the scattered debris of their meals and the smoothed patches of floor where they sleep. The landscape remains untouched, undeveloped, a wilderness of rock that they merely inhabit. They move through it in patterns that never change, following routes etched into the stone by generations of bare feet, carrying their tools and their young, their voices rising in song or warning or simple conversation, blending with the shrieks of cave-swiftlets and the wet, heavy breathing of the great salamanders that lurk in the pools.

Everything here is in equilibrium. The dynamic is zero, or near enough: a frozen moment, a time-invariant system. The erosion is young, but it does not progress; the sharp edges remain sharp, the fresh breaks remain fresh. The population of the Hollow Ones does not grow or shrink. The fungi glow with the same intensity. The water drips in the same rhythm, pooling, evaporating, condensing, returning. There are no gradients, no changes, no seasons underground—only the eternal, cool, damp present. It is a world that achieved its balance in an instant and has held it ever since, a regional expanse of caverns where life swarms in overwhelming, sparse, loud, and absolutely unchanging abundance.
