You stand in the **Humidarium**—a basin no wider than a cathedral nave, ringed by walls of black glass that rise sheer and unweathered, still sharp enough to catch the light like knife-edges. This place is young: the obsidian floor is only centuries old, cooled lava that hasn't yet surrendered its edges to time. There is no soil here, only a thin skin of mineral grit over glass, and the ground is never dry. It weeps.

Every surface condenses. The obsidian walls are draped in constant rivulets, water sliding down in clear sheets, pooling in the low places as shallow, steaming mirrors that reflect nothing because the air itself is saturated with vapor. It is post-rain without the rain, or rather, rain that never finishes falling—it simply hovers, dripping from leaf to glass to ground in an endless, silent cycle.

There are six of them in this bounded space, spaced thirty meters apart, each one a column of verdant excess. The ecosystem is too young, too strange, still assembling itself from the thermal vents and the engineered mist.

The spires rotate—imperceptibly, millimeters per hour—adjusting their fractal faces to catch the shifting thermal currents.

Above, the peaks are sharp and young, **erosion 1.4**, the mountains still rising, still being born. Below, the heat waits.
