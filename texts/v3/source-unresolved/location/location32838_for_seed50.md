The rules of this place are few but absolute. First: the sound is structural. The deafening roar is not merely noise; it is the acoustic scaffolding holding the embryonic chamber open. It seeps into cracks, freezes (though the temperature is cool, not freezing), expands, and cracks the stone further—a positive feedback of erosion that is actually *accelerated* formation, the mountain being sculpted by its own flood in reverse.

You float in the green light, surrounded by the roar, feeling the cool pressure of a world that is still under construction. Above you, the mountain rises, sharp and new, into a sky you cannot see. Below, the vents whisper cold fire. Around you, the water churns and remembers, and the stone grows, and the sound never stops. It is a pocket of becoming—a single, deafening, turbulent breath held in the palm of the earth, filling with water, filling with time, filling with the strange, patient violence of a landscape that has not yet decided what it wants to be.
