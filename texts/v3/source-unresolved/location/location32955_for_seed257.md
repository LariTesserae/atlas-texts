You descend into a warmth that has no business existing at this altitude. The water is first thing to find you—thick, mineral-heavy, holding the heat of deep stone like a bath that has been steeping for ten thousand years. It presses against your skin with the gentle insistence of a held breath. This is not an ocean; it is a single, contained world, a chamber flooded to the brim, sitting high in the mountains where the air outside would be thin and sharp. Here, at the bottom of the fill, there is only liquid and the things that have learned to live in its slow, patient dark.

The walls are not rock. You realize this when your hand finds them—no cold granite, no grain of natural stone, but a surface that is pale, chalky, faintly porous, like unglazed pottery fired in a primitive kiln or a composite of crushed shell and clay packed by hand. It is all made. Every surface in this place is constructed, built up from the packed earth by craftsmen who worked with flint, bone, and simple wooden levers. They raised pillars and archways and layered chambers within chambers, creating a density that feels almost organic—a forest of architecture packed shoulder-to-shoulder, near-touching, leaving only narrow channels of water between them.

But time has had its way with their handiwork. The erosion here is ancient, primordial, a settling-into-final-form that has sanded every edge to a curve and every corner to a hollow. What were once crisp, geometric doorways—built to human scale, or perhaps to the scale of something else—are now smooth, featureless alcoves. The pillars have swollen into elongated teardrops, worn thin at their bases where the water has lapped against them in centuries of imperceptible motion. The floor, too, is constructed, a tessellation of hand-laid slabs now worn into a single, undulating sheet, near-flattened, as if the entire chamber has been gently planed down by the patient chemistry of warm, saturated water.

There is no plant life here. Not a single frond, not a speck of green, not a root breaking through the constructed substrate. The walls are bare, coated only in a thin, invisible film of microbial life—simpler than vegetation, chewing slowly at the minerals of the artificial stone. The ecology is built on this absence. The water is thick with drift-kin, translucent and abundant, filling every volume of the chamber in dense, layered schools. They resemble living lace—gelatinous, segmented, possessing too many fins and too much symmetry, as if folded from paper and given a slow, pulsing motion. They do not swim; they pulse, expanding and contracting in metronomic rhythms so slow that they appear to hang motionless in the gloom. They are filter-feeders, sifting the warm water for the microscopic detritus that rains down from the eroding walls, and they are everywhere, near-touching, brushing against the smooth pillars and against each other in a silence so complete it becomes a presence.

The darkness is profound. Light is a rumor here, a faint, greenish murk that filters down from a high oculus far above, a pinprick of mountain sky diffused through forty meters of mineral-silted water. Mostly, the chamber exists in a dim, aqueous twilight, the only illumination coming from the faint phosphorescence of the drift-kin themselves—a soft, internal glow so weak it barely registers, turning the water into a field of drifting, pale lanterns. You navigate not by sight but by pressure, by the shifting weight of the warm liquid, by the chemical signatures of the water, by the occasional brush of a tendril against your wrist.

Sound is almost entirely absent. There is no lapping, no bubbling, no rush of current. The water is too still, too warm, too perfectly saturated that it has achieved a kind of equilibrium. The drift-kin move with such viscosity and slowness that they create no ripple, no whisper. You hear only the muffled thrum of your own heartbeat, amplified by the density of the medium, and the occasional, rare drip of condensation falling from the ceiling—a single, bell-like *tick* that echoes for a long moment in the silence before the water absorbs it.

This is a world of endings and slow continuations. The builders are gone, or have become something else, their primitive tools and simple fabrication methods lost to the smoothing water. The chamber is a closed system, or nearly so, fed by the slow seep of snowmelt from the high peaks above, water that finds its way through cracks in the constructed roof and drips down to replenish the fill. The drift-kin live long, slow lives, cycling through generations measured in decades rather than days, grazing on the mineral film, occasionally consuming one another when a body sinks to the worn floor, a slow rain of nutrients in the dark.

Everything here is gradual. The convection currents in the water move in cycles so weak they are barely perceptible—warm rising, cool sinking, a thermal gradient so gentle that the water might as well be still. The erosion continues, but at a pace of millimeters per century, the constructed surfaces dissolving grain by grain into the warm, holding liquid. The scale is intimate despite the grandeur—a single hall, perhaps the size of a cathedral, but so densely packed with pillars and drift-kin that it feels like a thicket, a room, a single feature of the world that has been given over entirely to the slow, patient, silent persistence of water and the strange, abundant life that drifts within it.
