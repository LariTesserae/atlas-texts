You stand on a lip of raw basalt that has never known the softening touch of water. The stone beneath your boots is sharp, angular, *new*—as if the world was extruded yesterday and has not yet decided whether to round its edges. This is a place of emergence frozen at the moment of becoming: cliffs rise in perfect vertical sheets, valleys cut in clean V-shapes that have not yet learned to widen, alluvial fans that are still only intentions. The geology is under construction, but the construction has paused, held in a state of embryonic potential that will not resolve.

The elevation is high—you feel it in the pressure, in the way the light arrives unfiltered and slightly too bright. Yet the air is warm, a thick warmth that seems to rise from the stone itself rather than from any sun. There is no liquid water here; the parameter is absolute. No rivers, no rain, no dew. The chemistry of this place runs on other solvents—perhaps supercritical minerals, perhaps dry redox reactions, perhaps the slow sublimation of elements that on wetter worlds would dissolve. What grows here does not drink.

And yet the vegetation is lush.

Across the plateau, spaced with the terrible patience of continents, rise the lithovores. They are forests, but not of wood. They are crystalline cathedrals of silicon and copper, of quartz latticework and oxidized iron, growing in dense, verdant canopies that spread for kilometers at their crowns. They look like coral made animate, or like trees of glass and gear. Their "leaves" are mineral membranes, thin as mica, that exchange gases directly with the atmosphere—no transpiration, no stomata, only the silent diffusion of elements across solid surfaces. Their trunks are engines: piezoelectric cores that grind bedrock into powder, thermal vents that refine ores through heat alone, root systems that burrow with mechanical precision, processing the raw geology of the emerging crust into... something. Refined minerals. Atmospheric gases. The byproduct is heat, and silence.

These are the industry. There are no factories here, no built structures, no artifacts of intention—only the lithovores, which are simultaneously flora and machine. They are grown, not built, yet they function with industrial scale: vast networks of mineral processing, of chemical refinement, of energy conversion. A single specimen might process tons of rock per day, its canopy a solar collector for a spectrum of light that has nothing to do with chlorophyll, its roots a refinery that separates elements by density and magnetism. They are lush in their density, thick with mineral growth, heavy with the weight of geology made biological, but they are sparse in their distribution—there might be seven of them visible across an entire regional valley, each the size of a mountain, separated by tens of kilometers of empty, raw stone.

The density of the world is low. You can see for twenty miles in every direction. The space between the lithovores is a near-void of pale, unweathered mineral—salt flats that are not salt, dust that is not dust, drifts of crystalline powder that has never been moved by water because water does not exist here. The air is thin enough that sound struggles to propagate, yet the silence is not merely the absence of noise; it is a presence. The lithovores operate in infrasound, a pressure wave that you feel in your molars, in the hollow of your chest, rather than hear. Their grinding, their refining, their endless slow metabolism occurs below the threshold of hearing. Occasionally, a harmonic shudder travels through the stone—a frequency so low it registers as a change in gravity, a momentary heaviness—and then the silence returns, absolute and warm.

There are no animals here. No fauna to graze on the mineral canopies, no insects to pollinate, no scavengers to clean the byproducts. The lithovores exist in solitary grandeur, each one a complete ecosystem of mechanical-biological processes. They do not reproduce in the way animals do; they accrete. They grow by adding layers, by extruding new crystalline structure from their cores, expanding outward at a rate that is geologically significant but humanly imperceptible. A new branch might take a century to extend a meter, but the branches are kilometers long, and the trees are ancient beyond reckoning, yet they look young—their surfaces are unweathered, their edges sharp, as if they emerged fully formed from the uplift and have not had time to age.

And they have not had time. This is the final paradox of the place: it is dynamic and static at once. The erosion is emerging, pre-formation, the land still deciding its shape, yet nothing changes. The lithovores process the rock, yet the rock remains raw. The canopies spread, yet the spread is frozen, a potential energy without kinetic release. Time here moves in a different direction, or perhaps it does not move at all. You could stand on this ridge for a thousand years and see no difference—the same sharp cliffs, the same sparse distribution of crystalline forests, the same warm, silent, high-altitude air. It is a landscape caught in the moment of its own creation, a world under construction that has forgotten to finish, or that has chosen to remain forever in the state of becoming.

The light is normal, but it falls strangely on the mineral surfaces—reflecting off the crystalline canopies in prismatic bursts, casting no shadows because the sun is directly overhead and the lithovores are too sparse to shade the ground. The sky is a pale, washed color, perhaps high in particulates from the endless mineral processing, or perhaps the atmosphere is simply thin and warm and strange. There is no weather here, no gradients, no change. The wind does not blow because the air is too still, too equilibrated. The temperature is warm and constant, 1.5 on whatever scale measures such things—a tepid, geological warmth that seeps from the processing cores of the lithovores and the young, hot crust.

This is a world of non-aqueous chemistry, of sealed environments where the rules of water-based life do not apply. It is industrial without industry, built without builders, lush without water, alive without animals. It is a place where the boundary between the organic and the mechanical has dissolved into something else entirely—a geology that thinks, slowly, in frequencies too low to hear, processing itself into a future that never arrives, suspended in the eternal, warm, silent moment of emergence.
