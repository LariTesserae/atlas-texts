You descend into a chamber that breathes. The walls are not old—they are *new*, fractured basalt and limestone with edges still sharp enough to draw blood, as if the ceiling collapsed yesterday, or yesterday was when the drilling stopped and the earth was allowed to settle into this hollow. The rock smells of fresh mineral dust and ozone, unweathered by time, its surfaces freckled with condensation that has not yet learned to run in rivulets, hanging instead as a million suspended beads that catch what little light exists.

The scale is intimate but disorienting. You could shout across the space and hear your voice return muffled, swallowed by humid air, but the darkness above the chamber is absolute—elevation 0.5, the surface world a rumor of light far overhead. The floor is a jumble of fallen debris from the collapse, sharp shards interspersed with the scattered bones of industry: a concrete platform tilted at a drunken angle, a gantry of rusted steel that once held something heavy, pipes that snake across the stone like the veins of a buried god. These are remnants, sparse and scattered, not a factory but the suggestion of one—a geothermal station abandoned mid-operation, or perhaps begun and never completed, its machinery left to the slow digestion of heat and moisture.

It is hot here. A wet, heavy heat that presses against the skin, 2.8 on the scale of things, radiating from vents in the floor where the rock is stained ochre and sulfur-yellow. The air is damp but not flooded—intermittent moisture in the extreme. Steam rises in slow columns from cracks, condensing on the cooler ceiling to drip back down in a silence so complete you can hear each drop strike stone, a percussion of geological patience. The water is not rain but exhalation, the breath of the earth itself, collecting in shallow pools that steam gently where they touch the hotter rock.

And yet, against all expectation, it is lush.

Vegetation carpets every surface that receives light—and in this darkness, light is a precious, industrial commodity. The machinery, though scattered, still hums with a low, dormant electricity. Emergency conduits flicker with amber and sickly green illumination, casting long shadows across walls thick with growth. The plants are not green in the way of surface things; they are phosphorescent, bioluminescent, feeding on the chemical richness of the geothermal water and the artificial photons. They form dense mats of thermophilic algae in the warm pools, fronds of something like kelp but rubbery and pale, fungal colonies that bloom in caps the size of dinner plates, glowing with an internal turquoise light. They grow in the condensation, drinking the drips, thriving in the 60-degree heat that would kill anything with a heartbeat. They have colonized the rusted pipes, wrapping them in fibrous, glowing sheaths, turning industrial decay into verdant architecture.

There are no animals. Not insects in the fungal caps, not thermophilic worms in the mud, not the blind cave fish that might be expected in such a place. The chemistry is wrong—too much sulfur, too much heavy metal leached from the young rock, too sterile. Or perhaps the chamber is simply too new, the ecosystem a monoculture of plants without the complexity to support grazers, a garden without gardeners. The silence is therefore absolute, broken only by the *tick* of cooling metal and the *plink* of water finding its level in the dark.

The dynamic here is slow. The erosion is young, but it proceeds at the pace of centuries—stalactites forming drop by drop, mineral deposits crusting the machinery in layers of calcite, the vegetation expanding across the stone at a rate you could measure in millimeters per year. The industrial engines, if they ever ran, have long since seized, their pistons locked by rust and mineral scale, yet the electrical systems persist in a twilight state, siphoning power from a geothermal tap that still functions deep below, keeping the lights burning just enough to feed the glow-forest.

It is a cathedral of failed industry and improbable life, a room-sized world bounded by sharp, fresh rock, where the only motion is the rising steam and the falling water, and the only light is the pale, pulsing bioluminescence of plants that should not exist, growing in the dark, drinking the heat, waiting.
