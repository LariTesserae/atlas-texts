It is a plain that has forgotten it was ever a mountain.

The land has been ground down by time until it is almost perfectly level—a peneplain so flat that water cannot choose where to drain and therefore stays everywhere. The bedrock is exposed in vast, uninterrupted sheets, worn smooth by eons of rain and mineral time, now warm to the touch under the normal light. This is not desert; it is saturated. The ancient stone *weeps*. In the warm, condensing atmosphere, moisture gathers on the rock faces—exhaled from the pores of the earth itself—and drips in slow, patient drops, feeding shallow pools that dot the surface like scattered, breathing mirrors. Between the pools, the rock is slick with algae and mineral bloom, cracked into polygonal plates where the water has receded slightly, then re-wetted, then receded again across centuries of imperceptible change.

Vegetation is sparse, a low-saturation scattering across the emptiness. Here and there, a single tree rises—perhaps a gnarled, ancient fig or a stunted cypress—its roots gripping the thin, peat-rich skin of soil that barely veils the stone. These trees are widely spaced, sometimes kilometers apart, growing with glacial slowness, their canopies small and wind-shaped. Between these sentinels, the ground is bare rock or black mud, hosting only lichens and the occasional tenacious fern. The plants that do survive are adapted to wet feet and mineral-poor substrate, existing in a state of suspended animation, neither thriving nor dying, simply persisting.

But the emptiness is deceptive. The fauna is abundant, teeming in the vertical spaces—the water column and the humid air layers—rather than carpeting the ground. Frogs, by the dozens of species, call in a constant, loud chorus that rises and falls with the humidity, their voices bouncing off the flat stone without absorption. Insects hatch in clouds from the warm shallows: dragonflies stitching the air in iridescent arcs, mosquitoes humming in thick, drifting veils that shift with the slightest breeze. Wading birds stalk the pool edges—herons, egrets, ibises—their cries sharp and metallic against the drone of wings. In the deeper, sluggish channels that thread between the rock sheets, the water moves with ancient patience, carrying fish that flash silver, turtles that slide from logs, and the occasional reptilian shape—caiman or monitor—that watches from the murk with saurian stillness. The abundance is aquatic and aerial; life is stacked in the water and the moisture-laden air, leaving the stone itself strangely bare.

The sound is loud, a continuous roar of biological activity that never fully ceases. It is the buzz of ten thousand insects, the bellowing of amphibians, the splash of feeding fish, the creak of the scattered trees in a wind that moves across the flatness without obstruction, the constant *shhh* of evaporation and the answering *drip* of condensation. Yet the land itself is profoundly still. The dynamic is slow, a geological patience that makes the biological noise feel almost eternal. The water evaporates and condenses in daily cycles that have repeated for millions of years. The rock erodes by millimeters per century. The trees have stood for centuries, thickening by growth rings barely visible to the eye. Seasons barely register here—only subtle shifts in the water level, the insect populations, the angle of the light as the year turns with imperceptible slowness.

It is a place at the end of its long erosion, settled into a final form: a warm, wet, ancient plain where the mountains have surrendered their height to become this near-flat sheet of stone and water, where life has colonized the thin margin between rock and sky with sparse vegetation but abundant, noisy, slow-moving creatures. No human foot has pressed the mud here, or if it has, the impression has long since dissolved into the general wetness. It is pure nature, pre-tool, biological-only—a landscape that has reached its oldest stable state and simply continues, patient and loud, under the normal light, weeping and teeming in the endless, humid afternoon of the world.
