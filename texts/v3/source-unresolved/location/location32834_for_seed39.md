You stand where the world is still deciding what it wants to be.

The air is thin enough to make your lungs work, carrying a metallic tang—ozone and mineral dust. At nearly four thousand meters, the horizon curves away from you in a shallow bowl, revealing a plateau that extends past the limits of comfortable vision. This is the **Raw Expanse**, a high-altitude wetland born from violence and still shaking itself into shape. The mountains here are infants in geological time; the rock is gray, angular, and utterly unweathered—no soil, no lichen-smooth boulders, just fractured stone that crunches and shifts underfoot like broken pottery. The erosion is embryonic, which means the land is still *under construction*, rising millimeters each year, cracking open to release the heat below.

And the heat is everywhere, paradoxically, in a place where the ambient temperature hovers just above freezing. The cold bites—your breath crystallizes instantly—but the ground weeps. Water, water everywhere, but not as ice. The geothermal youth of the range forces liquid up through fissures in the new stone, creating a landscape of shallow, black pools and saturated flats. These are not lakes; they are wounds in the earth, saucer-shaped depressions no deeper than a man is tall, filled with mineral-rich water that steams in the freezing air. The rocks themselves weep, condensation beading on their surfaces and dripping into rivulets that braid across the stone. The atmosphere is a condensing soup, a perpetual twilight where steam and cloud merge. The light is dim—not the darkness of night, but the flat, pewter-gray luminescence of a world wrapped in its own exhalation. The sun, when it breaks through, is a pale, distant coin, diffused by ten thousand rising vapors.

Vegetation is a stubborn afterthought. In the warmest seeps, near the steaming vents, thick mats of gray-green moss cling to the rock, and in the shallows, ribbons of cold-adapted algae drift like shed skin. But for the most part, the land is stone and water. Sparse. Barren. Yet life is abundant here—a contradiction that resolves itself in the wetlands.

The fauna has colonized the water. The pools teem with pale, eyeless fish and massive, salamander-like amphibians that have never known warmth, filtering nutrients from the geothermal minerals. Above them, the sky is never empty: flocks of hardy waterfowl—thousands at a time—wheel over the plateau in dense, shifting clouds, their cries muffled by the mist and the thin air. On the rocky margins between the pools, herds move. They are shaggy, long-horned creatures, built for the altitude with barrel chests and dense, oily coats, grazing the sparse moss with methodical patience. Because the landscape is vast and empty—kilometers of bare stone separating each feature—these herds appear as dark, slow-moving stains on the horizon, abundant in number but sparse in presence, drifting through the emptiness like thoughts.

But you are not alone with the beasts. The **tech** here is industrial, yet there are no buildings. No factories. No roads. The `built` environment is zero—nothing permanent can take root in stone that is still rising, still cracking, still finding its level. Instead, the industry is *nomadic*.

Massive machines stalk the plateau on hydraulic legs—harvesters the size of cottages, their bodies low and crab-like, designed to traverse the unstable, newborn rock without sinking. They are mining the embryonic geology, extracting rare minerals that are still forming in the hydrothermal veins, pulling them out before the slow grinding of erosion can scatter them. Their exhaust vents steam that joins the natural mist, and their electrified undercarriages glow with a faint, blue-white light in the dimness. On the larger pools, flat, barge-like platforms float, processing the water—filtering minerals, perhaps harvesting the aquatic abundance, their decks crowded with robotic arms and conveyor systems that snake out across the surface like mechanical vines. These structures are temporary; when a vein is exhausted or a landslide shifts the ground, the machines simply walk or float to the next deposit, leaving no scar but a fresh gouge in the young stone.

The sound is mostly quiet. The thin air carries sound poorly, and the machines are spread across the vast, open scale of the plateau—separated by distances that swallow noise. You hear a low, continuous thrum, like a refrigerator heard from miles away, the harmonic drone of engines working in the cold. Occasionally, a geyser erupts with a wet, percussive *whump*, sending a column of steam twenty meters into the gray. A herd moves, their hooves clicking on stone, a sound that fades before it reaches you. The world is active—dynamic, in motion—with weather fronts sliding across the plateau, with the slow, grinding uplift of the mountains, with the constant migration of the herds following the warmth, and with the relentless, crawling advance of the industrial fleets. It is an ecosystem in fast-forward, a landscape being born and harvested simultaneously.

There is a rule here: permanence is impossible. The mountains rise, the water shifts, the machines move. What is solid today is mud tomorrow. The cold preserves, the wet nourishes, and the industry extracts, all in a place where the ground beneath your feet is still learning how to be ground.
