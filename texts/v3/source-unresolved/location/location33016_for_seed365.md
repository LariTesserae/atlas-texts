The place sits where two ridges of young limestone meet at a sharp angle, a V of grey stone thrust upward from the cool earth. The rock is unweathered—erosion has only just begun to soften its edges, leaving cliffs that are knife-sharp and fractured, faces of pale stone that catch no sun directly. At this elevation, the air is thin and perpetually cool, holding a dampness that never quite resolves into rain. Instead, moisture gathers in suspension, a fine condensation that beads on every surface: the cliff faces, the moss, the roofs of the settlement below. It is a landscape of dripping, but the dripping is so constant, so soft, that it registers as silence.

Light here is dim, a perpetual twilight. The valley is narrow, bounded by those sharp walls, and the sky above is a bruised grey that filters down a diffuse, watery luminescence. Shadows are deep but not absolute; everything is visible, yet nothing glows. The coolness seeps into the stone, into the timber, into the thick carpets of vegetation that claim the hollows and crevices.

The vegetation is lush where it exists—vibrant, almost aggressive in its growth—but sparse in its distribution. Thick cushions of emerald moss carpet the valley floor in patches, interspersed with bare rock. Ferns unfurl in the damp clefts, their fronds dark and glossy. Lichens paint the stones in crusts of pale green and sulphur-yellow. Between these verdant islands, there is empty space: grey stone, mist, the occasional dry boulder. The lushness is concentrated, a series of dense gardens separated by wasteland, as if the life here grows only where the condensation falls heaviest.

And there is a town.

It occupies the condensation line—the narrow band where the damp air meets the slightly drier rock of the valley’s center. The scale is small, intimate, a hamlet that could be circled in an hour, yet it is undeniably urban: organized, dense with infrastructure, layered with construction. The buildings are primitive—dry-stacked stone walls without mortar, frames of lashed timber, roofs of woven reed and bark. They were raised with hand-tools of flint and bone, shaped by manual craft, pre-metal or barely-metal technology. Narrow paths wind between the structures, paved with flat stones set into the moss. A central cistern collects the endless dripping, a dark pool that never overflows, never runs dry. The architecture is strange, organic in its curves, as if the builders followed the growth patterns of the moss rather than straight lines, creating a settlement that feels less constructed than cultivated.

But the town is not empty. It is full—overwhelmingly full—of fauna.

They are everywhere. Pale, eyeless creatures—perhaps salamanders, perhaps something without a name in any tongue—carpet the streets, cling to the walls, move through the houses in dense, silent herds. Millions of them. They slide on mucus trails across the stone, their bodies soft and segmented, moving in slow, undulating waves that flow around the buildings, through the doorways, over the sleeping forms of any inhabitants. And they make no sound. No croaking, no skittering, no rustling. The damp air swallows every motion, rendering the overwhelming presence of these creatures into a visual phenomenon only—a tide of pale, gliding life that is utterly mute.

The dynamic of the place is constant, though quiet. The condensation cycle runs endlessly: water evaporates from the moss carpets, rises into the cool air, condenses on the overhanging cliffs, drips back down in a soft, invisible rain. The herds of fauna move in their silent procession, a biological tide that pulses through the settlement. Outside the valley, weather moves on the peaks—storms gather and disperse—but here, in the crook of the young mountain, the air is still. The erosion continues, grain by grain, the sharp limestone slowly rounding, the cliffs retreating, the valley widening in geological time.

The weirdness settles over everything like the mist. The boundary between the built and the grown has blurred; the stone walls are porous, honeycombed with tunnels that match the paths of the creatures. The light never changes, neither brightening nor darkening, creating a sense of suspended time. The overwhelming fauna do not seem to interact with the settlement’s architecture, yet the architecture seems shaped by them, accommodating their passage, their density, their silence. It is a place where the loudest thing is the absence of sound, where the most abundant life moves without a whisper, where a primitive town exists inside a living, breathing, silent swarm, high in the cool mountains where the rock is young and the water never stops dripping.
