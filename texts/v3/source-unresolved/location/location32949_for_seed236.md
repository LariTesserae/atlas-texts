You cross a field of black glass. The obsidian is young — it cracked into plates yesterday, geologically speaking, and still holds the heat of its birth. No water has touched it; there are no streams, no puddles, no lichens softening its edges. The air is warm, thick, smelling of sulfur and something else — something sweet and heavy, like old honey left in the sun. You climb the last ridge of the flow, and the glass ends.

Then the trees begin.

They are not trees. Not exactly. They rise in columns of compressed mycelium, a material like bone or petrified wood, pale as old ivory against the black rock. Each stalk is thick as a cottage, wrapped in a bark of layered, papery sheets that peel in slow curls. They stand close together — so close you can touch three from where you stand — forming a dense forest that is less a wood than a hall. The space between them is narrow, a corridor barely wide enough for two of the creatures to pass. Above, the stalks branch into broad, shelving caps that overlap like shingles, a canopy thirty meters up that filters the light into a dim, green-gold gloom.

This is the Grove. It is small — you can see the far edge, where the pale stalks meet the black glass again, a boundary sharp as a wall. It is a room, a cathedral, a single feature in the high, dry world.

The ground is not soil. It is a crust of mineral salt and volcanic ash, sharp underfoot, and it is everywhere stained with amber. The amber drips from the stalks — not water, but a thick, golden resin, a hydrocarbon sap that the fungi synthesize from the gases of the vents below. It moves slowly. A single drop hangs at the lip of a crack in the bark, swelling, trembling, taking a full minute to fall. It lands with a sound like a stone into mud, but slower, and it does not splash. It spreads into a viscous pool that never evaporates, never dries, only thickens at the edges over weeks. The creatures drink from these pools. The pools are the only "water" here.

The stalks are not randomly placed. They grow in patterns — rows, circles, clusters — as if following a plan. This is the work of the inhabitants. They are everywhere. They are silent.

You see them first as shapes in the dim light: slow, massive, segmented. They look like woodlice the size of sheep, or perhaps like slugs armored in interlocking plates of chitin the color of old bone. They graze on the papery bark, on the fallen caps, on the resin itself. They move with a patience that borders on stillness. A creature will stand in the corridor for an hour, its antennae waving, tasting the air. Then it will take one step, and the step will take a minute.

They do not make noise. Not because they are quiet, but because there is no medium for sound here. The air is thick and still; the dense stalks absorb any vibration. Instead, they communicate by tapping. A sharp rap of shell against mycelial bark sends a pulse through the stalk, through the underground network of mycelium that connects every tree in the Grove, to the creature on the other side. It is a language of touch, felt in the feet, in the shell, in the slow pulse of the resin in their veins. To human ears, the Grove is silent. You can hear your own heartbeat. You can hear the slow, creaking growth of the stalks, a sound below hearing, felt in the sternum.

The creatures have built their city in the forest. Not with tools — there is no technology here, only biology. They have worn the corridors between the stalks smooth, generation after generation, until they are paths of packed ash and resin, wide enough for three to walk abreast. They have gnawed hollows in the lower stalks, chambers lined with resin and softened bark, where they sleep in heaps, warm and close. Higher up, they have strung bridges between the trees — not woven, but grown. They smear resin on the gap, and the mycelium of the two stalks, sensing the chemistry, grows across to meet it, forming a living bridge that thickens over years. The result is a layered infrastructure: ground-level streets, mid-level platforms, high walkways, all connected by ramps of hardened resin and grooves worn into the bark.

It is a settlement, though no architect designed it. It is the slow accumulation of instinct, of need, of the pressure of many bodies moving through the same space for centuries. The density is immense. Every surface is occupied. The caps are layered with smaller creatures — silent insects, sessile filter-feeders that strain spores from the air, crusts of mineral-lichen that grow in the resin-slick crevices. The fauna is abundant, a saturation of life, but it is a quiet abundance, a stillness.

The rules of this place are written in resin and time. Nothing happens quickly. The fungi grow a hand's width in a year. The resin drips once a minute. The creatures live long, move slow, and think in vibrations. There is no water, and so no thirst, no rain, no streams. The moisture they need is locked in the resin, in the thick air, in the mineral crust. The world is young — the lava is fresh, the fungi are recent colonizers, the city is still being built, layer by layer, tap by tap. But it is also patient. It will take a thousand years to finish a corridor. It will take ten thousand to hollow a cathedral. The Grove does not hurry.

Above the caps, the sky is normal. The light is normal. But here, in the high, warm, silent dark of the stalks, the world is different. It is a place where the sap is gold, and the inhabitants speak in tremors, and the city grows like a coral, slowly, from the inside out, a dense, layered, living architecture in the midst of the dry, sharp, empty world.
