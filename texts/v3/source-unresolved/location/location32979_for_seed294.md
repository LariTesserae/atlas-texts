The world is a **table**—a high, flat plateau suspended at altitude, yet entirely drowned beneath a column of water so deep and cold it might as well be an ocean pressed down from a grey sky. You stand (or drift) upon a surface that is not earth, not rock, but **construction**: a substrate of pale, laminated mineral that was once secreted, layer by layer, by the bodies of ancient, colonial organisms that knew no tools, only chemistry and time. Now it is petrified, lithified, a single continuous artifact the size of a region—yet worn down by two billion years of pressure and current to a near-perfect smoothness, a plain so flattened it reflects the dim light above like a sheet of frosted bone.

The elevation is a lie of the body: your ears pop with the altitude, the air (if you could breathe it) would be thin and freezing, but you are submerged, crushed beneath hundreds of meters of water that presses with the weight of the sky itself. The water is **everywhere**, filling the space between the scattered, low mounds of what were once towers or ramparts—now just gentle swells in the seafloor, spaced miles apart across this vast, sparse plain. No plants grow here. The cold is absolute (0.4), a thermal stasis that has preserved the silence of a pre-photosynthetic world; there is no green, no chlorophyll, only the faint, drifting luminescence of the fauna and the grey-blue gloom filtering down from above (light: 1.0).

Yet life is **abundant**. shoals of translucent, many-jointed creatures graze the mineral surface, their bodies filtering the water for detritus and chemical energy. They move in dense, writhing clouds—millions of them—yet they make no sound. The water around them is in constant, **turbulent** motion: a perpetual, slow-motion storm of currents that churn and eddy across the flat plain, dragging sediment in spiraling gyres, creating underwater weather systems that never calm. But this chaos is **silent** (sound: 1.1). There is no crash of waves, no roar—only the muffled, subsonic thrum of pressure against your hull or your skin, a vibration felt in the teeth rather than heard. The turbulence is visual and kinetic: particles of pale silt suspended in the churning water, obscuring vision beyond a few meters, creating a white-out of motion that is nonetheless hushed, a roiling quiet.

The dynamic is not violent in the way of a tempest, but **incessant**—the water is alive with a chaotic, searching energy, as if the ocean itself is remembering a fever. Thermal vents are absent (the cold is uniform), but the turbulence suggests a deep, geological restlessness, or perhaps the collective movement of the fauna stirs the water into this eternal maelstrom. The creatures do not build; they merely inhabit the ruins of the builders, swimming through the flattened canyons and over the worn plateaus of their ancestors' architecture.

Everything here is **made**, yet nothing was manufactured. The rules of this world are those of accretion and erosion: matter is laid down by biological secretion, then worn away by water and time until only the essential form remains—a landscape settled into its final state, ancient beyond measure, submerged at the top of the world. It is a place of **endings**: the end of construction, the end of growth, the end of sound, replaced by a silent, turbulent, cold abundance—a mineral desert at high altitude, drowned in a quiet storm, where the only movement is the endless, soundless churn of water over stone that was once alive.
