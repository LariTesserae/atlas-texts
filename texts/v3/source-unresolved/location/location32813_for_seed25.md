The valley is a bowl of black glass.

It sits low in the earth, a depression worn smooth over epochs by water that no longer flows in rivers but only weeps. The floor is obsidian—volcanic glass cooled in a hurry, then polished for millennia by a wind that carries no seeds, only dust. The surface is cool to the touch, always damp with condensation that gathers in the shallow basins between outcroppings. When rain comes—intermittent, hesitant—it pools in these hollows for an hour before seeping into cracks or evaporating into the heavy air. The glass sweats. It is never dry.

There is no green here. No lichen, no moss, no memory of leaf or root. The world is mineral, pre- or post-botanical, a substrate waiting. But the substrate is not empty.

It moves.

The fauna is a tide. A dense, churning carpet of bodies covers the glass, layered three, four creatures deep in places, packed so tightly that the individual is indistinguishable from the mass. They are small—no larger than a hand—armored in chitin the color of wet basalt, jointed legs splayed wide, mandibles clicking against stone and each other. They have no eyes, or eyes that see only vibration and chemical shadow. They are lithovores, grazing the mineral patina that the damp air deposits on the glass, or perhaps they eat the glass itself, scraping it down grain by grain, wearing the valley smoother still in a feedback of ancient erosion.

Because they are so numerous, because they touch and rub and scrape across the resonant volcanic floor, the sound is absolute. A deafening, physical roar—not a chorus of voices but the white-noise shriek of ten million legs rasping stone, of bodies shifting in the crush, of the wet *shhk-shhk* of mandibles opening and closing in the dark. It is a sound that bypasses the ears and enters the bones, a pressure that makes the teeth ache. You cannot hear yourself think because there is no silence to hold a thought in.

The light is scarce. The sky above the valley is bruised and low, filtering a dim, submarine gloom that the dense bodies of the creatures absorb and refract. They are dark, matte, light-eating. Where they are thickest, they form a second surface, a living shadow that blocks what little sun tries to penetrate. Bioluminescence flickers faintly beneath this carpet—not bright, but a slow pulse of deep blue and bruised violet, the creatures signaling to each other in wavelengths that barely register as illumination. It is a darkness lit only by the ghost-flicker of the swarm itself.

The scale is open but wrong. The valley extends past the immediate, a regional expanse of this glass plain, yet it feels enclosed by the density of life. You can see the horizon—a rim of weathered stone where the glass ends and the dryness begins—but your gaze keeps being pulled back to the near, to the writhing, near-touching mass of legs and armor. The creatures flow like a liquid, pooling in depressions, streaming up the gentle slopes in waves that have no beginning or end. They are in constant motion, a dynamic ecology of consumption and being-consumed, the weakest being crushed beneath the weight of the strong, their bodies breaking down into the mineral dust that feeds the next generation.

The strangeness is in the synchronization. Though they are individuals, they move as a single skin across the land. When they rise to feed, they rise together; when they settle, they settle in a ripple that travels across the valley like a pulse. They are colonial in behavior if not in body, a distributed hunger that thinks with the collective pressure of the crowd. They do not sleep. They do not stop. They are the erosion, the weather, the sound, and the dark, a post-botanical world where animal life has achieved the density of a geological force, wearing down the stone with the patience of a glacier and the fury of a storm.

The air tastes of copper and cold moisture. The glass beneath your feet is slick with the condensation of their passage. And the roar never ceases—only shifts in pitch as the swarm thickens or thins, a deafening heartbeat that is the only rhythm this low, cool, open place knows.
