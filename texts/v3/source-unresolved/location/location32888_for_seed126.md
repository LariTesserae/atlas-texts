The light arrives first, not as a beam but as a condition. It is the bright of 2.8/3, a luminosity that has no source in any visible star—perhaps the sky itself is a luminous membrane, or the ground emits a pale, phosphorescent glow from its dense, mineral skin. The air is warm (1.8/3), holding heat like a held breath, and thick enough to press against the skin.

You stand on a plain. It is low (1.4/3), a vast depression or a flattened world, stretching to a horizon that curves with the planet's immense scale (2.8/3). The ground is not earth. It is built (2.6/3)—every cubic centimeter of this world is manufactured, constructed, willed into being by minds and machines long past. Yet it is also ancient (2.6/3), eroded by time into a state of near-flatness, a final form where all the architecture has been sanded down by eons of non-water weather.

The surface is dense (1.9/3). It is not empty desert; it is saturated, layered, a palimpsest of construction pressed into near-touching strata. What you see are the eroded remnants of a megastructure—towers and arcologies and computational spires worn smooth by the slow, grinding action of atmospheric tides. They rise only inches above the plain now, ridges like frozen waves, or perhaps they are the exposed tops of foundations that extend deep into the planet's manufactured crust. Between these ridges, the material is glassy, fused, obsidian-dark in the shadows but gleaming where the light touches it.

The weather is active (2.1/3) but silent (0.3/3). There is no birdsong, no wind-roar. Instead, the world breathes. Thermal fronts move across the plain in visible waves—shimmering distortions in the heavy air where hot and cooler masses meet. These fronts carry precipitation that is not water (0.5/3). Perhaps it is a rain of liquid metal, droplets that fall upward in the local magnetic fields, or a snow of crystalline silicates that sinters onto the glassy ground, only to be eroded again by the next thermal cycle. The atmosphere is a supercritical fluid, dense and viscous, damping all sound to a subsonic hum that you feel in your teeth rather than hear.

The technology here is beyond modern (2.6/3). You can see it in the cross-sections where erosion has exposed the interior—strata of logic gates the size of mountains, now fossilized; veins of once-active nanofabricators that have become inert mineral; geometries that violate Euclidean expectations, corridors that loop back on themselves in ways that suggest the landscape is folded, compactified, a Klein bottle of architecture. The erosion is not natural decay; it is the landscape settling into its oldest stable state, the end-of-life compression of a civilization that built the entire world and then let it run down, or let it run forever, depending on your interpretation.

Vegetation is sparse (0.7/3). It appears as patches of grey-green lichen—crystalline, slow-growing, feeding on the ambient heat and the strange light. It colonizes the eroded ridges, softening their edges with a velvet of mineral moss that is not truly alive, or perhaps alive in a way that mimics life, a symbiosis between dormant machine and emergent chemistry.

Fauna is rare (0.6/3). In the distance, across the silent plain, something moves. It is large, or it is small—the scale is hard to judge in this bright, featureless light. It moves without sound, a maintenance entity or an apex predator that has evolved in the gaps between the machine-world's processes. It passes once, and then the plain is empty again, save for the weather.

The weirdness (1.5/3) is subtle but persistent. Shadows fall in directions that don't match the light. The eroded ridges, when you walk along them, sometimes lead you in a circle that should be impossible, a geometry that closes on itself. The silence is absolute enough that you can hear your own heartbeat, and sometimes, beneath it, the planet's own pulse—a infrasonic thrum of computation still running deep in the crust, processing data for a purpose no longer remembered.

This is a world that has forgotten it was made. The builders are gone, or they have become the weather, or they are the erosion itself. The landscape is a final form, settled and stable, a monument to a totality of construction that has outlasted its meaning, now existing in a bright, warm, silent equilibrium, slowly wearing itself smooth in the absence of water, singing only in frequencies below hearing.
