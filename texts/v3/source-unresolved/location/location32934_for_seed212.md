You arrive at the edge of the **Verdant Maw**—though “edge” is a fiction here, for the boundary dissolves before you reach it. This is low country, barely lifted from the water table, a plain of young earth so recently extruded from the mantle or thrust up by sleeping faults that the soil still remembers its violence. The ground is *sharp*: not eroded into softness, but fractured into fresh ridges of peat and mineral, jagged as broken teeth, veined with cracks that open and close like breathing wounds. Elevation is negligible; you stand perhaps three meters above the invisible sea that presses up from below, feeling it as a perpetual, humid exhalation.

The air is **warm**—2.5 on your scale—a wet heat that carries the weight of the tropics without the tropical sun beating directly overhead. Light is normal but filtered through a **density** of living matter so absolute that the world is layered like a city itself: from the root-mat underfoot, through the fungal-sponge strata of the understory, up through the canopy that blots out the sky, to the emergent towers that pierce the cloud line. Everything touches everything. There are no gaps, only membranes—translucent, pulsing, veined with sap that moves too fast to follow with the eye.

And then there is the **sound**.

It is not wind. It is not rain. It is the city itself, **deafening** in its industry. You hear the *crack* of cellulose, the wet tearing of new cell walls as trunks expand by centimeters in the span of a heartbeat. You hear the hydraulic *hiss* of xylem under impossible pressure, water pulled up from the saturated ground through vascular straws the size of your arm, popping and sighing in rhythm with the pulse of the forest. You hear the *scream* of friction—millions of leaves, packed so densely that they rub against one another in a perpetual, grinding storm, a dry ocean of chlorophyll. And beneath it all, a subsonic *thrum*, felt in the sternum, the resonance of a biomass so vast it generates its own weather.

This is the **Built** environment, but built without tools. Tech is zero—no stone, no metal, no fire, no hands that shape. The architecture is entirely **vegetal**, grown rather than constructed. The “buildings” are massive, hollowed megastructures: the calcified husks of titanic woody fungi, or the lignified remains of trees that have been coaxed—by what? by the land’s own dreaming, by chemical gradients, by the sheer pressure of overgrowth—into occupying geometric space. They form streets, plazas, aqueducts. The “infrastructure” is a network of vascular channels, open-air conduits of sap that glisten amber in the diffused light, carrying nutrients and water in torrents that roar like rivers. Bridges are living suspension cables of woven vine, thick as pythons, that tighten and loosen as they grow.

But the **weirdness** is total. Reality has begun to fray here. The erosion is young—the land is so new, so *fresh*—that the plants have not yet learned to obey the old rules. Growth is visible. You watch a wall of bark knit itself closed across a window in the time it takes you to blink. Gravity is patchy: in the plaza ahead, condensation pools hang suspended in the air, fat silver droplets that drip *upward* into the waiting stomata of overhead leaves. The geometry is non-Euclidean—streets that are longer on the inside than the outside, staircases that ascend to the same floor, angles that do not sum to one hundred eighty degrees. The city exists in a state of quantum superposition: it is simultaneously a seed and a metropolis, a ruin and a foundation, collapsing and erecting itself in the same moment.

**Water** is present but erratic—0.7 on your scale. It is not a place of rivers, though you can hear them underground, a dull rushing in the dark. Instead, moisture arrives as **condensation**. The air is saturated; every surface weeps. The “stone” of the buildings—compressed cellulose and peat—sweats constantly, dripping a sweet, resinous liquor that collects in the gutters of the streets. Occasionally, a warm rain falls, but it is drunk within seconds, sucked into the thirsty architecture with a sound like a thousand straws slurping. The streets are dry channels, cracked and sharp, but they fill and empty in cycles, fed by the city’s own transpiration, creating flash-floods of sap and pollen that surge through the lower districts before being reabsorbed.

There are **no animals**. Fauna is a silence here, a negative space. No birds pierce the canopy. No insects hum in the understory. The ecosystem is a pure monoculture of plant-life, perhaps too young to have invited colonizers, or perhaps actively hostile—emitting frequencies, or chemical barriers, that make the flesh of animals curdle. The food web is internal: the city consumes itself. Dead districts collapse into mulch, are composted by fungal mats, and are regurgitated as new growth within the hour. It is a closed loop of verdant cannibalism, a metabolism without mouths.

And it is **turbulent**—a storm-state of perpetual becoming. The **dynamic** here is not weather but *metamorphosis*. The city is hyperactive, in a state of constant, chaotic creation and destruction. A tower of woven branches might achieve its full height at dawn, and by noon be a heap of compost, its nutrients already racing up the vascular systems of its neighbors to fuel a new spire three streets away. The air is thick with spores—not the quiet dust of a forest, but explosive clouds, *deafening* in their release, golden and green particulate that forms temporary bridges, temporary walls, temporary weather. Storms of pollen flash across the valley like green snow, solidifying into structures that last for minutes before dissolving into the humid haze.

You walk on a substrate that is not earth but **root-mat**, a spongy, ankle-deep carpet of interwoven tissue that shifts underfoot, sometimes sucking at your boots with a wet *schlick*, sometimes springing back with hydraulic force. The young erosion means the ground is unstable—fresh sinkholes open beneath you, swallowing the path, only to be sealed by a carpet of rapid moss within the hour. Sharp features abound: the edges of new growth are knife-like, the bark of the young trees is paper-thin and cutting, the mineral veins in the soil glitter with fresh, unweathered crystal.

This is a town-sized organism, a valley-sized plant, an urban ecology without precedent. It is warm, and wet, and screaming with the sound of its own impossible, accelerating life. It is a place where the distinction between the natural and the constructed has dissolved entirely—where the city is the forest, the forest is the city, and both are growing faster than reality can keep up, layer upon layer, in a deafening, green, turbulent roar.
