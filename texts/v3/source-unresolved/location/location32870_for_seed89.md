The place is a wound in the mountain. At four thousand meters, where the air should be thin and the cold a blade, there is a depression—a caldera that collapsed inward centuries ago, settling into a bowl no wider than a city block, perhaps three hundred meters rim to rim. The walls are basalt, but not the sharp black glass of new lava; this is old stone, weathered by centuries of freeze and thaw into something almost organic, rounded shoulders and smooth flanks the color of wet slate. They rise steeply, enclosing the space like the interior of a throat, and they are so high, so sheer, that direct sunlight rarely strikes the floor. The light that enters is filtered, diffused through a permanent haze, leaving everything in a state of perpetual twilight—grey-green, dim, the color of deep water.

The temperature defies the altitude. It is warm here, twenty-two degrees Celsius even at the rim, because the bowl sits atop a lattice of geothermal vents. Steam rises from cracks in the floor, not as violent fumaroles but as a constant, patient exhalation, filling the lower half of the crater with a dense, white mist. The air is thick with moisture—not rain, which falls only occasionally and briefly, but condensation, a dampness that settles on every surface. Water beads on the cliff faces, dripping in slow, rhythmic ticks into pools that gather in the hollows of the eroded stone. The ground is a mosaic of mineral crusts, soft mud, and shallow warm springs that steam gently where they meet the cooler air.

Vegetation is sparse, clinging to the margins. Orange and grey lichens paint the upper walls in crusty, map-like patterns. In the warm seeps near the vents, mats of moss grow thick and spongy, the only green in a world of stone and mist. A few stunted shrubs—gnarled, wind-twisted things—perch on the rim, their roots gripping the ancient rock, but they do not descend into the bowl. The interior is mostly mineral, mostly life of a different kind.

For the bowl is full. The density here is overwhelming—not of plants, but of bodies. The fauna is a press of movement and sound, thousands of small, grey-furred creatures that have made this enclosed world their territory. They are tool-users, practitioners of a primitive technology: they chip stone with practiced hands, fashioning scrapers and points from the flint nodules that litter the floor. But they build nothing. There are no structures, no walls raised by intention, no artifacts of construction. They dwell in natural cavities, in the overhangs of the eroded basalt, moving through the mist in loose, noisy bands. Their presence is layered and near-touching, a saturation of life that fills every niche of the small space.

Their sound is constant and loud—a babble of calls, high and resonant, bouncing off the curved walls. They drum on stone with their tools, a rhythmic percussion that joins the hiss of the steam vents and the whistle of wind through the crater rim. The noise is not frantic; it is a deep, settled roar, the sound of a crowded room where everyone is speaking at once, or the hum of a massive hive.

The creatures themselves are unusual—adapted to the strange warmth of this high place, their fur thick and their eyes large for the dim light. They move with a slowness that matches the geology around them; their migrations through the bowl follow gradual cycles, taking days to shift from one seep to another. Time here is measured in erosion, in the slow wearing down of the basalt walls, in the patient accumulation of mineral deposits. The dynamic is minimal-flux: the mist rises and settles in a daily rhythm, the steam vents pulse with a geological heartbeat, and the creatures drift through this stable, ancient system like sediment in water.

It is a place of boundaries—the rim defines it, the mist obscures it, the walls contain it. A cathedral of steam and stone, small and dense and dark, warm at the top of the world, filled with the loud, slow, tool-chipping life of its overwhelming inhabitants.
