The limestone is newborn. It has not yet learned the patience of erosion, still wearing the fractures of its uplift like fresh scars. At this elevation—2.9, the roof of the world—the rock is a palisade of white-grey spires, each edge sharp enough to draw blood from the thin, cool air. Vegetation clings only to the margins: sparse, desperate lichens in shades of bone and rust, a few stunted saxifrages that have found purchase in the scree. The temperature sits just above freezing, a constant chill that seeps through any garment, carried on a wind that never stops.

You approach the edge of the plateau not by walking a path, but by finding the place where the ground has forgotten how to be flat. Here, the young karst has collapsed inward, creating a throat—a vertical shaft plunging into the mountain's heart. It is a tiankeng, a sky-hole, but smaller than the great ones of the south. This is cathedral scale, intimate and terrible. The walls of the shaft are striated with the violence of their formation: fluted, chiseled, abrupt. Water does not flow here in rivers; it exists as a promise. Condensation beads on the rock, gathering in the flutes, dripping in slow, patient drops that vanish into the dark before they reach the bottom. The damp is a presence, a humidity that fills the lungs and settles in the joints.

You descend. The light fails quickly. At first, there is the violet glare of extreme altitude, the sun hammering down on snow that is not here, on air that is too thin. Then, the shaft narrows, the walls leaning in, and the darkness becomes absolute—a darkness so complete it has texture, a velvet weight against the eyes. The temperature drops further. Your breath clouds.

At the bottom, the chamber opens.

It is a room, bounded and specific, perhaps sixty meters across and forty high—a single feature, a hollow in the stone womb of the mountain. The floor is not flat but a chaos of fallen blocks, young scree that has not yet been worn smooth. In the hollows between these stones, water collects: stagnant, dark pools that reflect nothing, that drink the condensation from the air. The damp here is total. Every surface weeps. The air is cool, heavy, saturated.

And it is full.

The fauna are overwhelming. They do not merely inhabit the chamber; they constitute it. Thousands—perhaps tens of thousands—of pale, eyeless things press against one another in layers, a dense, near-touching mass that fills the volume from floor to ceiling in places. They are slow. Their movements are geological, patient, taking minutes to complete a single step. They are strange: humanoid in suggestion but wrong—too many joints in their limbs, translucent skin that shows the dark pumping of their organs, mouths that open in vertical slits. They have no built structures; they construct nothing. They simply exist, a dense, breathing carpet of flesh that shifts and sighs in the dark.

Yet they possess technology. Primitive, yes—stone and bone and sinew. They work the sharp limestone of the young rock, flaking it into tools: scrapers, awls, crude hand-axes hafted with gut. You see these scattered across the floor, or clutched in the multi-fingered hands of the creatures. They do not build shelters, do not raise walls. Their "architecture" is density itself, the packing of bodies, the layered saturation of presence. The tools are for cutting, for scraping, for the slow work of survival in this lightless place.

The sound is a roar.

It does not come from the creatures, though their collective respiration adds a wet, rhythmic undertone—a slow inhale, a slow exhale, like the tide of a sluggish sea. The roar is the wind. At this elevation, the gales are constant, screaming across the plateau above, finding the throat of the sinkhole, and funneling down with a force that turns the chamber into a resonating cavity. It is a low-frequency drone, a subsonic moan that you feel in your sternum, in your teeth, in the marrow of your bones. It is loud—1.6, nearly overwhelming—but it is steady, patient, a dynamic of slow pressure rather than gust or chaos. It has been blowing since the mountain was young, and it will blow until the limestone wears down to sand.

Time here is slow. The dynamic is minimal-flux, gradual cycles across long durations. The condensation freezes into rime on the walls during the cold hours, then thaws in slow drips. The creatures move through their lives with glacial patience, breeding in cycles that take years, dying and being consumed by the mass, their bones picked clean by smaller things that scuttle in the dark. The mountain itself rises, imperceptibly, millimeter by millimeter, while the wind scours it. Everything is young and everything is ancient, caught in the tension of a landscape that has just been born but is already being erased.

Weirdness permeates the geometry. The walls, though sharp and young, curve in ways that defy the angles of the shaft above. The chamber seems larger inside than the mouth suggests—a cathedral that swallows space. The creatures stand in rows, facing the walls, their slit-mouths pressed against the stone, listening to the roar. They have been doing this for generations. Their tools lie where they dropped them, or where they placed them with ceremonial precision. There is no light, only the darkness, and in that darkness, the mass of them breathes, and the wind screams, and the water drips, and the mountain dreams its slow, sharp dreams.
