You stand on a ridge of **basaltic glass** that has not yet learned the softness of age. The rock is black, jagged, and singing with the memory of its own violence—sharp shards angled at forty-five degrees, scree slopes that have not yet slumped into the rounded talus of maturity. This is a high place, scraped raw by wind and cold, where the air is thin enough to taste of metal and the horizon curves visibly against a sky the color of bruised plums. The elevation here is not merely high; it is *tectonic*—a young range thrust upward in the last few million years, still wearing its birth-sharpness, erosion barely begun to file down the edges of the world.

And yet, growing from this austerity, is **total vegetation**.

Not a carpet. Not a forest in the conventional sense. Instead: **The Resonant Colossi**.

They rise like solitary cathedrals from the sharp dark rock, spaced so far apart that from any given vantage you might see three, or five, or only one against the vastness. Each is a megastructure of living matter—towering spires of pale, woody tissue that stand two, perhaps five kilometers high, their trunks broader than a city at the base and tapering to needle-points that vanish into the low, dark cloud layer above. They are plants in the way that a glacier is water: architectural, slow, utterly dominant. The substrate itself is alive; the ground between the Colossi is not soil but a thin, crusty membrane of lichen and extremophile moss that clings to the volcanic glass, feeding on condensation and the mineral dust of the young mountains.

But they are **empty**.

Density here is near-void. Each Colossus is hollow—a vast, cathedral-like interior of branching chambers and vertical shafts, honeycombed with air. The "forest" is therefore a paradox: total biomass, absolute ecological dominance, yet spatially it is mostly absence. You could walk for days through the sharp, empty valleys between these giants and encounter nothing but wind and stone and the occasional pool of perched water, before arriving at the next hollow mountain of green.

The climate is cold—hovering perpetually near the frost line. The Colossi are insulated by thick, cork-like rinds, pale grey-green in color, which trap what little heat the dim sun provides. **Light** here is a scarce commodity; the world orbits a distant, reddened star, or perhaps the upper atmosphere is thick with high-altitude ice crystals that scatter the already-faint illumination. The result is a perpetual twilight, a darkness that is not absolute but *subtractive*—as if the world were viewed through smoked glass. In response, the Colossi have become **bioluminescent**. Their inner chambers glow with a faint, greenish phosphorescence—colonies of symbiotic fungi and chemosynthetic bacteria that light the hollow spaces from within, turning each spire into a dim lantern visible for hundreds of kilometers across the dark, empty wastes.

**Water** is intermittent and precious. It exists here as condensation—moisture harvested from the thin, cold air that swirls around the peaks. It freezes on the outer rinds of the Colossi in delicate, lace-like patterns, then melts during the brief, dim days to trickle down internal channels, collecting in vast reservoirs within the hollow hearts of the plants, or pooling in the sharp, unweathered crevices of the rock. Occasionally, a squall of snow dusts the highlands, but it is the *pockets* of water that sustain life—the dripping condensation, the meltwater streams, the ice that forms in the shadows of the Colossi's bases.

Because the vegetation is total, the **fauna** has evolved to be arboreal in the extreme. Life exists in vertical cities. Vast herds of six-legged, furred grazers—something between a yak and a giant insect—migrate along the lower flanks of the Colossi, their hooves clicking on the sharp rock. Above them, in the mid-level hollows, live colonies of gliding predators, membranes stretched between elongated limbs, who nest in the warm, glowing chambers and dive between the spires on thermal currents. The Colossi themselves are not passive; they are slow-moving, their growth audible over years as a creaking, groaning of cellular expansion, and they host entire ecosystems of parasites, pollinators, and borers that live within their woody flesh.

The **sound** here is overwhelming. The wind—constant, fierce, shaped by the vast scale of the range into weather systems of its own—screams across the sharp rocks, creating a high, keening wail. But louder still is the **music of the Colossi**. Their hollow trunks act as organ pipes; as the wind passes through their internal chambers, they produce deep, resonant tones—bass notes that vibrate in the chest, harmonics that shiver through the empty valleys. When multiple spires are within earshot, they create beating frequencies, a planetary-scale drone that is felt as much as heard. The fauna adds to this: the bellowing of the grazers, the shrieks of the gliders, the clicking of countless legs on stone.

Scattered across this landscape are the marks of **primitive technology**. Not ruins of a lost civilization, but the current, ongoing work of a stone-and-fiber culture. In the lower hollows of the Colossi, platforms of woven plant-fiber and lashed bone create shelters. Rope bridges—thick cables of braided vine—stretch between nearby spires, though "nearby" might mean a crossing of ten kilometers. On the sharp rock faces, faint petroglyphs are pecked into the glassy surface: spirals, zig-zags, the crude outlines of the Colossi themselves. These are not grand monuments but *waymarks*, scattered and utilitarian, left by a people who use stone tools, bone needles, and fire-drills to carve their existence into the hollows of the living mountains.

The world is **dynamic**. The Colossi grow—slowly, visibly, their tips extending upward by meters each decade, their hollows expanding as new chambers are budded. The sharp rock erodes, grain by grain, fed by the acidic secretions of the lichen crust and the freeze-thaw of condensation in the cracks. Weather fronts form over the range and march across it, depositing snow on the peaks that melts into the Colossi's waiting maw. Predator and prey cycle through the vertical ecosystems. The bioluminescence pulses in circadian rhythms, dimming and brightening in waves across the dark landscape.

And there is something **unusual** here, a wrongness that is subtle but persistent. The Colossi are not quite plants as you understand them. They are *slowly motile*—their root systems, or holdfasts, can shift over the course of years, dragging the massive structure across the rock by millimeters, seeking better condensation or mineral seeps. They communicate, perhaps, through chemical signals passed through the fungal networks in the crust, or through the resonant frequencies of their wind-music, coordinating their growth so that they never crowd one another. They are ancient, these structures, older than the sharp mountains they stand upon, suggesting they grew elsewhere and migrated here, or that the mountains rose up around their already-present bases.

To stand in the empty valley between two Colossi is to feel the paradox of this place: the **totality** of life expressed through **absence**. You are surrounded by the architecture of giants, yet you are utterly alone in a void of sharp stone and screaming wind, lit only by the dim, green glow filtering down from the hollow heavens above. It is a landscape of vertical solitude, where the ecosystem is a series of isolated worlds, each spire a continent of living hollowness, separated by oceans of dark, empty air.
