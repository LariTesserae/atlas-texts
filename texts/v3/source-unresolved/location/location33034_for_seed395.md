The horizon curves. Not dramatically—just enough to remind you that you are standing on something larger than a landscape, something approaching the size of a sea that has forgotten how to be liquid. The ground is flat, low, a continental basin that has only recently stopped being a lake. That was long enough ago that the water is gone—evaporated, sublimated, locked into the crystal lattice of the stone—but recent enough that the crust is still settling, still remembering the shape of the waves that once lapped here.

This is the **Emergent Pan**, and it is young. The surface is a mosaic of hexagonal plates, white and ochre and the color of old bone, each one a fraction of a meter across, separated by hairline cracks that hiss with dry heat when the sun passes overhead. The plates are not fully formed. They shift. At night, when the temperature drops just below the threshold where moisture might condense, the crust contracts and the polygons grind against one another with a sound like distant teeth clicking, a geological percussion that is the only music the empty places make.

But there are no empty places. Not anymore.

Rising from the cracks, from the plates, from the very salt of the earth, is the **Bloom**. It is vegetation, if you can call it that—lush, saturated, extravagant in the way that only life can be when it has found a way to drink without water. The Bloom does not use H₂O. It uses the mist.

The atmosphere is thick, warm, a permanent soup of suspended particulates and heavy gases that never quite precipitates. It is not rain; it is not fog in any familiar sense, but rather a colloidal haze—mineral dust and organic aerosols and the metabolic exhaust of a billion billion organisms, all held in suspension by the heat and the density of the air. It clings to everything. It fills the lungs, coats the tongue, settles in the crevices of the salt crust like a damp velvet.

The Bloom drinks this. It is composed of **siphon-crystals**, towering structures that resemble termite mounds crossed with coral and sequoia. They have grown into a city without builders, a metropolis of biology. Streets exist here, but they are not paved; they are simply the valleys between the towers where the herds walk. The architecture is layered, organic, constantly accreting. Lower levels are mineralized, fossilized, dead; the upper levels are soft, pulsing, translucent with the sap that is not water but a viscous, amber fluid—silicone-based, perhaps, or sulfur-rich, carrying nutrients from the deep crust up to the photosynthetic surfaces that face the hazy sun.

This is a world under construction, packed with life that has found a way to thrive in the absence of the liquid that usually defines thriving. It is a city that is also a forest, a desert that is also a jungle, a young earth that is already old with the weight of its own abundance. It is warm, and vast, and utterly, densely full.
