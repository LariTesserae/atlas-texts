You stand on a surface that gravity seems to have forgotten: a plateau suspended at an altitude where the air should be thin and biting, yet here it hangs thick and warm, pressing against your skin with the weight of a held breath. This is the **Soporic Table**, or the **Flattening**—names given by no one, for there are no tongues here to name things, only the slow grind of stone against stone across epochs that make mountains seem like children.

The ground is not earth. It is **stone worn to satin**. Erosion has achieved its final, stable form here—billions of years of patient abrasion have sanded the bedrock into a vast, near-level plain that stretches in every direction until the horizon dissolves into a shimmering haze. The rock is warm, heated from below by the world's slow pulse or perhaps merely retaining the memory of ancient suns. Your palm pressed against it feels the temperature of a fever that never breaks. The surface is broken only by shallow depressions—natural bowls, no deeper than a saucer—where water collects. Not from rain; rain here is a myth, a once-per-millennium weeping that the stone has forgotten how to receive. Instead, the water comes from **condensation**. At dawn, the plateau sweats. Millions of droplets bead across the smooth expanse, gathering in the shallows, reflecting a normal sky—blue, with ordinary clouds—that seems obscene in its ordinariness at this impossible height.

Vegetation is a **rumor, a hesitation**. It exists only in the cracks, in the hairline fractures where dust has accumulated over geological time. There, a crust of something green-gray spreads in fractal patterns too regular to be chance—lichen that has forgotten photosynthesis, or fungus dreaming of being moss. It covers patches no larger than a dinner plate, separated by vast distances of bare, gleaming stone. Between these islands of sparse life, the rock is naked, smooth, and warm, occasionally streaked with mineral deposits that look like rust but move, very slowly, like bruises healing across the skin of the world.

But the **density** is here, and it is overwhelming.

Life carpets the Table in **layers**, a saturation that makes the eyes water. Closest to the stone: a second skin of small things, silent and sessile, no larger than your fist, packed so tightly they form a continuous membrane of flesh and chitin. They do not move, or move only when the century turns. Above them, a middle stratum of grazers—creatures the size of boulders, translucent and radially symmetric, possessing too many limbs arranged in patterns that hurt to follow, drifting across the plateau in herds so slow they resemble geological features. They filter the thick air through gills that flutter once per hour. And rising above them all, the **great ones**: sessile titans, transparent as warm glass, filled with slow-moving amber fluid that pulses with a rhythm older than thought. Each is the size of a house, or a cathedral, rooted to the stone by calcified threads that have grown for millions of years. They stand shoulder-to-shoulder across the plain, an endless forest of living crystal, their surfaces covered in a tracery of geometric growths—**calcium lace**, the architecture of their slow metabolism.

These growths are the **scattered built** things. Not constructed by hands—there are no hands, no tools, no technology, only biology and time—but grown. The great ones secrete crystalline structures around themselves: scaffolding that resembles the ribs of Gothic vaults, hollow towers that look like ruined keeps, spiral formations that suggest staircases leading nowhere. They are faint, partial, half-weathered into shapes that mimic intention. You will find a doorway here, a wall there, the suggestion of a room, all of it secreted by a creature that has been growing since before the first true mountain rose. Some of these structures have cracked and fallen, becoming cairns of white stone; others are still alive, still being added to, millimeter by millimeter, crystal growing on crystal in a silence so complete it has weight.

The creatures are **strange** in ways that accumulate like sediment. They possess eyes—dozens of them, arranged in spirals along their flanks—that see in spectrums that render the normal light dull, gray, insufficient. Their bodies are internally lit, glowing with a bioluminescence that shifts color over the course of decades, from amber to violet to a deep, throbbing red. They communicate, if it can be called that, through vibrations in the stone—pulses too low for ears, felt in the teeth, the sternum, the marrow. When one of the great ones shifts its weight, the entire plateau hums for hours, a subsonic thrumming that you feel in your feet as much as hear.

And yet the place is **mostly quiet**. The thick, warm air muffles everything. There are no birds, no insects screaming, no wind that howls. The wind here moves in slow currents, a patient breathing that whispers against the smooth stone—a sound like distant, underwater breathing. The only other noise is the **grinding**. As the plateau settles, as the great creatures grow heavier, their bases scrape against the bedrock. Once a day, perhaps, you will hear it: a sound like a glacier calving, or a mountain being born, a deep *shush* that travels through the stone for miles, felt before heard.

Water is a **presence, not a force**. It exists in pockets, in the condensation that gathers beneath the overhanging bodies of the great ones, dripping down in slow, patient drops to feed the sparse crusts below. There are no rivers, no waterfalls—only these gathered pools, stagnant and warm, hosting their own ecosystems of microscopic life, strange and slow, turning in spirals that take a week to complete. The dampness is a skin-level sensation, a perpetual humidity that keeps the stone slick, that allows the ancient erosion to continue its work, wearing the plateau down by a fraction of a millimeter each century, a pace so slow it is indistinguishable from stillness.

This is a world at the **end of its becoming**. The erosion has settled into its final form; the plateau is as flat as it will ever be, the life as dense as it will ever grow. Nothing rushes. The fauna, overwhelming in their layered saturation, exist in a state of suspended animation, growing, reproducing, dying, and being replaced on timescales that make the stone seem young. The dynamic is geological. The temperature remains warm, constant, a breath held since the beginning. The light is normal, ordinary, falling from a sky that has watched this place flatten itself across deep time.

You walk across the Table, and your footsteps make no sound, absorbed by the thick air and the living carpet beneath—the small things parting around your boots like water, closing behind you. The great ones do not notice you. They are too busy being ancient, too occupied with their slow photosynthesis, their patient filtering of the warm damp air, their secretion of crystalline architecture. You are a brief disturbance, a warmth passing over their translucent surfaces, and then you are gone, leaving only a damp footprint that will evaporate by noon, joining the condensation that feeds this endless, quiet, overwhelming plain where the world has finished changing and begun, instead, to simply *be*.
