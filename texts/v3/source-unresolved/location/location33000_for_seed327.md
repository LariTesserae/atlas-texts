You arrive at the edge of a plateau that ends not in a cliff but in a *decision*—the ground simply stops being ground and becomes air, a vertical drop of pale limestone that descends into a cylindrical throat. The feature is small enough to circle in an hour, bounded and self-contained, yet it sits at an elevation where the air tastes of metal and distance. This is a sinkhole, but not the jagged, collapsed kind. It is a drill-hole, a perfect cylinder of weathered limestone plunging thirty meters into the mountain, its walls smoothed by ten thousand years of water (erosion established, settled into its own slow geometry). The rock is bleached bone-white, streaked with grey lichen and the rust of mineral seepage.

The descent is silent until you are inside.

Then the sound begins. It is not quiet here; it is *loud*, a saturated, three-dimensional roar of moisture and life. The cylinder is a resonating chamber. From every pore of the stone, water weeps. Not dripping—*weeping*, a constant, fine percussion that builds into a roar where the walls curve. The air is at the edge of saturation, cool and heavy (temperature 1.2, water 1.5), carrying the green-sharp scent of wet chlorophyll and the mineral tang of limestone. Your skin prickles with condensation. The light that filters down from the circular sky above is diffuse, normal, but strange—it seems to pool in the lower half of the shaft.

There, the air changes. Below the twenty-meter mark, the cylinder fills with mist. Not a thin fog, but a dense, pearlescent cloud that behaves like a liquid, filling the bottom third of the void with a flat, trembling surface that rises and falls with the breath of the weather. This is the hollow: density zero, a near-void of suspended water droplets where the space between presences is vast. The mist is not empty, however; it is *full* of motion. It churns in slow eddies, driven by thermal currents, flowing up one wall and down the other in a perpetual, silent river. When you look down into it from the rim, the surface appears solid, like milk frozen in time, but it is only vapor held in suspension by the cool, pressurized air.

The walls themselves are gardens. Vegetation here is lush but vertical, a living tapestry clinging to the stone. Thick carpets of moss in impossible greens—emerald, jade, black-green—cloak every ledge. Ferns unfurl from cracks, their fronds dripping with condensation. Orchids and bromeliads anchor themselves in the rock, their roots drinking from the constant seep. Vines hang down like wet ropes, their tips dipping into the mist below, drinking from the cloud itself. The growth is ancient, established, in balance with the stone—neither conquering nor retreating, but negotiating.

Scattered across this vertical forest are the marks of hands. Built structures, primitive and scattered: wooden platforms lashed to the walls with fiber ropes, precarious huts perched on narrow ledges, stone cairns balanced on the rim. They are not many—perhaps a dozen in the whole circumference—and they look weathered, as if they have been there since before memory. The technology is stone and wood and cordage, simple fabrication. A stone knife rests on a ledge. A fire-pit, blackened with old ash, sits in a dry alcove. The structures do not dominate the space; they are incidental, small artifacts in the vast hollow, like furniture in an empty cathedral.

But the place is not empty of life. Fauna here is abundant, a teeming, loud ecology that fills the void with motion. Tree frogs—hundreds of them—cling to the moss, their throats pulsing as they call in a deafening, rhythmic chorus that echoes and multiplies off the stone walls (sound 2.5, fauna 2.1). Birds nest in the ferns, darting out to catch insects that swarm in the mist. Bats cling in the upper reaches, near the sky, their wings stirring the air. The mist itself seems to shiver with microscopic life, and on the wet stone, newts and salamanders move with glacial speed, hunting. The ecosystem is in constant, active flux—predator and prey, the drip and the drink, the breath of the cloud and the thirst of the green things (dynamic 2.5).

And there is the wrongness. The cylinder is too perfect. Karst sinkholes are usually jagged, collapsed, chaotic. This one is a geometric certainty, a plumb line dropped through the mountain. The mist surface, when the air is still, reflects the sky above with mirror-perfect clarity, creating the illusion of a second sky trapped below. But it is not still. The mist breathes. With a period of several minutes, the level of the cloud rises and falls, inhaling and exhaling, as if the mountain itself is drawing breath through this single nostril. When the mist is high, it laps against the lowest platforms, wetting the wood, and the frogs fall silent. When it is low, the stone floor is revealed—a jumble of boulders and shallow pools where the water collects before seeping back into the rock—and the roar of the dripping becomes a thunder.

This is a place of boundaries. The scale is intimate, a single room of the world, yet within that room, the density of being is almost nothing—just air, water vapor, and the occasional solid thing. It is a cathedral of condensation, a high-altitude lung, where the primitive shelters of the few who live here are scattered like seeds across the vast, mist-filled hollow. The weather inside is its own climate: it rains upward in places, as condensation forms on the upper walls and climbs, defying gravity in thin rivulets that trace the stone. The light is normal, but the shadows behave strangely, stretching long and liquid across the mist surface.

You stand on the rim. The wind is loud up here, screaming across the plateau. But inside, below, there is a different sound—a wet, organic cacophony of growth and dripping and calling, the noise of a world that is almost entirely water and green life suspended in a hollow, small, extreme, and quietly, deeply strange.
