You arrive at the edge of a world that breathes steam. The air is thin—*elevation* extreme, the atmosphere rarefied at this altitude, pressing lightly against your eardrums—yet it is saturated, heavy with moisture that refuses to condense into rain. Instead, it hangs in a perpetual, shimmering haze, a superheated mist that blurs the horizon into a watercolor wash of jade and ochre. The *temperature* is absolute: not merely tropical, but volcanic, a dry heat that radiates from the stone and a wet heat that rises from the valleys below, combining into an atmosphere that feels alive, almost viscous, as if you are wading through warm broth.

This is a landscape of **established erosion**, ancient beyond reckoning. The terrain is not jagged or young; it is weathered to a state of profound balance, the mountains rounded into vast, terraced domes that have sat in their own gravity for eons. The rock is **weeping**—not with sadness, but with condensation. Mineral-rich water seeps from every pore, drawn upward from deep aquifers by capillary forces amplified by physics that no longer obey simple thermodynamics. The stone sweats; droplets bead on the surface, gather, and trickle down channels that have been worn smooth over millions of years, yet the erosion never progresses beyond a certain elegant equilibrium. The water is near the surface everywhere—pools simmer in shallow basins, rivulets braid through the vegetation, and the ground itself is a sponge of mud and peat, black and gleaming.

**Vegetation** here is lush to the point of saturation. It carpets the terraces in dense, near-touching layers: a stratified jungle of slow-growing ferns with fronds like translucent lace, mosses that have accumulated into spongy hillocks meters thick, and trees with bark that drinks the ambient humidity and channels it down to root systems tunneling into the warm stone. The density is high—*1.5 on the scale of presences*—the air itself thick with floating spores, with the cotton-down seeds of epiphytic flowers, with clouds of steam-midges that darken the light. Yet despite this verdant abundance, the **dynamic** is slow, patient, almost glacial. A fallen log lies for decades before it softens; a landslide moves at the rate of fingernail growth; the seasons turn, but the landscape changes only in increments too small to perceive. This is a place caught in stasis, a moment of geological time held in amber.

Scattered across this verdancy—*built* at a level of faint marks and partial remains—are the artifacts of a **technology beyond modern understanding**. They are not cities, but monoliths: crystalline spires and half-buried arches that rise from the moss like the vertebrae of some great beast. They are weathered, their surfaces etched with patterns that shift when viewed peripherally, suggesting nanoscale machinery fused with the stone. These structures are the architects of the world's rules. They generate the heat, channeling geothermal energy from the mantle to maintain this impossible high-altitude sauna. They create the **weirdness**: localized gravity anomalies where water flows uphill in defiance of topography, temporal dilation fields where a minute inside the arch lasts an hour outside, and condensation engines that pull moisture from the thin upper atmosphere and weep it onto the land below. They are dormant now, or perhaps merely dreaming, their purpose long fulfilled, leaving the landscape in its settled, slow-turning state.

**Fauna** is abundant, filling the steam with motion and **sound**. The noise is constant and loud—*1.5 on the scale of volume*—a white roar of hissing vents, bubbling mud pots, and the membranous drone of vast insects whose wings beat at frequencies that make your teeth ache. Reptilian things, armored and sluggish, bask on the hot stones, their metabolisms slowed to match the world's tempo. Birds with wingspans like sails ride the thermal updrafts rising from the boiling pools, crying out in calls that echo strangely in the dense, humid air. The ecosystem is a high-saturation tangle of predator and prey, yet nothing rushes. Even the hunt is a slow ballet.

The **light** is normal, filtered through the perpetual steam into a diffuse, golden noon that never quite sets or rises. The **scale** is open, regional—you can see across the valley to the next plateau, three kilometers away, its silhouette blue and hazy, each terrace a step down into hotter, denser wetness, each hosting a slightly different ecology of moss and fern and steam-vent. It is a multi-feature landscape, a watershed of the sky, where the extreme elevation creates a thin, bright sky above while the valleys below are choked with tropical humidity.

Here, the rules of the world are written in steam and stone: heat does not rise, it circulates; water seeks not the lowest point, but the point of least temporal resistance; decay is a negotiation, not a law; and time itself moves slower, allowing the lush, wet, hot world to exist in a state of **established** grace, eroding not toward ruin, but toward a perfect, patient, eternal present.
