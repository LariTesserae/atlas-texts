You stand at the bottom of a wound in the earth.

The sky is not above you, exactly. It is a pale, rectangular bruise of light high overhead, framed by a rim of broken stone where the surface world still exists—grass and soil and the ordinary horizontal plane. You are in the Hollow, a place that did not exist a generation ago, or perhaps has existed forever but was only recently *opened*. The walls around you rise in jagged, unweathered limestone, their faces raw and pale as bone, scored with the chisel-marks of the first masons who came when the roof of the world cracked and let the light in. There is no soil here yet. Erosion has not begun to soften the geometry of the collapse; every edge is sharp, every surface dusted with the flour of freshly broken rock. The stone remembers being whole, and it resents the intrusion.

It is cool. The temperature holds at that particular chill of deep places, 1.4 on whatever scale measures earth’s memory—cool enough that your breath clouds, cool enough that the stone seems to sweat. Water is everywhere, but never flooding. It arrives as condensation, beading on the north-facing walls in constellations of droplets that tremble when the wind moves through the upper fissures. It arrives as thin rivulets tracing the tool-marks, carrying pale silt into the lower levels where it pools in shallow, still ponds that reflect the sky-bruised light like fragments of heaven. The air tastes of wet mineral and old dust, metallic and clean.

The settlement clings to the vertical. It is packed—saturated, really—with presence. Wooden scaffolding and walkways lace the walls at seven, perhaps eight, distinct levels, connected by ladders and inclined ramps carved directly into the stone. The structures are primitive but dense: timber frames lashed with fiber, roofs of slate shingle or woven reed, packed so closely that one dwelling’s shadow falls upon its neighbor’s door. There is no room between presences. You can reach out from your window—if you can call a gap between two timbers a window—and touch the wall of the house opposite. The architecture is under construction, or perhaps eternally so; new platforms extend outward from the cliff faces each season, and the sound of stone-on-stone scraping, muffled by distance, is the only percussion in an otherwise silent world.

Light filters down in a diffuse, grey-green glow. It is normal light, neither the harsh glare of the open surface nor the absolute dark of deep caves, but something in between—enough to read by, to work by, to distinguish the pale ochre of unweathered rock from the darker stains where water has begun, ever so slightly, to wear its first grooves. The vegetation is sparse and stubborn. Moss colonizes the dampest crevices in thin, electric-green patches. Lichen crusts the upper walls in maps of silver and rust. In the talus slopes at the base, where rubble has accumulated, a few hardy shrubs—willow and scrub alder—have taken root, their roots finding purchase in the cracks, their leaves small and waxy against the cool, moist air. There are no trees. There is no soil deep enough to hold them.

Fauna is rare. You might see a cave cricket, pale and blind, hopping across the stone. In the deepest pools, fish—if you can call them that—move in pale, slow circuits, eyeless and translucent. Bats roost in the highest fissures, near the rim, and at dusk they emerge in a silent, fluttering cloud to hunt above, outside your inverted world. But the Hollow belongs to the builders. The density here is human, or humanoid—packed tight, layered vertically, a town compressed into the geometry of a collapse.

The world is active, though it is quiet. Water drips—a steady, meditative percussion that never ceases, each drop striking stone or pool with a sound like a tiny bell. The mist cycles with the temperature: in the cool mornings, it gathers in the lower levels, obscuring the base of the walls, making the settlement seem to float in a grey void; by afternoon, the warmth from above draws it upward, and the air clears, revealing the full vertical extent of the place. The stone is settling. Occasionally, with a sound like a sigh, a flake of rock detaches from a high wall and falls, not crashing, but whispering down through the levels, to be caught by a net or a ledge, or to vanish into the rubble below. The city is growing. New chambers are being hollowed out behind the facades, expanding the habitable space into the raw geology, carving rooms where there was only stone a year ago.

There is infrastructure here—organized, if primitive. Channels carved into the rock divert the seepage away from the main walkways. Rope bridges span the wider gaps between levels. Platforms are reinforced with timber cribbing, filled with rubble, creating new flat ground where none existed. It is a town-scale organism, layered and complex, built with hand tools and simple fabrication—bronze chisels, stone hammers, wooden levers. No iron, or only the earliest, precious iron, worked by specialists whose forges glow in the deepest, warmest chambers, sending up thin threads of smoke that find their way to the surface through natural chimneys in the rock.

The silence is profound. There is no wind in the traditional sense—only the breath of air moving through fissures, whistling softly, a 0.4 on the scale of sound, a hush broken only by water and the creak of timber settling under weight. Voices are muffled. The packed density of the place absorbs sound; conversations happen in low tones, and the overall effect is one of suspended animation, as if the entire settlement is holding its breath.

Yet it is alive. The dynamic processes are geological and human intertwined. The water is beginning its slow work, the first micro-erosion of the raw stone, cutting channels that will, in centuries, become caves. The builders are extending their reach, following veins of softer rock, expanding the Hollow’s capacity. The mist breathes in and out with the daily cycle. A child drops a stone from a high window; it falls for three seconds before striking water, the sound rippling up through the levels like a question.

This is a place of emergence. Below the surface of the world, yet open to the sky. Packed with life, yet surrounded by the raw, unformed stone of recent creation. Cool, damp, quiet, and active—a city being born in the moment the earth split open, building itself upward toward the light it has only just learned to catch.
