The pocket sits high in the basalt spine of the range, where the rock is still young enough to remember its own birth. You enter through a fissure no wider than a shoulder, and the dark closes behind you like a throat swallowing. This is not a cave in the limestone sense—there is no water here, no dripping, no echo of moisture. The walls are raw volcanic glass, black and sharp, fractured into geometries that have not yet been softened by wind or time. The air is thick with mineral dust that never settles, hanging in the dimness like ash suspended in honey.

Heat presses against you immediately. It is not the wet heat of a jungle but a dry, dense warmth, rising from the depths in slow, patient pulses. The temperature sits at the edge of endurance, shimmering the air into visible waves. There is no light source except the faint, dull red glow of cooling magma somewhere below the floor, and the bioluminescent flickers of the inhabitants.

They are everywhere. Packed.

The chamber is perhaps the size of a large room, but it is saturated with life—creatures that have no need for plants, for water, or for the photosynthetic economy that governs most worlds. They are arthropod in their articulation, segmented and jointed, but their bodies are plated with mineral deposits they have harvested from the rock itself. They move with the slow, grinding deliberation of heavy machinery. Each one is a factory unto itself: grinding mandibles processing ore, internal furnaces converting sulfur compounds into the energy that drives their segmented legs, excreting waste that is more refined than the stone they consumed.

The sound is constant. A low, industrial roar that never ceases—millions of tiny grinding plates rasping against volcanic substrate, the hiss of superheated gas venting through their respiratory slits, the occasional heavy *clank* as one of the larger specimens shifts its bulk against the glassy walls. It is loud enough to feel in the teeth, a vibration that travels up through the soles of your boots and settles in the sternum.

They are packed so tightly that there is no empty space. They carpet the floor in a writhing, clicking mat, climb the walls in vertical columns, hang from the ceiling in clusters that resemble stalactites made of chitin and ore. When one moves, it pushes against its neighbors, creating a slow, dense wave that propagates through the mass. The movement is glacial—dynamic processes here operate on a timescale of hours and days, not seconds. A leg extends. A mandible opens and closes. The colony shifts its weight, and the whole chamber groans as tons of biomass adjusts to a new thermal gradient.

This is the industry. Not built, but grown—an emergent technology of biology and geology intertwined. The creatures are smelters, refiners, engines. They take the raw, emerging rock—the sharp, unweathered glass and pumice that constitutes this high, young landscape—and process it. They strip minerals from the stone, concentrate them in their bodies, excrete them as pure deposits that line the walls in shining veins. They are the mechanization of this place, the electrified, scaled production that hums in the dark.

The elevation is palpable. The air is thin despite the heat, pressing less against your skin, making the loud sounds seem somehow muffled and immediate at once. You are high up, near the spine of the world, in a pocket that is still forming—erosion has not yet rounded the edges of the glass, has not sorted the scree into gentle slopes. Everything is angular, new, raw.

There is no water to drink, no plant to eat. The ecosystem runs on chemistry and heat, on the slow, patient conversion of stone into motion and more stone. The creatures are abundant, saturated into every cubic centimeter of available space, a dense, living industry packed into a hidden, high, hot pocket of the world. They do not look up when you enter. They simply continue their work—grinding, smelting, moving slowly through the dark, loud and packed and endless.
