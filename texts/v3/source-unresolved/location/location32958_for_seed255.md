The valley breathes moisture. It is a low place, a bowl of cool air where the sky presses down like a damp cloth, and everything within it is either being built or being unbuilt by water. Light here is not a condition but a scarcity—diffused, gray, filtered through perpetual mist that clings to every surface. You cannot see the horizon; the world ends in a curtain of condensation three hundred paces away, where the terraced walls of the Works rise to swallow the sky.

This is a place of total construction. There is no earth. The ground is a mosaic of hand-laid flagstones, each one fitted by mallet and chisel into a substrate that has been entirely erased. The valley walls are being sheathed in a skin of masonry—some sections raw and bleeding geology, shattered by bone picks and stone hammers, others already sealed in fired clay and timber, weeping with moisture. The architecture is dense, a three-dimensional lattice of chambers and buttresses that leaves no room for soil or sky. It is emerging: half the valley is scaffolding and rubble, the other half finished and already slick with algae, the boundary between raw stone and worked surface advancing like a slow tide, one hand-stroke at a time.

Water is the architect here. With no vegetation to drink or transpire, the cool air saturates against the stone, and the structures are designed—whether by intention or accident—to harvest this. Condensation sheets down every vertical face, feeding into channels carved by obsidian flakes into the masonry. The channels braid and descend, loud with their own momentum, filling cisterns that glow with pale, fungal light. In the deepest chambers, the water pools black and still, inhabited by pale, eyeless things that drift like shed skin. Above, the aqueducts leak; the entire fabric of the place drips, a constant, whispering rain that never stops, soaking the workers who move through the tiers in cloaks of woven fiber and clay-coated hide.

The sound is immense. It is not the quiet of stone, but the roar of labor and hydraulics. Hammers strike in arrhythmic chorus—thousands of them, the primitive technology of the place expressed in the percussion of flint on granite. Water screams through narrow sluices, creating a white noise that drowns thought. The wind, funneled through the incomplete upper terraces, plays the wooden scaffolding like a vast, groaning instrument, producing low, mournful tones that resonate in the chest. Voices shout across the gaps, relayed from tier to tier by teams of carriers hauling blocks on wooden rollers and greased with animal fat. It is a landscape that is always being born, and the birth is loud.

Life is rare but present. In the condensation pools, colonies of pale, luminescent fungus—simple, non-photosynthetic, feeding on the mineral leachate of the stone—pulse with a soft blue-white glow, the only illumination in the lower levels. Hard-shelled insects skitter across the slick surfaces, drinking from the droplets. Once, perhaps, there were larger creatures here; now only a few remain—albino, blind, heavy-boned beasts kept in the deep cisterns, dragging themselves through the black water towing barges of unbaked clay, their presence known more by the smell of their wet hides and the grinding of stone against stone than by sight.

The strangeness lies in the geometry. The water channels do not simply drain; they spiral, they branch in fractal patterns that resemble neural tissue or dried river deltas, converging at nodes where the condensation forms into perfect, trembling domes that hum when the wind passes through. The structures are built at angles that make the eye water—staircases that appear to ascend but lead sideways into walls, chambers that seem larger inside than their exterior allows. The workers move with a purpose that seems ritualistic; they are building not shelter, but a machine for being wet, a vast, hand-carved engine for collecting and channeling moisture in a world that has no plants, no soil, only stone and the slow, grinding patience of pre-metal hands.

It is active, always. Stone is being removed from one place and added to another. Water is rising in capillary channels, falling in artificial cataracts, evaporating into the cool air, condensing again onto surfaces that have never known sun. The boundary between the raw, excavated rock and the finished, dripping wall advances daily, the dust of grinding stone mixing with the mist. It is a valley under construction by a civilization that has not yet learned metal, building a monument to dampness and sound in the dark, one wet, echoing layer at a time.
