You stand on a plain that has forgotten it was ever a mountain.

The **elevation** holds you at a height where the air tastes of metal and the sky is a pale, washed blue—**normal light**, but diffused, as if the sun shines through gauze. It is **cool** here, always cool, a temperature that seeps into the calcified husks of the great organisms that rise from the ground. The plateau stretches in all directions—**open**, **regional**—a vast, **ancient** expanse where **erosion** has done its final work. The stone is worn flat, near-flattened, a primordial pavement of gray-white mineral crust that predates memory. It is the end of geological life, settled into a final form: a tableland of bones.

But bones that breathe.

Rising from this flattened earth, spaced far apart—**sparse**, with kilometers of empty silence between them—are the **Arbortects**. They are the **vegetation**, and the vegetation is **total**: there is no grass, no lichen, no soil bacteria, no undergrowth. There is only the Arbortects and the machines that cling to them. Each one is a solitary megastructure, a plant-as-architecture that has grown to the scale of a city block, then a district, then a small metropolis. Their "bark" is not wood but a silica-laced, **fossilized** tissue—bone-white, porous, ancient-looking even as sap still moves through their vascular systems. They are cool to the touch, and they weep.

**Water** is scarce but present in its strangest form: **condensation**. The high altitude and cool temperature mean that moisture gathers on the vast surfaces of these organisms—beading on the calcified plates, dripping from the undersides of their fungal caps, pooling in the hollows where the **built** environment has taken root. It is a dampness that comes from the plants themselves, a transpiration in reverse, a mist that rolls across the plateau in slow, tidal waves, pooling in the low points between the Arbortects before evaporating back into the thin air.

These are not forests. They are **urban**—**built**—in the truest sense: **organized construction**, **infrastructure-density**, **layered** like sedimentary cities. Each Arbortect is a vertical settlement. Within the hollows of their massive fruiting bodies, **industrial** machinery has been grafted—**tech** at 1.9, **mechanized**, **electrified** in a way that seems anachronistic against the primordial stone. Steam engines chuff and hiss from within the woody hollows. Pneumatic tubes pulse along the grain of the calcified stems. Rail lines don't cut through the plateau; they follow the vascular channels of the plants, the tracks laid in grooves that the Arbortects grew specifically to accommodate them. It is a symbiosis of industry and biology, but the industry is old—rusted, green with verdigris, the metal fused into the plant-matter so thoroughly that you cannot tell where machine ends and organism begins.

The **sound** is **loud**—a constant, overwhelming **industrial-botanical** cacophony. The wind, funneling through the hollow chambers of the Arbortects, produces a deep, organ-pipe drone that never stops. The steam valves hiss. Gears grind—ancient, massive gears that turn with the slow inevitability of tectonic plates. And the plants themselves make noise: a grinding, **stone-on-stone** creak as they continue their impossibly slow expansion, fracturing their own fossilized bark, shedding plates of calcified tissue that fall with a sound like distant thunder. This **dynamic** world is **active**—weather cycles move across the plateau, mist banks roll in and out, the machines run their endless cycles, and the erosion continues its patient work, wearing the stone flatter even as the plants grow taller.

But there is **weirdness** here—**reality breakdown** at 2.6, a hairline fracture in the laws of physics. The Arbortects photosynthesize, yet their leaves—if they can be called leaves—are translucent, glass-like, and they glow with a faint bioluminescence that has nothing to do with the **normal light** of the sun. Shadows fall in directions that don't match the sun's position. Time moves differently near the great plants: watch a metal railing for a minute, and rust blooms across it like a fungus; wait an hour, and you can see the Arbortect itself shift, its branches creeping upward with visible, jerking movements. The condensation doesn't fall—it sometimes rises, drifting upward against gravity, collecting on the undersides of overhangs. The plants are heavier than they should be, or the air is lighter; objects feel buoyant here, as if the high altitude has thinned reality itself.

**Fauna** is **rare**—almost absent. You might see, once in a long day, a bird—but it flies wrong, its wings beating against air that's too thin, or perhaps it's a mechanical bird, a clockwork thing with brass feathers that whirs past on the thermal currents. Sometimes, in the condensation pools, you glimpse small, pale things—insects, maybe, or the motile spores of the Arbortects themselves, drifting in the mist. But they are few. The ecosystem is **pure-plant**, a **plant-as-substrate** world where the only life is the slow, grinding, photosynthetic machinery of the great organisms and the industry that feeds upon them.

This is a place at the end of its life and the beginning of another. The **erosion** has flattened the world, the **vegetation** has colonized the flatness with structures that are simultaneously ruins and cities, and the **industry** hums within them, extracting the last moisture from the air, grinding the last ore from the stone, keeping the lights on in a world where the sun shines normally but the shadows lie. It is **damp** with the sweat of giants, **cool** with the breath of deep time, and **loud** with the sound of a world that refuses to die, grinding on in its fossilized, photosynthetic, industrial afterlife.
