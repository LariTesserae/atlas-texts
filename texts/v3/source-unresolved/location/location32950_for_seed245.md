The air is thick enough to drink, hot and saturated with itself, a soup of suspended droplets that catch the dim light and scatter it into a featureless, bruised amber. You are low—very low—standing in what feels like the bottom of a planetary bowl, though the horizon curves away in all directions with a gentleness that suggests you might be inside a vessel, or that the world is still deciding whether to be round or flat. The light never brightens; it pools in the hollows and evaporates from the high points, leaving everything soft-edged, uncertain, as if viewed through gauze soaked in honey.

The ground is not ground. It is *work*—raw, unfinished, the detritus of an immense and ongoing fabrication. There is no stone here that was not placed, no soil that was not mixed and tamped. Yet it does not look built in the way of walls or roads. Instead, the earth itself is the artifact: a continent-spanning ceramic, half-fired, still bearing the fingerprints of its makers. The surface is a pale, porous clay, the color of bone left in the sun, scored with the marks of simple tools—flint chisels, wooden paddles, bronze scrapers no more sophisticated than a child’s toys. But the scale is wrong. These primitive gestures have been applied to a landmass the size of a hemisphere. A single groove, scratched by a hand-tool, runs for miles, straight as a ruler, cutting through valleys it refuses to notice.

The clay is wet. Not muddy—*weeping*. The heat (you can feel it pressing against your skin, a wet weight) forces moisture from the porous constructed material, and the surface sweats. Water beads on the tool-marks, runs down the gentle slopes in silent rivulets, pools in the vast, shallow depressions between the raised forms. The atmosphere is so close to saturation that every exhalation adds to the mist; the low elevation means the moisture has nowhere to drain but sideways, across these artificial plains, gathering in lakes that have no shores, only gradually shallower depths where the clay becomes a slurry.

There is no life. No plants have colonized these surfaces—the clay is too new, too actively mineral, exuding salts that sterilize. No animals traverse the emptiness; the heat and the humidity would cook anything with blood, and besides, there is nothing to eat, nothing that grows. The ecology is a null set, a blank space waiting for the construction to finish before biology is permitted to begin.

Yet the world is not still. It is *turbulent*, though the turbulence is silent. The clay is cooling, contracting, settling. Because the construction is so vast—planetary—and because the material is still soft, still embryonic (the erosion here is not wearing down but *emerging*, the landscape coming into being like a photograph developing), the ground moves. Not with the violence of tectonics, but with the slow, viscous inevitability of glass thickening or honey spreading. Ridges rise overnight, imperceptibly, pushing up through the mist. Valleys deepen as the substrate slumps into itself. You can feel it if you lie on the surface: a subsonic thrum, a vibration of settling, like the groan of a glacier, but muffled by the thick air and the wet clay. The “built” structures—low, sprawling walls of tamped earth, causeways of fired brick that crumble at the touch—do not stand firm. They lean, they sag, they are slowly being reclaimed by the ground that made them, not through decay but through *revision*. The builders left, or died, or finished their shift, and the work continues without them, the clay remembering the shape of the tools and continuing to deform in their absence.

This is where the weirdness seeps in. Reality here is *provisional*. The geometry is tired. Straight lines, when you walk them, curve gently upward, or loop back on themselves with a softness that suggests the world has not yet decided on Euclidean rules. The water does not always flow downhill; sometimes it climbs the clay slopes, following invisible gradients of intention, pooling on the high points before evaporating into the dim sky. The light is inconsistent—brighter in the west, though the sun is not there; darker in the east, though that is where the heat comes from. The constructed surfaces are porous to more than water; they absorb sound, so that your footsteps make no noise, and your voice, if you shout, comes back wrong—delayed, or pitched down, or speaking words you did not say.

The density is sparse, a balance with emptiness. Between the constructed forms—between the low, eroding walls and the raised platforms and the great, shallow dishes that might be foundations for something larger—there are vast distances of bare, weeping clay. Miles of it. The silence is total except for the *drip* of condensation falling from the mist into the pools below, a percussion so regular it becomes a kind of heartbeat, and the low, resonant hum of the cooling mass, a frequency felt in the teeth rather than heard. The scale is such that weather has its own weather here—localized fogs that form and dissipate in minutes, pockets of stillness where the humidity is so thick you swim through it, and sudden, silent clearings where the heat radiates up from the clay in visible waves.

This is a world under construction by primitive hands, but the construction is not of buildings—it is of *place itself*. The builders, whoever they were, used simple metal and wood and muscle to shape a planet, working slowly, geologically, leaving the job half-done. And now the world is finishing itself, quietly, in the dark, wet and hot and dreaming its own geometry into being.
