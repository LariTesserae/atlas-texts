The world here is a high plateau worn down to its bones. At extreme elevation—perhaps four thousand meters above some distant sea, though the warmth defies the usual arithmetic of altitude—the land has reached the end of its geological youth. Erosion has worked for epochs, grinding mountains into rounded mesas and vast, shallow basins. The terrain is ancient, near-flattened in its macro-form but intricate in its micro-detail: wind-sculpted stone that resembles frozen waves, pocked with hollows and cupped depressions where moisture gathers, the rock surfaces smoothed to a satin sheen by centuries of grit and lichen.

But the stone is not the surface. The surface is the Mat.

Vegetation is total here. Every exposed face of rock, every crevice, every flat summit is encased in a living crust—a thick, spongy substrate of mosses, fungal networks, lichen colonies, and low mat-forming plants that have fused into a continuous, breathing skin. It is plant-as-architecture. You do not walk on rock; you walk on the Mat. It is several feet thick in places, a yielding, elastic carpet that covers the entire region, extending past the immediate horizon in an unbroken expanse of verdant, ochre, and grey-green. Yet despite this total coverage, the density of the place is near-void. The Mat is mostly air. Vast chambers, tunnels, and hollow pockets thread through its structure, creating a landscape of negative space. The volume between the towering, eroded mesas is filled not with solid matter but with this porous emptiness—cavernous hollows beneath your feet, silent and vast, broken only by the drip of condensation.

The air is warm and heavy, thick with humidity. Water exists here not as rivers but as atmosphere—intermittent, condensing. The elevation forces a perpetual cycle of mist: warm, moisture-laden air rises to meet the cooler stone of the high mesas, weeping fog that the Mat drinks greedily. The vegetation is perpetually damp, exhaling its own mist into the normal light that filters down from a vast, open sky. In the shallow basins between the mesas, pockets of standing water gather in the eroded hollows—dark, still pools that reflect the sky, the only liquid surfaces in a world of damp sponge and stone.

And the sound is deafening. It is not merely the wind, though the wind screams through the eroded stone arches and flutes through the ancient hollows in a constant, low moan. It is life. Fauna is overwhelming here. Not large beasts, but multitudes. Swarms of winged creatures rise in clouds that darken the sky. Herds of small, sure-footed grazers move across the Mat's surface in synchronized waves, their hooves or many legs creating a rolling thunder. Crawling things, burrowing things, things that live in the hollow spaces beneath the crust—they all contribute to a roar that never ceases, a buzzing, chirping, stridulating ocean of noise that fills the vast emptiness and makes the hollow spaces feel pressurized, alive with vibration. The open scale of the landscape—regional, valley-sized, extending far past any single view—allows this sound to travel for miles, a constant, overwhelming presence that drowns out thought.

Scattered across this expanse are the faint marks of primitive intelligence. Stone circles half-swallowed by the Mat, their boundaries blurred by centuries of growth. Cairns that have become cairns of lichen, indistinguishable from natural rock piles except for their deliberate geometry. The occasional shelter built from flat, fitted stones, now roofless, filled with the spongy growth, its walls weeping moisture. These structures are sparse, almost lost in the total vegetation, appearing as faint interruptions in the green-brown-gold surface. They were built by hands that used simple tools—knapped stone, bone, woven fiber. The people who made them, or whatever passed through, lived within the Mat, harvesting its moisture, perhaps tending the fungal gardens that form its heart.

The world is active, in constant motion. The Mat pulses. You can feel it rise and fall beneath your feet with a slow, respiratory rhythm, expanding outward at its margins, secreting acids that eat into the ancient stone. The mist rolls in and out in cycles, soaking the fauna, driving them to migrate in vast, deafening clouds from basin to basin, following the moisture. Predator and prey move through the hollow tunnels beneath the surface, through the empty, near-void spaces that make up most of the physical volume of this place. The ecosystem is a machine of moisture and sound, turning the damp air and the eroded stone into a living, roaring, hollowed-out world.

And it is slightly wrong. The Mat breathes. The water in the pockets glows with a faint, green bioluminescence when the light fades. The fauna moves as a single organism, a collective overwhelming presence that is simultaneously everywhere and nowhere, filling the empty spaces with impossible sound while the physical density remains that of fog and hollow air. This is the High Hollow: a warm, ancient plateau at the top of the world, where the ground is alive, the sky is vast and empty, and the spaces between things are cavernous, resonant, and waiting.
