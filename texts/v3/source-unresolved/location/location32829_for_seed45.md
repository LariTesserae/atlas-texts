You stand on a plateau of newborn stone, where the world is still learning how to be solid.

The elevation is extreme—these are the peaks of a crust that has not finished buckling, jagged ridges thrust upward by a mantle that burns too hot for its own good. The air is thin here, yet strangely warm, carrying the mineral tang of heated basalt and the faint, metallic scent of cooling obsidian. There is no moisture in this place; water, in any form, has never condensed at this altitude. The dryness is absolute, a vacuum of hydrogen and heavy gases that leaves your throat chalk-dry and your skin prickling with static.

Darkness pervades. Not the darkness of night, but of a world still swaddled in its own birth-veil—a perpetual twilight where the star above is a dim, ruddy ember, its light swallowed by thick veils of volcanic ash and the dust of a million fracturing crystals. Visibility extends only a few kilometers before the gloom swallows the horizon, yet the warmth radiates from the ground itself, a geothermal breath rising from vents that steam with dry heat.

The terrain is raw and embryonic. Erosion has barely begun to claim these rocks; they are sharp-edged, unweathered, born yesterday in geological terms. The ground is a mosaic of hexagonal basalt columns, still forming, still pushing upward through the crust like teeth through infant gums. But the landscape is alive with a violence that is not yet destructive—only *constructive*. The dynamic here is hyperactive, a storm of creation. Crystal spires grow at the speed of memory, thrusting up through fissures, their facets catching the dim light and throwing it back in prismatic shards that vanish as quickly as they appear. Whole valleys rise and subside in the span of a century; tectonic plates grind against each other in slow, grinding ballets, birthing new ridges while swallowing old ones.

Scattered across this vast, open expanse—sparse, like thoughts half-formed—are structures. Not built by hands, for there are no hands, no tools, no technology but the blind chemistry of stone. Yet they exist: perfect arches of volcanic glass spanning chasms, platforms of obsidian so smooth they resemble worked marble, monolithic pillars that stand in geometric perfection, their angles too precise for random geology. They are the scaffolding of the world's becoming—temporary architectures erected by the crystallization process itself, destined to collapse and reform elsewhere in the endless turbulence. They look like the ruins of a civilization that never was, or the blueprints for one that might be.

The silence is profound, broken only by the infrasonic groaning of the earth as it rearranges itself. Occasionally, a crystal fractures with a sound like a struck bell, the note hanging in the dry air before dissipating into the quiet. The ground shifts beneath your feet, not with the jolt of an earthquake, but with the slow, viscous flow of warm, plastic rock—stone behaving like warm honey, oozing and reshaping itself in a constant, silent storm.

There is no life here. No plants, no animals, no fungi threading through the mineral dark. Only the slow, patient chemistry of a world assembling itself, the weird geometry of emerging order—Penrose stairs of basalt that lead nowhere, Möbius strips of cooled lava, gravity that seems to lean sideways in certain hollows where the mass is still settling.

You are witnessing geology in its adolescence: too young to be eroded, too turbulent to be stable, too warm to be dead, too dark to be welcoming. It is a nursery of mountains, a landscape under construction, dreaming itself into permanence while the ground beneath you continues, relentlessly, to become.
