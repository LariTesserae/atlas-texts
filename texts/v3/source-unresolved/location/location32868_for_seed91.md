You descend into a valley that exists beneath the valley—a broad, subterranean chamber floored in permafrost and broken shale, its ceiling lost in fractured darkness overhead. The air is cold enough to hurt, a constant near-freezing damp that coats every surface in hoarfrost and condenses into slow, heavy drips. This is not a deep place; the rock above is thin, porous, young. You can see the blast scars on the walls—sharp, angular geometries where excavation stopped recently, leaving jagged teeth of limestone and granite that have not yet softened under time’s patient erosion.

The space is open, extending for kilometers in a rough crescent, a regional cavity that swallows sound and light alike. But it is sparse. Emptiness dominates. Between the presences—the machines, the structures, the living masses—there are vast, echoing gaps of bare rock and frost.

And then the plants.

They rise from the floor like architecture. Each one is immense—a single specimen spreading forty meters across, a towering column of pale, fleshy fronds or perhaps calcified fungal tissue that has been trained, over slow decades, to serve as load-bearing substrate. They are spaced widely: one here, another two hundred meters away, their canopies brushing the low ceiling in some places, leaving open sky-of-rock in others. Where they grow, they are total—the ground is theirs, a thick, spongy mat of mycelium and root-mass that has swallowed the original stone. Pipes and conduits run through their fibrous cores. Industrial platforms bolt into their sides. They are the infrastructure, the living scaffold upon which this place is built. Yet because they are sparse, the overall impression is one of scattered green-gray monoliths standing in a frozen, echoing void.

The machinery is industrial, heavy, deafening. Pumps the size of houses thrum with a bass frequency that you feel in your sternum more than hear. Conveyor belts, thick as roads, crawl overhead on gantries that shake the frost loose from the ceiling in constant, glittering avalanches. Ventilation shafts scream with forced air, creating a whiteout of ice crystals in the dimness. The sound is 2.8—absolute, crushing, a roar that has no silence in it, no rhythm, just the continuous grind of extraction and processing that has been running since the excavation began.

Light is scarce. Most of the illumination comes from harsh, sodium-vapor arcs hung from the plant-columns at irregular intervals—dim, yellow, casting long shadows that stretch across the frost. Between these, the plants themselves provide a faint, bioluminescent pallor, a sickly blue-green glow that makes the moisture in the air visible as drifting motes. Everything is wet. Condensation sheets down the rock walls, freezing into curtains of ice that grow, slowly, over months, encasing cables and machinery in glacial grip. Pools collect in the hollows between the plant-masses, dark and still, reflecting the dim lights, hosting the only fauna: pale, blind things that drift in the cold water, rare and unhurried, adapted to the slow dark.

Scattered through this are the built elements—small, utilitarian, dwarfed by the scale of the vegetation and the machinery. Control sheds made of corrugated metal, half-buried in frost. Broken equipment, rusted and ice-locked. A single rail line that disappears into a tunnel, its cars frozen in place, waiting. These are faint marks, partial remains of an industrial presence that does not fill the space but punctuates it, like punctuation in a long, empty sentence.

The dynamic here is slow. Despite the deafening present-tense roar of the machinery, nothing moves quickly. The plants grow at a glacial pace, their tissue expanding millimeter by millimeter, slowly enveloping the pipes that feed them nutrients. The ice accumulates in layers, year over year. The erosion of the young rock faces proceeds in geological time—sharp edges that will not round for centuries. The condensation drips, freezes, thaws, drips again. It is a place of patient processes, of gradual cycles, where the violence of the sound is matched only by the patience of the cold.

You walk on the spongy, frozen substrate of the plant-mats, your boots crunching on frost and fiber, the roar of the pumps making conversation impossible. The air tastes of metal and moisture and the faint, sweet rot of the vegetation. It is normal here—this is simply how this place is: a cold, wet, loud factory where the architecture is alive but growing slowly, where the machines scream but the world turns at the pace of ice.
