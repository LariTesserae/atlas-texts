You enter through a gap in a low, moss-fused wall and immediately the air changes—thick, warm, carrying the scent of wet iron and chlorophyll. This is a room carved from the world, a sunken courtyard no larger than a modest house, yet it feels vast because the ceiling is only a suggestion. Above you, a canopy of vegetation has knitted itself into a continuous membrane, so dense that normal daylight filters through in pale, watery coins of light. It is never truly dark here, but never bright; the illumination is diffused, green-tinged, humid.

The ground is not ground. You step onto a substrate that is entirely botanical—a mattress of interwoven root-mass, fungal hyphae, and succulent stems packed so tightly that no soil is visible. It is three feet thick, perhaps more, a spongy, breathing turf that oozes when you press your weight into it. Water sits everywhere: in shallow pools that reflect the green canopy above, in the hollows between hummocks of vegetation, and in the constant percussion of condensation dripping from leaf to leaf, a rain that never stops. The water table is at your ankles, always. Your boots sink slightly with each step, and the plant-mass sighs around them, releasing bubbles of trapped gas that smell of fermentation and copper.

Scattered through this verdant density are the bones of industry—not a factory, but its ghost. Three concrete pads rise from the turf like tombstones, weathered smooth and rounded at the edges, half-swallowed by the root-mat. One supports a single, massive engine: a diesel heart, perhaps, or a steam turbine, its flywheel turning with geological patience, one revolution every forty seconds. It is loud. The *whump-thump* of its cycle vibrates through the plant-substrate, a bass note you feel in your sternum, accompanied by the hiss of steam venting from a rusted pipe that snakes through the vegetation like a sleeping snake. The pipe is warm to the touch, and where the steam escapes, it does not dissipate but is immediately captured by the surrounding leaves, which seem to drink it, growing plump and glistening.

The plants themselves are strange. They are not merely growing *around* the machinery; they have incorporated it. Their stems contain visible strata of rust and concrete dust. Their leaves, broad and waxy, display patterns that resemble circuit boards—veins that carry not sap but a faint, bio-electric luminescence, pulsing with a soft blue light that synchronizes with the engine’s rhythm. They are a hybrid ecology, photosynthesizing the waste heat and stray voltage of the industrial remnants. When the wind moves through the canopy (or rather, when the engine’s vibration shudders through the root-network), the leaves chime together with a sound like wet metal or glass, a tinkling, discordant music that fills the small space.

Because the area is bounded—enclosed by the low wall and the overarching dome of leaves—the sound has nowhere to escape. It is saturated. The slow, heavy engine-beat. The constant *drip-drip-drip* of condensation from a thousand leaf-tips. The squelch of your movement in the saturated turf. The crackle of electricity arcing between leaf-veins during moments of high humidity. It is a loud place, but the loudness is muffled, underwater, as if the air itself is too thick to carry sound cleanly.

There are almost no animals. You might, if you wait for an hour, see a single creature: a pale, eyeless thing the size of a cat, segmented like a centipede, moving through the root-mass with a wet, dragging sound, feeding on the decaying metal. Or you might see nothing but the slow, peristaltic movement of the plants themselves, expanding and contracting as they process the steam and light. The fauna has been replaced by the machinery, or perhaps the machinery has become fauna—slow, warm-blooded in its own way, breathing steam.

The place is established, in balance. The erosion has reached a equilibrium: the concrete is softened, the metal is pitted but not gone, the plants have grown into every crack and are now holding the structures together as much as undermining them. Water evaporates from the pools, condenses on the leaf-canopy, drips back down, feeding the roots, which in turn stabilize the soil that holds the concrete pads. The engine runs on a fuel source you cannot identify—perhaps geothermal heat from deep below, perhaps the slow combustion of the peat-like plant-mass itself—and it will continue to turn, slowly, for decades more, a heart beating in a green, wet room.

The boundaries are clear. You can see the edge of the world here: the low wall, the ring of taller trees that form the perimeter, the dome of leaves overhead. It is a single feature, a hamlet-sized ecosystem, a cathedral of chlorophyll and rust. When you leave, the sound follows you for a moment, a phantom *whump-thump* in your ears, and the wetness clings to your skin, the strange blue glow of the circuit-leaves lingering behind your eyelids.
