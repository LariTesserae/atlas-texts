You stand at the edge of a high plateau where the air is thin enough to taste metallic but carries a warmth that surprises—eternal spring at three thousand meters. The land stretches open before you, a regional expanse of saturated earth and sky, the horizon distant and pale under a normal, diffuse light that casts no hard shadows. This is young country. The stone has not yet learned to be round. Sharp ridges of freshly uplifted rock jut through the moss like broken teeth, their surfaces weeping with condensation, water seeping from every fissure and collecting in glassy pools that never ripple. The ground is a marsh-edge turned absolute: peat soaked to its limit, water standing at the surface everywhere, the water table so near that a kicked stone sends up a cold spray.

Vegetation here is lush but low—tussock grasses and dense carpets of moss and sedge, bronze-green and emerald, thriving in the perpetual damp. There are no trees, only the suggestion of stunted shrubs clinging to the few dry hummocks, their leaves broad and dark, motionless.

Scattered across this wet expanse sits a town. It is urban in its organization—paths of woven reed and raised causeways of packed earth connect the structures, a central stone platform serving as a gathering place—but sparse in its density, the buildings spaced far apart like islands in a shallow sea. Each house is built of local materials: dry-stone walls without mortar, timber frames lashed with cord, roofs of slate or thick thatch. The technology is primitive, hand-wrought: stone tools, wooden pegs, simple fabrication. No metal gleams; iron is absent or scarce, worked only by hand if at all. The construction is layered and deliberate—foundations sunk deep into the peat, walls thick against the damp, but never dense, never crowded. You can see from one dwelling to the next across fifty meters of shining water and grass.

And yet there is no life in the living sense. No birds cross the sky. No insects rise from the grasses when you walk. The pools contain no fish, no frogs; the moss harbors no beetles, no spiders. The fauna parameter reads as absence—an ecosystem of plants and stone and human construction, but no animals, wild or domestic. The town exists in a silence so complete it has texture. There is no wind to stir the reeds. No current to lap against the stilts. No buzz, no croak, no wing-beat. Even the water falling from the weeping rocks makes no sound as it drips into the pools below; it simply arrives, a slow, silent accumulation.

Most strangely, nothing changes. The world here is time-invariant, frozen in equilibrium. The sharp ridges of young stone do not erode; they remain angular, fresh, newly born. The water level does not rise or fall with rain or season. The mist, if it is mist, does not lift or thicken. The light is constant—neither brightening toward noon nor dimming toward evening. The town stands in its current state, the thatch neither rotting nor renewed, the paths neither muddier nor drier. It is a place that has reached a perfect, static balance, where the forces of decay and growth have canceled each other out, where the young land has paused in its youth and the wet world has settled into a permanent, humid stillness.
