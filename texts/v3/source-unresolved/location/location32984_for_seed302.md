The light arrives first, unfiltered. At this elevation the atmosphere is a thin membrane, and the sun strikes the plateau with a clarity that bleeds color from the world. Shadows are knife-edged, black as basalt, and the brightness has a physical weight—it presses against the skin, bleaches the retinas, turns the sky a pale, washed-out blue that hurts to look at directly.

Then the sound. It is not merely wind; it is a *presence*, a continuous, deafening roar that fills the cranial cavities and vibrates in the sternum. The plateau is a stone forest—a dense, packed thicket of mineral spires, tufa towers, weathered limestone pinnacles rising from a shallow, mineral-saturated flood. The formations are jammed together with no room between, a petrified thicket where each column presses against its neighbors. Between them, the high-altitude gale—constant, invariant, blowing from the same quarter since before memory—is forced through gaps no wider than a hand. The result is a cacophony of shrieks and groans, a white-noise thunder that never modulates, never pauses, never changes. It is the sound of the world breathing through a constricted throat.

The ground is wet. Not marsh-wet with peat and decay, but mineral-wet. The water table sits at the surface, a shallow, cool bath of milky, calcium-heavy water that laps at the bases of the stone towers. The stone itself weeps. Condensation beads on the cool surfaces—chilled by altitude and shadow—and trickles down in countless rivulets, feeding back into the saturated basin. The air is thick with mist, a condensing atmosphere that clings to the stone, softening its edges even as the wind sharpens them. Droplets fall with a sound like distant, constant rain, though it never actually rains here; the moisture simply cycles—evaporating under the bright sun, condensing in the cool stone, dripping, collecting, evaporating again—in a closed loop of perfect equilibrium.

There are no plants. The stone is too alkaline, the water too mineralized, the altitude too severe for chlorophyll. Vegetation is absolute zero. Instead, the surfaces are coated in a thin, pale patina—perhaps bacterial colonies, perhaps fungal films, perhaps simply mineral efflorescence—that stains the white stone in streaks of ochre, rust, and gray. The ecology is mineral-first: the stone supports only the simplest life, or none at all. Where there is life, it is *fauna*, rare and hardy. A creature might cling to a high tower—a lizard-like thing with stone-colored scales, or an insect with wings like mica—waiting for the wind to bring prey. But they are scarce, scattered thin across the vastness like motes of dust. Mostly, the forest is silent of biology, save for the roar.

The erosion has established its forms. These towers are not young; they are ancient, weathered smooth in some places, pitted and honeycombed in others, carved by the same persistent wind and water that now maintain them in stasis. They are in-balance—shaped by forces that have reached a standstill with the stone. Nothing grows taller. Nothing crumbles to dust. The formations stand in their packed density, extending past the immediate horizon in every direction—a regional scale, a valley-sized expanse of petrified wood, though no tree ever grew here. The openness is vertiginous; from any high tower, one sees only more towers, a sea of stone breaking only at the distant rim of the plateau where the land drops away.

This is a world without construction, without tools, without the artifacts of minds. It is pure geology and meteorology, a pre-tool landscape. The weirdness is subtle but pervasive: the way the bright light makes the wet stone gleam like bone; the way the deafening sound seems to emanate from the stone itself, as if the towers are singing; the absence of green, of softness, of growth. It is a post-rain landscape that never dries, a marsh-edge of the mineral world, frozen in a moment of perfect, terrible equilibrium. Time here is not a river but a standing pool. The wind will blow tomorrow exactly as it blows today. The water will sit at exactly this level. The stone will not move. It is a landscape that has finished becoming itself, and now simply *is*—bright, wet, packed, and roaring.
