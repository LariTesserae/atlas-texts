The heat arrives first, not as a wave but as a pressure—thick, metallic, tasting of copper pennies left too long in the sun. You stand on a plateau of **fused slag and heavy-metal salts**, elevation high enough that the horizon curves with a visible, unsettling gentleness, yet the atmosphere clings dense and viscous, a soup of sulfur dioxide and vaporized minerals that should not exist at this altitude. The light is ordinary—painfully, cruelly ordinary—casting a white-gold glare across a landscape that should be frozen but instead simmers at 2.7, the thermal energy trapped by a sky that is not empty.

And the sky is not empty.

It is **overwhelming**.

Above the barren, salt-crusted flats—where erosion has had eons to grind the bedrock into geometries that resemble factory floors, cooling towers, and vast, rusted gears—drifts an ocean of life. The **fauna** are aerial, suspended in the superheated thermals, ranging from clouds of microscopic crystalline motes that shimmer like heat-haze made solid, to leviathans kilometers in length that blot out the sun. They are **silicate and iron**, their bodies lattices of glass and refined ore, translucent in places, opaque in others, housing within their thoraxes **combustion chambers** that burn not with water and oxygen but with sulfur and metallic vapors. They are engines given flesh, or flesh that has evolved into engine—**tech 2.4 made biological**, a biomechanical industry that requires no builders, no blueprints, only the slow, patient metabolism of stone and heat.

They are **loud**. The sound (2.2) is a constant, grinding roar that never ceases—the subsonic hum of a thousand thermal vents inside their hollow bones, the shriek of mineral dust being filtered through gill-like arrays, the groan of tectonic plates of flesh shifting against each other as the great beasts drift past one another at speeds measured in centimeters per century. It is the sound of industry without architects, of a factory that is also a creature, of manufacturing as a form of digestion.

The ground itself is **empty** (density 0.4)—vast, hollowed spaces between the mineral formations, a near-void of baked earth where nothing grows. Vegetation is absent (0.3), a concept as foreign here as snow; instead, the "growths" are **lithic accretions**, fungal-mineral hybrids that are technically fauna, or the shed exoskeletons of the aerial giants that have fallen and fossilized in place, creating a landscape that looks like the ruins of a machine city but is entirely **natural**, **ungrown by hands**, **built by nobody** (built 0.5). These fallen husks are weathered (erosion 2.4) into smooth, impossible shapes by the abrasive wind, which carries not sand but **industrial grit**—pulverized ore, glass shards, the byproducts of the creatures' own metabolism.

Water is a myth here (0.2). There are no rivers, no rain, no moisture in the air that could condense. The creatures carry their own sealed oceans—pockets of heavy water or supercritical fluid trapped in vacuoles within their crystalline bodies, recycled for millions of years, never spilled. You might find a single droplet preserved in a geode, a bubble of ice suspended in the heat by **weirdness** (2.9), a temporal anomaly where the world remembers when it was wet. The fauna are **anhydrous**, their chemistry based on molten salts and metallic solutions, their "blood" a slurry of dissolved copper and sulfuric acid that would boil your skin but sustains them.

And the weirdness is the binding agent, the physics-defying glue that holds this contradiction together. **Reality breaks down** around the leviathans. Look at one as it passes overhead, blocking the normal light with its vast, translucent wing-membranes, and you see it **simultaneously** as a juvenile, a fossil, and a cloud of spores. Time pools here (dynamic 0.9); the creatures move so slowly that they exist in geological time, yet their industrial processes—refining ore, crystallizing compounds, excreting pure metal that rains down as a scalding, glittering snow—happen in bursts of temporal acceleration, a mountain processed into a perfect gear in the span of a single afternoon, then left to cool for ten thousand years.

The scale (2.2) is regional but infinite-feeling—a valley, perhaps, or a high basin stretching to every horizon, filled with these drifting, grinding, singing machines. They are **overwhelming** (fauna 2.6) not because the air is thick with bodies—though it is—but because they dominate the sensorium entirely. The horizon is a wall of them. The sky is a cathedral of their passage. Even in the empty spaces between them, you feel their psychic weight, their ancient patience, their **industrial hunger**.

This is a world where the distinction between the living and the mechanical has dissolved, where industry is ecology, where the absence of water has bred a life more durable and strange than anything aqueous, and where the slow grinding of stone against stone is the only clock that matters.
