You stand on a continental shelf that has forgotten it was ever sea. This is the **Warm High Meridian**, a plateau raised to the thin-air edge of the world, yet swaddled in a dense, pressure-heavy atmosphere that traps heat like a blanket soaked in broth. At 1.6 elevation, the horizon curves away into a haze so thick you could drink it; at 2.6 scale, the landmass generates its own weather systems—slow, patient cyclones that drift across the megacity like drowsy gods. The air is warm (1.5), humid to the point of saturation, and moves with a viscous, soupy weight.

**Terrain and Material**
The ground is not soil but **lithic patina**—a crust of weathered volcanic glass and sedimentary stone, worn smooth over eons by the patient teeth of erosion (2.5). There is no green here. Vegetation registers at a near-absolute zero (0.3): no leaves, no chlorophyll, no photosynthesis. Instead, the stone breathes with a slower metabolism—vast, spreading mats of lithotrophic bacteria and mineral-fungus that stain the rock in bands of rust-orange, sulfur-yellow, and bruised slate. These are the "plants" of this world, chemosynthetic and ancient, feeding on geothermal vents and the slow dissolution of the bedrock itself. The stone weeps (1.7). Water is not merely present; it is the medium. Artesian springs punch up through the city’s foundations; condensation beads on every metal strut and ceramic tile; mist rolls in from the high passes in slow, tidal waves. The architecture is built from this same water-worn material—layered, terraced construction (2.3) that looks less like human engineering and more like geological strata that have learned to house machinery.

**The City**
This is an industrial megalopolis (1.9) of petrified grandeur. The infrastructure does not gleam; it **grinds**. Brass and iron and sintered ceramic rise in vertical layers, but everything is encrusted with mineral deposits—calcite, gypsum, the strange precipitates of hot water under pressure. The machines are massive, archaic, and deliberate (0.8 dynamic). They do not race; they **turn**. Gears the size of city blocks rotate with the inevitability of continental drift, powered by geothermal taps that vent steam into the humid air. The city is old. The erosion has rounded the original masonry into something organic, as if the buildings are slowly melting back into the plateau. Streets are canals, really—channels where warm, mineral-rich water flows constantly, carrying away the detritus of the fungal mats and the excrement of the teeming life. The density (1.5) is vertical and compressed; structures press against one another, near-touching, creating canyons of shadow and steam where the light struggles to penetrate.

**Ecology: The Overwhelming**
Without plants, the food web is inverted and strange. The base is the bacterial crust—thick, rubbery, tasting of iron and heat. Upon this, the fauna (2.7) have achieved a density that borders on the geological. They are **lithovorous grazers**: shelled, multi-legged creatures ranging from the size of a fist to the size of a locomotive, their carapaces plated with the same minerals they consume. They move in herds—slow, grinding processions that take days to cross a single district. The ground is never still; it is a carpet of scuttling, clicking, scraping life. Smaller swarms of arthropod analogs—wingless, eyeless, filtering the mist for spores and mineral dust—fill the air in clouds so thick they dim the normal daylight (2.3). They are overwhelming not because they are dangerous, but because they are **everywhere**. You cannot step without crushing one; you cannot breathe without inhaling their spores. They are the city’s immune system, its sanitation crew, its weather. They have no plants to eat, so they eat the rock, each other, and the waste of the machines, sustaining a biomass that would be impossible in a photosynthetic world.

**Atmosphere and Sound**
The air is a medium, not a void. It carries sound with a heavy, liquid quality (1.7). The noise is constant and layered: the **drip** of condensation from ten thousand surfaces, a percussion that never ceases; the **groan** of ancient machinery shifting in its foundations; the **roar** of steam vents releasing pressure from the depths; and beneath it all, the **hum** of the fauna—a bio-acoustic drone of billions of legs skittering over stone, of herds scraping their shells against the terraces, of the smaller creatures chirping and clicking in frequencies that make your teeth ache. It is loud, but the loudness is slow, a gradual crescendo that has been building for centuries. The light is normal in spectrum but diffused through the perpetual mist into a milky, shadowless glow that makes distances deceptive. At this elevation, the sky is a deep, bruised violet near the zenith, but the humidity scatters it into a uniform, warm amber at street level.

**Rules of the World**
Time here is measured in sediment, not seconds. The dynamic is glacial (0.8): the city does not grow; it **accretes**. New layers of mineral deposit slowly bury the old, while erosion carves new channels through the streets. The fauna and the industry exist in a slow symbiosis—the machines provide heat and mineral tailings; the creatures consume the waste and the bacterial overgrowth, preventing the city from being choked by its own lithic flora. Nothing is fast. A storm takes a week to cross a district. A building takes a century to weather into its final, settled form. The fauna do not migrate; they **drift**, carried by thermal currents and the slow turn of the massive gears that still pump water through the urban aqueducts.

You are high above the world, warm and wet and surrounded by a life that does not need the sun, in a city that is slowly becoming mountain again. The sound is a roar. The ground is alive. And the rain never stops falling, soft and mineral-tasting, on skin and stone and shell alike.
