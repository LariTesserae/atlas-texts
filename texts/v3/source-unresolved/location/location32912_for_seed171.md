You stand on a continent that is still being born.

The air is thin at this elevation—high enough that your lungs work twice as hard—but it is so saturated with moisture that every breath feels like drinking. The atmosphere condenses against your skin, against the crystalline leaves that press in from every side, against the black glass of the bedrock that juts through the vegetal mat in serrated, unweathered ridges. This is a place where geology is embryonic: the erosion here is a rounding that has not yet happened, the stone still sharp and volcanic, obsidian and basalt that have not known the patience of millennia of wearing. The plateau is rising, or perhaps it was always here and is only now becoming *real*, solidifying out of some pre-formational dream of rock and water.

The ground is not ground. It is a thickness.

You are walking on a substrate that is ninety percent water by volume, held in suspension within a lattice of engineered biomass. The vegetation is total—it is the architecture, the soil, the weather system, and the only technology that exists. There are no animals here; the fauna parameter sits at half-past-nothing because the plants have replaced every ecological niche. They are the predators, slow and patient. They are the prey, consumed by fungal networks that are also part of the same organism. They are the infrastructure: photosynthetic crystal-wood that conducts quantum information through mycelial highways spanning the continental shelf.

This is the tech—beyond modern, beyond built. Nothing is constructed. Everything is *grown*. The stalks that tower around you—some hollow, resonant chambers kilometers high, others dense as neutron-star matter—are not trees but computational organs. They process light into thought, and the thinking makes noise.

The sound is deafening.

It is not wind, though the wind contributes. The wind here is a continental weather system of its own, a cyclone that lives inside the wetland, and when it passes through the hollow reed-cathedrals of the high plateau, it plays them like instruments, a pipe organ scaled to the curvature of the planet. But beneath that, deeper and more constant, is the sound of metabolism: the roar of cells dividing across a landmass the size of a continent, the hydraulic hiss of water being pumped through capillary networks of xylem that are also fiber-optic cables, the subsonic thrum of a photosynthetic mind dreaming in frequencies that vibrate your teeth. The plants are thinking, and you are standing inside the reverberation of their cognition.

The light is dim—green and bruised-purple, filtered through seventeen layers of canopy that are also solar collectors, also antennae, also skin. The sun is a pale disc through the moisture, but the true illumination comes from within: bioluminescent veins pulsing in the root-mat below your feet, a slow arrhythmia of blue and amber that suggests the ground itself is nervous tissue. Everything glows faintly in the saturated dark.

Water is everywhere. It weeps from the undersides of leaves that are too heavy with condensation to hold their shape. It runs in sheets down the crystalline bark, not as streams but as *films*, as a continuous wetness that blurs the boundary between air and liquid. The humidity is so total that your clothes are always damp, always growing heavier, as if the atmosphere is trying to digest you. In the valleys between the sharp, young ridges of bedrock, the water pools—not lakes, but *sponges*, vast shallow seas held in the biomass, black and mirror-still, reflecting a sky that is mostly cloud and mist.

The density is absolute. There is no empty space here. Between the stalks, the density does not thin; it shifts from cellulose to water to fungal lace to the compressed, peat-like memory of ten thousand years of growth that has not yet finished accumulating. You cannot see more than a few meters in any direction, not because of fog, but because the world is *packed*—with matter, with moisture, with the presence of the growing, thinking, humming vegetation that fills every cubic centimeter of the high, cold, wet air.

And the world is active. The dynamic is constant: the water table pulses with a heartbeat that moves continents of fluid through the root systems. Weather forms and dies in hours within the canopy layers. The plants are migrating, slowly, toward the edges of the plateau, extending their quantum-networks into the sharp, raw geology, digesting the stone, turning it into more of themselves. The erosion is only just beginning, but it is happening—accelerated, catalyzed by enzymes and nanoscale drills secreted by the roots, the bedrock dissolving into the wet green mass that will eventually, in another million years, become soil.

There are no artifacts. No tools. No buildings. Only the grown, the weeping, the deafening, the packed, the thinking green dark of a continent that is wet, high, cold, and only just coming into being.
