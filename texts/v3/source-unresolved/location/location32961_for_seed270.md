It is a pocket in the world’s blind spot—a sealed cavity, perhaps the hollowed thorax of a dead orbital foundry, or a geode that grew around a machine core and forgot to stop. You enter through a fissure no wider than a shoulder, and the world narrows to this: a single vast chamber, intimate in its enclosure yet endless in its flatness. The scale is hidden (0.3), embedded-within something larger; you are inside a room that feels like a cell, a single feature contained, a micro-environment pressed between walls of compressed time.

The terrain is not soil. It is a sediment of ten thousand years of industrial decay—a near-flattened plain (erosion 2.8) of rust, pulverized concrete, and metal dust so fine it behaves like dry silt, though it weighs more. It carpets the low elevation (1.1) in a layer that shifts underfoot, gritty and dense (2.5), tasting of copper and ozone. There are no hills, only the gentle undulations of ancient foundations worn smooth by aeons of foot traffic and chemical weathering. The floor is cool (0.9) to the touch, a thermal mass that has been absorbing the chill of the deep for geological ages.

The air is impossibly dry (0.2). No water has existed here in liquid form since before the machines were young. Instead, the atmosphere is a non-aqueous soup—thick with hydrocarbon vapor, metallic particulates, and the anhydrous exhalations of the life that fills the space. It does not feel like air; it feels like a fluid, a heavy medium you must push through. Visibility is strange: normal light (1.9) filters down from crystalline vents in the ceiling, or from the pale, steady glow of ancient luminescent colonies, casting everything in a flat, mineral glare that erases shadows and makes the distance feel compressed, immediate.

And then, the life.

It is overwhelming (3.0). The floor is not merely covered; it is *made* of fauna. They are small—beetle-sized, or perhaps segmented worms of tempered glass and iron filings—and they exist in a state of high saturation, near-touching, a writhing carpet of exoskeletons that extends to every horizon of the chamber. They have no need for water; their biochemistry is based on sulfur, silicon, and the heavy metals they consume. They are the color of the place: rust-orange, gunmetal grey, the yellow of oxidized brass, the white of salt deposits. They skitter and grind and click against one another in a constant, roaring motion (sound 2.0). The noise is not merely loud; it is a physical pressure—a subsonic rumble that vibrates in your teeth, the sound of a billion tiny mandibles chewing on metal dust, on oil residue, and on each other. It is a roar without wind, a static without silence.

There are no plants (0.1). The ecology is a closed loop of industry and hunger, a post-botanical world. The creatures are detritivores of the extreme, lithovores and oleovores, feeding on the grease that seeps from ancient engine seals—grease that never dries in this sealed cool—and on the mineral substrate itself, breaking down the rust into finer dust, excreting crystalline structures that grow like fungus across the metal. They are the maintenance crew turned feral, or perhaps the original purpose of this place: a biological factory floor where the assembly line is hunger.

For scattered among them (built 1.1) are the faint marks of the civilization that birthed them. The structures are not ruins in the romantic sense; they are fossils. A massive engine block here, worn by erosion (2.8) to a smooth, near-flattened nub, its pistons fused into a single lump of slag. There, the rib-cage of a conveyor gantry half-buried in the dust, its rollers clogged with the calcified bodies of creatures. Occasional intact machines still pulse with dormant electricity (tech 1.6)—a control panel here glowing with a soft blue light, a turbine there spinning in a slow, grinding cycle that stirs the dust into a cyclone.

The technology is active (dynamic 2.4), industrial (1.6) in a way that blurs into biology. Gears turn in a cycle that takes hours to complete one revolution, powered by the creatures themselves—they crawl into the mechanisms, lubricating them with their oily bodies, becoming part of the machine, wearing themselves down to feed the gears. It is a symbiosis of engine and organism, a weather cycle of rust and consumption. The creatures swarm in tides (fauna 3.0), moving from one machine to the next in waves that darken the floor like a tide of oil, consuming, excreting, wearing the metal down further into the sediment. Predator and prey are the same thing; the strong eat the weak, and both are eaten by the machines, which grind them into nutrient paste that feeds the next generation of the swarm.

The light is normal, but strange. It comes from the machines—arcs of electricity jumping between corroded terminals, the steady hum of tubes that have burned for millennia without burning out. It illuminates the density (2.5) of the swarm: you can see only a few inches into the writhing mass before it becomes a solid wall of chitin and chrome, a living wall that breathes and shifts and hungers.

You are embedded within it (scale 0.3). The walls are close, lined with the same dust and the same creatures, curving overhead in a dome of mineral accretion and fossilized industry. The coolness seeps into your bones. The roar is constant, a loud (2.0) and active (2.4) drone of motion. And around you, the fauna moves—overwhelming, near-touching, ancient, and utterly without water, a dry tide of unusual (0.8) life consuming the last of the machine, turning industry into biology, and biology into the dust of the end of all things.
