You are submerged. The water presses against your skin with a cool, mineral weight—sharp, clean, tasting of ozone and metal. It is not dark here. Light filters down from above in a diffuse, blue-white haze, normal and even as daylight, though you cannot see the surface. You are too deep for that, or perhaps the surface is a ceiling of artificial luminosity, a planetary grid of submerged lamps that turn the entire volume into a lit chamber.

The floor is not earth. It is a surface—smooth, pale, geometric. Where your light reaches, you see a material like compressed ceramic or alloyed stone, freshly laid, with edges that catch the illumination like knife-blades. There is no sediment, or only a thin dusting of construction debris still settling. The terrain rises in sharp terraces and angular ridges, the scars of recent excavation and fabrication. Everything here is young. Nothing has had time to soften. The metal outcrops are un-rusted, the concrete seams are crisp, the artificial reefs of scaffolding and conduit stand at perfect angles, unweathered by current or time.

This is a built world, total and complete. There is no natural substrate. The geology is synthetic, a planetary-scale foundation poured and machined to hold an ocean. And the ocean is vast—continental, endless. You could swim for days in a straight line and encounter nothing but water, cold and moving, before the next structure rises from the abyss.

But structures there are. They are sparse—islands in a sea of liquid space. A factory complex here, a pumping station there, connected by pipelines that stretch across the floor like the veins of some great, sleeping machine. The density is low; the emptiness is profound. Between these installations, there is only the water, and the drifting particulate of industrial output—microscopic metal flakes, chemical precipitate, thermal plumes that rise and dissipate. It is a hollow place, a cathedral of negative space filled with cold, luminous water.

The structures themselves are industrial. Engines thrum deep within their housings. Water is drawn through massive intake grates, filtered, processed, and expelled with force. You hear it before you see it—a constant, overwhelming roar. The sound is loud, a physical pressure against the ears. It is the white-noise of planetary plumbing: the groan of turbines, the hammer of pistons, the rush of fluid through pipes the diameter of canyons. Superimposed on this mechanical cacophony are the voices of the fauna.

Life is abundant here, impossibly so, given the scarcity of green things. Vegetation is sparse—only a thin biofilm clinging to the sunken walls, patches of chemosynthetic bacteria glowing faintly near thermal vents, the occasional stand of engineered kelp that has been planted and largely ignored by the grazers. But the animals thrive. Schools of silver, scale-armored fish flash through the light in synchronized spirals. Eel-like predators, long and segmented, patrol the pipeline shadows. Massive filter-feeders, translucent and bell-shaped, drift near the outflows, straining the nutrient-rich water for the detritus the machines shed. Crustacean things the size of dogs clamber over the artificial reefs, scraping the bacterial mats, their shells clicking against the metal in a rain of tiny percussion.

The ecosystem is a closed loop of industry and biology. The machines produce heat, vibration, and particulate matter. The sparse plants and bacteria convert this into biomass, however meagerly. The abundant fauna consume, reproduce, and die, their bodies adding to the detritus that feeds the next generation. It is a food web built on rust and coolant, on the exhaust of engines rather than the sun.

The water is always in motion. Currents pulse through the vast chambers, driven by the circulation systems that keep the entire planetary ocean from stagnating. Thermal vents—artificial, precise—release warm plumes that rise and stratify, creating layers of temperature that the fauna navigate like highways. The world is active, dynamic, a machine that breathes water.

Temperature is cool, a steady chill that seeps into the metal and the flesh alike. The light remains constant, neither brightening nor dimming, a perpetual noon filtered through fathoms of liquid. There is no weather here in the conventional sense, but there are cycles—the rhythmic pulsing of the pumps, the migration of the fish schools from one processing node to the next, the slow settling of construction dust, the endless, grinding turnover of water through the planetary filters.

You are in a young world, freshly made, submerged in its own engineered sea. It is loud, cold, vast, and hollow, filled with the echo of machinery and the silver flash of life adapted to the machine.
