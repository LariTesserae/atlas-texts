The light arrives first—not warm, but *insistent*, a high-altitude brightness that seems to come from everywhere at once, bouncing off surfaces that have no shadow deep enough to hold it. You stand—or rather, you balance—on a plane of packed, pale stone that extends in every direction until the air itself seems to thin into the structure. There is no soil here. No gap between one constructed element and the next. The density is absolute: a continuous tessellation of hand-worked mineral, each piece fitted against its neighbor with the patience of glacial time, yet the edges remain sharp, *young*, unweathered by the soft rounding of age.

This is a place recently made. The erosion is in its infancy—flakes of stone still litter the micro-channels between walls, freshly cleaved by tools of flint and obsidian, perhaps a smattering of worked copper that has not yet greened with age. The builders used only what the mountain offered and what their hands could shape: no mortar, no metal framework, just stone upon stone, held by gravity and geometry and the sheer *press* of so much material crowded together. Every surface is artificial. There is no "ground" in the natural sense, only the floor of the construction, layer upon layer of it, ascending and descending in a topography that mimics mountains but is entirely fabricated.

Water is the blood of this place. It does not rush; it *persists*. The air is cool and heavy with moisture, condensing on every cool surface, weeping from the stone itself—not from cracks, but from the very matrix of the rock, which seems to sweat. The atmosphere is saturated, a perpetual post-rain mist that never quite clears, that pools in the countless basins carved into the packed structures. These basins are everywhere: shallow depressions, channels a finger-width wide, cisterns stacked in vertical columns that defy easy counting. The water moves with glacial patience—filling a basin over the course of a day, then dripping through a narrow aperture to the level below, then evaporating into the bright, thin air only to condense again on a higher wall. It is a slow circulation, a breathing, a geological pulse measured in weeks rather than seconds.

Because the elevation is high, the coolness is not the damp chill of lowland swamps but something sharper, a temperature that keeps the condensation constant, that preserves the freshness of the stone. The light is so bright it hurts; the stone is pale—quartzite, perhaps, or a metamorphic shale that catches the sun and throws it back in a glare that makes the wet surfaces shine like mirrors. You can see your own distorted reflection in the water-films coating the walls, fragmented across a thousand surfaces.

There is no life. No lichen, no moss, no fungal bloom (though the damp might suggest them, the stone is too fresh, too mineral, too *built*). No animals traverse this labyrinth—no birds in the thin air above, no insects in the crevices. The silence is profound, broken only by the occasional *tick* of cooling stone, the soft *plink* of a droplet falling three inches to a waiting pool, the whisper of air moving through the packed channels. It is mostly quiet, but not empty—the water is always speaking, just too slowly to hear as speech.

The weirdness lies in the scale of intention. This is primitive technology—hand tools, muscle, simple levers and ramps—yet the result is a constructed environment so total, so densely packed, that it suggests a single organism or a crystalline growth rather than a collection of individual acts. The structures do not follow human streets or logic; they branch and bifurcate like capillaries, like the inside of a lung, or like the cooling vanes of some impossible engine. And yet they are clearly carved, clearly *made*. The geometry is too regular for nature, too irregular for mathematics—an artifact of countless hands working over generations, perhaps, or one patient hand working over millennia, following a pattern that is almost, but not quite, comprehensible.

Time moves differently here. The dynamic is slow—gradual cycles of saturation and evaporation, the slow settling of the packed stones as they compact under their own weight, the patient dripping that will, over centuries, wear new channels into the young rock. The landscape is in a state of suspended animation, fresh and sharp and wet, waiting. It is a machine for holding water, built by hands that are no longer present, at an elevation where the air is thin and the light is bright and the only motion is the endless, patient seepage of moisture through a world made entirely of stone.
