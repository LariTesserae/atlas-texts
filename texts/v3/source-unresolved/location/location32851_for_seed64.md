You descend into the **Throat-Pocket**, a caldera no wider than a city block, set at 4,900 meters where the air is too thin to hold warmth and too rarefied to carry moisture. It is a single, contained feature—an inverted bowl of basalt and schist, rimmed by peaks that scrape a sky the color of bruised pewter. The elevation is extreme, pressing down with a physical weight that makes your ears pop, yet the space itself feels intimate, claustrophobic, a hidden room carved into the spine of the world.

The floor is not soil. It is **anhydrous mineral pan**—a crust of gypsum, halite, and powdered magnetite, cracked into polygonal plates that have not shifted in an age. There is no water here, not as rain, not as dew, not as ice you can melt. Any H₂O is locked inside crystal lattices, inaccessible. The ground crunches underfoot with a sound like breaking glass, yet it is cool to the touch, drawing heat from your palm with the efficiency of a heat sink. The erosion that shaped this place is **established**, ancient and complete; the rock faces are weathered smooth, fretted into shapes that suggest water once flowed here, but the water is gone, and what remains is a topography frozen in its final, arid form.

Yet the basin is **lush**.

Vegetation saturates every cubic centimeter. It is a **packed** jungle of *lithophytes*—silicate-carbon hybrids that metabolize sulfur and iron rather than sunlight and water. They appear as dense, metallic moss: carpets of copper-green filaments so thick they form a mattress you could not penetrate without a blade. Between these mats rise **glass-stalked towers**, translucent columns of silica fiber that photosynthesize through thermal gradients, their surfaces beaded with condensation that is not water but liquid sulfur dioxide, gleaming amber in the dim light. Vines of braided tungsten drape over everything, heavy and inert, while fungal brackets—**packed** shelf-like and shelf-like, layer upon layer—erupt from the mineral crust in shelf upon shelf of rust-red and ochre. There is no room between presences. The growth is saturated, a fully-occupied volume where stalk presses against stalk, where the machinery of the place is embedded *within* the biology, grown around and through. It is a verdant desolation, a forest without sap, a jungle without juice.

Scattered through this vegetal density—**scattered** but unmistakably **industrial**—are the remains of extraction. A drilling rig stands frozen, its bit half-buried in the mineral pan, cables draped like dead vines across the metallic moss. A small processing hut, no larger than a garden shed, squats among the glass-stalks, its corrugated walls weathered to a matte, rust-bloom patina. Conveyor belts, made of some alloy that has not corroded to powder, snake through the thicket in loops that serve no purpose now, their rollers locked. Pipes pierce the ground, venting nothing, humming instead. These are not ruins of collapse but artifacts of **arrest**—partial remains, occasional structures, a mechanized presence that never grew beyond outposts and rigs.

The light is **dim**. At this elevation, the sun should be a blade, but the pocket is shadowed by its own walls, and the air—though thin—carries a perpetual, fine dust of mineral particulate that diffuses the illumination into a flat, blue-grey twilight. The glass-stalks catch what photons filter down and refract them into a faint, internal glow, so the jungle seems to smolder from within. It is never bright here, never dark; a perpetual, low register of visibility.

And it is **loud**.

A sound fills the pocket—a **resonant drone** at approximately 50 hertz, felt in the sternum, vibrating through the dense vegetation and the frozen machinery alike. It is the voice of the place itself: a standing wave, perhaps, generated by the caldera’s geometry and the constant, high-altitude wind that scours the rim but never enters the bowl. Or it is the harmonic of the dormant industrial cores, reactors that are not off but *stilled*, vibrating at a frequency that suggests power without motion. The sound is **loud**, a physical pressure that makes conversation impossible, that you feel in your teeth and the hollow of your throat. It never varies. There is no gust, no lull, no change in pitch. The loudness is a constant, a time-invariant roar that has no beginning and no modulation.

There are **no animals**. No birds wheeling in the thin air, no insects scraping against the glass-stalks, no microfauna in the mineral dust. The fauna register is empty, a biological silence that contrasts with the acoustic violence of the hum. Life here is only the **strange** vegetation and the frozen machines.

The world operates under a rule of **frozen equilibrium**. Nothing grows, though the plants look lush; nothing decays, though the machines look weathered; nothing moves, though the sound suggests vibration. A leaf does not fall. A gear does not turn. The erosion that shaped the rock happened in a time before, and now the forms are fixed, settled into their own state, in balance. The pocket is a single, contained moment—an intimate room at the top of the world where industry and alien botany reached a stalemate and were then **preserved**, locked in a stasis that mimics death but is instead a perfect, unchanging stasis.

To stand here is to be inside a held breath. The air is cool and dry against your skin. The metallic moss prickles your palms if you touch it—hard, crystalline, utterly desiccated. The hum presses against your eardrums, loud and unceasing. Around you, the packed jungle of glass and tungsten and silica stares back with vegetal patience, while the scattered rigs and huts stand as monuments to a mechanized ambition that stopped, mid-motion, at the edge of the sky. It is a hidden place, a pocket-scale world, extreme in its elevation and intimate in its containment, where the only thing that happens is the sound, and the sound never changes.
