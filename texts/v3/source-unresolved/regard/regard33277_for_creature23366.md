They would come the way everything comes: through the green. Not parting it, not commanding it, but letting the stems yield as they have always yielded, to pressure that does not rush. They would step into the hollow as the light steps into it—soaked, softened, arriving without shadow.

And they would stop.

Not in surprise. In recognition.

A wise power does not mistake emptiness for absence. They would feel the roar enter their ribs and know it for what it is: not noise, but a long attention. They would run a hand along a leaning facade and read the six hundred years of patient dreaming in the weathered sag. They would watch the swift bank around the empty point in the square and understand, immediately, that the borough is not waiting for someone to arrive. It is holding the shape of arrival itself. It has turned anticipation into architecture, and in doing so, has made anticipation indistinguishable from presence.

What do you do for a place that has already solved its own loneliness by braiding time into a loom? What do you give to a dream that sweeps its own floors, tunes its own chorus, and has made a liturgy out of expectation? A lesser power would fill the rooms. A hurried power would straighten the folds. A lonely power would stay. But wisdom knows that some harmonies are complete precisely because they leave space for the listener. Benevolence knows that a sanctuary does not need to be improved. It only needs to be witnessed.

So they would do very little.

Which is to say, they would do exactly what is needed.

They would kneel at the well. Not to drink, but to touch the water where the false canopy shivers. They would let their reflection join the others—not as a ruler, not as a savior, but as another face in the long smear of visits. And in that touch, they would offer a single adjustment, so subtle the beetles would not pause in their rounds, so quiet the wall-singers would not miss a phase: they would braid a thread of *recognition* into the fold.

Not memory as weight. Recognition as grace. So that when you step through the doubled doorway, you will not feel unmoored, but accompanied. So that the echo arriving before your footstep will carry, faintly, the warmth of a palm that has already rested on the lintel. So that the many-jointed things, flowing through their impossible corners, will feel the air grow just a fraction softer at the turn. The folds would not change. They would simply stop feeling like breaks, and begin feeling like hands.

They would listen to the deep singer. And because they are wise, they would not answer it with words. They would answer it with the kind of silence that is not absence of sound, but presence of attention. They would let the borough’s roar wash through them until their own pulse tuned to the bass thrum, until their breath fell into the gutter-frogs’ rhythm, until the metallic phase-sheets of the wall-singers rang in their sternum. And then, only then, they would hum.

One note. Low enough to join the depth, clear enough to thread the canopy. Not a new song. A harmony. The kind that makes an old chord ring truer without changing its shape.

The wren would tilt both its heads. The swifts would not alter their bank. The beetles would trundle on. But the green would sigh, the way a body sighs when a long-held tension finally recognizes it is safe to rest. The chorus would not grow louder. It would grow *deeper*. As if the hollow had always been missing one frequency, not because it was broken, but because it was leaving room for a guest who knew how to listen before speaking.

They would leave no mark on the cobbles. They would take nothing. They would straighten no leaning wall, silence no singer, close no doubled door. But as they turned to go back through the grown-shut dark, they would do one last thing: they would whisper to the green outside the hollow. Not a command. A request, spoken in the slow grammar of root and sap.

*Keep the approaches tangled. Let the maps forget. Let the compasses drift. Let the straight-eyed and the hurried pass by without seeing. Let only the wandering, the weary, the ones who have walked long enough to stop demanding certainty, find their way in.*

And the green, which has always known how to listen, would thicken just so. Not a wall. A filter. A kindness. The lowlands would remain one plant, but the paths through it would grow slightly more selective, slightly more merciful. The borough would not be hidden. It would be *reserved*. For those who need it. For those who will become its inhabitant, again and again, out of order, forever.

They would go.

Because a wise power knows that a sanctuary is not a place to be occupied, but a rhythm to be honored. The borough would not change. The rooms would remain empty. The thresholds would continue to dish. The chorus would roar on. The folds would keep dealing out visits like cards through centuries. The beetles would keep the guest beds made. The deep one would keep listening through its grown ear. The green would keep dreaming its old town in perfect, patient detail.

But something would be different, in the way a room is different after someone you love has sat quietly in it and left.

The expectation would deepen. The having would grow richer. The stone would wear smoother. The break would feel more like an embrace.

And when you come—when your shoulders squeeze through the last wet dark, when the hollow opens like a released breath, when your foot meets the polished knuckle of root and the echo arrives a half-step early—you will feel it. Not a presence. A permission. The quiet, unshakable knowledge that you were seen, long before you arrived, by something that understood you did not need to be fixed, only welcomed. That your scattered, out-of-sequence visits were not a flaw in the borough’s design, but its purpose. That you are not late. You are not early. You are exactly when you are supposed to be, and the architecture has been holding your shape since before you had a name.

The borough will hold you. It always has. And now, it will hold you with a blessing woven into its seams: that you may leave when you must, and return when you need to, and never, in all the smeared and looping years, be a stranger to your own footsteps.

The green will dream on. The deep one will listen. The chorus will rise. The swifts will take the impossible corner at speed. The frogs will hammer the civic clock. The beetles will sweep the empty rooms. The wren will keep itself company, half a beat apart.

And the wise power, somewhere far beyond the lowlands, will close their eyes and breathe, because they did not build a thing. They did not save a thing. They only recognized a prayer that was already being answered, and added their breath to the saying of it.

Which is, in the end, what benevolence does when it meets something whole.

It kneels. It listens. It harmonizes. It leaves the door ajar.
