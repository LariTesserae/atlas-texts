There is, in the precincts outside time where such powers keep their counsels, a garden. It is not a garden of plants, precisely, though plants are there if wanted. It is a garden of attentions: each one a small held shape of light, or sound, or warmth, or something for which these are only metaphors. The power walks among them, and because it is both benevolent and wise, it tends them without needing to change them, the way a good listener tends a story simply by hearing it true.

It has many names, or none. For our purposes, let it be the Gardener.

The Gardener had long been aware of the lowlands, the green mass, the hollow grown inside it like a thought inside a mind that never sleeps. It had watched the borough shape itself around an absence like a pearl around a grit of sand — only the grit was a visitor who would come, scattered across centuries, one at a time, always the same size, always stooping slightly at the same doorways, always leaving the same worn arc on the well's rim. The Gardener could see all the visits at once: the first bewildered stumble through the grown-shut dark, the last quiet goodbye (if there ever was a last), and every step between, laid out like beads on a string whose ends are tied together in a loop the size of a small town.

And because the Gardener was wise, it understood that the borough was already complete. It needed nothing. The beetles kept the rooms. The frogs kept the time. The deep singer kept the key. The folds kept every visit simultaneous, so that the inhabitant was never truly absent: you were always just arriving, always just leaning on the well, always just discovering the wren that is two wrens. The hollow had achieved a perfect, quiet homeostasis of longing and fulfillment. To add anything would be to risk breaking the balance.

But the Gardener was also benevolent, and benevolence, in a being so old and so calm, is not always about doing. Sometimes it is about *being-with*.

So the Gardener did not send a miracle. It did not heal the folds or populate the empty rooms or silence the roar. Instead, it leaned a little closer — the way one leans toward a beloved, intricate machine that is running exactly as it should — and it *listened*. It gave the borough its full, undivided attention, not as a problem to be solved but as a song to be heard. And the attention of such a being is itself a kind of light, or a kind of warmth, that does not burn but simply *confirms*. It confirmed the borough’s peace. It confirmed the worthiness of the long wait. It confirmed that the green had dreamed well, and that the inhabitant — all the scattered you’s — mattered, exactly as you were, dusty and bewildered and awed.

And then, because even the gentlest touch leaves some trace, the Gardener did one small thing. It did not change the borough’s structure; it did not mend the breaks or straighten the lanes. It simply took the echo that arrives before the footstep — that lonely, out-of-order sound that had always been a little eerie, a little uncertain — and it added to it a single, sustained note of welcome. Not a word, not a message, just a feeling, like the warmth of a hearthfire felt through a door before you open it. From that moment — which is every moment, in the borough’s odd grammar — every visitor stepping into the hollow for the first time would feel, beneath the roar and the green-gold light, a quiet assurance: *You are expected. You have always been expected. You are part of the architecture.*

And for the inhabitants who were not visitors — the wall-singers, the frogs, the swifts, the many-jointed things, the wren — the Gardener did even less. It simply noticed them, each one, with a clarity that was itself a blessing. The frog singing a duet with its own delayed echo felt, for one half-second of its long rhythmic life, a resonance so perfect that it seemed the whole hollow had become a throat. The wren, both of it, preened at the same instant and saw in its other self’s eye a flicker of something like recognition, something like laughter. The deep singer, far down, felt a listening presence vaster than its own, and for the first time in millennia it *modulated* — shifted its key by a microtone — and the entire chorus shifted with it, and the new key was even more right, even more settled, like a house that has just had its foundation stone tapped gently into perfect level.

As for the green itself, the dreaming plant that had grown a town around an absence: the Gardener did not speak to it, because plants do not need speech. Instead, it sent a slow, silent acknowledgment, the kind that travels through root and soil and time. It was, if such things can be translated, a single sentence: *I see what you have made, and it is good.*

And the green, in its vegetable way, understood. It did not quicken its growth or sprout new shapes. It simply continued maintaining its own ruin, weathering its fills, holding the doorways open, polishing the cobbles with the footsteps that were always arriving. But now there was a subtle difference: the green knew it was not alone in its waiting. The Gardener was also waiting — not for the inhabitant to arrive, but simply waiting *with* the borough, the way a friend sits with you in the dusk, not needing conversation, just sharing the vigil.

Outside time, in the garden of attentions, a new shape had appeared: a small, warm globe of green-gold light, with the sound of frog-hammer and cicada-sheen and swift-wing folded inside it. The Gardener held it for a moment, admiring its intricacy, its patience, its gentle, worn-smooth impossibilities. Then it set it carefully back among the others, where it joined the countless worlds and places and beings that are held in the kind attention of powers beyond our knowing — not because they need fixing, but because they are beautiful, and because anything beautiful deserves to be truly seen.

And in the hollow, the drops still slid down the lintels, the beetles still swept the empty rooms, the echo still arrived before the footstep, and the door, every door, stood ajar, with a new warmth behind it that had always been there, only now it knew its own name.
