They do not inhabit the pocket so much as they are the pocket learning to move. Flesh and mineral share the same grammar here. Every creature is a verb conjugated in cold, heavy water: *secrete, settle, filter, fold, wait, harden*. There is no clean line between resident and residence, only gradients of density, chemical plumes, and the slow exchange of dissolved things. What lives here does not occupy space. It becomes it.

The pale, jointed ones are the first you register, though registration is too quick a word. You feel them before you see them: a subtle thickening in the current, a shift in the water's taste that leans toward lime and oxidized iron. They cluster along the rising facades, bodies interlocked like living scaffolding, each segment sheathed in a fresh rime of precipitate. They do not build. They exhale. Their gills draw in the brine, strip it of silica and calcium, and spin the remainder through abdominal pores. The filaments emerge soft, gelatinous, then shock-hard in the cold, fusing instantly to whatever they touch. They move with the patience of settling silt, shifting only when a neighbor's secretion alters the flow or a temperature gradient tilts. Their joints do not click; they slide, lubricated by their own mineral sweat. When they molt, the discarded carapaces do not drift. They catch in the eddies they helped shape, become nucleation sites, and within days are indistinguishable from the walls. The old ones do not die in any familiar sense. They slow. Their secretions thicken. Their legs fuse to the ledges they once climbed. Eventually, only the faint outline of a thorax remains in the crystal, a fossil still breathing through the pores of the city.

Above them, in the engineered avenues, the drifters pulse. They are the size of cupped hands, translucent except for the dark smudge of gut and the frilled, ciliated corona that rings their mouths. They do not swim. They adjust ballast through microscopic chambers that fill or empty with gas and dense brine, rising or sinking to ride the currents the jointed ones have shaped. Their frills taste the water continuously, sifting microbes, sloughed cells, the fine organic dust that never settles. When a pressure wave announces danger, they do not flee. They invert. The frills fold inward, the body contracts into a tight, mineral-textured sphere, and they become just another nodule on the wall. Their pulsing is not random. Each contraction pushes a microcurrent downward, feeding the architects below, stirring the chemical gradients that guide the next layer of growth. They are the settlement's breath, and they do not know it.

In the narrowest alleys, where the water is forced into whispering threads, the eels slide. Their bodies are nearly invisible, save for the dark line of intestine and the silver tracery of lateral lines that read displacement like braille. Vertebrae show through the skin like drawn wire. They do not hunt with speed. They drift, heads slightly raised, gills fluttering in silent rhythm, tasting for the chemical signature of vulnerability. When they strike, it is a short, precise arc, jaws closing on a drifter that failed to fold, or on the soft, unhardened edge of a new accretion. Their skin sheds continuously in fine, glassy sheets. These sheets do not dissolve. They catch in the slower eddies, layer upon layer, becoming the smooth floors of the plazas. The eels are the polishers, the quiet maintainers of flow, their passage leaving behind the very ground others will walk.

On the facades, where terraces step into shadow, the lattice-walkers climb. They have too many legs, each jointed, each ending in a micro-hook that grips fresh crystal without fracturing it. They are the bridge-makers. Where two growing ledges nearly meet, where a current splits and leaves a gap, they span it. From spinnerets beneath their thorax, they extrude a thread of calcified silk that hardens the moment it touches the cold. They do not weave for prey. They weave because the water's tension demands it, because the temperature gradient pulls the filament taut, because their bodies are tuned to the geometry of meeting flows. They molt often. Each discarded shell is caught by the current, tumbled into a plaza, and cemented into the floor. In a year, a single walker will have contributed more to the city's mass than its own body weighs. They are the tension-readers, the gap-closers, the ones who turn *almost* into structure.

In the alcoves, where the light pools and the water goes still, the flat ones wait. They are nearly two-dimensional, mottled in shades of ash, bone, and bruised green, their skin dusted with a fine precipitate that matches the surrounding crust exactly. They do not patrol. They anchor. Metabolism drops to a crawl. Gills barely stir. They become texture. When a drifter pulses too close, when an eel's lateral line misreads an eddy, when a lattice-walker's silk catches the wrong current, they detach. Not with a lunge, but with a release. They fall through the water like a dropped shadow, striking in a silent bloom of displacement. The kill is instant, clean, leaving no thrashing, no cloud of disturbance. They consume quickly, then return to their alcove. Their waste is dense, calcified, sinking to become a new foundation stone. They are not murderers. They are pruners. Without them, the channels would clog, the flows would stagnate, the place would suffocate in its own growth.

On the sharpest overhangs, where the refracted light gathers in soft pools, the pale fronds tremble. They are not plants. They are something between colony and crystal, photosynthetic filaments that grow like frost, branching in fractal patterns where the dim glow lingers. They anchor not with roots, but with chemical dissolution: they secrete a mild acid that etches a microscopic grip into the crystal, then immediately re-precipitate the dissolved minerals as a seal. They do not sway. The cold stiffens them. They tremble only when a current shifts or a predator drops. Their photosynthesis is slow, inefficient by surface standards, but here it is enough. They release oxygen and trace organics that feed the drifters, and in return, the drifters' waste feeds them. When they die, they do not rot. They vitrify. Their bodies become glassy veins in the walls, catching the light, bending it, creating new pools for the next generation. They are the light-catchers, the slow exhalers, the ones who turn dimness into sugar and glass.

Deeper in the walls, where the water barely moves, the embedded colonies pulse. They are not individuals, but mats of microscopic polyps, fused with the mineral matrix, their bodies indistinguishable from the stone they inhabit. Their light is not for signaling. It is metabolic, a byproduct of slow chemical exchange, tuned to temperature gradients and the rhythm of the currents. They pulse asynchronously, never in unison, creating a slow, breathing luminescence that has nothing to do with day or night. Their light attracts certain microbes, which attract drifters, which shape flow, which guides the architects. They are the settlement's metronome, though they keep no time humans would recognize. Their rhythm is measured in millimeters of growth, in the slow turn of chemical plumes, in the patient waiting of the flat ones.

None of them see well. The water is too thick, the light too bent, the particulate too heavy. They navigate by taste and pressure. Lateral lines read displacement like topography. Chemoreceptors map the water in gradients of iron, salt, ammonia, lime. A flick of a tail three tiers above is not heard; it is felt as a soft wave against the ribs, a shift in current that says *movement, direction, mass*. A colony pulsing in an alcove is not seen; it is tasted as a ripple of oxygen and trace metals. Mating, when it happens, is a slow convergence of chemical plumes in a still plaza, a drifting together, a release of gametes that sink and crystallize into new settlements. There is no courtship, no display, only chemistry and patience. Silence is not absence here. It is the medium. Sound would shatter the delicate pressure-reading, would announce death too early, would waste energy. They speak in displacement, in gradient, in the slow language of becoming.

Birth is settlement. A larva, a spore, a drifting fragment catches on a fresh crystal edge, secretes its first filament, and begins. Growth is accretion. Every meal, every breath, every molt adds to the structure. Death is incorporation. Nothing leaves. Sloughed skin, spent fronds, hollow carapaces, the dense waste of predators, the vitrified remnants of frost-fronds—all are caught in the slower eddies, layered, cemented, becoming wall, floor, arch, alcove. The place consumes its own dead and builds them into its foundations. There is no grave, only foundation. No ruin, only raw edge waiting for the next layer. Even outsiders are claimed. Minerals settle on gear, on skin, on seams. The cold seeps into joints, slowing pulse to match the rhythm. Currents learn the shape, part, then close. The pocket does not reject. It incorporates. Given time, a visitor would become a contour, a new eddy, a fresh nucleation site. The inhabitants do not know they are building a city. They are simply living, and living here means becoming architecture.

They are not residents. They are the place learning to hold itself together. Pale joints secreting stone. Frilled mouths tasting flow. Flat shadows waiting in alcoves. Glassy veins catching dim light. Colonies pulsing in slow, asynchronous breath. Every movement shapes a current. Every current guides a secretion. Every secretion becomes a wall. Every wall creates an alcove. Every alcove holds a predator. Every predator leaves a stone. Every stone catches a larva. The loop closes. The water thickens. The cold preserves. The silence deepens. They do not build. They accrete. They do not speak. They displace. They do not die. They settle. And the pocket, heavy and dim and patient, holds them all, letting them become more themselves with every precipitated crystal, every silent pulse, every millimeter of slow, relentless becoming.
