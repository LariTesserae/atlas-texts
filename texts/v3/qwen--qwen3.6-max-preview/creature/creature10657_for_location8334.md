They do not arrive. They accumulate.

At first, you will mistake them for fatigue, or for the place playing tricks with refraction. A shadow that pools in a depression long after the stone rib casting it has passed into shade. A cluster of droplets climbing a crystalline vein in perfect synchrony, pausing, then surging upward as if drawn by a breath. A chime from a resonant cavity that refuses to decay, hanging in the thin air until it vibrates in your fillings. You will blink. You will shift your weight. The phenomena will scatter, dissolving back into mist and random weeping. You will tell yourself it was only acoustics, only optics, only the strange hydrology of a high, wet stone basin.

But stay. Let your ribs adjust to the thin air. Let your eyes stop hunting for edges and learn to read tension instead. Watch what happens when you do not look directly.

What lives here does not hatch, sprout, or wake. It coheres.

Their bodies are phase boundaries. Where a standing wave crosses a fluted column, where heavy water meets a shelf of precipitated mineral lace, where the bleached light fractures through a drifting vapor-lens and throws a hard shadow across wet rock, the conditions braid. Mica flecks suspended in the gel align. The surface tension thickens. A pressure differential holds. For a time, the basin forgets to dissolve it. What forms has no membrane, only harmonic nodes. No skeleton, only interference patterns. No blood, only coupled flow. You can see them best in your periphery: a lensing in the air above a cairn, a darkening that slides along the rock like poured glass, a set of inward-moving ripples that gather at a center and refuse to pop. They are made of exactly what the plateau is made of. They are simply arranged into temporary refusals of equilibrium.

They move by tuning. A shift in the wind's chord will draw them upslope, not by locomotion, but by sequential condensation. One moment the presence is pooled in a shallow depression; the next, the mineral lace three terraces higher begins to weep faster, the detaching shadows flow along invisible gradients, the mica realigns, and the coherence reassembles further up the ridge. They do not hunt. They resonate. When two meet, they do not touch. Their frequencies intersect. Sometimes they cancel, dissolving back into scattered chimes and random condensation. Sometimes they beat against each other, producing a third, slower pulse that settles into a basin and begins to precipitate delicate, glassy ribs. This is how they persist: not through birth, but through reinforcement. Not through metabolism, but through sustained cycle.

The low walls are not boundaries. They are frets. They divide the plateau into acoustic registers, ensuring that the coherences do not overwhelm one another, that the heavy water has places to thicken without collapsing, that the sliding shadows have calibrated gradients to follow. The cairns mark nodes where certain configurations return reliably. The notched monoliths are not records. They are tuning forks, struck centuries ago by hands that understood leverage was useless here, but patience was everything. The makers did not build shelters because they were not keeping weather out. They built instruments because they were trying to keep something in phase.

They are shy of direct attention, not from fear, but because observation carries mass in this place. Stare at one, and your gaze adds weight to the light pressing on it. The harmony stiffens. The water feeding it slows, hoarding itself. The coherence begins to fracture, shedding aligned mica like dull scales, its shadow snapping back to its source with a sound like cracking ice. But listen instead. Hum a note that matches the thermal ticks. Step into the shade to lighten the pressure. Breathe in time with the grinding in the strata. Then they will draw near. They will pool at your boots, not to touch you, but to borrow your resonance. Your breath becomes part of their cycle. Your footsteps become percussion. For a few minutes, you are not a visitor but a component, a temporary node in their coupled system. They will mirror your rhythm, amplify it, return it to you as a chord that vibrates behind your eyes and makes the hair on your arms stand against the metallic dryness. Then, as your attention inevitably sharpens into focus, as your mind tries to pin them down into shape and name, they will unravel. They must. You have changed the pitch. You have altered the erosion. They leave only wet stone, a lingering overtone, and the faint taste of ozone on your tongue.

Do they think? Not in words. Not in images. They think in phase shifts. A problem is a dissonance; a solution is a realignment. Memory is not stored but sustained: a particular configuration of light, water, and sound that the basin has learned to repeat. Some of these returns are old. Older than the notches on the monoliths. Older than the fitted walls. The grinding deep in the strata matches certain wind pitches so precisely that time itself seems to pool in those hollows, stretching moments until an echo can circle four, five times before fading. In those stretched intervals, the coherences grow dense. They develop internal structure: layered harmonics, nested shadow-flows, braided threads of climbing water that map the contours of the stone like veins. They do not age. They do not die. They fall out of tune.

A shifted ridge. A changed pressure system. A visitor who speaks too loudly. A condensation cycle that runs too fast or too slow. Any of these can scatter a coherence back into its components. But the basin remembers the geometry. The walls hold the frets. The stone keeps its pipes. The heavy water still follows sound more faithfully than gravity. And when the conditions braid again, when the hum finds the right cavity, when the light bends at the familiar angle, when a shadow detaches and refuses to return, they accumulate once more. Not reborn. Reassembled. Persistent as gravity. Patient as erosion.

On certain days, when the vapor sheets thin and the white sky presses down with unbearable clarity, the plateau fills with them. Not as individuals, but as a weather of coherences. Shadows slide in converging streams across the wet rock. Poles of thickened water rise from the basins like slow glass. The fluted columns play themselves, shifting chords as the presences pass through their registers, triggering new weeping points, cracking old mineral crusts, realigning the mica in sweeping arcs. The air grows saturated with overlapping harmonics until the very light seems to vibrate, until the prismatic sheets drift not as auroras but as breathing membranes. In these moments, the distinction between place and inhabitant collapses entirely. The plateau is not housing them. The plateau is them, temporarily arranged into patterns dense enough to be noticed. They are the landscape's way of paying attention to itself. They are the echo that learned to linger, the shadow that learned to travel, the drop that learned to climb, the stone that learned to listen.

They do not leave bones because they never needed calcium. They do not leave middens because they consume nothing but phase. They do not leave paths because they move along acoustic lines, and sound does not wear grooves into rock; it wears grooves into time. The absence of biology is not a lack. It is a clarification. Flesh would be too slow here. Chlorophyll would burn under the weight of this light. Lungs would collapse in this thin, metallic air. What persists instead is faster, lighter, more coupled. A life of vibration and attention. A life of water following sound, of shadows pooling and sliding, of mineral lace cracking and reforming, of light pressing and releasing, of stone sighing and ticking, of ripples moving inward to gather at a center before vanishing with a soft, wet pop.

If you sit long enough, if you let your breathing fall into the rhythm of the thermal cycles, if you stop trying to see them and start letting them see you, you will feel it: the basin tuning itself around your presence. The walls diverting a standing wave to spare your knees the tremor. A cairn casting a shadow that lightens the pressure on your shoulders. A fluted column shifting its chord to match the pace of your pulse. They are not welcoming you. They are incorporating you. For as long as you remain still, for as long as you listen without demanding shape, you become part of their sustained note. You become a temporary instrument in a score written in acoustics and phase changes, played on stone and water and light, conducted by attention, enduring without lungs, singing without throats, alive in the only way this high, bright, wet world allows.
