They do not arrive. They emerge.

At first, you notice only the adjustments in the basin's rhythm. A hare pauses mid-stride, ears canted toward a shadow that hasn't moved. A grazer lifts its head, nostrils flaring at a scent the wind hasn't yet delivered. The glass-winged birds tilt their bank a fraction wider. The predator on the ledge goes still, not in hunting crouch, but in recognition. The air itself seems to draw inward, thinning further, holding its breath. And then you see them: not as figures stepping into view, but as concentrations of the hollow's own habits, gathered into shape.

They are built for the altitude the way the moss is built for the damp, the way the stone is built for the cold. Narrow ribcages that expand in slow, measured draws. Long limbs jointed for balance on fractured edges. Skin cured by sun and sublimation into a texture like wind-smoothed bark, crosshatched with fine lines that mirror the cliff's fracture patterns. Their eyes are pale, rimmed in dark, adapted to the surgical glare; they do not squint. They narrow. Their hands are broad-palmed, fingers long and knuckle-calloused, nails thick and opalescent like the ice veils that form at dawn. They wear layered hides and woven grasses, oiled with rendered fat and crushed seed until the fabric sheds moisture like the waxy shrubs. Their boots are soled with compressed violet moss and cured sinew, leaving no print, only a temporary depression that springs back within the hour. They move as the basin moves: without hurry, without waste, weight distributed, steps timed to the soft percussive pops of freeze-thaw stone.

They do not speak. Not here. The thin air swallows vowels, and they have learned to let it. Communication is a shift of shoulder, a tap of knuckle against rock, a breath held or released. When they must sound, it is a low click, a soft exhale through parted teeth, a hum pitched to match the frequency of the wind-strung cords. They do not dominate the quiet. They enter it.

Their presence is seasonal, but season here is not a calendar. It is a gradient. They come when the hoarfrost begins to weep in earnest, when the stone cups fill and empty in predictable intervals, when the condensation channels run silver in the morning light. They stay through the turning, reading the basin the way a hand reads a face. They kneel by the shallow runnels, not to drink, but to watch the meniscus tremble. They count the seconds between drops. They trace the spiraling water-carved grooves with fingertips that know the difference between erosion and intention. They understand that the dampness does not fall randomly; it follows a geometry older than the uplift, a slow unspooling that draws root, fur, and footfall into the same quiet orbits.

They harvest without taking. Moss is parted with carved bone combs, the outer rings lifted clean, the heart left intact to pulse and regrow. Lichen is scraped with volcanic glass, only where it has thickened beyond the stone's need. Hares are caught in braided fiber loops that tighten without tearing; the young, the pregnant, the old are released with a touch to the spine that stills their panic. Grazers are never hunted here. They are watched. Their horn-sheddings are collected after the spring rub, polished by wind and frost, drilled with stone awls, and hung on the cords between the spires. When the wind strikes true, the bones hum. The people feel it in their ribs, in their teeth, in the hollows of their collarbones. They close their eyes and measure the vibration against their own pulse. It is how they know the thaw is coming. It is how they know when to leave.

They do not build shelters. They arrange thresholds. The low windbreaks are not walls but deflectors, stacked to split the gale and let it sing through the markers. The cords are not boundaries but instruments, tensioned to catch the basin's breath and translate it into frequency. The stone knives rest in niches like offered teeth. The sinew coils wait in bark cups, dry and patient. Nothing is fixed. Everything is adjustable. When a storm shifts the rim's snow-load, they move the windbreaks a hand's breadth. When the sun's angle changes, they retension the cords. When the moss spreads, they step back. They leave no scar because they do not press. They align.

They know about the center. They do not cross it.

Like the hares, like the grazers, like the white predator that flows along the ledges, they give the polished dark mirror a wide berth. But they face it. When the sun strikes noon and the bare rock flares like a blind eye, they sit at its rim, backs to the stone, eyes closed, feeling the cold radiance against their spines. They do not pray. They do not chant. They listen. The center is not empty. It is a lens. It pulls the basin's silence inward, compresses it, holds it until the air itself feels dense. Condensation avoids it. Moss will not root near it. The spiraling water channels curve around it like orbits around a dark star. The people do not explain it. They do not need to. They understand that some thresholds are not meant to be crossed, only witnessed. They sit until their breath matches the basin's, until their pulse slows to the rhythm of the dripping stone cups, until the hum in their bones fades into the larger quiet. Then they rise. They step back. They leave the mirror polished, untouched, waiting.

Over seasons, their presence becomes indistinguishable from the hollow's own habits. Their shed hairs catch in the lichen, becoming nesting down for the iridescent insects. Their discarded seed pods split and take root in the runnels. Their sweat salts the stone, altering the condensation patterns by fractions of a millimeter. When they sleep, curled in the lee of boulders beneath layered hides, their breath plumes and joins the sublimating frost. When they wake, they shake the ice from their garments and watch it vanish into the pale rock. They do not separate themselves from the cycle. They are another expression of it. Another lung. Another rhythm. Another way the basin breathes.

They leave as they arrive: without announcement, without trace. When the cold deepens and the clouds begin to snag and hold, when the stone cups freeze solid and the moss pulls inward, they unknit their camps. Cords are loosened. Windbreaks are shifted. Knives are wiped with violet fronds and returned to their niches. Bone beads are unstrung and buried in shallow runnels, where the thaw will carry them into the root-mats. They do not pack. They dissolve. They step back into the gradients, into the thresholds, into the thin, bright air that swallows sound and preserves motion. And when they are gone, the basin does not miss them. It simply continues.

The hares drift through the moss. The grazers pick their terraces. The predator flows along the ledge. The glass-winged birds bank wide. The condensation gathers, drips, freezes, melts. The light shifts from white to silver to cold blue. The stone cracks softly. The cords hum when the wind strikes true. The moss grows in concentric rings. The center remains bare, polished, dark.

They are not permanent. They are periodic. They live in the spaces between wet and dry, light and shadow, presence and absence. To call them visitors is to misunderstand the basin. To call them residents is to claim too much. They are the hollow's attention turned inward. Its patience given shape. Its silence given hands. They do not conquer the cold. They wear it. They do not hoard the water. They follow it. They do not break the quiet. They listen until the quiet speaks back.

And when the frost begins to weep again, when the first condensed drops fall into the stone cups, when the air thins and the light sharpens and the basin draws its next slow breath, they will emerge once more. Not as strangers. Not as owners. As another turn of the same relentless, unhurried grace.
