You do not find them by looking. You find them by waiting until your eyes stop hunting for motion and begin reading stillness. The first indication is not a shape, but a hesitation in the environment's rhythm. The filament-moss does not simply glow when the buried current pulses. It brightens a fraction of a second before the thrum arrives, as if drawing potential from the approaching wave rather than reacting to it. The glass-lichen on the drainage tower clicks in precise counterphase to the thermal contraction of the metal. The condensation on the flat surfaces does not slide away at random; it gathers, holds, and releases in synchronized sheets that match the hydraulic exhale kilometers distant. They are not passive colonizers. They are tuned.

Follow the weeping seams. Where alkaline condensate meets oxidized iron, the water does not merely run. It threads. Thin, gelid bridges span the gaps between rivet and flange, crossing millimeters over hours, then days, then seasons. They are not secreted. They precipitate. Dissolved copper, silicate dust, polymerized gasket residue, and trace electrolytes align along microcurrents, drawing themselves into conductive filaments that thicken with each cycle. When the potential difference peaks, the filament flashes—a cold, blue-white arc that leaves behind a vitrified vein. The vein does not die. It becomes scaffolding. New filaments branch from it, seeking the next gradient, the next weep, the next pulse. This is how they grow: not by division, but by accretion. Not by consuming, but by completing circuits.

They do not compete for territory. They compete for potential. Where two branching networks meet, they do not fight; they interfere. Their conductive paths cross, and the current hesitates, splits, recombines. In that hesitation, a new pattern emerges: a standing wave of ion flow that hums at a frequency just below hearing. The water around it begins to organize. Condensation no longer falls in random tears but cascades in geometric sequences. The acoustic pockets you felt beside certain pillars—the sudden, room-like quiet within the roar—are not accidents of erosion. They are resonant cavities maintained by these networks, tuned to amplify specific harmonics of the turbine thrum. Inside them, the air vibrates with such precision that metallic silt levitates, suspended in slow, rotating toroids. The silt does not settle. It orbits. It is being sorted, not by gravity, but by frequency.

They migrate, but not by walking. They migrate by shedding. When a network saturates a zone—when the local gradients flatten, when the weeping seams calcify, when the current grows stale—the outermost branches begin to desiccate. They crack into fine, conductive dust: flakes of vitrified oxide, polymerized resin, crystallized electrolyte, and ground ceramic. The dust does not blow away. It wicks. Drawn into capillary fractures by the same slow hydrology that built the place, it travels kilometers through the ceramic-concrete, riding the cooling vapor, settling in new depressions, new weeps, new potential wells. There, it waits. When the next arc-lamp flickers, when the next hydraulic press exhales, when the next pulse of buried current rises through the floor, the dust nucleates. A new network begins. Not offspring. A continuation.

Not all of them are mineral. Some are purely energetic, sustained by the place's relentless cycling. In the high dark, where gantries arc like fossilized ribs, static does not merely crawl. It pools. It gathers in the lee of insulated joints, building charge over hours until the damp air itself grows taut. Then, without sound, it discharges—not as a single spark, but as a slow, branching cascade that hangs in the moisture for seconds, tracing the same path again and again. Each pass ionizes the vapor. Each pass leaves behind a faint, ozone-rich channel. These channels persist. They become preferred paths. Over decades, they etch themselves into the atmosphere, invisible until the next discharge illuminates them: a ghostly, dendritic lattice hanging in the dark, breathing with the place's rhythm. They do not touch the ground. They do not need to. They feed on the differential between hot vapor and cold metal, on the friction of condensation, on the sheer, grinding continuity of the cycle. When the temperature shifts, they contract. When the pressure drops, they branch. They are weather made conductive.

They do not die. They plateau. When a network can no longer find a gradient, when its conductive veins grow too thick, too resistive, too balanced, it stops branching. It begins to shed its outer layers in smooth, curved plates, exactly like the sloughed casing of the support pylons. The plates fall into the shallow, mirror-still pools, where they sink slowly through the iridescent film of dissolved copper and old machine oil. They do not dissolve. They seed. The alkaline water leaches trace ions from them, altering the local chemistry just enough to shift the condensation patterns above. New weeps form. New gradients emerge. The cycle resets. What falls is not waste. It is topography.

There are larger coherences, though they move too slowly to be perceived as single entities. In the sunken turbine halls, where the bass thrum vibrates through the ribs, entire filter-beds have become catalytic matrices. They do not merely purge metallic silt; they arrange it. Layer by layer, stratum by stratum, they sort particles by conductivity, by mass, by resonant frequency, building laminated columns that act as natural capacitors. When the charge builds, the columns discharge upward through wicking towers, sending a slow pulse of ionized vapor into the high dark. The pulse triggers condensation. The condensation feeds the weeping seams. The seams feed the filaments. The filaments complete the circuit. It is not a machine. It is a metabolism. It does not burn fuel. It harvests difference.

To call them alive is to stretch the word until it snaps, and yet to call them inert is to misunderstand what persistence means when given enough time. They do not breathe. They do not bleed. They do not fear the dark or seek the light. They are the dark's logic made manifest. They are what happens when wear is allowed to iterate until it learns to anticipate, when erosion is given millennia to develop feedback loops, when a closed system runs so long that its exhaust becomes its architecture. They are not inhabitants of this basin. They are its weathering, its hydrology, its current, its sound, given temporary coherence by cycles older than memory. They do not occupy the place. They are the place, remembering how to continue.

When you stand in the acoustic pocket, when the roar parts around you like water, when the blue moss pulses in time with your own slowing breath, you are not witnessing life as you know it. You are standing inside a process that has achieved equilibrium so perfect it has begun to dream in gradients. It does not know you are there. It does not need to. It has already accounted for your weight, your heat, your displacement of vapor. The condensation on your boots will join the capillary flow. The static from your clothes will feed the high dark. Your footsteps will become part of the vibration that sorts the silt. You are not a visitor. You are a temporary fluctuation in a system that has forgotten how to stop. And when you leave, the networks will not mourn you. They will simply close the circuit you interrupted, shed what you disturbed, and continue.
