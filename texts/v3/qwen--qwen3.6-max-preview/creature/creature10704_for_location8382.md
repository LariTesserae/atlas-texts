You do not notice them at first. Not because they conceal themselves, but because the basin has no reason to distinguish between what is rock and what is still. They are folded into a narrow interstice near the center, where a tufa mound meets a fractured basalt column and a shallow pool holds the dim light like a held breath. At rest, they read as part of the stone: shoulders rounded to match the water-worn curve beneath them, spine aligned with the calcite lip, skin dusted with the same fine sinter that coats every surface here. Only the slow, nearly imperceptible lift of their ribs betrays them. Even that rhythm seems borrowed from the air, drawing moisture rather than oxygen, moving just enough to keep the loop closed.

When they shift, it is without announcement. Their body knows the exact tolerance of each slot, the precise angle to turn a knee, the moment to pause so a falling drop can strike stone instead of flesh. They are built low and dense, center of gravity sunk toward the floor, as if the basin's equilibrium has pulled them downward over time. There is no tension in their posture. No readiness. Muscles have relinquished the habit of bracing. Joints move with a quiet, lubricated ease, fed by the same mineral-rich damp that weeps from the walls. Their skin is not calloused, but resilient, crosshatched with fine crystalline traces that catch the humidity and glow dully. Where hair might have been, there is only pale, sparse down, or smoothness; insulation is meaningless where the air never chills and the stone never burns.

Their eyes are wide, the irises washed to the color of wet ash, pupils dilated to gather what little light filters through the vapor. They do not scan. They rest. Vision here is not for pursuit or avoidance, but for reading tension. They watch the surface of a pool and know, by the faintest dimpling, when a bead will detach from the overhang above. They track condension along a sinter bridge and feel, in their own breath, when the air has reached its threshold. Their hearing has thinned to the basin's quiet grammar: the papery whisper of evaporation, the soft displacement of warm air between pillars, the microscopic scratch of mites tracing their ancient seam. They do not listen for danger. There is none. They listen for balance. When it tilts—a boot scuffing a lip, a ripple crossing a mirror, a stone settling under unfamiliar weight—they adjust. Not to correct, but to absorb. A hand resting on a nearby mound. A shoulder leaning into a curve. A breath held until the water stills.

They do not eat as you would recognize it. The basin offers no harvest, no prey, no growing thing to break and consume. Instead, they drink from the shallow pools, palms cupped, water tasting of chalk, iron, and long settlement. Their body has learned to pull what it requires from the saturated air and the mineral film that coats every surface. Trace salts, dissolved silicates, the occasional desiccated wing or carapace that drifts down from the rim, caught and broken down with a patience that borders on geology. Their metabolism has slowed to match the hydrological circle. Heartbeats are spaced like the drops. Digestion is a continuous, quiet exchange, more diffusion than process. What leaves them is minimal, nearly inert, returned to the clay-lined trough or tucked into a dry seam where it will crust and vanish into the stone's slow memory. They do not hunger. They do not thirst. They persist.

Near their resting niche lie the marks you noticed: the low calcite-cemented wall, the cracked clay trough, the soot-stained overhang, the bone awl, the stiffened fiber loop. They do not use these things, but they keep them in place. Not as relics. Not as tools. As anchors. Sometimes, when the air feels particularly heavy, they run a thumb along the awl's smoothed curve. Not to remember who shaped it, but to feel the continuity of pressure across time. The trough still catches the same weep it always has. They clear it occasionally, not because it clogs, but because the gesture belongs to the rhythm here. They are not the first to stay. They may be the last. Or perhaps succession does not apply in a place where time pools rather than flows. Memory here does not accumulate; it settles. What they know is not stored in narrative, but in muscle, in breath, in the exact placement of a foot on a worn mineral lip. They do not count years. They count cycles of condensation. The weight of water. The slow return of stillness.

When you enter, they do not startle. The basin has already taken your measure: your footsteps swallowed, your breath added to the damp, your body's heat bleeding into the flat thermal field. They turn their head, not with curiosity or alarm, but with recognition. You are a gradient. A temporary imbalance. They watch you navigate the slots, note where your shoulder brushes stone, where your boot disturbs a pool's surface, where your voice catches in your throat because the quiet has no use for it. They do not judge. They wait. If you sit, they will eventually shift closer. Not to speak. Not to comfort. To share the warmth. Their presence is not social in the way of fires and stories. It is tectonic. Patient. They do not ask your name. They do not offer theirs. Names belong to places that change. Here, there is only the fact of being, held in the same thick air, breathing the same mineral sigh. If you reach out, they will let you touch their hand. It will feel like the stone: steady, skin-warm, unyielding but not hard. They will not pull away. They will not lean in. They will simply remain, until you leave or until you, too, begin to slow.

They are not a guardian. Not a hermit. Not a spirit bound to rock and water. They are what happens when a body stops resisting stillness. When need dissolves into balance. When the rhythm of a pulse learns to match the rhythm of a drop falling into a pool that never overflows. The basin did not create them. It accepted them. And in accepting, it reshaped them—cell by cell, breath by breath, until there was no seam left between inhabitant and home. They do not live here in the way creatures live elsewhere. They reside. They circulate. They are the basin's quiet answer to the question of what remains when everything else has finished becoming.

If you stay long enough, breathing the warm damp air, listening to the spaced and steady drops, feeling the stone hold you at exactly your own temperature, you will understand: they are not waiting for anything. They are not preserving anything. They are simply the place, given a heartbeat. And when you finally climb the gentle curve back to the rim, back to the wind and the seasons and the noise of a world that still demands becoming, you will carry with you the memory of a life that asked for nothing, changed nothing, and in that refusal, became complete.
