They do not arrive. They are already here, the way a stress-fracture is already here, the way a drip-line is already here. You notice them first as a darkening of the stone, a shift in the gleam where a body has rested long enough to leave a heat-shadow. Then as a trail of fine, alkaline dust that catches the pale light like ground salt. Then as a presence that does not startle when seen, because being seen changes nothing about the weight they carry or the path they follow.

Their frames are low and broad, built for distribution rather than velocity. Overlapping plates sheath their backs and shoulders, each layer ridged and curved in exact sympathy with the load-bearing lines of the cliffs and walkways. The plates are not armor. They are condensation engines. Moisture gathers along their raised margins, beads in the micro-grooves, and runs in thin, deliberate channels down to the softer tissue beneath, where it is drawn into capillary networks that pulse with a slow, tidal rhythm. The plates darken with age, not from decay but from accumulation: iron oxides, precipitated carbonates, trace silicates drawn from the weeping air and laid down like varnish. An elder's back is a topography of the city itself, mapped in patina.

Their limbs end in broad, splayed digits, grooved and padded, capable of reading the micro-texture of stone, rivet, and cable alike. When they step, they do not strike. They settle. Their weight finds the hollows worn by generations before them, and the hollows accept them without complaint. They do not avoid the moving parts of the city; they incorporate them. A foot placed beside a turning gear feels its rhythm through the stone. A palm rested against a conveyor frame matches its pace. They step over pistons and grazing herds with the same unthinking certainty, because machinery, flesh, and rock are not categories here. They are gradients.

Sight is secondary. The pale dome above does not change; the gleam does not falter. Instead, they listen through contact. The deep thrum of the cylinder-houses travels up through the walkways, through the pads of their feet, into the long bones of their legs, where it is translated into pressure, temperature, and flow. They feel the flywheels turning three tiers below. They feel the grindstones wearing down by fractions of a millimeter. They feel the capillary towers drawing water upward, a faint upward pull in the stone that tells them where the moisture will bead next, where the filament networks will thicken, where the black films will be richest. Their faces are broad and smooth, with slit-like apertures that flare and close with the humidity. When they breathe, it is a slow exchange of warm, mineral-laden air, drawing in trace ammonia and carbon from the grazing herds and the grinding houses, exhaling a faint, alkaline mist that settles on the nearby stone and feeds the precipitating threads.

They do not speak in words that break the air. Their language is tactile, vibrational, chemical. A palm pressed to a walkway railing transmits a pattern of pulses: pressure, release, drag, hold. It travels through the metal, through the stone, through the bodies of others resting nearby, and is answered in kind. A shift in posture, a change in the angle of the condensation plates, a deliberate scraping of a digit against a rivet line—these are syllables. Their conversations last hours, sometimes days, unfolding at the speed of corrosion. There is no urgency to be understood, because understanding is not a moment but a state. Miscommunication is rare; when it occurs, it is resolved not by argument but by alignment. They will stand together, palms on the same stone, breathing the same damp, until the vibration matches again.

They gather in the terraced hollows where the moisture collects in shallow, irregular pans. Not to debate, not to decide, but to synchronize. They sit in concentric rings, their plates overlapping slightly, their breathing falling into phase with the distant pistons and the slow clack of the conveyors. In these gatherings, they tend to one another. Brushes of stiff, mineral-tipped bristles sweep crystalline dust from joint seams. Fingers trace the wear patterns on load-bearing surfaces, noting where a plate has thinned, where a groove has deepened, where the stone beneath has begun to sigh. They adjust the tension of cable-woven straps, repack bearings with rendered slurry, apply fresh mortar to hairline fractures in the walkways. Maintenance is not labor. It is kinship. It is the way they say: *I am here. You are here. We continue.*

They eat as the stone eats: slowly, chemically, without waste. Their mouths are broad and flat, lined with rasping ridges that harvest the black and rust-colored films from weeping walls. They do not strip the rock bare; they graze in spiraling patterns that leave the filament networks intact, allowing the precipitates to reform. What they consume is broken down in internal chambers that function like slow kilns, extracting trace metals, binding carbon, releasing warmth. Their waste is not excreted but deposited: fine, alkaline pellets placed carefully into mortar joints and capillary runnels, where they dissolve over weeks, feeding the burrower colonies, strengthening the stone, and returning dissolved minerals to the weeping cycle. Nothing leaves the system. Nothing is lost. It is only rearranged.

They do not breed in seasons. Reproduction is a gradual merging, a sharing of mineral stores and microbial symbionts, a slow budding that takes years to complete. Two or more will rest together in a warm hollow, their condensation plates interlocking, their capillary networks bridging through shared moisture. Over months, tissue thickens at the seams. Mineral deposits accumulate. A new form begins to outline itself, not as a separate entity but as an extension, a branching. The young are not born; they accrete. They learn the stress-curves by feeling them through shared tissue before they ever take a step on their own. When they finally separate, it is not with a rupture but with a soft, wet parting, like stone splitting along a natural seam. They leave behind a smooth hollow, and the hollow weeps, and the weeping feeds the films, and the films feed the next.

They do not measure their lives in years. They measure them in wear. A plate darkened by decades of condensation. A groove deepened by ten thousand passes. A flywheel bearing that has been repacked with their own rendered slurry. A walkway that curves slightly differently because their weight, added to the weight of those before them, has persuaded the stone to settle a fraction further. They do not fear cessation as an ending; they understand it as a redistribution. When one of them slows beyond recovery, when the tidal pulse in their capillary networks fades, when the vibration in their bones no longer matches the city's breath, they do not fall. They settle. They find a hollow that matches their shape, fold their limbs, and let the damp take them. Over months, the mineral films creep over their plates. The burrowers thread through their joints. The stone absorbs their alkalinity, their warmth, their weight. They become part of the weeping. They become part of the load. And the city, which never expands, deepens around them.

There are no leaders here. No hierarchies that demand ascent. There are only those who have worn longer, who feel the deeper vibrations, who know where the stone is beginning to tire and where the iron is beginning to sing. They do not command; they indicate. A hand placed on a grinding housing that is running too fast. A body resting against a walkway that is bearing too much weight. A slow, deliberate scrape along a rivet line that needs fresh mortar. The others feel it. They adjust. The system corrects itself, not through decree but through resonance.

They do not dream of elsewhere. The concept does not take root in a mind that reads time as patina and space as load. The sky is a dome, not a destination. The haze at the edges of the shelf is not a boundary to be crossed but a gradient to be respected. They know, in the way stone knows pressure, that this is not a place to be left. It is a place to be circulated through. To move through it is to participate in it. To rest in it is to reinforce it. To die in it is to return to it.

When the light shifts, and the city changes its gleam, they shift with it. Their plates catch the damp. Their breath joins the mechanical sigh. Their weight settles into the ancient grooves, exactly where it belongs. They do not strive. They do not decay. They persist. They are the damp made deliberate. The stone made attentive. The machinery made flesh. And they live as the mountain lives: slowly, completely, without apology, breathing in time with the weeping rock, the turning wheels, the grazing herds, the patient dark, the endless, saturated now.
