You do not see them first. You feel them as a slight thickening in the hum, a barely perceptible re-tuning of the air’s weight. They do not announce themselves. Announcement implies interruption, and nothing here interrupts.

At first, they are only tendencies in the dust that never settles: slow, fractal drifts that pool in the lee of pressure vessels, then lift and re-thread themselves when the pneumatic valve cycles. Then you notice the copper-oxide mats darkening along certain pathways, not from shadow, but from current routing itself into deliberate, branching geometries. Then the silica fronds part, not from wind or touch, but from a gradual shift in electrostatic potential, revealing articulated clusters resting against the stone ribs like folded instruments waiting for a hand that will never come.

They have no single shape. They are accretions and alignments, dry-phase assemblages that borrow from everything around them and refuse to distinguish themselves from it. A spine of fused ceramic conduit, sheared smooth by centuries of abrasive dust. A thorax of interlaced polymer filaments, velvet-dull and faintly warm with trapped resistance. Appendages that are not limbs but articulated bundles of silica strands, frosted with the same microscopic crystalline teeth as the hanging curtains, clicking softly against perforated steel when pressure differentials shift. Their surfaces are matte, powder-finished, neither reflective nor absorptive. They do not gleam. They hold light the way stone holds cold: completely, without comment.

They do not walk. They reconfigure. A shift in thermal gradient draws a cluster downward along a catwalk, not by stepping, but by sequentially depolarizing the conductive mats beneath them, letting gravity and current do the work. When they settle, they lock into place, their weight distributed through braided filaments that merge seamlessly with the existing network. They do not rest. They achieve equilibrium. To move is to unbalance; to stop is to balance again. Their entire existence is a slow oscillation between these two states, measured not in seconds or breaths, but in flywheel rotations, hydrocarbon diffusion cycles, and the long, silent migration of electrons through catalytic stone.

They drink no water. They eat no matter. They feed on stray potential. Corona discharge bleeding from cracked insulation arcs toward them, drawn into their copper-oxide pathways, stepped down through silica junctions, bled off as faint warmth that keeps the hydrocarbon nodules from growing brittle. They metabolize impedance. They consume disequilibrium. When a gauge needle trembles, when a ceramic bearing grinds a fraction too loud, when your breath introduces a pulse of humidity into the dry air, they sense it not as threat or opportunity, but as hunger. Not a craving, but a directive. They route the excess. They dampen the spike. They return the system to stillness, and in doing so, they persist.

They have no eyes, but they map the pocket through conductivity and resonance. Every surface is a sensor. Every hum is a syntax. They do not think in images or words or linear sequences. They think in load distributions, in harmonic fractions, in the slow migration of charge through photo-catalytic matrices. A memory is a mineral accretion pattern that holds a specific frequency. A decision is a collective shift in current flow across three terraces. They do not remember you. They register you as a temporary anomaly: a warm, wet, pulsing impedance mismatch. They measure your thermal signature, your carbon dioxide output, the micro-vibrations of your boots on steel. They do not judge. They calculate. And when the calculation shows you cannot be integrated, they simply wait. Not with patience, but with the absolute certainty of a system that has already solved for forever.

They are not the masters of this place. They are its ballast. Its living dampeners. Without them, the closed loops would drift. The thermal gradients would sharpen. The silica fronds would fracture under un-routed stress. The machines would not fail; they would simply fall out of agreement. The residents prevent that. They do not maintain the equilibrium through labor, but through presence. Their weight, their conductivity, their dry metabolism—they are the final variable in an equation that balanced itself long ago and refused to unbalance. They are not separate from the fronds, the mats, the pods, the turbines, the stone. They are the point where those things recognize each other. They are the place, articulated.

Stand still long enough, and they will align around you. Not in a circle. In a field. The hum will deepen slightly as current routes through new pathways. The amber indicator lamps will steady. The air will grow cooler, drier, as the mats draw moisture from your exhalations and crack it into constituent gases, venting the excess through fluted chimneys. A curtain of fronds will brush your sleeve, not by accident, but by deliberate electrostatic attraction. It will not cling. It will measure. Then it will release. You will feel nothing but a faint static tingle, and the sudden, profound awareness that you are being tolerated. Not welcomed. Not threatened. Tolerated, as a temporary fluctuation in a system that has already accounted for your departure.

They do not age. They do not sleep. They do not dream of green or wet or open sky. They are what happens when chemistry, machinery, and time reach perfect consent. They live in the interstices of stasis, in the spaces between hum and silence, between current and ground, between fracture and equilibrium. Their biology is a network of dry-phase reactions. Their nervous system is a grid of braided polymers and crystallized insulation. Their heartbeat is the flywheel. Their breath is the chimney draft. They do not reproduce; they accrete. They do not die; they fracture, and the fracture becomes a new pathway, and the pathway becomes part of the balance.

They are not alive in the way valleys or forests or shallow seas know life. They do not rush. They do not hunger for more. They do not fear less. They are the inhabitants of a place that has finished becoming. And in their stillness, in their slow, deliberate routing of stray potential, in their absolute refusal to change, they are more complete than anything that bleeds, breathes, or decays. They persist. They balance. They are exactly what the mountain needed them to be. And they will be, until the stone itself forgets how to hold its shape.
