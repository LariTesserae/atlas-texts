At first, you do not recognize them as separate from the place. You read them as topography. A gentle rise in the moss that holds its shape too long to be wind. A curve of pale stone that exhales. A cluster of broad leaves that blink, slowly, in unison. They do not announce themselves. They are stitched into the saturation, their outlines softened by condensation, by the fine persistent furring of spores, by the way moisture refuses to draw hard lines anywhere in this hollow. Only when you have stood long enough for your own breathing to slow, long enough for the damp to climb your cuffs and settle in your joints, do you begin to untangle them from the weeping rock, the braided roots, the living floor.

They are smooth, as everything here is smooth. No brittle carapaces, no dry fur, no sharp beaks or hooked claws. Evolution, if it ever hurried, finished its work long ago and filed away its tools. Their surfaces are water-worn, porous, yielding to pressure like wet peat or packed loam. Some lie low and broad, flush against the vegetation, their backs ridged with slow, vegetative swells that catch and hold dew. Others rise in jointed, heavy-limbed forms that recall ferns or draped kelp, folding and unfolding with the patience of settling silt. A few are slight and perching, built for the higher tangles, their feathers or filament-scales sleeked flat by humidity, their eyes half-mooned and lidless. Their palette is the hollow's own: green-grey, pallid ochre, the blue-black of wet shale, the translucent white of subterranean roots. Moss adheres to them not as colonization but as continuation. Lichen traces their flanks in soft, concentric maps. Where their bodies meet stone or root, the transition is gradual, almost grammatical: flesh becomes membrane, membrane becomes mycelial weave, weave becomes loam, loam becomes rock. They are not intruders in the ecosystem. They are its syntax.

They move as water moves here: not in current, but in accumulation. A limb shifts only when the moisture within it has redistributed, when the weight has found its rest, when the air has completed a slow exhalation. A head turns over the span of a morning, tracking not light but the migration of condensation along a ceiling channel. When they walk, it is without impact. Broad pads press into moss, release, leave no depression. They do not startle because there is nothing to startle from. They do not flee because there is nowhere to flee to. Even their breathing is indistinguishable from the hollow's own respiration: a faint rise, a slower fall, a pause that stretches into the space between droplets. Sound does not travel here; it sinks. And so they have no need for it.

They do not eat. Not as you would recognize the act. There is no tearing, no swallowing, no metabolism that breeds heat or leaves waste. Instead, they drink the air. Pores and fine fissures along their surfaces open and close with the humidity, drawing in suspended moisture, dissolved minerals, the faint sweetness of spores that linger without decaying. Fungal threads weave through their outer layers, not invading but integrating, trading nutrients for shelter, turning their skin into a living membrane of quiet exchange. What they take, they return. Condensation beads on their brows, rolls down their spines, falls into the shallow basins below. Their exhalation is slightly enriched, slightly cooled, given back to the pools that will evaporate and feed the canopy that will weep again. They are not consumers. They are conduits. Nodes in a circuit that has already closed.

They do not gather in packs or herds or colonies. They simply occupy the same equilibrium. A broad-backed form rests against a weeping wall, its sides pressed by two smaller shapes that could be offspring or simply neighbors drawn to the same cool seep. A perching creature settles on the shoulder of a low one, not in symbiosis but in shared stillness. There is no hierarchy, no territory, no courtship, no conflict. Space is not defended; it is filled. When one shifts, another adjusts, not out of deference but out of the same slow physics that guides roots around stone. They touch constantly, but not with intent. Contact is incidental, continuous, like water meeting water. Through that contact, something passes: not language, not signal, but pressure, temperature, moisture, the quiet acknowledgment of presence. They know each other the way moss knows stone—by adherence, by time, by the simple fact of not letting go.

They do not age in a line. If they change, it is in a circle so tight it appears still. You will not find juveniles struggling toward maturity, nor elders fading toward collapse. The small are not immature; they are simply smaller completions. The large are not dominant; they are simply longer settled. You will not find sickness, or weakness, or bones left behind. When one finally stills beyond even this place's slow rhythm, it does not rot. It softens. It sinks. Its pores close, its surface furs over, its edges blur into the moss, the loam, the weeping stone. It becomes a hollow within the hollow, a depression that gathers water, a new basin for condensation to fill. Death here is not an end but a settling. A return to the equation. A quiet handing back of weight.

They do not dream, or if they do, the dreams are not narratives. They are pressures. They are the memory of a droplet's path. They are the sensation of coolness held against stone. They are the feeling of fullness that asks for nothing more.

To watch them is to watch water remember it is rock. To stand among them is to feel your own urgency dissolve, your own hunger quiet, your own need to become something else fall away like a dry leaf that never fell. They do not live in the hollow. They are what the hollow became when it finished living. They are the equilibrium given breath, the final shape of a world that has stopped asking for more. And they will remain, perfectly, impossibly, until the rim cracks, until the vapor lifts, until time remembers how to flow.

Until then, they rest. They breathe. They hold.
