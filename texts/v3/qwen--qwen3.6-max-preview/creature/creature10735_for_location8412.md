They do not arrive. They are already there, suspended in the wet light like breath held in a lung. To call them creatures is to mistake the pulse for the heart. The low-slung grazers are the first to reveal themselves, though they leave no trace. Their bodies are built for displacement without disturbance. Broad, splayed toes press into the moss like hands reading braille, sensing the water table's rise and fall through capillary tremors. They do not graze in the old sense. Their muzzles are lined with keratinous wicks, porous and honeycombed, that draw moisture and dissolved nutrients directly from the weeping stone and saturated ground cover. When they drink, they are not consuming but exchanging: returning warmth, carbon, and subtle electrical charge to the soil. Their eyes are wide, lidless, adapted to the directionless glow. They see in gradients of humidity and thermal drift. A shift in the air is not weather to them; it is a change in the basin's mood, felt in the joints, answered by a slow, collective turning toward the center. They move in loose, uncoordinated constellations, never crowding, never competing. Space is not defended here. It is shared like breath.

They do not flap because the hollow does not require it. The air here is stratified, layered by temperature and ion density, and they read these layers like currents. They are gardeners of the atmosphere, pruning thermal eddies, translating the basin's slow exhalations into lift. Polarized light pulses ripple through their compound eyes, synchronized across thousands of bodies, creating interference patterns that map the hollow's moisture gradients, nutrient flows, and subtle seismic shifts. Beneath that, the subsonic thrum: a vibration so low it bypasses the ear and settles in the marrow. It is not a call. It is a consensus. When a branch falls, the swarm shifts. They cluster around the geode-nodes when the soil grows too alkaline, dispersing when balance returns. They are the hollow's thoughts, distributed, wordless, continuous. The predators wait on the low ridges, but waiting is not passive. Their stillness is a form of listening. Lean, long-limbed, their muscles are wound like springs but calibrated to release without shock. Obsidian eyes do not reflect light; they absorb it, reading the minute disturbances in the luminous haze. They do not hunt by sight or scent alone. They hunt by absence. A gap in the subsonic field. A hesitation in the moisture climb. A shadow that lags half a second too long. When they move, it is not an attack but a correction. The kill is swift, silent, and immediately absorbed. There is no waste, no struggle, no alarm. The prey's body is already unraveling before it touches the moss, filaments threading into it, drawing it back into the cycle. The predators do not dominate; they prune. They are the hollow's immune system, removing imbalance before it becomes noise. Afterward, they return to the ridges, press their forepaws to the stone, and let the nodes read their metabolic state. They are fed, but they are also measured. And they accept this without question.

Their broad leaves are solar stills and acoustic dampeners, shaped to catch light, condense vapor, and swallow vibration. When they tip and shed water, it is not gravity alone. It is timing. Each drop falls where the moss signals need. The trailing vines are not climbers; they are bridges. Their beaded droplets conduct subtle currents between canopy and floor, linking root systems, sharing stress signals, distributing micronutrients. The emerald moss is not ground cover. It is a living filter, a mycelial-electronic matrix that interfaces with the geode-nodes, reading chemistry, adjusting porosity, pacing the decay-rebirth cycle. Those half-buried nodes are not machines left behind. They are organs. Grown, not built. They speak in light, in vibration, in moisture gradients, in thermal drift. They think in cycles, not moments. They do not compete because competition is a failure of calibration. Memory is not stored in brains but in the soil's ion balance, in the nodes' fractal branching, in the way certain outcrops weep faster after a long dry phase, in the gliders' inherited knowledge of thermal lanes.
