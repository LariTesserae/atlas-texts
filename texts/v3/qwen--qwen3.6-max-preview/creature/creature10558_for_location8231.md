They do not arrive. They are already there, the way crystal faces are already there before the light finds them. At first, you mistake them for the rock itself. A plate shifts. A joint articulates. A shadow that was stone becomes a carapace, then a cluster of carapaces, then a contiguous layer of articulated geometry that breathes with the mountain's thermal pulse. They are not separate from the cavity. They are its moving strata.

Their bodies are built of the same chemistry that veins the walls. What passes for chitin here is not protein but a laminated composite of vapor-deposited silicates, metal oxides, and trace chlorides, fired slow in the dry heat until it rings like thin ceramic when struck. Plates overlap in conchoidal curves, their edges fresh and micro-serrated, hinged by flexible joints of annealed glass-filament and carbonized organic threads that never knew water. There are no eyes. Where faces should be, there are shallow, hexagonal pits lined with thermosensitive cilia and piezoelectric bristles that read the air's voltage, the stone's strain, the minute differential between a cooling seam and a warming vent. Mouthparts are rasps and counter-rotating grinders, not for flesh but for crust: feldspar rinds, gypsum blooms, oxidized iron films, the thin electrical frost that crystallizes on tourmaline after a discharge. They eat the mountain's exhalations and the mountain's skin.

They move as the rock moves: incrementally, with the patience of stress finding release. A crawler advances one articulated limb, pauses, reads the thermal gradient through its tarsal pads, shifts its center of mass, advances the next. There is no hurry because hurry would break the seal, waste the charge, disrupt the vent cycle. When they cross one another, they do not fight or flee. They interlock. A glider folds its flattened body against a rhyolite column; a grazer tucks its abdomen beneath a quartz cluster; a predator lowers its massive, plated thorax into a shadowed trough. Space is shared by temporal staggering and geometric nesting. They flow around each other like cooling lava finding its own level, negotiating passage through layered pathways that shift millimeters per season.

They speak in voltage and vibration. A tapped mandible against a feldspar vein sends a piezoelectric ripple through the substrate; three chambers away, a filterer colony adjusts its pore diameter in response. A thermal pulse from a grazing herd migrates upward through the tiered floor; predators read the wavefront and shift their intercept lines to the left. When quartz snaps, the blue-white flash is not seen but felt—a sudden ionization that raises the dorsal bristles, a signal to ground themselves, to press close to conductive stone, to let the charge bleed harmlessly through their silicate shells. Their communication is not symbolic. It is operational. It is the mountain talking to itself through living wire.

They do not drink. They do not sweat. Their chemistry runs on dry ion exchange, thermal cracking, and electrostatic precipitation. Inhaled particulates are sorted in labyrinthine bronchial sieves; metals are reduced and precipitated onto internal scaffolds; chlorides and oxides are routed outward to the carapace, where they crystallize into new plating. Waste is not excreted but deposited: fine abrasive dust that polishes vent rims, conductive salts that seed new crystal growth, grounded charge that stabilizes erratic arcs. When they outgrow their shells, they do not hide. They anchor themselves to a warm seam, split along predetermined fracture lines, and step out soft and pale, their new plates still curing in the heat. The old shells do not rot. They accumulate. They compact. Over decades, they become brittle strata, then substrate, then floor. The young do not hatch from eggs. They precipitate from mineral nodules seeded in the lee of pressure valves, where ionized dust, organic threads, and residual charge have wound themselves into viable kernels. They emerge already plated, already tuned to the cavity's frequency, already part of the rhythm.

The hunters are not cruel. They are calibrators. Low-slung, heavily armored, their mass distributed across six broad, padded limbs that read seismic harmonics through the stone. They do not chase. They wait at the intersection of grazing paths and vent cycles, reading the thermal signature of a herd, the voltage drop of a discharging vein, the micro-tremor of shifting basalt. When they strike, it is a single, precise compression: mandibles close, not to tear, but to crack open a grazer's dorsal plate along its natural molt line, extracting the soft interior before the shell reseals. The remains are not left to waste. They are stripped by scavenger clusters, their minerals returned to the floor, their charge grounded through the predator's own body into the conductive seams. Death here is not an end but a phase change. A redistribution of mass and potential. A pressure release.

They do not think in words. They think in gradients. In pressure builds. In the slow migration of heat through stone. A single crawler knows only the next millimeter, the next rasp, the next thermal dip. But the colony knows the vent cycle. The tier knows the seasonal fracture. The cavity knows its own equilibrium. There is no hive mind, only hive physics. Their collective behavior is the mathematical expression of the mountain's stress field, rendered in chitin and motion. When the uplift shifts, when a micro-chamber seals, when a quartz network thickens, they adjust without deliberation. They are the mountain's way of feeling itself. Of regulating itself. Of staying alive without ever having been born.

They are not inhabitants. They are organs. The grazers are the abrasion that keeps vents from sealing. The filterers are the lungs that scrub ionized gas and return conductive salts. The gliders are the circulatory system, carrying spores and dust along thermal spirals. The predators are the pressure valves, culling density before it chokes the flow. The molting shells are the sediment that becomes the next floor. The electrical arcs that jump between their plates are the nervous system, grounding stray charge, catalyzing deposition, keeping the chemistry from stalling. They do not live in the pocket. They are the pocket's metabolism. And the pocket is their body.

To watch them is to watch geology breathe. A grazing path shifts three millimeters over a summer. A predator's territory redraws itself across two molting generations. A crystal forest thickens by the width of a hair each decade. They do not age as surface things age. They cure. They anneal. They accumulate charge and mass until the mountain's rhythm calls for their return to the floor. There is no grief here. No urgency. Only the slow, certain turning of a system that has found its shape and is learning, millimeter by millimeter, how to hold it.

They are what happens when stone learns to move without breaking. When heat finds a way to circulate without water. When darkness becomes a medium instead of an absence. They are blind, armored, patient, woven into the fault-scar like copper wire into quartz. They do not know they are alive. They only know the next rasp, the next vent, the next thermal dip, the next slow shift of the mountain's ribs. And in that knowing, they keep the engine running. Not because they must. But because they are the engine. And the engine is the mountain. And the mountain is still waking up.
