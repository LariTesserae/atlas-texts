They do not announce themselves. You notice them first as a redistribution of weight on a terrace, a sudden stillness in a rope line that has been humming the same low tension for years, a shift in the way dust settles across a sunken plaza. They move the way condensation moves: gathering, pausing, reading the slope, yielding to gravity without ever seeming to hurry. Their passage leaves no echo. Their bodies are wrapped, padded, or naturally dampened so that even a quick descent down a worn stairwell sounds like a palm brushing wet linen. They are sparse, but never alone. A figure on an upper tier adjusts a woven baffle; three levels down, another feels the change in airflow and slides a flat stone into a trickle-channel. The city breathes through them. They are its current arrangement.

To look at them directly is to watch the environment clarify itself into anatomy. They are lean, built for leverage and balance rather than speed, with long, multi-jointed fingers that know the exact pressure required to seat a stone without mortar, to retwist a fraying cord until it gleams like horn, to trace the hairline fracture in a clay pipe and seal it with resin and ground shell. Their skin is not a single texture but a record of contact: pale and cool where it meets the air, mottled with mineral salts where it brushes weeping walls, crosshatched with fine, fibrous layers that shed moisture like the underside of a fungal shelf. These layers are not clothing in the overworld sense; they are grown, woven, and molted in cycles, harvested from pale reed-composites and cured biofilm mats, retwisted and rewrapped until they become a second topography. Their eyes are wide, shielded by translucent nictitating lids that diffuse the bright, directionless light. Their pupils do not contract to darkness but to shifts in humidity, thermal draft, and the faint polarized shimmer of drifting spores. They do not blink often. They watch the air.

Speech, as surfaces know it, would drown here. The porous stone drinks it. The damp, silt-heavy air swallows it. So they have learned to speak through what remains. A tap of a copper-edged chisel against a load-bearing lintel sends a vibration through the terrace that blooms three levels down as a concentric shiver in a catchment basin. They read rope tension like sentences. They mark thresholds with patterns of mineral dust and spore-fall, swept into temporary glyphs that mean *channel clear*, *glider tide rising*, *stone settling*, *rest here*. Their hands are rarely still when they are near one another; they sign, they trace, they press palms to shared stone to feel the minute tremors of work being done elsewhere. When they do vocalize, it is low, resonant, felt in the ribcage more than heard: a hum that matches the frequency of dripping water, a click that mimics detritivore mandibles, a sharp intake of breath through teeth to signal a shift in the mist. Sound is not projected. It is placed. It travels through stone, through rope, through the soles of padded feet. It moves sideways.

Their labor is not construction but conversation. They do not build walls; they persuade stone to hold. They do not dig wells; they listen for where the air weeps and carve channels to meet it. A cycle begins not with light but with the thickening of condensation on the mica-lined shafts. They move out with baskets and wooden mallets, not to conquer the hours, but to attend to them. They sweep fine silt from catchment basins before it chokes the iridescent biofilm. They adjust clay counterweights on pulley lines as the temperature drops and the braided cords contract. They harvest pale fungal crusts not by cutting, but by lifting edges and allowing the spores to fall into woven trays, leaving the mycelial mat intact to weep again. They follow the glider migrations not to hunt, but to read the thermal maps the flocks draw in the air, adjusting shade-cloths and wind-scoops to keep the lower terraces cool enough for the crustacean pulses in the channels. Nothing is taken without a return. A shed pearlescent carapace is ground into pipe sealant. A frayed haul-line is unraveled, retwisted, and hung as a moisture-wick. A collapsed roof is not rebuilt; its stones are rearranged into a draft-guide that shepherds the next faunal tide. They do not manage the ecology. They participate in it.

They do not count years. They count saturations. A life is measured in how many times one has retied the main grid-line, how many condensation cycles one has read in the salt-crust patterns, how many silt-glyphs one has swept and reswept. There are no rulers, only keepers of tension. Authority is not held; it is borrowed by whoever's hands are closest to the failing joint, the drying channel, the fraying cord. Knowledge lives in the muscles, in the calluses, in the way an elder's thumb automatically tests the give of a travertine step before weight is committed. Kinship is not bloodline but water-line: those who share a catchment, who maintain the same rope grid, who read the same mist patterns. When a young one learns to walk, they are not taught to run; they are taught to feel the draft beneath a terrace, to place a foot so it does not disturb the detritivore trails, to listen to the stone through their soles. Inheritance is not property. It is responsibility for a specific curve of wall, a specific pulley, a specific weeping seam. To be given a section of the city is to be given a conversation that began long before you and will continue long after.

They do not fear erosion. They recognize it as the place breathing. A sagging lintel is not a failure; it is an invitation to redistribute weight. A courtyard filling with silt is not abandonment; it is a new basin waiting for the next moisture cycle. Their deepest reverence is reserved for water, but not as a resource to be stockpiled. Water is memory. It carries the taste of the upper shafts, the dissolved salts of ancient evaporations, the exhalations of stone, the spores of fungal crusts, the shed skins of filter-feeders. To waste it is to break a thread. To channel it is to weave. They believe nothing is truly still. Even the deepest bedrock is settling, weeping, adjusting. Permanence is not resistance; it is the skill of moving with what moves. When they die, they are not buried. They are laid on high, porous shelves where the condensation gathers, where the blind detritivores and lace-like fungal mats can do their quiet work, returning them to the mineral loop within a season. Their bones become part of the silt. Their braided cords are retwisted by other hands. Their names are not spoken aloud; they are felt in the tension of the ropes they maintained, in the smoothness of the steps they walked, in the way a particular channel still weeps exactly where they taught it to.

To live here is to become a verb. They do not occupy the terraces; they are the terraces' current posture. When the light shifts and the fine mist rolls through the lower colonnades, they move with it, adjusting, sweeping, tying, listening. They are the quiet hands that keep the negotiation going. They are the reason the ropes still hold, the channels still weep, the stone still breathes. They do not conquer the bright underworld, because there is nothing to conquer. They do not fight time, because time is the water on their skin, the dust under their feet, the slow, luminous settling of everything into everything else. They are here. They are adjusting. They will be here tomorrow, doing the same, which is not repetition, but continuation. The place does not need them to endure. But it has learned their rhythm. And they have learned to wear it, as the stone wears the water, as the water wears the stone, bright and quiet and endlessly in motion.
