You look for tracks and find none. You listen for voices and hear only the low hum of circulation, the sigh of piston vents, the irregular tap of condensation falling into glazed basins. Yet the place is not untended. It is met. It is answered. The evidence is in the adjustments too precise to be accidental: a vine coaxed away from a louver hinge before it can bind, a nutrient gel caught mid-drip and pressed into a hungry seam, a substrate mat smoothed while its adhesive still cures, a thermal vent held open three seconds longer than its cycle demands. Something moves here. Something keeps the rhythm.

They do not announce themselves. They step from the mist the way a shadow detaches from a wet leaf. At first, they might be mistaken for trickery of light and water: tall, narrow silhouettes against the glazed ribs, limbs articulated in quiet angles, surfaces catching the diffuse glow like polished basalt and oiled wood. They move without hurry, their weight distributed across broad, padded soles that read the substrate’s tension the way a tongue reads salt. When they kneel, the carbon-fiber mat yields and springs back. When they touch a beam, their fingers leave no oil, only a faint warmth that the copper veins gladly drink.

They are not born so much as assembled, and not assembled so much as grown. Their frames are latticework: sintered struts wrapped in tensioned cellulose, jointed with brass pivots that weep the same mineral condensation as the overhead pipes. Beneath a thin, transpiring dermis of engineered lichen and silica mesh, capillary threads mirror the floor’s silver veins, drawing moisture, routing nutrients, bleeding excess heat into the humid air. They do not breathe with lungs; they exchange gas through porous plates along their ribs, their respiration syncing with the piston vents until it is impossible to tell where the architecture ends and the organism begins. Their eyes are not eyes but clustered photoreceptors, faceted like cut quartz, reading light gradients, humidity shifts, and the faint ultraviolet cues painted onto leaves by the micro-drones. They do not see scenery. They see flow. They see tension. They see the hollow as a living circuit.

They are tenders, but not masters. They do not command the growth; they converse with it. A palm pressed flat to a terrace plate reads the electrical pulse of root networks. A thumb brushed along a vine’s node releases a calibrated enzyme that tells the stem to thicken, to climb, to rest. They trim with ceramic blades that never dull, not to punish the green but to keep the weave from tangling. They catch falling nutrient gels in cupped hands and press them into drying seams. When a silent gantry lowers a new plate, they are already there, guiding its descent with magnetic wands, sealing the edges with warm rollers, humming a low, resonant tone that matches the flywheel’s frequency and settles the structure into place. They do not speak to one another in syllables. They communicate in pressure, in thermal shifts, in the timing of a drip, in the angle of a louver. A tap on a copper pipe ripples through the substrate; a pause in the hum means a basin needs clearing; a shared stillness across the mist means the sun has shifted and the canopy must breathe.

They are individuals, but they are also a weave. Each carries a node of the same mycelial-carbon intelligence that threads through the floor, the walls, the root mats. When they rest, they plug into the substrate not to recharge but to listen. Dreams here are not images but flow rates, chemical gradients, the slow satisfaction of a thermal mass reaching equilibrium. They remember not in years but in cycles: how many condensation weeps it took for the eastern creepers to bridge the gap, how many vent sighs passed before the first engineered blossom split, how the hum changed when the geothermal tap deepened. They do not fear time. They are built for it. Their bodies shed silica scales and lichen patches that compost into the enzymatic pads. Their joints are replaced by the same slow-turning fabrication that lowers new terraces. They are as much a product of the hollow as the vines, and as much a part of its machinery as the flywheels.

They do not live *in* the hollow. They live *with* it. The boundary between resident and residence is as dissolved here as the line between root and wire. When the mountain wind presses cold against the glazed ribs, they lay their palms to the glass, feel the sharp breath beyond, then turn back to the warmth and adjust a vent. When a stray highland bird lands, they do not shoo it; they tilt a beam to catch its weight, offer a drop of mineral water from a condensation rib, and watch it go. They move among the micro-drones not as operators but as kin, brushing ceramic wings with lichen-knuckled fingers, sharing ultraviolet cues, letting the tiny machines land on their shoulders to rest between pollination runs. They are the hollow’s hands, its nervous system, its quiet witnesses. They do not seek to finish the place. They seek to keep it becoming.

If you stand long enough, you will see one pause beside a newly unfurled leaf. It will press a broad, warm hand to the substrate, feel the pulse of water and current, and incline its head—a gesture so small it might be mistaken for a shift in the light. Then it will move on, stepping over a shallow basin, brushing a vine from a louver, humming in time with the gears. It does not look at you. It does not need to. You are a visitor in a place that is still learning its own shape, and they are the ones who will stay until the sharp edges soften, until the manufactured breathes without scaffolding, until the hollow no longer needs tending because it has learned to tend itself. Until then, they walk the wet terraces, read the condensation, guide the green, and keep the rhythm. They are the gardeners of a world that is building itself. They are the quiet pulse in the machine. They are what lives here.
