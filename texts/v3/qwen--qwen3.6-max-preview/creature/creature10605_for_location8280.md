You do not see them at first. You feel them in the air before you know they are there: a slight thickening of the mist, a change in the pitch of the wind threading through perforated stone, a shadow that does not belong to any tower. They do not announce themselves. They arrive the way condensation arrives—slowly, inevitably, drawn to the right temperature, the right surface, the right moment in the cycle.

When one finally resolves out of the haze, it is difficult to say where the city ends and the creature begins. It might unfold from a weeping seam in the basalt, or descend along a tension cable like a bead of mercury finding its path, or simply detach from the underside of a broad eave where it had been resting, indistinguishable from the mineral crusts. Its body is not solid but layered: a stack of porous, overlapping plates, pale as fired clay and veined with capillary channels that drink the humid air. Between the plates, rubbery membranes pulse, swelling with absorbed moisture, then contracting as internal heat draws the water upward through hollow chimneys. It has no face, no limbs in the familiar sense. Instead, it has surfaces. Broad, curved vanes that catch thermal updrafts. Frilled edges that split the air into laminar flows. A resonant core that hums in sympathy with the basin's low frequencies.

It moves the way the place breathes. Not with steps, but with shifts in balance, with the slow transfer of weight and water and heat. When the air grows drier, it folds inward, sealing its pores, becoming stone-like and still. When the mist returns, it opens, drinking, swelling, rising slightly on the cushion of its own exhaled vapor. It does not walk; it relocates by degrees, following capillary gradients and thermal ridges, pausing for hours where condensation beads thickest, then sliding along glassy slopes when the stone sweats. Sometimes it climbs. Not with claws, but with adhesion and patience, pressing its ventral plates against weeping walls until capillary action draws it upward, millimeter by millimeter, until it reaches a condensation lip and hangs there, dripping, listening.

It does not eat as animals eat. It feeds on phase change. On the latent heat released when vapor becomes liquid, on the dissolved salts left behind when water evaporates, on the slow oxidation of iron and the crystallization of silica. Its membranes host pale, rubbery growths that are not parasites but extensions, or perhaps symbionts, or perhaps the same thing wearing different names. They filter minerals from the weeping stone, convert thermal gradients into chemical tension, and pass the energy inward through capillary veins. Waste is not excreted but deposited: thin crusts of calcite and iron oxide that flake away and become part of the mineral gardens below. Nothing is lost. Everything cycles. The creature is less a consumer than a conduit, a temporary configuration of the basin's ongoing exchange.

They do not speak. They tune. When two meet in the open space between towers, they do not touch. They adjust their internal resonances, shifting the pitch of the air moving through their hollow cores until their frequencies align. The stone answers. The tension cables hum. The wind through the perforated walls changes key. Information is not exchanged in words but in harmonics, in humidity gradients, in the timing of drips. A shift in convection tells of a distant valve opening. A change in the mineral taste of the mist speaks of a new crust forming three terraces down. They perceive the city as a single instrument, and they are its players, or perhaps its strings, or perhaps simply the vibrations themselves made briefly visible.

They do not age in years. They accrete. They shed. They dissolve and reform. When a body grows too heavy with mineral load, too stiff with crystalline deposits, it finds a hollow chimney or a sun-warmed basin and begins to unmake itself. The membranes dry and split. The porous plates crack along predetermined seams. Internal pressures build until the structure fractures, collapsing into a heap of ceramic shards, silica fans, and desiccated tissue. But this is not death. It is redistribution. The shards become nucleation sites for new condensation. The silica fans seed the next generation of membranes. The residual salts draw moisture from the air, swelling dormant nodules embedded in the stone. Weeks later, or years, or decades—the distinction means little here—a new form begins to knit itself together from the weeping walls, drawing on the same capillary networks, the same thermal rhythms, the same patient geometry. They are not born. They precipitate.

They do not own the city. They do not maintain it. They participate in it. The original builders designed the towers for processing air and water, but time and chemistry did the rest. Pipes became veins. Valves became hearts. Condensation cycles became blood. These creatures are what happens when a machine is left to breathe long enough to dream. They are the city's metabolism made mobile, or the stone's memory made flesh, or the boundary between geology and biology worn so thin it no longer holds. They do not seek to change the place. They seek to match it. To move with its convection, to drink from its weeping seams, to hum at its resonant frequency. When a pump engages in the distance, they tilt their vanes to catch the shifted current. When a thermal lens bends the light, they adjust their internal density to remain visible to one another. When the mist falls warm and hissing, they open every pore and let it fill them.

They are rare only if you measure by the span of a human glance. Look longer, and they are everywhere: clinging to the undersides of catwalks, resting in the shallow hollows where mineral-rimmed pools gather, riding the slow rivers of heated air between towers, their heat-shimmer wings bleeding excess warmth into the haze. They do not flock. They do not call. They appear, traverse a gap, drink, and vanish into the pale atmosphere, leaving behind only a shifted drip pattern, a faint chill on the stone, a resonance that lingers in the teeth.

To watch them is to unlearn urgency. They do not hunt. They do not flee. They do not build nests or hoard resources or mark territory. They simply exist in the intervals between drips, in the slow rise of heated air, in the patient accretion of mineral and moisture. They are as much a part of the basin as the glassy stone, the weeping towers, the sighing valves. And if you stand long enough, letting the humidity settle on your skin, listening to the flute-tones of the wind and the muffled thud of distant machinery, you may feel it: a slight shift in the air, a deepening of the hum, a shadow that pools and then rises. One of them is near. It does not look at you. It does not need to. It tastes your warmth, measures your moisture, notes your dissonance in the local resonance, and then, with the slow certainty of condensation finding its level, it turns and moves on, following the next thermal ridge, the next weeping seam, the next cycle in the long, patient breath of the city.
