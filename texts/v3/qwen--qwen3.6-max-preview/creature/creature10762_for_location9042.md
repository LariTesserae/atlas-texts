They do not arrive. They are already there, the way the damp is already there, the way the *tink-tink-tink* is already there. You notice them only when the mist thins for a breath, or when your eye catches the rhythm of a shoulder turning, an arm falling, a palm pressing wet stone. They move through the terraces not as inhabitants but as currents. Their bodies are the basin's syntax.

At first, they read as extensions of the masonry. Their skin is the color of steeped chalk, pale and faintly luminous in the amber seep, porous as unfired clay. It weeps constantly, not with sweat but with the same alkaline condensate that beads on the vaulted ceilings. Run a finger along a forearm and it comes away slick with dissolved calcite and iron. A fine, mineral-crusted down lies flat against their skulls, shedding water like the rust-colored tiles above. Elsewhere they are smooth, their contours blurred by perpetual humidity. They do not shiver. They do not pant. Their internal temperature matches the cisterns, the kilns, the deep strata: a steady, radiating warmth that makes the air around them shimmer faintly.

Their hands are the first truth of them. Long-fingered, hypermobile at the knuckles, the pads hardened into glassy, ceramic calluses from generations of gripping bronze and wet hide. The nails are thick, dark, filed to flat edges that double as scrapers, gauges, and tuning forks. Between the fingers, the skin is loosely webbed, not for swimming but for transduction: minute vibrations travel through the damp air, through the stone, through the water in the channels, and their palms read them like pressure maps. They do not need to look at the block they are dressing. They feel its grain, its fault lines, its exact weight in the hydrology of the terrace. When they strike, the chisel does not chip; it parts. The stone yields because it knows the hand.

Their faces are broad, flat-planed, built to shed moisture. Eyes lidless, dark as wet slate, pupils wide and fixed, adapted to the dim wash but rarely used for navigation. They perceive in gradients: the shift in thermal pressure, the change in acoustic density, the taste of sulfur and iron on the tongue. Their noses are low, flared, drawing in the thick air in slow, measured pulls. Their mouths are seldom open except to breathe or to voice the chant. When they do vocalize, it is not with words but with tones that vibrate in the chest, hum in the throat, resonate through the zygomatic arches. The sound does not travel through the air so much as through the stone. A worker on the seventh terrace presses a palm to a weeping wall and feels the reply from the twelfth: a low thrum, a shift in pitch, a confirmation of load, of flow, of balance. Language here is not exchanged; it is conducted.

They eat what the basin gives, which is everything and nothing green. Their meals are taken in the vaulted courtyards, sitting on warm flagstones, passing clay bowls of pale fungal paste, blind crustaceans poached in mineral broth, strips of scaleless fish cured in iron salts. They chew slowly, deliberately, their jaws built for grinding grit and chitin. Their digestive tracts are short, efficient, lined with symbiotic microbes that break down sulfur compounds and sequester heavy metals. What they cannot use, they excrete as fine, chalky powder that is swept into the mortar pits. Nothing is wasted. Nothing leaves the system. Their bodies are filters, converters, nodes in the chemosynthetic web. When they drink from the cisterns, they tilt their heads back and let the warm, alkaline water run over their tongues, through their throats, into their blood. Their veins run thick with dissolved minerals. Their bones are dense, heavy, laced with calcite and trace copper. They do not suffer from the weight. They are made for it.

They do not age as you understand it. There is no youth, no prime, no decline. There is only saturation. The young emerge from the shallow spawning beds at the basin's floor, soft and translucent, their skin still drinking light, their fingers uncallused. They are carried up the terraces in woven reed slings, fed fungal milk, taught not by instruction but by immersion. A child's hand is placed over an elder's on the chisel. The rhythm is transferred through bone. The chant is learned not by ear but by sternum. By the time their skin begins to opacify, by the time their joints begin to accumulate their first crusts of white bloom, they are already part of the cadence. They do not play. They do not rest. They circulate.

The old do not weaken. They calcify. Their movements grow slower, more deliberate, their voices dropping to the lowest registers of the work-chant, a frequency that vibrates in the flagstones and settles the silt in the lower channels. Their skin thickens, cracks in fine, geometric patterns, weeping thicker slurry. When the weight of the bronze grows too great, when the calluses fuse to the bone, they do not stop. They descend. They walk down the stair-flights that double as watercourses, past the kilns, past the burrowers' scrapings, to the lowest terraces where the mist is thickest and the sound is a solid pressure. There, they lie down on the raw stone. They do not close their eyes. They let the water run over them. They let the fungi take root in the cracks of their skin. They let the basin reclaim its minerals. Within days, their flesh softens, dissolves, returns to the microbial mats. Within weeks, their bones are cleaned, crushed, mixed with clay and iron bloom, pressed into new tiles, laid into new joints. They do not die. They are redeposited. The population does not grow or shrink because every birth is balanced by a return, every new hand by a settled stone.

They have no names. They have cadences. A worker is known by the exact spacing of their chisel strikes, by the pitch of their hum when they test a corbelled arch, by the way their bare feet scuff the damp flagstones. If you listen long enough, you can pick them out of the acoustic mass: the one whose rhythm leans slightly to the left, the one whose breath catches on the third beat, the one whose mallet strikes with a hollow resonance that means the wood is drying and must be soaked. They do not correct these variations. They absorb them. The chant is not a unison; it is a harmony of micro-deviations, constantly adjusting, constantly balancing. To fall out of rhythm is not to fail; it is to signal a shift in the equilibrium. The others adjust. The water adjusts. The stone adjusts. The basin holds.

They do not build toward anything. They build because stopping would be a rupture. The scaffolds are not temporary; they are permanent fixtures, lashed and relashed, hide ropes replaced strand by strand as they fray. The kilns are never cold. The chisels are never new. Every tool is a continuum, passed hand to hand, sharpened on the same whetstones, oiled with the same rendered fat, worn into the exact shape of the grip that holds it. Innovation is not forbidden; it is unnecessary. The form is perfect because it is stable. To change the angle of the chisel would be to change the angle of the water. To change the water would be to change the sound. To change the sound would be to break the breath. They do not fear stagnation. They revere it. Equilibrium is not a state to them; it is a practice, a devotion, a metabolism.

They are aware of you, if you stand among them. They do not look at you. They step aside, the way water steps aside for a submerged rock, and resume. Your dry skin, your rapid pulse, your erratic breathing register as dissonance in the stone. They do not judge it. They accommodate it. A worker will place a damp hand on your shoulder, not to greet you, but to ground you, to let your vibration bleed into the terrace until it matches the frequency of the place. If you stay, your lungs will learn the thick air. Your eyes will adjust to the amber. Your footsteps will fall into the cadence. Your skin will begin to weep. You will not become one of them. You will become part of the circulation. And when you finally understand that there is no exit, no completion, no before or after, only the endless, deafening, beautiful act of maintenance, you will pick up a chisel. Not because you are told to. Because the stone is waiting. Because the water is moving. Because the breath must be held.

They are the basin's keepers, its children, its mortar, its voice. They do not live in the city. They are the city, persisting. And as long as the mist holds, as long as the channels weep, as long as the bronze strikes the raw edge, they will continue: unhurried, unchanging, unbroken, becoming exactly what they have always been.
