You do not meet them. You notice that the mountain is being attended to.

At first, there is only the suggestion of arrangement. A line of gravel sorted not by current but by intent, largest stones bracing the outer curve of a channel, smallest nested in the hollow where water hesitates. A patch of moss worn smooth in a repeating crescent, as if something heavy and rounded has rested there through multiple wet seasons. A dry-stone wall that leans at an angle that feels almost accidental until you trace the weeping seams and realize every joint has been coaxed to shed in the same direction. There are no footprints. The ground drinks them within minutes. But the drainage remembers weight. The stone remembers pressure. The settlement remembers hands.

Then the mist parts, not with a breeze, but with a displacement so gradual the air barely registers it. A surface thickens. It does not reflect light like wet rock, nor absorb it like shadow. It holds it, diffuses it, lets it slide. When the form steps into the open terrace, it does not arrive so much as resolve.

Low to the slope, but not crouched. Built for incline. The outer layer is neither hide nor scale nor cloth, but a living membrane, layered and slightly porous, the color of stone that has known water for centuries: basalt-dark where it stays shaded, pale and ash-mottled where it meets the light. It breathes through that surface. You can see it in the faint, rhythmic dimpling along the ribs, the way condensation gathers not as sweat but as a second skin, beading, merging, rolling down in slow, deliberate threads that join the channels below. The limbs are jointed for leverage, not velocity. Shoulders slope forward. Hinges sit deep. Feet are broad, splayed slightly at the toes, soles thick and ridged like riverbed stone, reading the ground through pressure and temperature rather than sight. Hands are palm-heavy, fingers short and dense, pads cross-hatched with micro-ridges that grip wet rock without scraping it. No claws. No nails. Just tissue that has learned to hold without tearing.

It moves by settling and rising. Weight transfers through the outer edge of the foot, then the ball, then the heel, always testing, always allowing the earth to answer before committing. When it reaches a water thread, it does not step over. It steps in. The cool film climbs the ankles, and the creature pauses. Head tilts. Not listening with ears, but with shins, with soles, with the long bones that conduct vibration better than air ever could. A palm presses against a leaning wall. Fingers find a seam. The stone is weeping too fast; a joint has loosened with temperature shift. With a motion so slow it barely disturbs the mist, two fingers hook under a rock, lift it a finger's width, rotate it, set it back. The trickle changes course. It meets a larger thread. The wall's tension redistributes with a sound like a long exhalation. This is not maintenance. It is dialogue.

They are never solitary, but they rarely gather. You begin to perceive the pattern only when you stop looking for crowds and start reading flow. A figure on the terrace above adjusts a clay-packed roof edge. Three levels down, another feels the change in volume against a courtyard wall and shifts a gravel baffle to catch the new pulse. Half-hidden in an alley, a third presses a forearm against a retaining face, reads the temperature gradient, and packs fresh moss into a drying seam. They do not call. They do not signal. The water carries the sentence. The stone carries the reply. Coordination is not commanded; it is conducted. The settlement is a single instrument, and they are its fingers.

Homes are not claimed. They are inhabited, then yielded, then reinhabited as the mountain's drainage migrates over decades. A cistern silts up; they do not excavate it. They let it settle, weave reed and compressed moss across the surface, and build a new step atop it. The old chamber becomes a filter. The new chamber becomes a dwelling. When a wall begins to lean beyond its equilibrium, they do not tear it down. They brace it with a lower terrace, redirect the weep, and let the structure become a retaining spine for the level below. Nothing is discarded. Everything is repurposed by gravity. Generations do not replace one another. They accumulate. You can see it in the stratigraphy of a single courtyard: the lowest stones worn smooth by ancestors whose names were never spoken, the middle courses patched by hands that learned the rhythm from those below, the upper layers still rough, still settling, still being read by the young who press their palms to the rock and listen for the mountain's next shift.

They do not measure time in days or seasons. They measure it in saturation. In the slow creep of velvet moss across a windowsill. In the deepening of a footfall hollow by a fraction of a stone's thickness. In the way a particular channel changes pitch after a month of heavy mist, singing lower, fuller, more resigned. Their cognition is not linear. It is hydraulic. A question is not answered; it is allowed to pool until the sediment settles and the clear water above reveals the shape beneath. A disagreement is not debated; it is routed around, given a new channel, watched until it finds its own gradient and ceases to erode. They do not speak in syllables. They speak in pressure. In the tap of a knuckle against wet stone. In the rhythm of drops falling into a shared basin. In the way a palm lingers on a wall to say *I am here. The flow is steady. Rest.*

When they rest, they do not lie flat. They lean. They let their weight merge with the incline, let the cool seep into their joints, let the mist bead on their shoulders and roll down their spines into the terraces below. Sleep is not withdrawal. It is participation. Their breathing slows to match the condensation cycle. Their skin drinks the damp. Their muscles unspool into the same patient tension that holds the dry-stone walls together. To watch them rest is to watch the mountain breathe through another medium.

You begin to understand, standing among them, that they did not arrive here and build. They are what the place became when it learned to tend itself. The high country weeps; they guide the tears. The stone settles; they ease the weight. The mist condenses; they wear it. There is no seam between their flesh and the mortarless walls, between their breath and the cloud veil, between their slow, deliberate hands and the patient erosion that shaped the terraces long before memory. They do not farm. They do not hunt. They do not store. They read. They adjust. They yield. They continue.

They are not residents. They are the landscape's attention. Its hands. Its quiet, continuous consent.

When you finally turn to leave, descending toward drier air, sharper light, louder ground, you do not feel you have visited a people. You feel you have stood inside a body that knows how to drink the sky and give it back to the stone. And long after the mist thins, long after the plink of falling water fades into wind and dust, your skin remembers the damp. Your ears strain for the space between drops. Your feet, without being told, begin to read the slope for the path of water.
