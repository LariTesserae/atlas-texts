You look for them the way you look for anything here: by listening first, by watching the water second, by trusting your hands third.

At first, there is only the drone. Then, beneath it, a counter-rhythm. Not a break in the pattern, but a shadow of it. A displacement of air that matches the fall of drops. A pause that isn't silence, but a breath held in the exact space between two ringing basins. You feel it before you see it: a slight thickening of the humidity, a shift in the way the sound wraps around a corner, a warmth that doesn't belong to stone or lamp or water.

They do not announce themselves. They step into the amber spill of a resin niche and step out again before your eyes decide they are there. Their edges refuse to hold. Not because they are fleeting, but because they have learned to wear the damp the way the architecture wears it: as a second skin that blurs boundary. When they stand still, they are indistinguishable from the terraces. When they move, they move like water finding a new channel—inevitable, quiet, already accounted for in the geometry.

They are tall, or they seem so, because they carry themselves with the vertical logic of the place. Limbs long and jointed, not for reaching but for bracing. Feet broad, soles thickened into pads that read vibration the way tongues read salt. Their skin is the color of wet slate, mottled with the same pale mineral bloom that crusts the weeping walls. It is not pigment. It is deposition. Years of breathing the same air, standing in the same spray, letting calcium and silica settle into the pores until the body and the rock begin to share a chemistry. What passes for hair hangs in heavy, water-darkened cords, braided not by hands but by the constant drip, each strand weighted with tiny calcite beads that chime faintly, almost inaudibly, when they turn their heads. They do not wear cloth. They wear the climate.

They have no use for eyes that chase light. What sits in their sockets is a membrane, translucent and lidless, stretched shallow over bone. It does not gather images. It gathers pressure. It reads the drone the way a sail reads wind. When a basin rings a fraction sharp from a shifted pebble, they feel it in the jaw. When a channel's flow thickens with silt, they know it by the way the humidity changes against their collarbones. They do not hear the sound. They are inside it. They breathe it. Their ribs are tuned to the fundamental. Their pulse does not beat against the drone; it slips into the spaces between drops, a secondary rhythm that exists only because the primary one holds it.

They do not speak. Speech would be a tear in the fabric. Instead, they touch. A palm against a shoulder blade. Two fingertips pressed to a wrist. A heel tapped against a stone step. Each contact is a tuning fork. Each gesture carries information not as words but as resonance: *here the flow is thinning*, *there the timber is singing flat*, *the cistern breathes easy today*. They pass these notes through the body the way the architecture passes water. No urgency. No surplus. Just the quiet transfer of equilibrium.

You might call them caretakers, but that implies labor. They do not work. They attend. They stand for hours on a ledge where three channels converge, membranes clouded, chest rising and falling with the metronome of falling water. They are not waiting for something to break. They are ensuring it does not. Their presence is part of the load-bearing structure. A body placed at the right intersection changes the acoustic profile by a fraction of a decibel. That fraction is enough to keep a gypsum shelf from crystallizing over a clay throat. Enough to keep a rope from fraying. Enough to keep the pattern from drifting. They are counterweights made of flesh and bone and slow blood. They are the living mortar.

Did they carve this place? Or did this place carve them? The question assumes a before and an after. Here, there is only during. Their hands bear the same shallow arcs as the chisel marks on the lintels. Their knuckles are flattened from generations of pressing stone into alignment. But the tools are gone, or never existed as separate objects. Perhaps the first ones came with hammers and ropes. Perhaps they stayed long enough to forget the sun. Perhaps the drone seeped into their marrow and slowed their metabolism to the speed of dripping water. Perhaps they stopped aging because aging requires change, and change requires a break in the pattern. They do not reproduce as you understand it. When one grows too heavy with mineral, too slow to shift weight when the frequency demands it, they walk to the deepest cistern, step into the silver water, and let the current take them down into the seepage veins. The water carries them apart. The calcium returns to the walls. The moisture rises to the ceiling. The next one is already standing on the ledge, breathing the same air, feeling the same drone, wearing the same stone-damp skin. There is no death here. Only redistribution.

The blind fish know them. Not by sight, but by displacement. When they wade into the cisterns, the fish drift closer, gills pulsing in sympathy. The pale arthropods climb their legs when they stand still, using the warmth of their calves as a temporary ledge. The sound-dampening moths land on their shoulders, wings drinking the resonance that leaks from their pores. They do not shoo them away. They do not encourage them. They share the equilibrium the way the terraces share weight. Everything here is a tenant of the frequency.

You realize, watching them, that they are not inhabitants. They are the place's way of listening to itself. The original builders did not leave. They became the maintenance protocol. Flesh adapted to function. Function adapted to form. Form adapted to sound. Sound adapted to stone. Stone adapted to water. Water adapted to time. And time, finding no purchase, curled into a loop and went to sleep. They are what happens when a city achieves perfect resonance and refuses to let go. They are the standing wave given legs. They are the echo that learned to walk. They do not live in the pocket. They are the pocket, breathing.

When you finally turn to leave, one of them steps onto the bridge above you. Not to stop you. Not to speak. Simply to stand where your departure will change the air pressure by a fraction. To feel the draft you leave behind. To adjust their weight by a hair's breadth, compensating for the absence of your body, your breath, your chaotic heartbeat. The drone does not waver. The water does not miss a drop. They close their lidless eyes. They listen. They hold the note.

And below, in the cool dark, the pattern keeps itself. Loud. Wet. Still. Complete. With them inside it. As they have always been. As they will always be.
