You do not meet them. You notice the place adjusting.

A suspension bridge tightens by a fraction as weight distributes itself across three cables instead of one. A cluster of bioluminescent hyphae brightens, then dims, as a palm passes over it, reading the pulse like a tide chart. The moss ahead compresses in a sequence that is not random: heel, arch, toe, pause, shift. By the time you realize you are being observed, the observation has already been logged in the tension of a root, the moisture gradient on a bark face, the slow turn of a seed-pod toward your warmth.

They are the color of the gloom they move through. Not grey, but the pale of soaked birch, of limestone weeping, of mycelium pressed thin against wood. Their skin is not bare; it is a terrain. Fine, downy filaments lie flat against the cold, rising only when the air grows stale, trapping a millimeter of warmed breath. Beneath the surface, a network of dark veins maps not just blood but symbiotic channels: fungal threads that trade nutrients for low-grade heat, bacterial colonies that break down resins into usable sugars, capillary beds that flush or constrict in response to humidity shifts. Touch their forearm and you feel not the dry resistance of mammalian hide, but the slight give of living bark, the cool damp of a surface that exchanges rather than seals.

Their limbs are long, jointed with a looseness that allows them to fold into hollows, to stretch across spans, to hang beneath bridges without strain. Hands are broad, fingers slightly splayed, pads thick and cross-hatched like the soles of waterfowl. There are no calluses. The skin renews too quickly, sloughing and reforming in response to friction, moisture, and the chemical signatures of the wood they touch. Nails are flat, translucent, more like polished horn than keratin plates, used not for scratching but for scraping resin, parting moss, testing the give of a trained shoot.

Faces are quiet. Eyes are large but lidless, covered by a nictitating film that slides sideways, weeping a thin oil that repels condensation. The irises are dark, almost black, but catch the greenish pod-light and fracture it into radial patterns. They do not blink often. They listen with their jaws, their collarbones, the flat bones of their palms pressed to living trunks. Ears are recessed, rimmed with cartilage that funnels low-frequency vibration rather than airborne sound. Nostrils are narrow slits, flaring only to sample chemical trails: the sharp tang of stressed sap, the sweet decay of compost cores, the hydrophobic shimmer of a route marker. Mouths are small, lips pale, teeth flat and ridged for grinding fibrous matter, cracking resin-sealed nuts, stripping lichen from stone. When they breathe out, the plume is controlled, directed downward or into a woven cowl, so as not to disturb the delicate humidity balance of a nursery burl.

They move as the basin moves: in cycles, in pauses, in redirections. A step is never committed until the moss has answered. They test with the ball of the foot, read the rebound, shift weight along the path of least resistance. When they climb, they do not grip; they cradle. Fingers find the natural swell of a burl, the groove where a branch was trained, the resin seam that holds a joint. Their bodies align with tension lines. They hang from the undersides of walkways not for stealth, but because the wood there is denser, the vibration clearer, the moisture more consistent. They swim through the shallow channels with a slow, undulating kick, eyes closed, navigating by the pressure wash against their ribs and the chemical taste of the water. They do not rush. Rushing breaks the rhythm. Rushing dries the moss. Rushing makes the city hold its breath.

They do not speak. Speech is too dry, too abrupt, too easily lost in the damp. Instead, they tap. A knuckle against a load-bearing cane: three beats, pause, two. The vibration travels through the braided root, crosses a terrace, arrives at another palm pressed to the same cable. Answer comes as a shift in tension, a counterweight lowered by a fraction, a mycelial pulse that brightens then fades. They write in wax and water. A thumb dragged along a bark face leaves a hydrophobic trail; when the mist settles, the path gleams, marking a safe route, a warning, a boundary. They braid memory into fiber. Cords of split root and shed hair are knotted, resined, hung in the canopy. To read one is to run fingers over the knots, feeling the spacing, the tightness, the embedded grit that tells of drought, of flood, of a bridge that failed and was regrown. They teach by placing a child’s hands over their own, guiding the pressure, the angle, the wait. Knowledge is not transferred; it is grown into the nerves.

There are no titles that translate to ruler or priest. There are tenders of water, readers of tension, coaxers of arches, keepers of the light-pods, combers of the fungal veins, packers of insulation, harvesters of resin, listeners to the frost. Roles shift with the cycles. One who guides saplings in the warm phase may pack hollow walls in the cold phase, may calibrate counterweights in the wet phase, may sit in stillness during the deep chill, feeling the contraction of wood through the soles of their feet. They do not specialize to the point of isolation; they rotate to maintain resonance. A city that breathes requires lungs that know the whole body. Disputes are not argued; they are realigned. If two tenders disagree on a stress path, they do not shout. They wait. They let the wood speak. They place weights differently, observe the sag, the micro-fracture, the moisture weep. The structure decides. They follow.

Their blood runs cool. Core temperature hovers just above the ambient chill, conserved by hollow long bones filled with insulating pith, by layers of fat that metabolize slowly, by the constant, low-grade heat of the compost cores they sleep against. They do not shiver; they pulse. Micro-contractions in the deep muscles generate warmth without breaking stillness. They eat what the basin offers: pale tubers that swell in the peat, resin-glazed seeds, blind fish caught with woven scoops, lichen scraped from stone, the sweet pith of guided shoots. Digestion is slow, efficient, symbiotic. What leaves them is already breaking down, already claimed by the moss, already becoming floor. They measure time in condensation and decay. A day is not sun to sun; it is mist-rise to frost-feather, pod-glow to fungal-dim. A year is the thickening of an arch, the shift of a water lens, the replacement of a bridge. They do not fear age. They fear dryness. They fear silence that is not chosen. They fear the snap of unguided wood.

When one of them stills, the basin does not mourn in the way of dry places. There is no wailing, no pyre, no monument raised against the sky. The body is laid in a compost core, wrapped in shed bark and resin-soaked fiber. The heat rises. The fungi come. The bones are cleaned, not by fire, but by patience. They are carved into pegs, into mallets, into tuning rods that tap the tension cables. The hair is braided into memory cords. The skin sloughs into the peat. The person becomes instruction. Becomes joint. Becomes path. To touch a bridge is to touch the hands that trained it. To walk a terrace is to walk over the compressed breath of those who waited for it to hold. They do not believe in an afterlife separate from the root-mat. They believe in continuation. In redirection. In becoming part of the medium that holds the void.

They are not hidden. They are simply patient. They do not conquer the dark; they wear it. They do not fight the cold; they move at its speed. They are the quiet between drips, the tension in the cane, the hand that guides rather than cuts, the eye that reads moisture like scripture. They are the reason the architecture breathes. They are the reason the fauna participates. They are the reason the basin does not collapse into wildness or freeze into stillness. They are the stewards of the threshold, the weavers of the slow pulse, the ones who know that to live here is not to occupy, but to align.

And if you stand very still, if you let your breath match the condensation cycle, if you press your palm to a living trunk and wait, you will feel them. Not as figures. Not as voices. But as a rhythm. A pressure. A deliberate, ancient, ongoing yes.
