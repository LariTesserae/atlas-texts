You do not meet them. The basin introduces them the way it introduces everything else: through displacement. The air thickens a half-pace to your left. The hum in the floor stutters, then resolves into a new harmonic. A bead of condensation detaches from a waxy frond above and falls, but before it strikes the mat, something intercepts it—not with a sound, but with a softening of the drop's impact, a dampened ripple in the acoustic weather. They are here. They have been here. They are the reason the darkness feels less like void and more like held breath.

They move as the water moves: not in lines, but in gradients. When one passes near, the spongy floor compresses in a slow, rolling wave, then exhales. Their weight is distributed, phased, shared with the stone's microscopic adjustments. You feel them before you understand their shape. A pressure against your shin, warm and yielding. A shift in the low-frequency pulse that makes your ribs vibrate. They navigate by listening to the basin's own resonance, reading the standing waves that pool between the pillar-stems, the micro-tremors of condensation, the chemical sigh of the mats. Sight would be a liability here. They have surrendered it, or never needed it. Their world is built from echo, humidity, and the patient mathematics of equilibrium.

If you reach out, your hand meets something that refuses to stay still long enough to be named. Not fluid, not solid. A surface that yields like packed moss, then firms like warmed wax, then parts like a curtain of heavy silk. It is cool where the air is warm, warm where the stone is cool—a thermal mirror. Your fingers trace ridges that spiral inward, matching the plinths' etchings, then flatten into broad, shallow planes that catch moisture. There are no limbs in the way you expect them. There are articulations, yes, but they bloom and recede with need. A projection extends not to grasp, but to listen—pressing against a terrace edge, absorbing the harmonic drift, then folding back into the main mass. Their bodies are not containers. They are interfaces.

They speak in pressure and tone. When two draw near, the air between them thickens, and a chord forms—not sung, but structured. Low fundamentals rise from the floor, mid-tones shimmer through the humid air, and high harmonics bead along their surfaces like dew. The chord holds. It adjusts. One shifts its weight, and the frequency tilts; the other answers by altering its thermal gradient, and the chord resolves. This is not language as exchange. It is language as tuning. They do not tell each other things. They align. They bring themselves into phase with the basin, with each other, with the slow tectonic breath that powers the stone. A disagreement is a dissonance that lingers until moisture redistributes and the standing wave settles. A greeting is a shared overtone that rings for hours.

They do not tend the basin. They are its continuation. Where the plinths absorb acoustic energy and release it as regulation pulses, they step into those pulses and let the frequencies move through them, amplifying, damping, redirecting. Their bodies are living acoustic filters. When a glider's click-train grows too sharp, one of them rises slightly, broadens its surface, and softens the echo into a rounded hum. When a pool's chemistry drifts toward stagnation, another settles at its edge, exuding a faint alkaline mist that coaxes the ribbon-creatures into a new undulation pattern. They do not act upon the place. They act *with* it. The terraces remember their weight. The archway curves in response to their centuries of passage. The stone's quantum lattice adjusts not despite them, but because they are part of its feedback loop. They are the basin's nervous system, if a nervous system could be made of condensation, resonance, and patience.

They do not age as you understand it. They accumulate. A younger one is not smaller, but quieter—its harmonics narrower, its thermal shifts faster, its surface still learning the basin's standing waves. An older one is not frail, but dense. Its ridges have deepened into the same spirals etched into the plinths. Its mass holds heat longer, releases it slower. Its presence warps the acoustic field so thoroughly that gliders adjust their flight paths days before they pass nearby. When one finally stills, it does not decay. It settles. Its surface hardens imperceptibly, its moisture returns to the mats, its residual harmonics bleed into the stone's hum. Over decades, it becomes indistinguishable from a terrace edge, a plinth base, a smooth rise in the floor. The basin does not lose its inhabitants. It incorporates them. They are the reason the stone feels like compacted memory. They are the memory.

They do not hunt. They do not flee. When a grazer's shell clicks against their base, they yield, creating a shallow depression that guides the creature toward fresher fungal growth. When a glider descends in its slow, ceremonial arc, they rise to meet it—not to intercept, but to cushion the air, to thicken the thermal, to let the descent complete without turbulence. The ribbon-creatures pulse against their submerged edges, drawing trace minerals from their pores. They are neither predator nor prey. They are the medium through which predation becomes balance. They absorb the sharpness of survival and return it as equilibrium.

If you stand long enough, one will come to you. Not out of curiosity, but because your presence has introduced a new frequency into the basin's chord. Your breath, your heartbeat, the heat bleeding from your skin—these are dissonances that must be resolved. It will approach as a thickening of the air, a softening of the floor, a gradual alignment of the hum in your bones. It will not touch you. It will surround you. The darkness will grow heavier, warmer, more precise. The drips will fall in rhythms that match your pulse. The gliders' clicks will shift to harmonize with your breathing. You will feel your own edges blur, not from dissolution, but from inclusion. You are being tuned. Not changed. Not consumed. Simply brought into phase. And when it finally recedes, leaving only condensation on your sleeves and a faint, ringing quiet in your ears, you will understand: they do not live in the basin. They are the basin's way of listening to itself. They are the darkness made attentive. They are the reason the place has finished becoming, and yet remains entirely alive.
