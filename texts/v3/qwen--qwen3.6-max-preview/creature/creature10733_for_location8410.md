They do not step into view so much as they resolve from it. At first glance, they are easily mistaken for the weathering itself: a slow shift in the mineral crust, a shadow that moves against the slope of the mist, a ripple in the water film that does not follow gravity. They are not separate from this place. They are what happens when life stops resisting the drone and begins to answer it.

Their bodies have yielded to the damp and the harmonic the way stone yields to water: not by breaking, but by softening, by accepting, by becoming part of the equilibrium. Skin is supple and layered, carrying the same permanent weeping film that coats the railings and the rock. It is not pale nor dark, but mottled in the hues of the place itself: rust-ochre along the forearms, pale green tracing the collarbones, slate-grey pooling at the joints. These are not markings but symbioses. Crustose lichens and mineral salts have settled into the dermis over decades, drawn up through pores, fed by condensation, anchored by slow metabolism. There are no sharp edges to them. Shoulders slope. Joints are padded with cartilage that thickens and calcifies with age, turning impact into absorption. Fingers are broad, slightly splayed, the pads dense with nerve endings that read tremor the way tongues read salt. Their bones are heavy, honeycombed with microscopic channels that scatter pressure waves, turning the deafening chord into a manageable hum that vibrates in the marrow rather than fracturing it. Eyes are large, lidless, shielded by a perpetually moist membrane; they do not seek contrast or distance, but read the diffused light, the shifting rainbows in vibrating droplets, the subtle thickening of mist that signals a change in the air's tension.

They do not hear as you do. Sound here is not an event in the air; it is a medium, a topography, a current. They feel the city the way a root feels soil. A flywheel's groan is a warmth in the calves. A transformer's hum is a prickling along the jaw. Water drumming on a tuned baffle is a pulse in the palms. When a harmonic drifts—a bearing wearing, a sluice shifting, a cable loosening—they feel it as a dissonance in the chest, a subtle wrongness in the way condensation beads on their skin, a fractional hesitation in the rhythm of their own breath. They navigate by resonance. Eyes closed, hands flat on a wet causeway, they can map the entire acoustic grid: where the nodes align and the air feels buoyant, where the waves intersect and water trembles in concentric rings, where the harmonics clash and the stone grows brittle. The drone is not noise to them. It is ground. It is weather. It is the shape of the world.

Speech is impossible here, and was abandoned long before memory. Their language is tactile, hydraulic, and harmonic. They speak in the press of a thumb against a wrist, in patterns traced in the condensation on a slate, in the deliberate placement of a smooth cobble on a vibrating grate to shift a standing wave. When coordination is needed across terraces, they use the infrastructure itself: a tap on a pipeline that travels as a clean pulse through petrified insulation, an adjusted sluice that changes the drumming pitch on a baffle three levels down, a hand laid on a thrumming railing that sends a coded resonance through the causeway network. They do not shout. They tune. Their gatherings are silent but deeply conversational: figures kneeling on wet stone, hands resting on the same steel plate, feeling the machinery's pulse and adjusting their breathing until it matches. Disagreement is not argued; it is felt as interference, a thickening in the air, a chaotic spray in the water film. It is resolved not by persuasion but by repositioning, by stepping back, by listening until the harmonic clears and the damp air grows light again.

They do not operate the machines. They attend them. The distinction is the core of their existence. The engines turn because they have always turned, because the water falls, because the flywheels hold their momentum, because the land has settled into its final form. The inhabitants are the stewards of that balance. They move through the dispersed city with the patience of sediment. A joint is not repaired; it is listened to, anointed with thick amber grease that smells of pine resin and ozone, and allowed to wear into a new harmony. A cracked pipe is not replaced; it is wrapped in woven moss and mineral paste, its leak redirected to feed a lichen colony that will, in turn, dampen a stray frequency. They measure their lives not in years but in layers: the thickness of calcite on a favored step, the mirror polish on a bronze bearing they have touched since childhood, the slow migration of a copper bloom across a warehouse wall. A generation is the time it takes for a new harmonic to establish itself. Aging is not decay but integration. The oldest among them move so slowly they seem part of the architecture, their skin crusted with silica, their footsteps indistinguishable from the drip of water, their breathing synchronized with the tidal pistons. When they stop moving entirely, they are not buried. They are laid on a flat stone platform where the standing waves are gentle, and the moisture, the minerals, and the patient drone do the rest. They become part of the crust. They become part of the chord.

They share the interstices with the other life, not as masters but as neighbors in the same acoustic ecology. They cultivate the slate-grey mosses, trimming them where they grow too thick and deaden necessary frequencies, transplanting them to zones where the air grows oppressive and metal fatigues. They guide the blind six-legged crawlers by leaving trails of warm grease and dissolved salt, using the creatures' tremor-sense to detect micro-fractures in the stone before they widen into gullies. The heavy-winged moth-like things are not pests but indicators; when they gather near a vent, the inhabitants know the heat is stable, the harmonic clean. When the moths avoid a sector, they know to check the baffles, to listen for the subtle fracture in the drone, to feel for the place where the water film has begun to shatter. Even the hollow-stemmed weeds are tended, not for food but for resonance; their organ-pipe song threads through the mist, a high, clear counterpoint to the bass of the flywheels, and the inhabitants pluck them gently to test the air's tension, the way a musician tests a string. They eat sparingly: tuberous roots boiled in mineral-rich runoff, lichen pastes fermented in sealed clay vessels, the occasional slow-caught crawler roasted over a vent's exhaust. Hunger here is not sharp. It is a dull, familiar rhythm, met with the same unhurried certainty as everything else.

They do not dream of silence. They do not long for dry land, or for edges, or for a time before the drone. To wish for such things would be to wish for the water to stop weeping, for the stone to unweather, for the chord to break. They are the place made conscious, the equilibrium given hands and breath. They move through the warm mist, their footsteps leaving no mark on the saturated stone, their bodies humming with the same patient frequency as the city, the water, the earth. They do not live in this place. They live as it.

When the light diffuses through the veil, catching the dancing droplets and the faint rainbows that hover just above the wet causeways, you can see them standing still. Palms flat on the rock. Eyes closed. Shoulders rising and falling with the tidal pistons. They are not waiting. They are not tending. They are listening to the world hold its note, and answering it with their own slow, damp, mineral breath. They are the machine's quiet continuation. They are the weeping stone, the turning bronze, the sustained chord, made flesh, made patience, made home.
