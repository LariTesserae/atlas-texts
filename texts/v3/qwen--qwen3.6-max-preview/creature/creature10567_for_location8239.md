You would not see them at first. You would step over one, or rest your weight against what you took for a root-boss, or watch a moss-draped hummock settle and assume it was only the sponge-mat adjusting to the shift in pressure. They do not announce themselves. They have long ago surrendered the habit of standing apart.

When stillness finally allows the eye to stop hunting for edges, they resolve not as figures but as textures that have learned to gather. Their outer layer is not skin but a living pelt: felted fiber, symbiotic lichen, and shed cuticle woven into a continuous, water-shedding rind that mirrors the green-gold dimness. Beneath it lies a stratum of spongy, reticulate tissue that swells with the plateau's weep and contracts in the dry sighs between thermal shifts. There are no bones. Support comes from braided vascular cords, tensioned like bowstrings but flexible as submerged kelp, arranged in overlapping spirals that yield and return. Limbs are jointed only in the way that thick runners are jointed: by nodes of concentrated fiber that permit slow, deliberate articulation. What might be called hands are broad, deeply grooved pads fringed with capillary filaments, designed not to grasp but to press, to wick, to listen.

Faces are absent. In their place are sensory fields: shallow pits lined with vibration-sensitive cilia, membrane windows that taste humidity and chemical gradients, and dark, lidless ovals that catch the diffused light like the surface of the tea-pools. These eyes do not track movement; they register shifts in light-density, spore-fall, and the slow migration of moisture through the air. To look at one is to realize that vision here is not for hunting or fleeing. It is for reading the atmosphere.

They move as the land moves: by seepage, by sigh, by patient redistribution of weight. A step is not a lift and fall but a slow compression and release, the body sinking a hand's breadth into the sponge-mat, drawing moisture upward through capillary fringes, then flowing forward as the mat exhales. They leave no footprints. The ground knits behind them as it knits behind a shedding frond. When they pause, they do not merely stop; they settle. Broad ventral surfaces press to the mat. Vascular cords align with the subterranean flow. They become, for hours or days, indistinguishable from the terrain. This is not rest as you know it. It is attunement. To rush would be to tear against the grain of the place. To startle would be to waste the water that holds them together.

Speech, if it can be called that, does not travel through air. It travels through water and fiber. A conversation begins as a subsonic thrum, generated by the rhythmic contraction of internal fluid sacs, sent downward through the mat and outward through the root-felt. It is answered not in words but in resonance: a shift in pulse-frequency, a counter-rhythm tapped through a nearby trunk, a coordinated release of bioluminescent spores that drift in patterned clouds and paint temporary syntax in the dim air. Meaning is layered, cumulative, and deeply contextual. A single exchange may span weeks, but it is not slow to them. It is complete. To hurry a thought would be to strip it of the humidity that gives it weight. Their language is not about transmission. It is about synchronization.

They do not build. They persuade. The circular depressions where the sponge-ground grows firmer, the arches of vine that curve in perfect repetition, the platform-like terraces of compacted root: these are not ruins of a past civilization. They are the result of generations of gentle insistence. Enzyme trails laid to encourage fusion. Low-frequency hums that guide tendril-growth. The patient weighting of runners until they remember a shape. Their settlements are not places of shelter but zones of intensified listening. Canopy leaves are coaxed into overlapping parasols that channel condensation into singing basins. Hollow trunks are cleared of decay not by cutting but by introducing symbiotic mites that consume only the soft rot, leaving resonant chambers that amplify the continental pulse. When thermal inversions draw the silver rain upward, they tilt their sensory membranes to catch it, not to drink, but to read the temperature of the sky. Nothing is owned. Everything is tended. To claim a grove would be to sever it from the flow, and severance is the only violence they recognize.

Memory does not reside in skulls. It resides in the mat. When one of them reaches the end of its long, slow arc, it does not fall. It unravels. The outer rind sloughs into the moss. The vascular cords separate, thread by thread, and weave themselves into the surrounding root-felt. The sensory membranes dissolve into spore-clouds that drift into the tea-pools and settle into the capillary networks. What remains is not a corpse but a pattern: a subtle shift in hydraulic resistance, a new harmonic in the trunk-thrum, a chemical signature that lingers in the weep for decades. The living read the dead by pressing into the ground and waiting. History is a taste in the water. Lineage is a rhythm in the sponge. There are no graves because there is no separation. There is only return. The land does not preserve ruins; it absorbs them. They have simply learned to participate in the absorption.

They do not mark time. They inhabit it. A year is not a cycle of seasons but a migration of moisture through the plateau's pleats, measured by the slow change in the air's sweetness. A generation is not counted in births but in the fusion of two canopy bridges, the deepening of a communal basin, the gradual shift in the spore-glow's dominant hue. Coming of age is not a ritual but a realization: the first time a juvenile feels the continental pulse traveling through a major trunk and adjusts its own internal tide to match. Mating is not an event but a convergence: two beings settling side by side, their capillary fringes intertwining, their fluid sacs falling into synchrony, their combined spore-cast released over months into the mist to find its own niche in the mat. There is no urgency. There is only readiness. Predation, when it touches them, is not feared but understood as a redistribution of moisture, a return of tension to the mat. They do not flee the ambush; they dissolve into it, leaving behind only a faint chemical farewell that the sponge reads as completion.

If you walked among them, they would not flee. They would not approach. They would simply adjust. They would read your dryness, your rapid pulse, your jagged rhythm, and they would wait. Over days, they might begin to mirror your breathing, not in imitation but in inquiry. They might guide you, by subtle shifts in their own positioning, to a weeping stem or a shallow pool, not out of hospitality but because your presence has created an imbalance that the mat seeks to correct. They would not speak to you. They would listen through you. And if you stayed long enough, if you let your own rhythm slow, if you pressed your palm to a trunk and waited for the thrum, you might feel it: the faint, vast attention of a place that has learned to notice itself through bodies like theirs.

They are not residents of this plateau. They are its awareness made manifest. Not a hive, not a colony, but a distributed attentiveness, as natural to the sponge-mat as condensation is to the leaf, as inevitable as the upward fall of silver rain. They do not rule the green expanse. They do not serve it. They are the equilibrium breathing. They are the quiet holding. They are the land, dreaming in moss, and occasionally opening its eyes.
