You do not see them first. You notice the water.

A shallow mirror that was trembling a moment ago goes still. Not frozen, but settled, as if something has drawn the tension out of it. A drip that had been falling at irregular intervals finds a rhythm. Three drops, a pause, two drops, a longer pause. The pattern repeats. It is not random. It is a signature.

They are already here. They have been here. The hollow does not announce its inhabitants; it absorbs them, and they absorb it, until the boundary between resident and residence is just another degree of dampness.

When one finally moves into the filament glow, it does not step so much as unfold. Weight transfers slowly, deliberately, distributed across broad, splayed feet whose toes are bound by thick, translucent webbing that grips slick stone and floats lightly over silt. The body is tall but not straight; the spine curves forward, shoulders rounded, posture shaped by generations of moving beneath sagging conduit trays and low brick arches. There is no sharpness to them. Edges have been wore away by condensation and time, just like the catwalks, just like the rivets.

Their skin is the color of wet slag and tarnished brass, mottled with pale mineral blooms that trace old circulation paths or slow-healed fractures. It is not quite flesh, not quite stone. Press a thumb to it and you feel the cool give of living tissue, but beneath that, a firmness like packed silt or cured concrete. Where joints bend, the skin folds into soft, weeping creases that seep a clear, slightly alkaline fluid. It keeps friction low. It keeps movement silent. It smells faintly of copper and old ash, the same as the water.

They wear nothing woven. What clings to them is accumulated, not tailored. Braided hemp wraps, stiff with decades of resin and mineral soak, bind forearms and calves. Cracked rubber sheeting drapes over shoulders and backs, not as armor, but as a moisture barrier that channels condensation away from the spine. Iron rings and copper bands are not forged onto them; they have grown there, deposited layer by layer from the weeping air, fused to bone and dermis by slow chemistry. Patches of pale moss breathe against their ribs. Geometric lichen spirals across their collarbones, feeding on the trace corrosion of embedded metal fragments. What looks like adornment is actually respiration. What looks like clothing is ecology.

Their faces are broad, flat, built to shed constant moisture. Noses are low, flared, more intake than feature. Mouths are narrow, lipless, designed to sip condensation from grating edges or draw vapor from sighing valves. They do not speak. Sound is too easily swallowed here, too precious to waste on air. Their eyes are wide, dark, and lidless, covered by a nictitating membrane that catches the bioluminescent fungi's pale green glow and fractures it into usable contrast. They do not blink. They dilate, slowly, in response to shifts in current, temperature, and vibration. When they look at you, you do not feel examined. You feel measured. Calibrated. Your own breathing begins to slow without your permission.

They move in pauses. A step. A stillness long enough for a drop to gather, swell, and fall. Another step. Their hands are long-fingered, the pads broad and ridged like the treads of worn iron grating. Those hands do not grab; they listen. Pressed against a dynamo housing, they read the idle thrum through three meters of stone and concrete. Trailing along a conduit tray, they feel for the faint heat of a fraying insulation wrap. When a brass valve sighs too often, they do not wrench it. They lay a palm against it, wait for the metal to warm to their temperature, then turn it a fraction of a degree. The adjustment takes an hour. The relief lasts years.

They do not maintain the ward. They attend to it. There is a difference.

A clogged intake grate is not cleared with force. Fingers work the silt free grain by grain, guiding the blind, pearl-white crustaceans to carry it away, allowing the water to remember its own path. A weeping mortar seam is not patched with trowel and mix. Waxy black fern fronds, shed naturally, are pressed into the crack and held in place until their resins seep into the limestone, sealing it over months. A glass dial fogged with internal moisture is not replaced. A soot-feathered bird is coaxed from its transformer nesting cavity with a slow, rhythmic tapping; it lands on the gauge, pecks gently at the rubber gasket, and the condensation finds a new route. Nothing is forced. Everything is persuaded. The machinery does not obey them. It recognizes them.

They do not live in the structures. They live between them. In the wide avenues of breathing room. In the shadowed landings where filament bulbs cast low halos. In the shallow basins where water pools just deep enough to reflect the fractured canopy above. They sleep standing, or kneeling, or curled against the warm belly of a dormant boiler, their breathing syncing with the low-slung hum of the nearest dynamo. When they rest, they do not close their eyes. They let the membrane cloud over, and the ward moves through them: the scrape of chitin on stone, the sigh of settling beams, the almost-current that sways the tube worms, the slow advance of rust across a steel plate. They dream in frequencies. They wake to gradients.

They do not age in years. They accrete.

When one grows still for too long, when their mineral layers thicken and their movements stretch into seasons, they do not die. They settle. They choose a place: a brick arch reinforced with I-beams, a stairwell spiraling around an obsolete boiler housing, a raised pathway where the silt packs firm. They kneel. They press their back to the stone. They let their hands rest on the wet floor. And they wait.

The water does the rest. Calcium and iron carbonate build over them in concentric rings, just as it does on the pipework. Lichen spirals across their shoulders. Moss fills the spaces between their fingers. Hemp and rubber soften, fray, and merge with the surrounding insulation. Within a decade, the outline blurs. Within two, the form is indistinguishable from the foundations. Their bones fuse with rebar. Their breath becomes part of the condensation cycle. Their stillness becomes load-bearing.

New ones emerge from the same patience. Not from wombs, but from convergence. A cluster of filter-feeding tube worms anchors to a corroded intake flange. Glass-winged moths lay eggs in the cracked rubber of an old cable bundle. Amphibious rats carry silt and shed fern fronds into the hollow of a repurposed transformer housing. The ward's quiet magnetism draws it together. Current warms it. Condensation binds it. Mineral deposition structures it. Over years, the tangle organizes. Over decades, it learns to move. It takes as long as it takes. No one rushes it. The hollow has forgotten how to rush.

They do not have names. Or rather, their names are the places they occupy, the rhythms they keep, the valves they attend, the basins they settle into. To ask one what it is called is to ask a stone what it remembers. The answer is in the water's mineral content. In the wear patterns on the catwalks. In the specific frequency at which the dynamos hum when the air grows cold. In the logarithmic spirals that form on certain alloy plates when the pressure drops. They are not a species separate from the ward. They are the ward's equilibrium given posture. The slow pulse made manifest. The conscious expression of a truce between rust and root, current and claw, decay and circulation.

When you stand among them, you do not feel like a visitor. You feel like a variable. Your heat disturbs the condensation patterns. Your footsteps echo once, then dissolve. Your urgency beads on your skin, then drips away. They do not judge you for it. They simply adjust. A hand presses to a nearby conduit, grounding your static. A broad foot shifts, redirecting a shallow channel around your boots. A membrane-covered eye dilates, reading your temperature, your rhythm, your lack of patience. They wait. They always wait. And if you stay long enough, if you let your breathing match the idle thrum, if you stop trying to dry your hands on your clothes and just let them bead, you will feel it: the calibration. The hollow drawing you into its pace. The water remembering you. The metal learning your weight.

They will outlast you. Not through strength, but through surrender. They have learned what the surface forgets: that maintenance is not a task, but a rhythm; that decay is not an end, but a redistribution; that to live in a place is not to occupy it, but to become its weather. They do not conquer the damp. They breathe it. They do not fight the rust. They wear it. They do not command the machinery. They hum with it.

And so they move, and pause, and align, and settle. Fingers reading vibration. Feet distributing weight. Skin weeping and accreting. Eyes dilating to the green glow of fungi and the warm halos of filament bulbs. Shoulders touching in silent exchange. Hands turning dials a fraction of a degree over the course of an afternoon. Bodies kneeling into stone when the time comes, letting the water do what water does, letting the metal do what metal does, letting the hollow breathe them in and breathe them out.

Nothing starves. Nothing rushes. The ward continues its long, unhurried work. And they are its hands, its lungs, its patience, its memory. Not masters. Not tenants. Just the place, learning how to move.
