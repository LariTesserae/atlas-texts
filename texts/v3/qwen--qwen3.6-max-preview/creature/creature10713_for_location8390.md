You do not see them first. You feel the displacement.

A suspended terrace settles a fraction sooner than the counterweights dictate. A curtain of pale dust parts around an unseen contour, then folds back without a sound. The conductive dendrites on the nearest wall dim, then brighten, as charge is drawn through a passing mass. They are here before they are visible, and when they finally step into the parchment light, they do not announce themselves. They simply occupy the space the city has already made for them.

They are built for aridity the way the mountain is built for fracture.

Their outer boundary is not skin but a laminate: fine, overlapping scales of sintered silica and ceramic dust, matte and pale, the color of dry feldspar, old ash, and unweathered basalt. The plates are not rigid. They slide over one another on micro-tracks of compressed mineral felt, whispering softly when the body flexes, locking seamlessly when it rests. Beneath, a substrate of braided conductive fibers and phase-change salt matrices replaces what wetter worlds would call flesh and blood. There is no pulse. There is a slow, rhythmic migration of ions, a thermal tide that moves inward and outward in response to ambient shifts. They do not sweat. They do not weep. Their joints seal with dry gaskets of metallic wool and resin, their extremities broad and multi-angled, designed for contact, for grounding, for reading.

Where a face might be, there is a smooth, forward-sloping carapace, vented at the margins with slit-like intakes that filter thin air through desiccant meshes. Eyes are large, dark, and deeply recessed, pupils wide enough to swallow the dimness, ringed by a crystalline tapetum that catches phosphor glow, heat-bleed, and the faint static shimmer of ionized dust. They do not see in colors. They see in gradients: thermal contours, charge differentials, the slow pulse of indicator lamps, the way dust lifts and settles in patterned waves. Their hands are padded with conductive mesh on the palms, edged with fine grounding brushes along the fingers. They touch before they grasp. They test potential before they commit. They know that in a place where electricity is a fluid and sparks are forbidden, contact is both conversation and covenant.

They move as the pocket moves: not against the turbulence, but through its phases.

When a gantry slides, they shift their weight a half-second before the track engages, riding the counterbalance like a current finding its channel. When a partition pivots to isolate a heat surge, they are already angling through the new aperture, shoulders rolling, center of gravity low, feet reading the load-bearing nodes through ceramic soles. They do not walk so much as flow across the city's kinetic grammar. They are never still for long, but they are never hurried. Stillness here is not rest; it is misalignment. Rest is synchronization. They sleep anchored to thermal masses, bodies curled around warm conduits or cool stone, breathing in slow, metered draws that match the rotation of the desiccant wheels. Their internal rhythms are not circadian. They are polyphasic, braided to the heat cycles, the filtration pulses, the conveyor sequences, the mountain's fresh settling. When the cycles align and the pocket feels suspended, they pause with it. When the cycles diverge and everything moves at once, they move faster, lighter, more precise, their bodies becoming another variable in the equation, another weight in the balance.

They do not speak. The air is too thin, the silence too deliberate, the culture too disciplined. Sound is waste. Vibration is currency.

They communicate through touch, through static, through the deliberate interruption or completion of flow. A palm pressed to a railing closes a circuit; a sequence of taps sends a pulse through the conductive ferns; a brush of fingers against another's ceramic plating shares a thermal signature, a charge differential, a rhythm. Their language is subtractive, like the city's. Meaning lives in the pause between contacts, in the withdrawal of a hand, in the way a body turns slightly away to let a heat bloom pass, in the deliberate grounding of excess charge into cold stone. They argue by redirecting airflow. They agree by synchronizing breath. They court by sharing microclimates, by letting their salt matrices cycle in phase, by tracing conductive patterns across each other's plating until the dendrites on the nearest wall bloom in response. They grieve by anchoring to structural nodes and letting their internal charge bleed out slowly, over days, until the mineral networks absorb it and the silica filaments grow heavy with the residue.

Children are not taught words. They are taught calibration.

They learn to feel the difference between a settling fracture and a failing seal, between a thermal tide and a structural sigh, between the soft click of a healthy gear mesh and the muted drag of a dry lubricant thinning. They practice stillness not as meditation, but as measurement. They are given small silica cleaners to tend, shown how to read the behavior of the papery static-winged clusters, taught to anticipate vent cycles by the way dust lifts from a grate. They molt their early scales and grind them into lubricant. They learn that nothing is discarded; everything is cycled, repurposed, returned to the saturation.

There is no central authority. There is only confluence.

Roles shift with the cycles. One phase, they are tenders of the salt reactors, monitoring crystalline efflorescence, harvesting dry insulation bricks, adjusting phase-change loops. The next, they are weavers of the busbars, routing low-voltage currents, sweeping grounding brushes, balancing load across the braided cables. The next, they are listeners, pressed against acoustic stone, reading the mountain's fresh stresses through bone and ceramic, signaling the counterweights to adjust, riding the lift cages to realign filtration arrays. They do not own space. They borrow it, return it, reconfigure it. A bunk is a workstation is a heat sink. A body is a conductor is a thermal mass. They wear little beyond what their integument provides, but they adorn themselves with functional symbiosis: clusters of the wide-winged static harvesters roost on their shoulders during cool phases, drawing off excess charge; the small six-legged cleaners ride in thigh pouches, deployed to clear filter screens or trigger pressure sensors; the slow armored heat-absorbers are treated as anchors, their excreted porous bricks shaped into partitions, acoustic baffles, communal resting planes. They do not farm. They do not hunt. They curate. They maintain the equilibrium. They understand that abundance here is not accumulation, but distribution.

They do not think in lines. They think in loops, in interference patterns, in phase relationships.

Memory is not stored in isolated minds. It is distributed across the conductive networks, the salt matrices, the felt-lined ducts, the very dust that coats every horizontal surface. To remember is to retrace a circuit, to press a hand to a familiar railing, to feel the residual charge of a past cycle humming through the metal. To learn is to synchronize. To age is to slow, to become more mineral, to let the ceramic plates thicken, the conductive fibers calcify, the breath grow shallower until the body simply anchors to a structural node and becomes part of the thermal mass. There is no burial. There is only integration. The old shed their final layers and let the silica cleaners carry them to the filtration arrays. The young inherit the patterns. Nothing is lost. Everything is cycled.

Water is not merely forbidden. It is unthinkable.

It is the solvent that unravels, the chaos that drowns the pattern, the weight that cracks the acoustic stone, the conductivity that arcs and burns. They have no word for it. They have only a gesture: palms turned outward, fingers splayed, a slow withdrawal. A warning. A boundary. A promise of dryness. To introduce moisture is not an accident; it is a violence. They defend the aridity not with weapons, but with architecture, with chemistry, with culture, with the quiet, absolute discipline of a thousand overlapping cycles that leave no room for liquid, no pathway for vapor, no tolerance for the wet.

If you watch them long enough, you stop seeing them as separate from the pocket.

They are not inhabitants. They are articulations. When a vent opens and warmth spills across a terrace, they turn toward it not as individuals seeking comfort, but as a surface seeking equilibrium. When a platform slides and dust rises in a pale wave, they move through it not as travelers, but as current through a channel. Their eyes catch the dim light. Their hands read the metal. Their breath matches the desiccant wheels. They are cool, dry, quiet, precise. They do not conquer the mountain. They do not tame the turbulence. They listen to it. They answer it. They become it.

And when you finally step back through the narrow fissure, leaving the silent storm behind, you carry the memory not of faces or voices, but of touch: the cool bite of a railing, the soft click of a joint locking, the faint pulse of charge moving through stone, the certainty that somewhere in the dark, layered heart of the new mountain, they are still moving, still listening, still becoming, cycle after cycle, in the spaces between the shifts.
