They are the **Smooth-Walkers**, though they have no word for themselves that can be spoken, for speech is a violence against the silence, and they have long since surrendered the need to break the air. To see one is to mistake it for a feature of the land until the feature blinks, or shifts its weight, or casts a second shadow that moves a heartbeat before the body does.

They are the final biological rhyme to the continent's geological meter. Just as the mountains forgot their names and became a plane, the inhabitants have shed the jagged urgencies of lesser life. They are tall, but not in the way of trees; they are elongated by the thin air and the stretching of distances, their limbs drawn out like taffy pulled by slow gravity. They move with a fluid, viscous deliberation, never jerking, never rushing, for haste here causes the geometry to knot, and a runner may find their own footprints waiting for them three days ahead.

Their skin is the color of the wet stone at dawn: a pale, translucent grey-blue that deepens to the hue of bruised moss where the blood runs close. It has no hair, no scales, no roughness. It is waxy and seamless, shedding the biting cold and holding the dampness in, glistening with a perpetual, healthy sheen of condensation. Touch one, and the flesh yields like firm clay, cool and dense, radiating a slow, metabolic warmth that feels less like fire and more like the heat of composting loam. In the hollows of their joints and along the ridges of their spines, patches of the blue-green symbiotic moss take root, not as parasites but as kin-gardens. These patches flush with bioluminescence when the bearer is agitated or deep in thought, pulsing in time with the fungal networks beneath the stone, turning their bodies into living maps of the plateau's nutrient flows.

Their faces are smooth masks, eroded of sharp angles. Noses are reduced to sleek, slit-like apertures that flare wide to drink the thin air; mouths are small, lipless seams that rarely open, for they eat sparingly and speak not at all. Their sustenance comes from the pressed cakes of fungal spore-meal, the waxy needles of the silver-conifers chewed to release their oils, and the water itself, which they drink from cupped hands or absorb through the porous soles of their feet when they stand in the carved basins. Their chests are barrel-vast, ribcages expanding like bellows with every breath, storing oxygen in spongy, secondary lungs that allow them to hold a single inhalation for hours while they work underwater or meditate in the time-pools.

It is the eyes that betray their mastery of this unmoored world. They are large, liquid, and entirely black, reflecting the sky with such fidelity that to look into them is to see the clouds passing behind your own head. They have no whites, no irises, only a depth that seems to curve inward. They do not just see light; they see the gradients of perception. They see the tension in reality where a path is about to loop, the shimmering heat-haze of a time-dilation hollow, the faint, braided threads of probability that indicate where a seed will vanish and reappear. When they look at a distant settlement, they do not judge the miles; they judge the *effort of arrival*. They see the world not as fixed objects, but as a negotiation of attention.

Their hands are their voices and their anchors. Fingers are long, multi-jointed, and tipped with pads as sensitive as a tongue, capable of reading the microscopic vibrations in the stone. They can feel the slow creep of a retaining wall settling, the pulse of water moving through a clay-lined aqueduct three terraces away, the faint tremor of a fungal tower preparing to shed its spores. With these hands, they shape the world. They carry tools of bone and copper, but the bone is not hunted; it is inherited. When a Smooth-Walker dies, their flesh is laid in the rooting hollows to feed the moss and the fungi, returning to the green-silver cycle. The bones, dense and polished by a lifetime of movement, are cured and carved by the kin. A chisel made from a grandfather's femur shapes the stone for a grandchild's hearth. To hold a tool is to hold a lineage; to build is to converse with the dead. The copper, soft and reddish, is used for inlays and conductors, hammered into the stone at perceptual anchors to ground the drifting geometry, acting as lightning rods for reality.

They wear no clothes, for the wax of their skin and the moss of their joints are armor enough. Instead, they wear harnesses of woven vine-fiber and reed-cloth, dyed in the subtle gradients of lichen and rust. These harnesses hold their tools, their water-skins, and their *memory-knots*: complex braids of fiber that record not events, but states of being. A knot might hold the sensation of a specific fog bank, or the angle of light that opens a shortcut across a gorge. They read these knots by touch, their fingers dancing over the braids to recall the navigation of a world that refuses to stay fixed.

Their movement is a form of collaboration with the strangeness. They do not walk in straight lines, for straight lines are lies here. They move in curves, in spirals, in braided paths that mimic the rivulets of condensation. When they approach a spatial loop, they do not resist; they lean into it, allowing their momentum to carry them through the fold so that they emerge where they intend, rather than where Euclid would dictate. They know that if they are tired, the distance will compress, and they can cross a mile in ten steps by surrendering their alertness. If they are anxious, the path will stretch, and they must sit, breathe, and calm their hearts until the stone relaxes and lets them pass. They are masters of their own internal weather, for their minds shape the local physics as much as the wind shapes the reeds.

Communication is a silent tapestry of gesture, light, and touch. In the hearth-plazas, where the circular longhouses huddle around the central warmth, they gather in stillness. A conversation might look like a slow dance of hands, fingers weaving shapes in the air that leave faint trails of condensation. Or it might be a exchange of bioluminescence: one flares the moss on their shoulder in a complex rhythm, and another answers with a pulse from their chest, the language of light passing between them faster than sound ever could. They also speak through the stone. By pressing their palms to the polished rock, they can send vibrations that travel for miles, tapping out codes of pressure and release that only another Smooth-Walker, listening with their feet, can decipher. The land carries their whispers; the moss dampens them from the sky, but the stone hums with their presence.

Their lifecycle is tied to the fungal towers and the time-hollows. Conception is not a biological accident but a deliberate planting. A pair, or a triad, will carry a spore-cluster from a sacred grove to a basin where time pools. There, in water that falls so slowly it hangs like glass beads, they graft the spore with a shaving of their own bone-dust and a drop of blood. The child grows not in a womb, but in the moss-lined basin, nurtured by the slow time, taking seasons to form a limb, years to open those black, sky-reflecting eyes. The parents tend the basin, singing to it not with voices, but by humming in their throats, creating resonances that shape the growing flesh. When the child finally rises, damp and smooth, it is already old in patience, its mind attuned to the rhythms of the plateau. It knows the loops before it takes its first step.

They are the caretakers of the erosion. They understand that the world is winding down, not into death, but into a state of perfect, resting habit. Their aqueducts are not just for water; they are sutures in the skin of reality, guiding the flow of existence as much as the flow of moisture. Their circular buildings prevent the accumulation of stress in the corners of the world. Their bare feet polish the stone, contributing to the great smoothing, wearing down the last resistances of matter. They are not fighting the end of physics; they are midwifing the transition. They are helping the continent become entirely itself.

There is no war here, for there is no scarcity, only distribution. There is no crime, for to harm another is to disrupt the perceptual anchor that holds the community's reality stable. If one becomes erratic, their shadows lagging too far, their geometry fraying, the kin will surround them, press their hands to the stone, and breathe in unison, anchoring the lost one back into the shared rhythm until their light stabilizes and their path unbraid.

At night, when the moonlight turns the condensation to scattered glass and the stars are unnervingly sharp, they stand on the terraces and watch. They do not sleep as animals sleep; they enter a state of low-awareness diffusion, their minds extending into the fungal network, dreaming the dreams of the moss, feeling the slow grind of the stone, the drift of the spores, the distant curl of a rain-veil. They are never alone. The silence is not empty; it is full of the land's listening, and they are the ears of the land.

They are the people of the breath and the bone, the weavers of the loop, the smooth flesh on the smooth stone. They have forgotten the difference between physics and habit, just as the land has. They are what remains when a species stops resisting the weirdness and becomes the weirdness, settled, ancient, and deeply, quietly alive. To walk among them is to feel your own edges soften, your own shadows hesitate, your own heart slow to the rhythm of the dripping water, until you too begin to understand that permanence is a negotiation, and that you are being included, gently and irrevocably, in the long, slow body of the world.
