You notice them first as a shift in the rhythm. Not a sound added, but a sound altered. A belt-drive’s hum drops half a tone. A water channel’s flow parts around an unseen weight. A drip pattern stutters, then resumes, synchronized to a new cadence. They do not announce themselves. They are already woven into the damp and the thrum, as much a part of the basin’s respiration as the condensation falling from the canopy. When you finally see one, it is not because they have stepped into view, but because your eyes have finally learned to separate them from the grain.

Movement comes before form. They walk with a rolling, grounded gait, knees slightly bent, weight distributed across broad soles that grip the wet humus without slipping. There is no hurry in the stride. Each step is placed, tested, accepted. They do not fight the give of the root-floor; they read it. Their bodies have internalized the language of tension, saturation, and slow deflection. When a living bridge sways, they sway with it. When a walkway sags under pooled water, they shift their weight before the wood complains. They move as if the basin were an extension of their own proprioception, and in many ways, it is.

Skin is the first thing that defies expectation. It is not pale from the lack of sun, nor darkened by exposure. It carries the fine, mottled grain of prolonged contact with tannin-rich water, airborne spores, and the constant press of damp materials. Along the forearms, shins, and spine, it has thickened into cork-like plates, flexible but abrasion-resistant, sealed by years of resin, sweat, and fungal colonization. Hair, where it persists, is sparse, stiff with mineral deposits, often braided with copper filament or bound flat against the skull with tarred cord. Faces are broad, nasal passages flared to filter the heavy air, lips often parted in a quiet, continuous exhalation that matches the steam vents’ rhythm. Eyes are large, pupils wide, adapted to the green-dim. They do not blink often. The humidity keeps them lubricated; the low light keeps them dilated. When they look at you, it is not with curiosity or alarm, but with the steady, assessing gaze of something that measures in ring-widths and graft-takes.

They wear little that is not grown, salvaged, or patiently repurposed. Garments are layered bark-cloth, felted moss, woven hemp stiffened with pine resin and iron oxide. Fastenings are brass toggles, bone pins, twisted wire. Nothing hangs loose. Everything is secured against the constant moisture, the brush of lianas, the pull of machinery. Tools do not hang from belts so much as merge with them: calipers with wooden handles swollen to fit specific palms, pruning hooks ground to mirror the curve of a particular load-bearing vine, wrenches whose jaws have been filed down over decades to match the exact wear-pattern of a single boiler’s valve. Their hands are the true revelation. Fingers are thick, joints enlarged from constant torque, nails reinforced with keratin and metal dust. The skin between knuckles is crosshatched with old scars, each one packed with tallow and ground bark until it healed into a flexible, waterproof seal. When they touch a machine, they do not grip; they listen. Palms flat against a housing, fingertips tracing a belt’s edge, ear pressed to a pipe. They feel vibration in their teeth, in their sternum, in the fluid of their inner ears. A misalignment is not heard; it is felt as a dissonance in the bone.

They do not speak in words, not at first. Communication is a vocabulary of knocks, scrapes, breath, and gesture. Two taps on an iron railing means pressure is building in the lower cistern. A long exhale through cupped hands means the fungal mat needs turning. A rhythmic clack of a tool against a support beam mirrors the water wheel’s cadence to signal equilibrium. When they do vocalize, it is low, resonant, shaped by lungs adapted to warm, saturated air. Syllables stretch. Vowels hum. Consonants are soft, rounded by moisture. Names are not given; they are earned through grafts. A tender who has guided a load-bearing root through a cracked turbine housing might be called *Ironvein* or *Knotkeeper*. Identity is not separate from function, nor is function separate from place.

They do not sleep as outsiders do. They rest in shifts, leaning against warm boiler casings, curled in cork-lined hollows, suspended in woven root-hammocks that sway with the basin’s subtle tremors. Waking is not a startle but a resumption. There is no morning alarm, no shift whistle. Only the continuous thrum, the drip, the turn. Time is not divided; it is accumulated. They do not count days. They count graft-takes, bearing seatings, ring-widths, the gradual polishing of a brass valve by condensation alone. Urgency is a foreign physics. Force applied too quickly splits wood, snaps cable, fractures ceramic. Pressure must be distributed. Change must be absorbed. They have internalized this. It shows in their posture, in their breathing, in the way they wait.

There is no hierarchy, only specialization. Some tend the upper canopy, climbing braided lianas with spiked boots and harnesses of woven cable, pruning deadwood, adjusting louvered vine-screens to regulate airflow. Others work the lower tiers, knee-deep in tannic pools, clearing duckweed from intake grates, repacking piston seals, listening to the knock of pumps. A few are grafters, their hands stained with sap and copper sulfate, their days spent scoring bark, inserting cambium strips, binding wounds with wet clay and cord until the wood accepts the new shape. None are idle. None are rushed. The work is the life. The life is the maintenance.

You might wonder how they persist without fauna. Where is the protein? The fat? The answer is in the substrate, in the fungal networks, in the slow alchemy of the basin itself. They cultivate oyster shelves on shaded trunk-walls, harvest protein-rich mycelial cakes from decomposition chambers, drink amber runoff filtered through crushed charcoal and living moss. Their bodies have adapted to a low-metabolism equilibrium. Heart rates are slow. Digestion is efficient. They do not burn energy; they circulate it. Sweat does not cool them; the humidity prevents evaporation. Instead, they vent heat through breath, through the thin skin of their palms, through damp cloth pressed to their necks. They are not cold-blooded, but they are temperature-matched. They do not fight the basin’s climate; they wear it.

When one dies, there is no burial, no pyre, no monument. The body is laid in a decomposition shelf, wrapped in bark-cloth, packed with spore-rich humus and powdered charcoal. Within months, the fungal threads take it. Within years, the nutrients feed a new root-shelf, a new walkway brace, a new graft. Calcium strengthens a load-bearing trunk. Iron returns to the pipes. Carbon joins the peat. Nothing is lost. Nothing is wasted. The rhythm continues. They do not mourn with tears; the air is already saturated. They mourn with adjustment. A walkway is re-tensioned. A belt is restitched. A valve is repacked. The work absorbs the absence.

To watch them is to watch the basin breathe. They do not stand apart from it. They are its feedback loop, its conscious maintenance, its slow memory. They do not call themselves inhabitants. They do not call themselves caretakers. Those words imply separation, imply that the place could exist without them, or they without it. The truth is simpler, and older. They are the ones who listen. Who adjust. Who wait. The basin does not need saving. It does not need conquering. It needs attending. And they attend. Slowly. Steadily. Entirely. As the water turns the wheels, as the roots thicken around the iron, as the steam rises and falls and rises again, they remain. Not masters. Not guests. Simply the next layer of the weave.
