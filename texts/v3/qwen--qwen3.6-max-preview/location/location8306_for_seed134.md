You are underwater, but the sky is right above you.

The basin is small enough to cross in three hundred slow strokes, a high-altitude fracture pool cupped in jagged, recently split rock. It sits perched where the air is thin and cold, yet the water fills it to the brim, held in by sheer walls of pale feldspar and black basalt that still bear the glassy sheen of fresh rupture. There is no soil on the ledges. No moss clings to the stone. No algae films the surface. The place is mineral-clean, stripped to geometry and fluid, and it hums.

Light falls straight through the open mouth of the basin, unfiltered by cloud or canopy, and the water is so clear it barely announces itself. Daylight penetrates to the bottom without dimming, scattering off suspended silicate dust into a shadowless, even glow that makes distance difficult to judge. Everything is visible at once: the terraced steps plunging downward, the knife-edged overhangs, the dense strata of life and stone layered through the column like pages pressed together.

The water is cool. Not biting, but steadily, deeply cool, the kind of temperature that settles into the joints and slows the breath. It carries a clean, sharp scent of crushed quartz and a faint ozone tang that prickles the back of the throat. When you move through it, the resistance feels wrong at first: yielding, then firm, as if the fluid remembers pressure and holds it for a moment before letting go. Under sudden force it stiffens; under patient motion it parts like cold silk. You learn to move slowly, not from caution, but because the medium rewards patience.

Nothing here was made. There are no walls laid by hands, no pipes, no rivets, no cut stone. Yet the basin functions with the relentless logic of a refinery.

Along the terraces, hollow silica chimneys rise from the rock like extruded glass. They are young, their edges still sharp, their surfaces ribbed with micro-layers laid down in rhythmic pulses. Warm, ion-heavy water exhales from their cores in measured breaths, meeting the cooler basin water and flashing into precipitation. You can watch it happen if you hold still: a faint cloud blooms from a vent, crystallizes mid-drift, and settles onto the chimney's lip, adding a fraction of a millimeter to its height. The process repeats. And repeats. The chimneys are growing, but on a scale of years. Their patience is absolute.

Between them, the water is thick with life. Not plants. Nothing photosynthetic survives in this mineral-heavy, vegetation-starved ecology. Instead, the basin runs on chemistry and conduction. Shoals of carapaced filterers drift in dense, near-touching formations, their shells faceted like cut glass, their gill-rakers combing metallic particulates from the current. They move in synchronized pulses, thousands aligning, turning, and settling as one organism. When they shift, the water itself seems to ripple with chitin and light. Larger forms patrol the ledges: segmented, multi-jointed creatures with trailing filaments that glow faintly copper when current passes through them. They siphon precipitates from the chimneys, scrape conductive crusts from the rock, and excrete fine, geometric pellets that sink to lower terraces to be consumed again. Some are nearly invisible except for the distortion they cast, their bodies transparent save for internal lattices of crystalline tissue that store and release charge.

The fauna do not graze. They process. They filter, precipitate, conduct, and reformat. A swarm will anchor along a voltage gradient, bodies aligning head-to-tail until the water between them shivers with potential. Charge builds. A faint arc snaps through the column, branching like frost on glass, and the swarm shudders in unison, metabolizing the discharge. The cycle repeats. Intake, filtration, precipitation, conduction, release. No smokestacks, no gears, no forged metal, yet the basin operates with the cadence and efficiency of an industrial plant. The machinery is geology and biology coupled in slow, precise feedback loops.

It is loud. Not with chaos, but with layered, resonant industry. Sound travels cleanly here, reflecting off the unweathered walls, multiplying into standing waves that vibrate in the sternum. The deep groan of settling rock forms the bass. Over it, the hiss of mineral vents, the percussive tick-tick-tick of crystalline growth, the synchronized clatter of carapaced shoals turning in unison. Every few minutes, a sharper crack rings out as an electrical discharge bridges a gradient, followed by a low, harmonic hum that lingers in the water like a struck bell. The acoustics organize themselves. Certain frequencies pool in the lower terraces; others channel upward along the chimneys. The fauna respond to these harmonic fields, adjusting their alignments, their feeding cycles, their migrations. The sound is not noise. It is infrastructure.

Despite the density, despite the loudness, nothing hurries. The basin changes on the scale of seasons and decades. A vent may shift its output over months, slowly redirecting a current. A terrace may fracture and slump in a single, thunderous event, then remain motionless for years. The faunal populations rise and fall in multi-year waves, their synchronized turns dictated by subtle shifts in conductivity and temperature. The loudness is constant, but the flux is glacial. You could watch a single chimney for a day and see only the faintest bloom of precipitate. You could listen for an hour and hear the same deep rhythm, unbroken. The place is settling into itself, learning its own long cadence.

Light behaves strangely near the discharge zones. Where potential builds and arcs form, the water bends illumination into prismatic halos that hang motionless for minutes before dissolving. These halos do not cast shadows; they pool in the water like suspended lenses, magnifying the creatures that drift through them. When a swarm passes through a halo, their faceted shells fracture the light into geometric patterns that linger after they've moved on, as if the water remembers their shape.

There is no decay here. Nothing rots. When a creature dies, its crystalline lattice does not soften or break down; it dissolves slowly, releasing stored ions back into the gradient. The carapace flakes into fine mineral dust, caught by filterers, precipitated onto chimneys, excreted as pellets, consumed again. Matter cycles without loss, only transformation. The ecology is closed, patient, and utterly mineral. Fungal analogs exist only as microscopic conductive mats that bridge rock and water, threading voltage through the stone like natural wiring. They are not plants. They are not quite animals. They are the basin's nervous system, silent and slow.

The scale intensifies everything. Two hundred paces across, perhaps three times that in depth, bounded by sheer rock on all sides. You are never far from a wall, a ledge, a chimney, a swarm. The horizontal constraint forces vertical density. Life stacks upon life, current upon current, sound upon sound. The basin feels like a submerged cathedral, not in architecture, but in presence: enclosed, resonant, saturated, complete unto itself. Above, the sky is open and pale. Below, the water continues into cool, clear dark where the chimneys thin and the fauna grow sparse. But within the bounded volume, the world is full.

To move through it is to move through a slow engine. You feel the pressure gradients against your skin, the faint tug of conductivity, the harmonic vibrations in your ribs. You learn to read the water by its resistance, the light by its halos, the sound by its standing waves. You learn that loudness does not mean urgency, that density does not mean chaos, that industry does not require hands. The basin refines stone into life, life into charge, charge into stone, in cycles so long they feel like stillness.

It is young. The fractures are fresh, the edges unsoftened, the chimneys still learning their shapes. But the rules are already written in the water: move slowly, align with the gradient, listen to the harmonics, let the current carry what it will. The place does not rush to become anything else. It is busy being exactly what it is, a high, cool, bounded vessel where mineral and fauna couple in patient, roaring industry, where light falls clear through water that remembers pressure, where nothing decays and everything recrystallizes, where the walls are sharp, the volume is dense, and the rhythm is measured in years.
