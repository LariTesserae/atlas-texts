You are high, though the darkness refuses to let you measure it. The horizon is a soft, indeterminate line where the ground simply stops asserting itself and the air takes over. Beyond the low, rounded ridges that cup this basin, there is only a deeper black, a drop into nothing that you feel in the inner ear before you understand it with the eyes. The air is warm, dry, and perfectly still. It carries no moisture, no scent of soil or decay, only the clean, faintly metallic breath of oxidized iron, crushed flint, and something like ozone held at room temperature. It does not circulate. It has not circulated in a long time. It sits over the plateau like a lid, heavy enough to muffle, thin enough to remind you of the altitude.

Beneath it, the ground is a single, continuous entity. What was once separate materials—stone, slag, ceramic, steel, glass, composite—has been worked together by epochs of dry abrasion into a fused, undulating plane. Every edge has surrendered to curve. Every fracture has been polished smooth. The surface is warm to the touch, radiating a gentle, even heat that rises not from above but from within, bleeding upward through capillary networks of mineral and metal that have long since forgotten their original purposes. You walk on what might have been streets, or factory floors, or the roofs of buried infrastructure. The distinction has eroded away. The terrain dips and rises in shallow, rhythmic swells, like a sea that froze mid-breath and then spent a million years learning to be stone.

Light is not given here; it is withheld. The overhead is a uniform matte charcoal, a suspended stratum of fine electrostatic particulate that absorbs rather than scatters. Sunlight, if it exists, never reaches this place. What illumination there is comes from the ground itself, and even that is reluctant. Hairline seams glow faint amber, tracing geometric lattices that might be tectonic stress patterns or might be buried conduits. Flush-mounted nodules, smooth as river pebbles, cycle through dim ochre to bruised violet over hours, like indicator lamps dreaming. Shadows do not fall; they pool. They collect in depressions, thick and viscous, refusing to sharpen or stretch. When you move, the darkness parts with a kind of resistance, then settles back into place behind you, seamless and untroubled. You navigate by warmth underfoot, by the subtle change in air pressure near apertures, by the way sound behaves.

This was urban. It still is, in the way a skeleton is still a body. The layout is unmistakably planned, though time has softened its grammar. Terraced foundations have subsided into concentric rings, their steps worn into gentle slopes that invite the foot rather than command it. Vast, low domes sit half-buried, their surfaces crazed with age, resembling dried mud flats more than architecture. Between them, straight, shallow trenches run for miles, perfectly parallel, their bottoms lined with ribbed impressions that might have been rails, or conveyor tracks, or utility races. Chimneys stand as smooth, hollow monoliths, their interiors glazed with crystalline deposits that chime faintly when the temperature shifts by a fraction of a degree. There are no doors, no windows, no signs of habitation. Only apertures, worn oval by centuries of dry particulate flow, leading into warm, dark interiors where the air is still and the walls hum at a frequency below hearing. The structures are sparse, deliberately spaced, each isolated by expanses of flat, glassy plain. It feels less abandoned than completed. A city that finished its work and chose to rest.

The industry here did not die. It idles. Entropy has been negotiated with, not defeated. Deep beneath the fused crust, thermochemical engines run on slow mineral oxidation, drawing trace gases from the sealed atmosphere and converting them to heat at a rate measured in decades per cycle. Piezoelectric matrices embedded in the ground harvest micro-vibrations—thermal expansion, stress relief, the rare footfall—and feed them back into closed-loop systems that maintain the plateau's equilibrium. Nothing accelerates. Nothing decays. Machines do not break; they settle into minimum-draw states, their moving parts lubricated by their own oxidized dust, their gears turning so slowly that a single rotation outlasts civilizations. Heat does not escape; it cycles. Light does not reflect; it is absorbed and re-radiated as warmth. The place is a factory that has achieved perfect efficiency by ceasing to produce anything but stillness.

Life persists, but not in forms that recognize water or chlorophyll. There are no leaves, no roots, no spores. Instead, there are the grazers: low, multi-legged things with carapaces of polished silicate and joints of flexible, dark ceramic. They move with metronomic slowness, absorbing trace atmospheric compounds and thermal differentials through porous underbellies. They leave behind faint, trigonometric tracks in the fine dust, patterns that overlap but never intersect. Higher up, drifting entities catch the occasional thermal sigh—collapsed, membranous forms stretched over desiccated wire armatures, gliding silently between the monoliths. They do not hunt. They do not flee. They exist in the same equilibrium as the stone. Once every few years, one settles onto a warm vent, folds itself, and becomes indistinguishable from the terrain. Biology here is not a struggle; it is a posture.

The rules of this place are quiet, but absolute. Geometry behaves with gentle insistence: parallel trenches appear to converge without ever meeting; distances compress when stared at directly, then stretch when viewed peripherally; the horizon seems to breathe, rising and falling by millimeters over hours. Time does not pass here so much as accumulate. There are no seasons, no weather, no gradients to drive change. The air pressure is slightly high, making sound travel differently—low frequencies carry for miles, bending around the domes and monoliths, while high frequencies die instantly, swallowed by the light-hungry materials. If you drop a stone, it does not clatter; it thuds, then sighs, then is absorbed into the ambient hum. The darkness is not an absence. It is a medium. It holds warmth, holds sound, holds the slow tick of a world that has finished changing.

Silence is the default, but not absolute. A subsonic thrum vibrates through the ground, felt in the teeth and the ribs, the idle frequency of a continent-sized mechanism resting at equilibrium. Occasionally, a sharp *click* echoes from a distant stress joint, clean and dry as a breaking twig. Sometimes, a long, drawn-out *hiss* as a sealed chamber equalizes pressure over the span of a season. The fauna make no calls, only the soft *scritch* of ceramic on glass, the faint rustle of membrane over warm air. If you stand still, the quiet deepens until you hear your own pulse, and beneath it, the slow, deliberate rhythm of a place that has achieved its final form. The basin stretches for miles, bounded by low ridges that blur into the dark overhead. Features are scattered, separated by vast flats of fused tile and glass. You can walk for hours without encountering anything taller than your knee, then suddenly stand before a structure the size of a cathedral, its surface smooth as a river stone, its purpose forgotten but its form perfect. The emptiness is not barren. It is architectural. It is the negative space of a machine that has finally come to rest, warm and dark and complete, holding its breath forever.
