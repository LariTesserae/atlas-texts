The basin opens like a cupped hand, low and wide, its rim lost in a permanent ceiling of warm mist. Light does not fall here; it seeps. It presses through the humidity in a dim, amber wash, catching on wet stone and pooling in shallow channels that vein every surface. The air is thick, comfortably hot, and carries the smell of damp clay, crushed limestone, and the faint metallic tang of mineral water rising from below. Nothing is dry. Nothing ever has been.

There are no plants. Not a blade, not a leaf, not a trace of green. The world is mineral and made. Every slope, every flat, every vertical face is built or laid bare. Terraces climb the basin walls in tight, overlapping tiers, connected by stair-flights that double as watercourses, by corbelled bridges, by narrow alleys that open without warning into vaulted courtyards. The stone is raw, sharp-edged, unrounded by time. Blocks sit with fresh chisel marks still visible in their faces. Mortar weeps pale slurry from joints. Clay tiles, fired to a dull rust, overlap like scales. Where water meets air, it leaves crusts of white calcite and iron-red bloom, but never moss, never lichen. The surfaces are perpetually new, perpetually unweathered, as if the city were laid down this morning and will be laid down again tomorrow.

Water is everywhere, but never in flood. It beads on walls, runs in capillary grooves cut by hand, drips from the undersides of arches, gathers in stepped cisterns that overflow into the next, and the next. It is warm to the touch, slightly alkaline, carrying dissolved minerals that precipitate the moment they meet the cooler air. The hydrology is gravity and patience: clay pipes buried under flagstones, weep holes drilled with bronze-tipped bow drills, channels lined with smooth gravel hauled from deeper strata. There is no pump, no wheel, only slope and seep and the constant, quiet surrender of water to stone.

Life thrives in the absence of green. The fauna is dense, layered, uncountable. Pale, smooth-skinned amphibians cling to the undersides of stairs, their throats pulsing in the dim light. Flocks of small, soot-colored birds nest in the eaves of corbelled roofs, their wings clicking against ceramic tiles. In the water channels, blind crustaceans sift microbial films from the silt, while long-bodied, scaleless fish drift in the deeper cisterns, feeding on falling detritus. On the terraces, communal burrowers—something between a rodent and a pangolin—scrape mineral salts from the weeping walls with keratin claws, their young tumbling over warm stone. Insects with translucent, veinless wings cluster around seep-points, drinking condensate. Nothing photosynthesizes. The base of the food web is chemical: iron-oxidizing bacteria, sulfur-reducing mats, pale rubbery fungal nodules that fruit in shadowed joints, grazed upon, broken down, returned to the water, beginning again. The ecology is recognizable, just shifted one rung down the ladder of light.

And through it all, the sound. It does not strike the ear; it occupies it. A deafening, unvarying pressure that has no beginning and no end. It is the hiss of water moving through ten thousand clay throats. It is the rhythmic, metronomic *tink-tink-tink* of bronze chisels striking fresh stone, echoing from terrace to terrace, never speeding, never slowing. It is the low, continuous murmur of thousands of creatures moving, calling, feeding, breathing. It is the scuff of bare feet on damp flagstones, the creak of hide ropes, the hollow knock of wooden mallets, the distant, wordless work-chants that rise and fall in exact, unchanging cadence. The sound does not echo chaotically. The dense, porous stone, the hanging moisture, the layered architecture absorb and blend it into a single acoustic mass. You do not hear individual sources after the first minute. You hear the place itself, breathing at a fixed rate.

The city is under construction, and always has been. Scaffolds of lashed pole and cured hide lean against half-finished walls. Quarried blocks sit on wooden sledges, waiting for the next shift of hands. Kilns glow at the basin's edge, baking clay into tile and brick. Workers move with deliberate, unhurried precision, passing tools that show generations of wear but no evolution in form. There is no blueprint, no foreman, no rush. The building is not progress; it is maintenance. Every stone laid replaces one sealed into a lower tier. Every channel cleared feeds the same volume it carried yesterday. The population does not grow or shrink. The water does not rise or fall. The temperature does not shift. The light does not brighten or darken. The city is emerging, perpetually, into exactly what it already is.

This is the rule of the basin: equilibrium as a verb. Nothing decays because nothing is allowed to weather. Nothing advances because nothing is permitted to finish. The raw edges stay raw. The weeping stays weeping. The sound stays deafening. The warmth stays warm. Time does not pass here so much as it circulates, like the water in the channels, like the creatures in the walls, like the chisels in the hands. To stand in it is to stand inside a held breath that never needs to be released.

You can walk for hours and never leave the same moment. The terraces repeat, the alleys fold back on themselves, the cisterns overflow into identical cisterns. Fauna part and return. Workers step aside and resume. The mist holds. The stone sweats. The sound presses. And the city, dense and low and bright with damp, continues its quiet, deafening act of becoming what it has always been.
