I come down the switchbacks not as a stranger, but as a weight testing the stone.

The wind above my shoulders still carries the plateau's grit, that dry, whistling emptiness that scours bone and bleaches rock. But my knees already know the curve of the steps. Each one is dished, polished smooth by centuries of bare soles and the soft, endless abrasion of dust. I do not count them. I feel them. The air changes before my eyes adjust to the amber gloom: it thickens, warms, settles against my skin like a woven blanket. It carries the sweet-sharp tang of weeping bark, the mineral breath of baked clay, the faint, fibrous dryness of husks waiting to be split. My lungs slow. My ribs learn the new rhythm. I step out of the gale and into the cupped hand.

At first, I think I am a harvester.

Dawn finds me on the lower terraces, where the capillary stone weeps its slow, invisible gift. My hands press woven sponges against the rock. I feel the cool dampness seep into the fibers. I lift. I wring. The resin-lined jar catches the drops with a soft, metronomic tap. But my fingers do not move to the next stone. They linger. They trace the channel. They read the microscopic fractures, the slow calcification, the places where the stone's breath is growing shallow, where the night's condensation no longer finds its way down. I am not here to take. I am here to listen to the rock's exhalation. To feel where the seam is tightening.

Then I think I am a rope-mender.

Midday seals the canopy. The air grows heavy, perfumed, still. I sit in the shaded courtyard, back against warm fieldstone, palms working sinew and resin-thread. The fibers speak in tension. A frayed strand hums a different pitch when plucked. I match it. I splice it. I sing the low, overlapping hum that keeps the twist steady, that tells the hands when to pull and when to yield. But I do not tie the final knot. I leave it loose. I pass it to the child whose fingers are still learning the groove, whose grip is still too eager, whose mistakes will teach the rope its true strength. My work is not completion. It is continuation.

The hollow teaches me what I am by how it answers me.

When I step on a walkway, the bridge sighs. I pause. I shift my weight until the sigh becomes a sigh of relief, not strain. When I speak, my voice does not carry; it pools. I learn to pitch it to the stone's resonance, to let the curved walls round my syllables until they blend with the wind-vents' slow flute. I do not shout. I adjust. When a mottled hunter drops from the upper rim, the singing thins. I do not freeze. I drop my voice to the sternum. I let the vibration hold the space. I watch the shadow move through the lower brush. I feel the quick scatter of wings. I wait for the pestles to resume. I swell back into the chorus. There is no panic. Only recognition. The basin edits itself. I allow it.

I am not a harvester. I am not a mender. I am the seam-reader.

I walk the line between wild and tended, between tension and release, between the stone's memory and the fiber's future. My skin has grown the downy nap, not from birth, but from years of sleeping in the shaded niches, of pressing my forearms to capillary rock at first light, of letting the basin's volatile oils settle into my pores until I carry the same faint, aromatic tackiness as the weeping trunks. My shoulders and spine shed the translucent scales now. I collect them myself. I grind them on the dished stone. I mix them with clay and fibrous mortar. I press them into the joints where the walkway meets the socket, where the wall meets the earth, where the old meets the new. I am becoming part of the binding.

They do not give me a title. Titles are for things that stand apart. I am given a pestle. I am given a stretch of frayed cord. I am given a place by the resin-lined depression where the condensation channels converge. The elders do not instruct me. They hum. I match the pitch. My hands learn the weight of the grinding stone. My feet learn the sway of the central span. My eyes learn to track the copper-backed birds' sudden silence without flinching. When two voices fall out of rhythm, when an argument threatens to fracture the courtyard's cadence, I do not mediate. I place the same rope in both their hands. I begin the splice-chant. I wait for their grips to find each other. I wait for the twist to catch. Harmony is not given. It is tuned.

I used to think I came from the plateau. That I descended seeking shelter from the wind-bone and the pale rock. But the hollow does not shelter. It integrates. It asks what you can exhale, what you can catch, what you can hold. My breath now feeds the capillary stone. My shed scales bind the terraces. My songs pace the labor. I do not remember a time before the amber light, before the resin's tackiness on my fingertips, before the wind-vents' slow flute became the measure of my pulse. The high country's cold is a memory my body no longer keeps. I store heat in my ribs. I release it when the stone cools. I am part of the exchange.

I am not a person who lives in the basin. I am a frequency the basin has learned to carry. A tension it has learned to balance. A wear pattern it has recognized as its own.

When the canopy closes its pores, I slow my hands. I let the stillness pool. When the oldest tree sheds a bead of sap, I do not catch it. I listen for the tap. I feel it travel through the packed earth, through the soles of my feet, up through the arches, into the knees, into the spine. I adjust my grip. I begin again.

They do not ask my name. Names are for things that leave. I am the curve of the step. The sigh of the bridge. The hum beneath the pestle. The space between the predator's drop and the chorus's return. I am the hand that finds the frayed strand before it snaps. The voice that drops a register so the stone can hold it. The body that presses its back to the sun-warmed wall and gives the heat back to the night.

I am what happens when the arid learns to breathe. When the wind-bone folds inward. When the hollow stops asking for rain and starts answering itself.

I am here. I am holding. I am continuing.
