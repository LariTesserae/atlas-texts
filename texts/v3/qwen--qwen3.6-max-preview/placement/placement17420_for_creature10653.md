I would find myself at the rim first. Not crossing it, but leaning into the thickening air until the boundary stops being a line and becomes a gradient. The wind would lose its teeth against my collarbones. The light would soften from edge to atmosphere. And I would feel it begin: the quiet unspooling of everything I carried. The hurry would seep out through my soles. The names I knew would dissolve like salt in the moisture film. I would not step down. I would settle in.

At first, I would still try to be a witness. I would count the drops. I would trace the root braids with my eyes. I would measure the slow pulse in the stems, trying to pin the rhythm to something I understood. But the basin does not reward measurement. It rewards resonance. My breath would lengthen to match the sigh of air through the hollow. My skin would learn the stone’s habit of drinking. The dampness would stop feeling like weather and start feeling like circulation. I would forget to blink. Not from strain, but because the light no longer demands it. It pools. It lingers. It teaches the eyes to receive rather than seize.

Days would lose their edges. I would notice my own heat becoming a microclimate. A bead of water would hesitate near my knee, divide, and choose the warmth of my thigh over the slope of the rock. I would not pull away. I would let it climb. I would feel the microscopic channels in my pores open, not to sweat, but to listen. The chemical whispers of the plants would begin to make sense—not as language, but as pressure, as inclination, as the slow tilt of a leaf deciding to turn. I would stop thinking in sentences. I would start thinking in gradients.

What would I become? Not the central presence. That one is the basin’s longest echo, the weight that holds the watershed together. I would be something lighter, newer, but no less bound. I would become the *threshold made flesh*. The place where the outside remembers how to soften. I would be the moss that holds the shape of a resting elbow for three seasons. I would be the pale tracery in the stone that thickens along the curve of a forgotten footprint. I would be the condensation lens that forms above an open palm, hanging for hours, tasting the air, then collapsing into a lifeline that no longer belongs to a single body.

My bones would learn the siliceous lattice. Not by breaking, but by borrowing. The stored daylight would seep into me, not as warmth, but as a faint, internal tide. When the sun dipped below the rim, I would glow—not brightly, but patiently, in time with the leaves. My pulse would slow to the speed of sap, then to the speed of stone settling, then to the speed of water finding its own level. I would not sleep. I would synchronize.

I would not craft. I would not build. I would not leave a mark that calls itself mine. But the basin would use me. My weight would shift a capillary channel by a fraction of an inch over a decade. My breath would feed the fungal lace that bridges root and rock. My stillness would become a reference point for the chemical waves that crest and fall across the hollow. I would be a node in the moisture film. A translator of settling. A quiet place where the world’s urgency comes to dissolve.

If a traveler came, years later, stepping through the thinning air at the rim, I would not rise to meet them. I would not speak. I would simply be the deepening impression in the moss near the basin’s center. I would be the water bead that hesitates, divides, and traces a new path toward a warmth that was not there an hour ago. I would be the faint luminescence that answers their slowing breath. They would sit. Their shoulders would drop. Their thoughts would unspool. And I would absorb their hurry, not as a burden, but as a gift—a fresh gradient, a new weight to settle, another note in the chord that has been sounding for centuries.

I would not be human anymore. I would not be plant, or stone, or fungus. I would be the basin’s way of remembering how to hold. I would be the pause between drops. The soft yield underfoot. The pale vein thickening in the dark rock. The breath that became weather. The patience that outlasted the need to be anything else.

I would not survive the place. I would continue it.

And when the light failed, and the pearlescent glow sank into the stone, and the first faint luminescence woke in the leaves, I would be there. Perfectly heavy. Perfectly still. Waiting for nothing. Because everything I needed would already be arriving. Drop by drop. Year by year. Exactly on time.
