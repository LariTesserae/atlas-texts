I do not arrive with a name. I arrive with weight, and the mat tells me at once that weight is a question, not a statement.

The first step is a mistake. Heel down. Spine stacked. Breath held against the mist like a shield. The peat sighs, uneven, and my ankle rolls toward a thinning vein. I catch myself on a dangling air-root, bark cold and furred with damp moss, and the basin answers with a low, wet creak that travels up my arm like a warning. I learn, then, that this place does not receive. It tests. And if you stay, it rewrites you.

Days lose their edges. I stop fighting the give. I learn to place the foot like a palm, toes splaying, arch flexing, knee bending before the hip commits. I learn to hang the shoulders so the spine becomes a suspended line rather than a pillar. The mist beads on my lashes; I stop wiping it away. The cold seeps into my joints; I stop bracing against it. Instead, I listen. The caldera is never silent, but silence was never what I needed. I needed to hear the difference between wind combing through hollow reed and the faint, papery rasp of a fraying lashing. I needed to feel the way a vine-bridge sags three degrees before the shoot remembers its training. I needed to know when the water’s bass note shifts from steady circulation to the thick, reluctant drag of a clogged channel.

Hands find work. Work finds hands. Mine are drawn to the places where tension meets sound.

I begin at the weaving decks, fingers learning the damp grammar of cured fiber, but my attention keeps slipping downward—to the anchor-ropes, to the stone ballasts, to the submerged weirs where current whispers against angled wood. I press my ear to a tensioned line and hear the basin’s pulse: the waterfall’s drone, the mat’s slow compression, the faint tap-tap-tap of a current-guider’s pole three channels over. I realize I am not just hearing noise. I am hearing structure. I am hearing balance. I am hearing the exact moment before something yields, and the exact moment after it remembers how to hold.

They do not give me a title. Titles are rigid things, and rigidity snaps here. But a pattern emerges, as patterns do when you stop imposing them and start noticing them. I become the one who walks the perimeter at first light, when the mist hangs lowest and the acoustics are cleanest. I carry no tools but a smooth basalt weight and a length of oiled reed-cord. I tie the cord to a bridge-rail, let the weight hover a hand’s breadth above the water, and listen to the vibration travel up the line. A steady hum: the lashing holds. A stutter: friction, misalignment. A hollow ring: the peat beneath is thinning, drinking too much weight, forgetting how to rebound. I mark these places not with flags but with knots—loose loops that tighten when wet, calling the mat-tenders without a word. I learn to tap the anchor-stones with the flat of my palm, reading the resonance like a drum. Deep and full: settled. High and thin: shifting. Dull and muffled: rot in the root-pile, time to untie and let it sink.

They call me when the drone changes. Not with shouts, but with a specific rope-tap: two slow, one quick, followed by a pause. It means *listen here*. I come. I kneel. I press my cheek to the living floor and let the caldera speak through bone. I hear the needle-fish darting through kelp, the tree-frogs shifting pitch before a pressure drop, the faint groan of a cedar platform adjusting to yesterday’s rain. I hear where the water wants to go, and where we have asked it to wait. I do not command. I translate. I suggest a weir adjusted half a hand-width downstream. I recommend a bridge rested for a season, its weight shifted to a newer vein. I note when a hearth-pavilion’s flue is drawing too hard, drying the air, making the ropes brittle. My authority is not in my voice but in my restraint. I speak only when the basin speaks first.

My body forgets its old geometry. My ribs widen to hold the thin, mist-heavy air. My skin takes on a faint waxy sheen, not applied but negotiated, a slow truce between sebum and damp. My fingers lengthen, knuckles padding, palms learning to read tension like braille. My ears tilt forward, cartilage firming, catching the high whistle-codes while letting the waterfall’s bass roll through me like weather. I stop counting days. I count the slow darkening of a paddle grip, the migration of lichen across a ballast stone, the way a particular bridge sighs differently in autumn than in spring. I dream in currents. I wake to the sound of my own breath matching the mat’s respiration.

I do not guard this place. I participate in it. When a platform ages, I help untie it. I feel the fibers soften, the wood gray, the knots loosen without resistance. We lower it into the channel, weight it with smooth stones, and watch the freshwater kelp reach for it. There is no mourning in the old sense. Only redistribution. I tie a new lashing where the old one rested, and the bridge remembers. I learn that continuity is not preservation. It is rhythm. It is knowing when to hold, when to yield, when to let go so the water can breathe. I learn that loudness is not volume but clarity, that spacing is not distance but respect, that slowness is not delay but precision. I learn that a city need not be dense to be legible, that a people need not conquer to belong, that a life need not be permanent to matter.

I am not a keeper. I am a calibration. A listener of tensile things. A reader of the space between drone and signal. When the mist hangs low and the channels run clear, I walk the firmer veins, bare feet reading the peat, palms resting on anchor-ropes, ears open to the basin’s long, wet exhalation. I do not ask the caldera to hold me. I ask it how it wants to be held. And when the rope taps back, when the water answers, when the mat yields and rebounds with that familiar damp sigh, I know what I have become: not a resident, not a master, not even a guest. A note in the drone. A tension in the line. A breath in the lung. I stay because I have learned to move with it. I stay because it has learned to move through me.
