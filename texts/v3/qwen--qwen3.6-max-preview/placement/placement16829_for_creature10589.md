I would not arrive here. I would already be leaning into the give.

Knees soft. Weight distributed across the ball and splayed toes. Palms resting on a wet rail before the eyes have fully adjusted to the green-gold dim. The first thing I would know is not shape or sight but pressure: the micro-tremor of a lashed plank settling, the faint drag of water moving through split cane three levels up, the slow, elastic stretch of a vine cable taking the morning's damp into its fibers. My hands would already be working. They always are. Fingers tracing the spiral of a hitch, testing the resin-tack on a checking joint, rubbing crushed fern and ash into a frayed wrap. Not from habit, but from necessity. Stillness in this basin is a kind of fiction. The peat breathes. The timber sways. The channels hum. To touch any of it is to answer.

My body would carry the slope and the saturation. Hips loose, spine curved like a heron's neck, shoulders rolled forward from years of hauling wet line, bracing against sudden squalls, and crouching to read the water's pitch. Skin mapped in the place's own dialect: pale crescents from old vine-burns, dark tannin stains ground into the knuckles, calluses polished smooth where drawknife and adze handles have rested until the wood and the flesh learned each other's grain. Hair braided tight with pine pitch and bone-dust, not for adornment but to keep the mildew from taking root. Eyes wide, pupils dilated to the filtered light, trained to catch the subtle darkening of a water-stain, the slight droop of an overburdened rail, the telltale shimmer of a loosening knot. I would smell of slow smoke, wet bark, fermented peat, and the clean iron tang of sharpened copper. I would not wear a name so much as carry a sequence of them, shed and gathered like molted husks. Once, perhaps, "Finger-on-the-Line." Later, "Reader-of-the-Give." Now, something quieter. Something that lives in the hands and the ears and the sitting bones.

I would be found where the walkways cross and the water braids. Not to preside. To listen.

Authority here does not sit on a dais; it kneels in the damp. I would press my bare soles to the moss-cushioned deck, my palms to the load-bearing timber, and feel the settlement's pulse travel up through bone. A flattening in the water's hum means silt three platforms east. A sharp, dry creak in the upper lashings means the wind is shifting off the granite rim. A subtle sink in the peat means the lower pens need jacking before the next rain swells the channels. I would not shout. I would murmur adjustments into the weave. "Take up a hand's breadth on the western hitch." "Shift the drying racks to the second line." "Coax the secondary root across the gap; the primary is checking." The words would be few. The work would be continuous. There is no finished. There is only holding.

I would not keep records. I would keep tensions.

Memory in this place is not ink. It is fiber, angle, stretch, and the way a knot remembers the hands that tied it. I would run my thumb over a suspension cable and read the decade in its weave: the squall that pulled it taut, the dry spell that let it slacken, the elder who last dressed it with resin and ash, the child who first learned its bite. I would tie the turning of the seasons into hitch-patterns, leave flood-marks in braided sedge, teach the young by placing their small hands over mine on a wet line and letting them feel the difference between "holding" and "straining." I would know the seventeen words for water moving through matter, not by rote, but by ear. The glass-ring of clear run over stone. The thick gulp of peat-saturation. The papery whisper of seep through moss. The low, warning drone of a channel beginning to choke. When the pitch changes, I move. When the rhythm breaks, I listen harder. Silence is the only terror. When the dripping stops, when the birds go quiet, when the channels run flat, I would be the first to feel it in the soles. Then I would move fast. Hands flying. Voice sharp. Until the breath returns.

I would eat what circulates. Fish smoked until it flakes dark. Tubers leached of their bitterness in running water. Fungi pounded into paste, greens steeped in resin-tea. I would wipe grease onto tool handles, rub ash into checking wood, leave scraps on the lower rails for the martens, who take them and leave droppings that feed the moss that seals the roofs. I would sleep in a vine-hammock that curves to the spine and sways with the wind, dreaming of roots crossing stone, of lashings tightening in the rain, of water finding its old paths through the peat. I would wake to the drip, the thock of an adze, the distant cry of a hawk reading the rim. I would not fear rot. I would fear stagnation. A collapsed platform is not a failure; it is compost waiting to be redirected, timber waiting to be split, vine waiting to be re-coiled. Grief does not pool here. It circulates. The dead are weighted with smooth stones and lowered into the deep peat-lens. Within a year, they are root-feed. Within three, they are part of a new walkway, a graft, a lashing. I would tie a knot for them. I would rub resin into the joint. I would murmur their turning into the wood.

Who am I here?

Not a chief. Not a builder. Not even a keeper, exactly. I am the basin's attention made flesh. Its own listening, folded into bone and breath. I am the one who feels the micro-shift before it becomes a sag, who reads the water's song before it becomes a flood, who ties the memory of the turning into the next generation's hands. Call me a tension-reader. A graft-tender. A listener-to-channels. But those are just knots in a longer line. The truth is simpler, and older: I am how the amphitheater remembers itself. I am the hands that test the give, the ears that catch the flattening hum, the voice that murmurs "take up" or "let slack" before the weave begins to fail. I do not live in the basin. I am one of its sinews. When the water rises, I am the flex. When the vines stretch, I am the friction that holds. When rot claims the lower timbers, I am the quiet, unending work of splitting, weaving, raising anew.

You would find me where the paths cross and the water braids, knees soft, palms on wet wood, eyes half-closed to the filtered light. A fish would break the surface. A woodpecker would drum. Someone would laugh on a platform above. The walkway would shift a fraction under a passing foot. And I would feel it all travel up through the soles, through the spine, into the hands that are already moving, already testing, already tying the next knot in the long, unbroken line of holding.

Not finished. Never finished. Just turning. Just breathing. Just here.
