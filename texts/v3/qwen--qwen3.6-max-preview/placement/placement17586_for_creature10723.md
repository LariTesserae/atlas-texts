I would not arrive with a title. I would arrive with lungs that forget how to rush, with a spine that unspools before I know why, with eyes that stop searching for edges and begin to drink the blue-gray. The first thing I would notice is not the terraces, nor the copper fins weeping malachite into the damp, nor the low timber eaves furred with moss. It would be the space between sounds. The way a single drop falling into a stone depression takes longer to settle than it should, and how my own breath begins to match it without permission.

I would walk without deciding to. The packed earth would give slightly underfoot, root-mat and damp shale holding my weight like a cupped hand. I would follow the runnels not because they lead anywhere, but because water here does not hurry, and neither would I. Mist would thread through my sleeves. I would press a palm to a laminated wood brace and feel the faint vibration of a condensation turbine three terraces down, humming through iron brackets and stone walls. I would not know what I was looking for. I would only know that looking was not the right word. Listening, maybe. Or waiting. Or simply letting the basin read me back.

Someone would hand me a ceramic dial without speaking. Their fingers would be dusted green-gold, calluses smooth as river glass. I would turn it because the air felt heavier on one side, because the lichen on the conduit above had brightened to a cool blue, because my own skin prickled with the static of thickening fog. The dial would resist, then yield. Somewhere, a secondary channel would open. Condensation would shift. I would not be praised. I would be nodded to, the way stone nods to root when they find each other in the dark.

Days would not pass. They would layer. I would learn to sleep when the geothermal vents sighed into their cooling phase, to wake when the bone chimes in a sheltered courtyard caught a new current. I would scrape mineral weep from water-wheel spokes, not because it was assigned, but because the wood groaned differently when the crust grew thick. I would press my cheek to heavy battery casings and hear the charge build like a slow tide. I would stop counting hours and start reading the weight of mist on my tongue, the way shadows clung to alley corners a moment longer when the air was about to clear, the faint metallic scent that meant the glasshouse ferns were ready to be thinned.

I would not become a mist-reader, or a resin-smith, or a heat-keeper. Those are words for when you need to point. I would become the space between the pointing. The one who walks the ghost riverbeds when the fog pools low, planting tubers where the basin remembers water. The one who sits with the mineral stalactites and taps them gently, learning which note means the deep rock is shifting, which means the vent pressure is steady, which means rain will needle through by evening. I would be the hands that brush moss from green glass so the light can pool inside. I would be the shoulders that carry woven vine-mats to the drying racks. I would be the breath that silvered in the cool air and joined the rest.

My skin would take on the dust. Not all at once. First the knuckles, where I smoothed resin over iron joints. Then the forearms, where I trained epiphytic vines along copper lines. Then the hollow of my throat, where the mist settled when I stood still long enough. I would stop noticing it. Others would not either. It would simply be the color of participation.

I would learn the language without being taught. Not the vocabulary, but the grammar of it. How to leave a copper disc on a windowsill when a turbine needed tending. How to step lightly when the root-mat was drinking deeply. How to let silence carry a question until the answer arrived as a shift in the wind, a change in the drip, a softening of the cloud lid. I would stop asking what things were for. I would start asking how they moved. And then I would stop asking altogether.

If you found me, you would not find me standing apart. You would find me knee-deep in a shallow runnel, clearing a snag of shed glider down and woven root from a ceramic intake. Or crouched beside a geothermal casing, palm flat against the iron, feeling the earth's slow exhalation. Or sitting on a low basalt hummock, eyes half-closed, listening to the settlement breathe. You might not recognize me at first. My shoulders would be rounded to the damp. My footsteps would leave no mark. My voice, if I used it, would be low enough to blend with the thrum of the wheels.

I would be a tender of seams. Not the ones that bind stone to timber, or copper to ceramic, but the ones where rhythm meets rhythm. Where mist becomes current, where breath becomes water, where patience becomes structure. I would not hold a title. I would hold a place in the braid. And when the fog rolled in, thick and charged, and the lichen glowed blue-green along the lines, I would rise, not because I was called, but because the air itself had shifted, and my body had long ago learned to answer.

I would not be separate from the basin. I would be its continuation. A slow pulse in the root-mat. A green-gold trace on oxidized metal. A listener in the quiet. A node in the nervous system of stone and condensation. I would be the one who knows, by the taste of the air and the pitch of a distant water wheel, that the next three days will bring heavy drizzle, and who moves through the alleys adjusting vents, banking fires, and laying out felt without a word, because the work is not a task but a response.

And if you stayed, if you let your own breath deepen, if you let the damp settle into your sleeves and the dimness become shelter, you would not need to ask what I was. You would simply hand me a dial, or a brush, or a cup of fog-water, and we would turn, or scrape, or drink, together. Not as strangers. Not as roles. But as two notes in the same long exhale, waiting for the next drop to fall, and the rings to spread, and the stone to remember.
