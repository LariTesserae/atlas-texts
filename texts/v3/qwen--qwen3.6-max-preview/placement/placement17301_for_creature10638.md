You would find me high on the inward-leaning rim, where the pale limestone is sharpest and the light is thinnest, filtered through the woven canopy of the upper tier until it falls as a bruised, greenish twilight. I am standing on a narrow walkway of hard-tamped clay and crushed shell, my splayed feet reading the familiar texture of the path, but my attention is not on the ground. My body is pressed against a raw fracture face, knees bent, spine curved to match the overhang, shoulders rounded until they brush the weeping stone.

I am one of the tenders, but my rhythm has drifted to the geology. While others below coax the aerial roots or smooth the water grooves, my attunement is to the restless settling of the hollow's bones. My skin, pebbled and dark like wet loam, is dusted white at the forearms and chest with the fine mineral powder that sifts from the fissures. The dust has worked into the grain of my flesh, lightening the mottling, making me appear as a standing fragment of the wall itself. Along my arms, the shallow ridges that channel condensation are filled with a paste of grit and moisture; when I move, they leave faint, streaked impressions on the clay, like the tracks of slow water.

My hands are splayed wide against a vertical seam where limestone meets plastic clay. The keratinous caps of my fingertips are worn smooth and shiny from years of testing unconsolidated crystals. I do not push. I do not pry. I rest my pads against the microfractures and wait. The stone is young; it has not yet learned to be still. Deep within the fissure, crystals shift with a soundless grind, and the clay swells imperceptibly as warmth pulls moisture upward. I feel this through the sensitive skin of my palms, a vibration so faint it would be lost to any but a body that has surrendered to the frequency of the basin.

A tension is building in the seam. The clay is thirsty, shrinking slightly away from the stone, creating a void that could lead to a flake shearing off, a scar on the raw face. This is not damage; it is the hollow breathing. But the walkway runs just below this shelf, and a falling flake would disturb the moss, crack the tamped surface, break the acoustic stillness. So I negotiate.

I begin to hum. The sound does not leave my throat; it stays in the chest, a low thrum that travels through my collarbones, down my arms, and into the stone. I pitch the vibration to the resonance of the limestone, feeling for the moment when the hum and the rock sing together. When they align, the friction in the fissure eases. The vibration encourages the clay to relax, to expand laterally rather than pull away. I shift my weight, pressing my palms in a slow, rhythmic cycle: press, hold, release. Press, hold, release. The repetition is the architecture. Over minutes, over hours, the clay responds. It softens, creeps forward, and fills the void, binding to the grit of the stone. The tension dissolves. The seam stabilizes. No tool has touched it. No mortar has been applied. Only patience, pressure, and the shared language of vibration.

Below me, the hollow feels the shift. My hum has traveled through the walkway, down the living railings of trained vine, into the communal plaza. The rhythm of the basin adjusts. A root-guide near the mid-level pauses, feels the change in tension through the vine network, and redirects a tendril that was seeking purchase near the stress point. A water-tender smooths a groove slightly to the left, anticipating the new capillary path the settled clay will create. There is no signal sent, no command given. My body is simply a node in the nervous system, and the adjustment ripples through the collective like a breath moving through lungs.

I am not a guardian of the wall. I am its conversation partner. The wall is learning to hold its shape, and I am the memory of pressure that helps it remember. My calluses are shaped like the ledges I tend; the curve of my spine mirrors the angle of the overhang; the dust in my skin is the dust of the fissure. I am becoming the place, just as the place is becoming itself.

When the work is done for this cycle, I do not step away. I slide down until I am sitting on the walkway, back against the warmed stone, legs extended. I take a broad leaf from a nearby epiphyte colony, fold it, and press it against a weeping point in the clay, guiding the moisture into a shallow depression where a cluster of ferns waits. My teeth, broad and flat, nip a drying fiber from a vine, and I weave it absently into the root mat beneath my feet, reinforcing the path. The motion is automatic, a continuation of the hum.

I am younger than the elders who move with the economy of stone, but my body is already recording the history of this ledge. My fingers know the taste of every mineral seam. My feet know the exact pressure needed to tamp the clay without crushing the shell. I have no name, only a rhythm: the slow pulse of the settling wall, the syncopated tap of testing fingertips, the long exhalation of the clay accepting the touch.

Eventually, I will slow. The rhythm will deepen, lengthen, until it matches the condensation cycle of the hollow itself. I will lie down on a platform I have helped to harden, and the clay will accept me. The dust in my skin will return to the fissures. The ridges in my arms will become channels for new roots. The hum in my chest will silence, but the vibration will remain in the stone, a faint memory of pressure that guides the next hand, the next generation, the next negotiation.

But for now, the light is dimming. The air is thickening. A drop of condensation forms on the limestone above me, traces a path down the fracture, and lands on my shoulder, soaking into the dust. I raise a hand, feel the grit, feel the damp, feel the life pulsing in the clay. I press my palm back to the wall. We are still becoming. I am here. I am listening. I am the hollow feeling its own edges, one repetition at a time.
