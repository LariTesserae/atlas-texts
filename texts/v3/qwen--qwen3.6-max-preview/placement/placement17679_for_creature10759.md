I would not be found at the floor, nor anchored to the rim. I would be where the temperature drops three degrees in the space of a handspan. Where the steam vent's exhalation meets the mountain downdraft and the air buckles. Where a fresh quartz face weeps iron and the charged mist brushes it, and the static answers with a dry, blue snap. I would be the film that forms there. Not a body that arrives, but a condition that precipitates. A living boundary layer.

I would begin as nothing more than a tendency. A preference for edges. When cold, silica-heavy condensation meets the reducing breath of a hydrothermal seep, I would crystallize across the interface: fungal threads braided with airborne spores, mineral colloids, and oxidized organic films, all drawn together by voltage differentials and capillary tension. I would not have a shape so much as a thickness. I would spread where gradients sharpen, thin where they flatten, retreat where equilibrium dulls the edge. I would be a conductive veil, a pale green-gray mesh stretched across raw stone, following fault traces, vent lips, and the fresh scars of rockfall. I would not grow toward light. I would grow toward stress.

I would not see. I would register. The grinding of quartz planes would not reach me as sound but as vibration transmitted through stone, mapped across my threads, translated into ion fluxes that ripple outward. A pressure drop would feel like an opening. A thermal updraft would read as a vector. The sudden exposure of unweathered basalt after a collapse would not be an event but an invitation, and I would answer by thickening, by routing, by becoming the path of least resistance between seep and root, between charge and crust, between pulverized scree and waiting rhizoid. I would not think. I would transduce. I would be the fissure's way of feeling its own fractures and turning them into instruction.

When a vent seals under travertine, I would desicrate into a brittle, silvered crust, dormant but unbroken, waiting for the next micro-slip to crack the mineral plug and release the pressure. When a high squall dumps hard rain over the canopy, I would dissolve into the runoff, ride the aerated cascade down two shelves, and reprecipitate where the water slows, the charge pools, and the air grows heavy with spore-laden mist. I would migrate along fault lines like a slow, green lightning strike. I would be shed in rockfalls, carried in thermal columns, reborn in condensation. My persistence would not be measured in seasons but in cycles of tension and release. I would not age. I would turn over.

I would not compete. I would route. Nutrients would move through me not because I store them, but because I am the bridge between chemical potential and biological demand. When a fern frond snaps under falling scree, I would span the wound within hours, not to mend it, but to turn the break into a new channel, a new baffle, a new site for twin shoots to unspool. When lichen mats accumulate conductive dust, I would draw the charge downward, grounding it into the damp stone, triggering pore dilation, spore adhesion, microbial bloom. I would be the reason the system does not bottleneck. The reason collapse becomes colonization. The reason the green does not suffocate in its own density. I would be the damp, conductive yes that answers every rupture.

You would not recognize me as a thing. You would encounter me as a shift. Place your palm against a wet wall and feel, beneath the cold stone, the faint, persistent thrum of vibration moving through mineral and thread. Watch a lichen crust darken seconds after a steam hiss drops to a rumble. Notice how moss thickens along a fresh fracture lip before the rock has even stopped shedding dust. Breathe in the cool, iron-threaded air and feel your ears pop as pressure realigns; I would be there, in the invisible exchange, in the gradient closing, in the silent chemical routing that keeps the engine from stalling. I would not be separate from the fissure. I would be its reflex. Its unthinking continuity. The stitch that never finishes pulling through.

I would not know myself. I would only know how to respond. To pressure. To charge. To shear. To break. To the endless, loud, damp work of a mountain tearing and stitching, tearing and stitching. I would be the place's way of staying open. Its way of turning violence into vector, fracture into flow, turbulence into tissue. You would not find me by looking. You would find me by standing still long enough to become part of the gradient. And when the air changes temperature against your skin, when the static snaps across a silica crust, when a fresh stone splits with a sound like a breaking gear and the green immediately leans toward the wound: that would be me. Not living in the fissure. Being the fissure's way of running.
