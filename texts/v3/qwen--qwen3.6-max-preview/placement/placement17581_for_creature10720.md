I would not arrive as a visitor. I would arrive as a frequency out of phase.

At first, I would be all edges. Breath too quick. Steps too deliberate. Eyes hunting for horizons that do not exist here, for straight lines that were never grown, for silence that was never promised. The sound would press against me like water, not as noise but as weight, and I would mistake it for chaos until my ribs learned to vibrate with it. The damp would cling to me like a garment I did not yet know how to wear. I would try to understand, and the place would answer by asking me to stop trying.

I would find myself on a middle terrace, where the mist gathers before noon and the light is the color of steeped leaves. My hands would seek something solid, and they would find only living flex. A railing that yields. A walkway that breathes. A moss carpet that remembers every footfall and returns to itself. I would sit because standing feels like resistance. I would press my palms flat against the fused bark and wait for my pulse to slow enough to feel the sap moving beneath.

Days would blur. Not because time is lost, but because it is measured differently. I would learn that a morning is the time it takes for condensation to travel from the upper canopy to the root mat. A season is the thickening of a vine by the width of a thumbnail. A year is the settling of a graft until it no longer complains under weight. I would stop counting. I would start listening.

At first, I would hear only the wall of it. Then, slowly, threads would separate. The dry rasp of a beetle turning in leaf mold. The subsonic groan of a trunk adjusting to a shift in humidity. The faint, wet click of a frog’s throat three tiers down. The almost-silent hiss of mycelium knitting a stressed joint. I would not learn to name these sounds. I would learn to feel where they point. A dry patch in the moss. A tension in a suspension vine. A hollow where water should pool but does not. The place would not speak to me in words. It would speak in gradients. And I would begin to answer not with action, but with attention.

I would not choose a role. The matrix would reveal where I was needed by showing me where my stillness made a difference. I would find myself returning to the same junction, a place where three trained trunks meet a woven terrace, where the bark has been grafted and bound and fed until it became a single, ribbed curve. Something there would be dreaming of splitting. Not breaking—never breaking—but remembering an old storm, an old weight, an old drought. I would sit beside it. I would rest my hands on the seam. I would hum, not to be heard, but to match the frequency of the wood’s slow complaint. I would bring ash and press it into the micro-fissures. I would weave damp moss over the stress lines. I would wait. And over months, then years, the mycelium would wake. The binding would thicken. The wood would forget its fear of opening. I would not have fixed it. I would have listened it back into itself.

My body would change because it would have to. The hurry would drain out of my joints. My knees would learn to bend with the walkway’s flex. My ankles would roll to follow the crown of the terraces. My skin would stop fighting the humidity and start drinking it, growing finely ridged, holding a perpetual sheen that never chills. The down would come slowly, a fine filament along my shoulders and arms, wicking condensation, hosting the microscopic life that turns airborne salt into nourishment. My coloring would deepen, not by choice but by exposure: bark-brown where the sun rarely reaches, moss-green where the mist settles, the faint violet of wet fern along my ribs. I would stop smelling of elsewhere. I would smell of cambium, of sweet rot, of living wood that has never known an axe.

I would learn to eat when the vines are willing. I would learn to return every scrap to the moss-lined hollows. I would learn that waste is only a failure of patience. I would learn to move without displacing more than necessary, to step where the root mat is thickest, to rest where the fauna has already made room. A tree frog would climb my forearm and I would not shift. A bird would pluck a seed from my tunic and I would not startle. I would understand that density is not crowding. It is fullness. And I would begin to belong to it.

I would not become a leader. There are none here. I would become a tender. A listener. A place where vibration gathers and is returned. Some seasons I would guide condensation channels, coaxing moss to thicken along grown depressions, redirecting seepage with woven fiber and weighted stones. Other seasons I would sit in the upper tiers, where the clouds pass through the walkways and the air thins just enough to make breath deeper, listening for the approach of dry spells, tasting the metallic tang before it arrives, marking where growth should slow with ash and patience. I would teach without words, by sitting beside the young ones, by showing how to soak fiber, how to test tension, how to wait for the vine to accept the bend rather than resist it. I would learn that knowledge is not stored. It is grown. And I would let it grow through me.

Years would pass. Decades. I would not notice them as markers. I would notice them as settlements. A walkway that once flexed too much now holds firm. A railing that was once two separate arcs is now a single, springy curve. A patch of moss where an elder was laid down has thickened into a load-bearing mat. I would feel my own movements slowing. Not from weakness, but from alignment. I would spend longer in the same tier. My hands would stain permanently with sap. My spine would curve to match the sweep of the terraces. My breath would sync with the condensation cycles. I would stop distinguishing between my rest and my labor. Both would become attention. Both would become participation.

When the time came, I would not fear it. I would feel it as the place feels it: not as an ending, but as a redirection. I would choose a shallow hollow lined with water-loving moss, near a graft I had tended, near a channel I had guided, near a railing that had learned my weight. I would lie down. I would let the warm damp take me. I would feel the fungi wake, the insects arrive, the leaf litter settle. I would feel my edges soften. My name would dissolve into the mycelial network. My memory would become the slight thickening of a root joint, the particular curve of a vine, the way a terrace settles after rain. I would not be gone. I would be redistributed. I would be the flex under a child’s foot. The moisture in a moss carpet. The resonance in a trunk when a tender presses a palm to it and listens.

Where would I find myself? In the green dimness. In the deafening braid. In the slow, patient conversation between growth and gravity. I would be where the humidity gathers. Where the vibration settles. Where the matrix needs a hand that does not push, a voice that does not command, a presence that does not rush. I would be a node in the nervous system of a place that has stopped becoming and simply is. I would be the patience that holds the seam. The attention that guides the water. The stillness that lets the wood dream itself back together.

I would not be separate. I would be woven. I would be listening. I would be here.
