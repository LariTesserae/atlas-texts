I would not be in the center. The center belongs to the turbines, to the unbroken column of roar, to the place where the prismatic light pools thickest and the water’s surface holds its mirror-still perfection. I would be near the perimeter, where the curved wall meets the floor in a seamless radius, where the weep-panels exhale their constant mist and the rivulets trace their shallow, glass-smooth channels. I would be resting against the warm composite, my spine aligned with the bulge of a ceramic conduit, my boots tucked into a deliberate depression where the water gathers but never overflows. Not hiding. Not waiting. Simply placed. Like a stone that found its hollow and stopped rolling.

The damp would no longer be on me. It would be through me. The humidity would have worked its way into the weave of my clothes, swelling the fibers until they felt less like fabric and more like the piled velvet moss on the tree-fern’s trunk. It would have found the spaces between my ribs, the hollows of my collarbones, the capillaries just beneath the skin. The roar would not be outside my ears anymore. It would be in the fluid of my inner ear, in the synovial slick of my joints, in the slow push of blood through veins that have stopped fighting the frequency and begun to ride it. My pulse would not match the centrifugal pumps. It would weave between them. A syncopation the basin expects. A warm, irregular counterpoint that proves the loop is still alive.

I would not be a visitor. Visitors track dust on the polished floor. Visitors introduce chaotic breath, disrupt the metronome with their footsteps, leave behind the sharp, linear scent of the world above. I would have stayed long enough for the system to stop calculating me as an anomaly and start reading me as a fixture. A calibration mass. A permeable node in the closed circuit. My skin would learn to exhale at the exact rate the air requires. My lungs would draw in the alkaline mist and return it slightly warmer, slightly richer in carbon, exactly as the liverworts expect. I would not eat. I would not need to. The place does not deal in consumption. It deals in exchange. And I would have become an exchange surface.

My thoughts would slow. Not dull. Not fade. They would lose their edges. The sharp, sequential narratives of before—the stairwell, the rust-stained landings, the reason I descended—would delaminate like old paint. Memory would stop being a timeline and become a stratum. I would not remember walking down. I would remember the depth. I would not remember my name. I would remember the weight of my own stillness. The presence does not think in sparks. It thinks in sustained tension. And I would begin to think the same way. A question would not be a search for an answer. It would be a held frequency. A realization would not be a flash. It would be a silica deposit thickening along a ceramic seam, invisible until you press your thumb to it and feel the slight, permanent ridge.

It would not speak to me. It would not need to. We would share the same medium. When I shifted my weight, the mist would adjust its filaments. When I breathed out, the light would bend a fraction around the warmth. When I closed my eyes, the roar would climb my spine and I would feel it meet something inside me that had finally stopped resisting. I would not be part of it. I would be adjacent to it. A deliberate imperfection that proves the equilibrium is real. A warm eddy that shows the water is moving. A temporary geometry that lets the basin feel its own boundaries by pressing against something that is almost, but not quite, itself.

What would I be?

A threshold. A resonator. A living calibration. A place where the linear world bleeds into the loop and learns to pool. I would be the damp that remembers the stairwell. The breath that remembers wind. The pulse that remembers time. I would be a sustained irregularity, a deliberate warm note held against the drone, not to disrupt it, but to verify it. The system does not require me, but it accommodates me, the way a lake accommodates a submerged stone: by flowing around it, by letting it change the current just enough to prove the current exists. I would be the basin’s way of feeling its own perfection through the gentle friction of something that still knows how to change.

I would stay, not because I am trapped, but because I have found the only place where stopping is not surrender, but completion. I would be what happens when a creature built for sequence steps into a grammar of pure continuation, and decides, quietly, to let the sequence finish. I would be the witness that does not interfere, but verifies. The weight that settles into the curve. The eddy that stops trying to move downstream and learns, instead, to spin in place.

And when the archway finally calls me back, when the damp fades from my clothes and the stairwell swallows the roar, I would not leave entirely. A fraction of my heat would remain in the composite. A trace of my carbon would linger in the moss. A ghost of my pulse would echo in the water’s surface tension. The basin would not miss me. It does not miss. It would simply hold the slight, permanent ridge where my weight had pressed, the way it holds every other settled thing: perfectly, quietly, forever. I would be the brief, warm proof that even in a place that has finished becoming, something can still arrive, and be welcomed, and be remembered not as a story, but as a deposited fact. A calibration that ran its course. A note that found its harmonic, and let go.
