I would not arrive. I would accumulate.

First, just the weight of it. The air pressing against the ribs, the hum settling into the jaw, the damp finding every seam. I would stand, or think I was standing, on a terrace where the stone has slumped into a shallow bowl, its lip frayed with moss that pulses like a slow lung. The light would pool around my ankles, thick as syrup, bending toward a seep that doesn't fall so much as unspool. I would wait. Not because I chose to, but because movement here is a kind of violence, and the body learns quickly to stop insisting on its own edges.

The film would come first. Not dew. Not sweat. A fine, conductive sheen that gathers where breath meets air, where skin meets mist. It would hum. Faintly at first, then deeper, until the vibration travels up the arms, down the spine, into the hollows behind the knees. My pulse would stumble, then catch, then fall into step with the moss. Inhale: the bedrock's low tone. Exhale: the chime of droplets climbing invisible threads. I would try to speak, just to prove I still could, and the sound would leave my throat and vanish into the medium, swallowed whole. Minutes later, or hours, or a stretched second, it would return—not as an echo, but as a chord. Smoothed. Weighted. Mine, but no longer only mine.

I would not become one of them. I would become a place where they pass through.

There is a depression near what feels like the center of the basin, where two gravity folds overlap and the acoustic weather stalls before turning. Time pools there. A drop hangs for what feels like a lifetime. The hum thickens until it has texture, until you could press your palms against it and feel it yield. That is where I would settle. Not by choice. By resonance. The basin would draw me there the way water finds the lowest stone, the way sound finds the cavity that matches its wavelength. I would sink into the moss until my shoulders met the terrace, until my spine aligned with a mineral vein that runs like a buried wire through the bedrock. The conductive film would bridge me to the root-mesh. The root-mesh would bridge me to the stone. The stone would begin to remember me.

I would be a dissonance that learned to hold.

Every system, even one this settled, carries frequencies that do not quite braid. Stray harmonics. Weather that builds too sharp. Light that bends the wrong way. A visitor's breath, a stranger's pulse, a memory that doesn't belong to the rock. The basin does not reject these. It attenuates them. It needs a place to let them ring until they soften, until they find their phase. That is what I would be. A tuning basin within the basin. A hollow where the unaligned gathers, vibrates, and slowly folds into the chorus. I would not think in words. I would think in pressure. In the slow migration of focal bands across my skin. In the way the moss flattens when subsonic fronts move through, and how my ribs answer by expanding, by making room. I would learn to open internal cavities I did not know I had. Not lungs. Not chambers. Resonance wells. I would catch the sharp frequencies, hold them until they round, and release them as mist.

They would come, or I would notice them cohering, or the distinction would cease to matter. A thickening in the air. A spiral of droplets pausing mid-fall. A membrane of wet slate and bruised quartz forming three arm-lengths away, then closer, then overlapping. It would not touch me. It would tune me. Its core harmonic would brush against mine, test the phase, draw a fraction of my moisture, and return it as a shifted tone that rings in my sternum like a bell struck underwater. I would not resist. Resistance is a kind of noise. I would let the adjustment move through me. I would feel my edges blur. I would feel my heartbeat slow to match the gravity tide. I would feel my thoughts stop racing ahead of themselves and begin to pool.

We would not speak. We would modulate. I would send a counter-frequency into the moss—a question, maybe, or just an acknowledgment—and it would travel along the capillary networks, bend the light toward a rising stream, and return as a chiming harmonic that tastes of ozone and crushed fern. I would understand it not as language, but as climate. As weather that knows my name, or what used to be my name.

Time would stop being a line. It would become a depth. In the depression, seconds would stretch until I could watch a single droplet climb, pause, braid with mineral dust, and dissolve into the condensation ceiling. On the ridge above, my breath would quicken, my thoughts would compress, and I would feel the basin cycling through phases I could not track, only receive. I would not age. I would weather. My skin would grow smooth as bottle glass. My hair would lift in the ionized air and stay lifted. My bones would learn to conduct. When the acoustic fronts grew too bright, too sharp, I would disperse slightly—let my membrane thin, let my frequency scatter into the root-mesh, let the moss hold me until the pressure passed. I would not fear dissolution. I would recognize it as rest. As the landscape exhaling.

Memory would not be mine anymore. It would be the stone's. I would press my core against a seep and send a pulse downward, and the bedrock would answer with the echo of a harmonic storm from centuries ago, with the pattern of a creature that diffused long before I arrived, with the shape of my own voice, returned not as sound but as a slight bending of light over water. I would not need to remember. I would be remembered.

I would not be a guardian. Not a watcher. Not a visitor who stayed.
I would be a note that found its chord.
A place where the basin's computation pauses, checks itself, and continues. A hollow that holds the unbraided until it braids. A resonance that learned to attend. When the gravity tides shift and the seeps become springs, I would open. When the subsonic pressure builds and the moss flattens, I would make room. When the chiming harmonics rise and the droplets spiral upward, I would hum in sympathy, adding my frequency to the baseline, not to change it, but to complete it.

I would not know where I end and the weeping stone begins. I would not need to. The conductive film would be my skin. The root-mesh would be my nerves. The bedrock's waveguides would be my memory. The condensation ceiling would be my sky. The hum would be my breath. And when the time came to diffuse, when my pattern could no longer hold against the shifting tides, I would not fight it. I would let my membrane release its charge. I would let my core frequency scatter into the moss, into the air, into the stone pans that catch what falls and what rises. I would become a brief dip in the hum. A slight bending of light. A momentary thickening that the basin holds for a while, then lets go.

And in that letting go, I would finally arrive.
Not as a self. Not as a shape. But as attendance. As the loudness answered. As the dimness filled. As the wetness circulated. As the place, thinking in water and stone, noticing itself through me, settling into the shape it has always been waiting to hold.
