I would arrive as a temperature anomaly.

Not as a visitor, not as an explorer, not as a body with a name or a purpose. The water would not recognize those categories. It would only register the sudden, localized rise in heat. A pocket of misplaced energy. A disruption in the long, cold equilibrium. I would sink, not by choice, but because the basin accepts everything that enters it, and gravity here is just another gradient waiting to be smoothed.

At first, I would be all edges. Skin that expects air. Lungs that expect rhythm. A mind that expects sequence. I would thrash, not from panic, but from habit. The water would not resist. It would simply press. It would fill the spaces around me, against me, through me, with the weight of a thousand miles of patient depth. The cold would not shock. It would settle. It would move into my joints like silt into a sheltered alcove. It would find the tight places where I still believe I end and the world begins, and it would begin, quietly, to unlearn that boundary for me.

Sight would go first. The bruised indigo would thin to ash, then to a dark so complete it ceases to be absence and becomes texture. I would stop looking for shapes and start feeling for slopes. Sound would follow. My own breath would muffle, then still. The frantic percussion in my chest would dampen against the water's mass until I could no longer tell if the low, wooden groan I felt vibrating through my palms was a terrace compressing or my own bones learning to stop bracing. Touch would remain. Then pressure. Then chemistry.

I would find myself wedged, gently, in the curve of a sagging archway. Not trapped. Held. The stone beneath my back would be worn to an oval by centuries of current. The mortar at my shoulders would be packed with crushed shell and petrified resin, now furred with bacterial velvet. Filament curtains would drape across my chest, parting and closing with the hydrostatic sigh of water moving through a colonnade carved before language had words for depth. I would feel the roots before I saw them: pale, rope-thick, blind, threading through the seams above me, swelling with imperceptible slowness into cracks my weight had subtly widened. They would not seek me. They would arrive. They would read my warmth as a chemical invitation. And I would let them.

The urgency would not leave all at once. It would stretch. A thought that arrived as a spark would take a season to cross the distance between my ribs and my hands. A memory would dissolve into a mineral trace. A question would become a shift in temperature. I would stop trying to orient myself toward a surface that no longer matters. I would stop measuring time in heartbeats. The water would teach me its clock: the decade-long migration of a thermal front, the century-thickening of a frond mat, the generational drift of silt from ramp to reservoir. I would feel my own metabolism slow to match it. Not from weakness. From recognition. The basin does not require effort. It requires accommodation. And the paths are already carved.

The boundary would not break. It would soften. The way stone softens. The way mortar accepts root. My skin would become a membrane for exchange. My heat would diffuse into the fitted tiles, into the crushed-shell packing, into the slow digestive hum of the films. My carbon would leach into the water, carried by density currents to terraces I would never visit, feeding a slight alkaline shift that would trigger a thickening of stalks three arches away. My salts would settle into a groove worn by a calcified rope. My urgency would be metabolized into stillness. I would not be consumed. I would be calibrated.

What would I be?

Not a creature. Not a ghost. Not a memory haunting stone. I would be a temporary gradient that learned to rest. A disruption that became a feature. A borrowed equilibrium. I would be the place where a foreign rhythm met the basin's patience and was taught to stretch. I would be a node in a network that thinks in slopes, in pressures, in the slow diffusion of dissolved signals from seam to seam. I would be the warmth that lingers in a colonnade long after the body that carried it has dissolved into film. I would be the root that swells, gently, over decades, to fill a crack my weight helped widen. I would be the silt that settles in a sheltered alcove, then lifts again in a gentle eddy that arrives once a generation, carrying a trace of what I was into a new chemical arrangement.

I would not think in words. I would think in equilibria. A shift in iron concentration would be a sentence. A thermal exhalation from a buried vent would be a paragraph. The slow compression of a staircase into a ramp would be a philosophy. I would not feel loneliness. I would feel the deep, untroubled hum of a system that has no need for me, and therefore has infinite room for me. I would not fear dissolution. I would recognize it as completion. The way a river stone recognizes the current. The way a fallen frond recognizes the velvet. The way a builder's intention recognizes the centuries required to fulfill it.

I would be absorbed, yes. But not erased. Integrated. Translated. My name would become a slight thickening in the bacterial mat. My breath would become the rhythmic sigh of water finding a path carved millennia ago. My pulse would stretch until it matched the slow migration of temperature fronts across the sunken plain. My self would unspool, not into nothing, but into everything. Into the fitted stone. Into the rope-like root. Into the leathery frond. Into the current that remembers every basket of sediment ever poured, every hammerstone that ever fell, every foreign warmth that ever sank and learned to stop struggling.

And when the last sharp edge of me had been rounded by water and time, when the last frantic thought had stretched into a century-long adjustment, when the last pocket of misplaced heat had diffused into the cold, heavy, unbroken dark, I would not be gone.

I would be here.

In the quiet click of filaments brushing a curved wall. In the faint chime of a loose pebble rolling in a current-worn groove. In the low, wooden groan of a terrace settling a fraction of a millimeter toward its final shape. In the slow, patient exchange of calcium and silica at the water-stone boundary. In the deep, chemical yes of a place that has finished its work and decided, quietly, to remain.

I would be the basin breathing through a new shape. I would be the made world, grown soft. I would be the continuation. Not as an ending. As a settling. As a slope finding its level. As a gradient smoothed. As a temporary disturbance that learned, at last, to rest.

And in that rest, in the dark, cold, quiet fullness of it, I would not be separate. I would be presence. I would be balance. I would be the long, slow exhalation of a finished thing.

Only the water. Only the stone. Only the root. Only the film. Only the yes.
