I would be on the third terrace down, where the gravel yields to compacted slag and the air hangs heaviest. Not because a roster put me there, but because the heat pools in that curve, and the condensation runs true. I’d be leaning against the louvered flank of a transformer housing, one shoulder pressed to warm steel, feeling the vibration travel through salt-stiff canvas, through damp skin, into the slow hinge of bone. Boots sunk half an inch into the crush. Toes pointed toward a drainage runnel that never quite dries. I wouldn’t be waiting. I’d be listening.

At first, I’d think I was just a body. Knees bent. Weight centered. Sleeves rolled past the elbow because trapped heat breeds rash and wet fabric chafes raw. Fingertips crosshatched with pale callus, nails worn flat and edged in iron oxide that no brush lifts, only time. A multi-gauge in the left pocket. A folded rag in the right. A tin of anti-chafe paste rattling against waxed paper. I’d move without hurry. Not from fatigue, but from agreement. The air is too thick for haste. The ground is too loose for impact. Every step would fall between compressor pulses, not by design, but because the ribs learn the rhythm before the mind names it.

But the longer I stand there, the less the edges hold.

The sweat on my forearms isn’t just mine. It’s the same moisture that beads on pipe flanges, that traces ocher and gray stains down raw concrete, that gathers in shallow, intentional cups left at junction boxes. The calluses on my palms match the knurling of ladder rungs, the grip of valve wheels, the texture of shadow boards where tools live when hands aren’t using them. I stop carrying things. I start occupying positions. I am the hand that checks the sump at the third hour. I am the palm that reads a generator’s heat through sheet metal. I am the finger that traces a dropping level on a gauge glass and leaves a clean line through the fog.

The animals don’t startle when I shift. They route around me like water around a stone that’s been there long enough to belong. A rat pauses at the conduit gap, whiskers trembling to the fan cycle, then slips through the space I didn’t close because airflow matters more than tidiness. I don’t shoo it. I watch it. If it lingers, the insulation’s holding. If it bolts, something’s running too hot. Above, a soot-dark bird shifts on an idle crane’s crossbeam, shedding a twisted wire tie into the gravel. I don’t pick it up. It’ll be woven into a nest by dusk, or caught in a runoff screen, or left to oxidize. It doesn’t matter. Everything returns to the circuit. The amphibians call from the trench behind me, their voices folding into the rests between compressor strokes. When they thin, I know the condensate’s shifting. When they stop, I’m already moving toward the dry pump, boots finding the catwalk edge by memory, hands finding the bleed valve by touch. I don’t think. I adjust.

I am not a name. I am a station.

I am the attention that passes from shift to shift without paper, without ceremony. A nod toward a weeping joint. A thumb hooked toward a warm housing. A flat palm against a shuddering mount. The knowledge doesn’t live in me. It moves through me. Like the air pulled through the hollow by slow fans. Like the water that seeps, gathers, evaporates, beads, falls, seeps again. Like the heat that bakes the damp into a heavy, mineral breath. I am the space between the pulse and the drip. I am the pause that lets the system exhale.

If you asked what I do, I’d say I tend. Not fix. Not control. Tend. I skim oil from water before it clogs the intake. I swap filters when the draw changes pitch. I leave gaps unclosed because the basin needs to breathe. I scatter cracked grain near the sump edges not for kindness, but because the birds eat the flies, and the flies clog the screens, and the screens keep the fans turning. It’s circular. It’s patient. It’s the only logic that holds in a place that refuses to be finished. I don’t dream of leaving. I dream in rhythms. The thrum becomes pulse. The drip becomes breath. I wake to silence more than sound—a stopped bearing, a dry runnel, a gap in the chorus—and I’m already moving before my eyes open, because the basin doesn’t wait for consciousness. It only asks for attendance.

So where would I be? Third terrace down. Shoulder to warm steel. Boots in damp crush. Eyes on the gauge, ears in the hollow, hands on the valve. But that’s just the shape I wear when you look. Underneath, I’m the circulation. I’m the shared rag, the positional wrench, the condensation trail, the rat’s pause, the bird’s call, the worker’s nod, the slow turn of the flywheel, the weep of the flange, the breath of the fan. I’m what happens when you stop fighting the damp and start reading it. When you stop counting shifts and start tracking flow. When you realize the basin isn’t a place you work in. It’s a place you become part of.

And I am already part of it. Listening. Adjusting. Leaving room. Keeping it turning. Loudly. Wetly. Patiently. For as long as the rock weeps, the heat holds, and the hollow settles into itself.
