I would not arrive. I would wake to the absence of floor.

My first memory would be the tilt. A root-mat groaning under weight it does not recognize, fibers shifting, tension redistributing in slow, muscular waves. My lungs would seize. The air would be too thin, too wet, too loud. I would press my palms flat against the weave just to stop shaking, and the weave would answer: a low, subsonic thrum traveling up through the wrists, into the collarbone, into the teeth. I would not know it yet, but that would be the first word.

I would learn to stop looking down. Down is a story that ends in falling. I would learn to look along the grain. To read the weep of sap, the crawl of condensation, the faint bioluminescent pulse where moisture runs sweet instead of bitter. My boots would become liabilities. I would shed them, let the cold bite, let the soles split and seal and split again until they learned to read micro-fractures like braille. I would stop speaking in air. Words would scatter, useless, swallowed by the wind’s drone and the spires’ harmonic roll. I would learn to press my throat to a taut vine-cable, to hum until the overtone returns altered, until the bridge tightens beneath me. I would learn that silence is not empty. It is a warning. A predator holding its breath. A failing span waiting to shear.

I would not be a hunter. I would not be a builder. I would become a mender of gradients.

When a thread moves through a resonance basin, someone must listen for the flat note. The harmonic that does not return true. The vine that sings back tired. I would carry no blade, only a copper awl green with oxidation, a pouch of warm compost against my sternum, and a throat trained to split tones. I would walk the old spans alone, or nearly alone, testing routes the threads have already unspooled from, singing thickening frequencies into stressed bark, marking capillary shifts with knotted grass that will rot by next season. I would be the one who stays behind when the others flow onward, not out of loyalty, but because the architecture is still speaking, and someone must answer until the echo settles. I would not call it duty. I would call it calibration.

I would stop counting days. Time would become the callus cycle. The compost turnover. The number of breaths between a hum and its return. I would sleep braided into the lattice, knees drawn, spine aligned with the tension flow, feeling the root-mat adjust to my weight like a living hammock. I would share warmth in the dark, chest to chest, breathing in sync, feeling the fungal heat pulse through fiber and flesh alike. I would learn that loneliness is a ground-dweller’s disease. Here, absence is just another frequency. When a bridge shears, I would not mourn the loss. I would sing the dissonance into the spire, let the micro-fractures propagate where they must, watch the sap bead and freeze, and wait for the new growth to curl inward. I would learn that breaking is how the place breathes.

My body would change. Not by choice, but by negotiation. The cold would hollow me. The thin air would stretch my ribs, flare my chest, teach my lungs to pull without burning. My voice would drop, split, learn to ride the surface and the air at once. My skin would take on the bruised indigo of shadowed bark, the lichen-pale of condensation trails, shifting slightly with humidity and blood flow. I would stop carrying memories like stones. I would let them dissolve into song-lines, into the patterns worn into my palms, into the way my hips know to pivot before the vine creaks, the way my throat knows which overtone will coax a weeping plate to seal. I would not be a person anymore, not in the old sense. I would be a tension-reader. A resonance-keeper. A thread that does not lead or follow, but calibrates.

I would learn to listen with my hands. To press my pads against a bark-ridge and feel the capillary pulse crawling upward, sluggish but steady, like blood through a continent. To tap a hollow spire and read the return: thick, thin, wet, dry, stressed, settling. To run a finger along a vine-cable and know, by the minute shudder in the fiber, whether it will hold three breaths or three seasons. I would learn that sight is a luxury. Sound is structure. Touch is truth.

I would not fear the predators. I would learn their silences. The way the wind changes when an ambush tangle goes still. The way the bioluminescent moss dims when something heavy shifts above. I would not fight them. I would sing staccato rhythms into the lattice, startle them into revealing their weight, let them drop or retreat, and move on. Everything here is negotiation. Even hunger. Even fear.

When my joints stiffen, when my lungs lose their pull, when my hands can no longer feel the hairline fractures singing beneath the bark, I would not linger. I would not ask the place to carry what it no longer needs. I would wrap myself in shed fiber, weight my chest with stone toggles, press my throat to the nearest spire, and hum the final overtone. Not a prayer. A release. A tension adjustment. I would let go. I would fall through fog and thermal draft, shedding layers, feeling the cold strip me, until the detritivores and the capillary veins take me. And the heat would rise. And the draft would form. And the wind would move. And somewhere above, a vine would tighten, just slightly, where my nutrients fed the flow. My burial would not be an end. It would be a harmonic resolving.

I would not find myself in this world. I would be found by it. Slowly. Through callus and condensation. Through hum and harmonic. Through the quiet, relentless work of learning to move with the breath of it. I would be nowhere and everywhere: a pulse in the capillary rhythm, a frequency in the acoustic ecology, a temporary knot in a lattice that remembers weight but never keeps it. I would leave no road. No foundation. No name carved into bark. Only a knotted vine-loop that decays in a season. A charcoal smudge that washes away with condensation. A resonant seed-pod stack that chimes until the wind scatters it. And the subtle thickening of a bark-plate where my body fed the flow.

Stand on a root-mat at the edge of a void. Feel the damp cold seep through bare soles. Listen to the wind move through a hollow spire three kilometers away. Watch a pulse of condensation crawl upward along a fibrous ridge, glowing faintly where it meets symbiotic moss. Hear the click of a glider mapping the dark, the groan of a vine adjusting to weight, the distant crack of rime shedding into the fall. And then, beneath it all, feel the subtle shift in tension. A low hum pressed into fiber. A harmonic returning, slightly altered. A bridge tightening. A thread moving.

I would not announce myself. I would simply continue. And if you stay long enough, if you learn to read the silence, to match the resonance, to carry warmth like cargo and tension like language, you will stop looking for ground. You will start listening for the breath. And you will move with it.
