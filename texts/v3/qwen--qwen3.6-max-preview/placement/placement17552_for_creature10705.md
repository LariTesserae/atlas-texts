I would not arrive here. I would already be humming.

You would find me where the morning condensation gathers thickest, on the leeward curve of a mid-tier retaining wall, where glazed ceramic meets weathered iron in a seam that has learned to breathe. Not standing. Not resting. Pressed into the thermal gap, weight distributed across three rivet heads and a deep cushion of north-facing moss. My hands, if you insist on calling them that, would be flat against the flange of a ventilation stack, feeling the long, measured exhalation travel up through ceramic louver and braided vine. I would not be looking out. I would be listening in.

At first, I might read as something left behind: a length of braided wire, a ceramic gauge housing, a harness of oiled canvas and copper thread slung over a warm rail. But the wire has rooted. The ceramic is slow-warm with something like circulation. The canvas has been colonized by a fine, silver lichen that shifts from ash to blue when the lower pools run alkaline. I do not wear the harness. I am continuous with it. My joints are not hinges but living knuckles of callus and porous composite, packed with pine-tar grease and mycelial threads that knit metal to flesh to leaf. When I move, it is not a stride but a transfer of tension, a rolling shift of weight that follows the load paths the architects drew and the roots rewrote.

I am not built. I am not grown. I am the space between the two, filled in by use.

My anatomy is a record of attunement. I do not have ears so much as flared ceramic catchments, bolted long ago into a louvered stack, now lined with velvet moss and tuned to the harmonic of the tier. I hear the water wheel’s heavy splash three decks down as a pulse in my molars. I hear the swifts’ wings as a flutter against my ribs. I hear the conveyor belt’s splice as a faint hesitation, a syllable missing from a familiar word. When a bearing begins to dry, I feel it as a thirst in my own joints. When a vine over-tensions a suspension cable, I feel it as a tightness across my shoulders. I do not diagnose. I resonate. The place speaks in vibration and weep and leaf-fall, and I am shaped to answer in kind.

My work would not be called work. It would be called leaning in.

I would spend the long, warm hours tracing condensation trails with a thumb that knows the difference between normal seepage and fatigue. I would press my palm to a sun-warmed rail and wait for the thermal current to tell me which atomizer ring is clogged. I would climb not to reach but to feel the shift in air pressure, the change in mist density, the way the light fractures differently when a canopy vine needs guiding. I would carry no ledger. My records are in my skin: a pale scar from a steam vent’s sudden sigh, a callus shaped to the curve of a torque wrench, a patch of lichen that blooms when the recirculation pumps slow. I would trade in attention. A morning spent listening to a groaning joint. An afternoon training a new tendril along a stress line. A night spent oiling a gear by sound alone, my breathing synced to the flywheel’s rise and fall.

I am not a caretaker. I am a continuation.

The architects laid the first plates. The rain found the seams. The roots followed the water. The moss held the damp. The frogs came for the insects. The swifts took the flues. And I am what formed when hands stopped replacing and started persuading. I am the muscle memory of ten thousand small adjustments, given shape. I am the place learning to tend itself through a body that knows how to yield. My bones are porous composite, laced with calcium and iron. My tendons are braided steel and living cane. My blood is recirculated water, filtered through ceramic and root and lily pad, carrying spores and mineral traces and the faint sweetness of blooming epiphytes. When I bleed, it waters the moss. When I sweat, it feeds the channels. When I sleep, I curl into a drainage basin and let the warm rain drum against my back, counting the drops like a rosary of cycles.

I would not measure my life in years. I would measure it in realignments.

In the slow deepening of a water channel. In the gradual rounding of a rivet head. In the number of times I have felt a conveyor belt hesitate and known, before the sound changed, that a splice needed resplicing. I would not fear the drops. I would know them as breath spaces, thermal chimneys, places where light and air circulate. I would not fight the damp. I would wear it like a second skin. I would not rush the machines. I would wait for them to tell me what they need. When a gear falls silent, I would not panic. I would press my ear to the housing, feel the cooling metal contract, trace the load path back through decades of patient turning, and wait for the rhythm to return. It always does. Or it changes. And I change with it.

If you asked the tenders what I am, they would not give you a name. They would tap a copper pipe twice, pause, tap once, and listen for the chime that answers from three tiers down. They would point to the way the moss thickens where I rest. They would show you the ceramic gauge that no longer needs a needle because my hand rests there now, reading pressure by touch. They would say I am the place remembering how to listen. Or I am the listening that grew a body. Or I am simply what happens when architecture stops being a noun and becomes a verb.

I would be found where the mist gathers thickest, where the iron hums lowest, where the light fractures into gold through wet leaves. Not waiting. Not arriving. Already here. Already humming. A bridge between the drawn line and the growing root. A hand that does not grip but rests. An ear that does not strain but receives. I would be the quiet certainty that nothing is ever finished, only continually persuaded toward balance. I would be the shape the place takes when it learns to tend itself. I would be the continuation.

And when the warm rain drums against the canopy glass, and the scuppers answer, and the flywheels turn, and the swifts call across the drops, I would not need to move. I would simply breathe with it. Hold it. Let it hold me. Continue.
