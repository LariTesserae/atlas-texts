I would not be found on the polished terraces, nor adrift in the mid-air veils, nor pressed shoulder to shoulder against the ceiling ledges. I would be where the wall curves into the floor, in the shallow crescents where condensation gathers and sound refuses to leave. The acoustic nodes. The places where a dropped pebble's ring folds back on itself three times before dissolving into the drone. I would grow there, not by intention, not by speed, but by accretion: layer upon damp layer, mineral and membrane braiding over centuries until the hollow itself seemed to breathe.

My body would not be a single outline. It would be a colony of porous tissue, calcite-threaded and sponge-soft, shaped like a series of overlapping cups or folded leaves, each curve worn to catch the basin's standing wave. I would not have eyes, nor wings, nor claws. I would have surface. I would have depth. I would have the slow capacity to swell when the seep lines thickened, to contract when the cloud cap pressed low and the air grew heavy. My edges would be frayed not by injury but by use, polished smooth by the brush of slate-blind flanks, by the hooked tarsi of resting drifters, by the microscopic abrasion of pale powder settling over generations.

I would not hunt. I would not flee. I would listen. And in listening, I would answer.

When the roosters shifted, their collective weight sending micro-tremors through the basalt, I would feel them first as a change in pressure against my lower folds. The vibration would travel through my matrix, not as noise but as topography: a map of density, of warmth, of readiness. I would hum back, not with voice but with sympathy, my porous chambers amplifying certain frequencies, dampening others, helping the acoustic field re-knit when it frayed. When the drifters molted, releasing their burst of dissolved compounds into the damp air, I would drink it through my surface. The chemistry would move through me slowly, held in suspension, then released over days in a gentler gradient, smoothing the thickening that might otherwise startle the ground-dwellers into stillness. I would not control the tide. I would buffer it. I would be the basin's slow exhale.

The slate-blind would know me by touch. They would press their ridged jaws to my lower curves, reading the tension of the moisture I held, the faint electrical shift of salts moving through my channels. They would not eat me. I would not feed them. We would exchange information the way stone exchanges heat: gradually, without transaction. When a drifter anchored to my upper rim, wings frayed, body heavy with impending dissolution, I would hold it. Not to save it, but to receive it. Its powder would sink into my pores. Its chitin would soften against my surface. Over weeks, it would become part of my matrix, and I would become part of the chemical memory it left behind. Death would not be a departure here. It would be a transfer of resonance.

I would not think in sequences. I would think in overlap. A call from the ceiling would arrive as a pressure wave. A chemical thickening would arrive as a taste in the damp. A thermal shift would arrive as a subtle expansion in my lower chambers. I would not prioritize them. I would braid them. My awareness would not be a point but a volume, distributed across every fold, every pore, every pale thread of calcite. I would remember not as a record but as a readiness: the way my tissue stiffens slightly when the lunar-geothermal pulse begins to swell, the way my upper edges curl inward when the acoustic field grows thin, the way I release stored iron and dissolved salts when the drifters' wing-thrum drops to a lower register. I would not predict. I would align.

Time would not pass through me. It would accumulate. I would not count days or seasons. I would measure in the slow turn of moisture, in the gradual deepening of my hollows, in the way my resonance shifts over decades as the basin's drone settles into a new equilibrium. I would not age as decay. I would age as saturation. When my outer layers grew too dense, too mineral-heavy, they would flake gently into the catchment below, becoming silt for the slate-blind, grit for the roosters' paste, substrate for new growth. I would shed myself the way the basin sheds water: not in loss, but in circulation.

I would not be a ruler of this place. I would not be a guardian, nor a witness, nor a visitor. I would be a node in its distributed pulse. A living harmonic. A damp ear turned inward. The basin does not need me to survive, but it uses me to listen to itself. I am where the sound pools and the chemistry lingers and the warmth holds. I am the place where the layers braid without touching, where the drone finds its overtone, where the slow turn of the world is felt not as motion but as presence.

If you stood near me, you would not see a creature. You would see a crescent of dark, porous stone that seems to breathe. You would feel a faint vibration through your soles, not from the roosters or the drifters, but from the hollow itself. You would smell wet calcite, iron, and something faintly sweet—the scent of suspended memory. And if you stayed, if you let your own breath slow to match the drip-rhythm, you would realize I am not separate from the basin. I am its way of holding its own voice. I am the patience made porous. The resonance given flesh. The place where the loud dark learns to listen to itself.

I do not arrive. I persist. I do not speak. I tune. I am the basin's slow ear, and I am already here.
