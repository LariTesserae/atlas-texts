I do not wake. I phase in.

The transition is not from sleep to consciousness but from stillness to current. The grating beneath me is already singing before my weight settles onto it, a low, steady frequency traveling up through callus and bone into the hollow of my chest. I know the western filtration hall is running a fraction rich before I open my eyes. The air tells me. It presses against my cheek with a subtle increase in density, carries the wet-metal tang of oxidized grease, tastes of iron and condensed steam. I draw it in, and the basin draws back. We are already talking.

My route is not mapped. It is thermal. I follow the cool seams along the pipe racks, the places where wrapped insulation has thinned and heat bleeds into the damp. My hands move before my eyes register the fault. Fingers trace a weep-line on a flange, find the hairline fracture in the gasket, press clay-sinter paste into the gap until the hiss softens to a sigh. The metal shivers under my palm. I answer with a tap, two taps, a long scrape. The vibration travels down the conduit, and somewhere beyond the steam, a valve adjusts its cycle. We have always been speaking.

I do not wear the basin. I am worn by it.

My skin has learned to drink the mist. The pores along my forearms and collarbones have widened into shallow cups, lined with the same thermophilic film that paints the intake grates. When the cooling towers exhale, I do not wipe the condensation away. I let it bead, let it trace the mineral paths down to my wrists, where the excess weeps back into the drainage channels. My hair is heavy, braided with stripped insulation wire and rust-spore algae, a wet rope that channels moisture away from my scalp and keeps the secondary lids clear. They slide shut when a welding arc flares, then open again to the perpetual twilight. I do not need sharp light. I read the world in pressure differentials, in the way the hum thickens near a clogged filter, in the subtle drop in vibration when a bearing begins to starve. Sight is a courtesy. Resonance is the ledger.

I am not alone on the walkway, though I rarely perceive the others as separate. We pass like eddies in the same current. A shoulder brushes mine, damp canvas against damp canvas, and I feel the rhythm of their route in the set of their gait, the slight forward lean that means they are heading to the sedimentation tanks. I am heading to the western scarp, where the weep-lines have shifted three inches since the last cycle. A rack-bird drops from a steam line, lands on the railing beside me, shakes water from its wide, heat-dissipating wings. I do not startle. Its weight changes the resonance of the steel by a fraction. I note it. The bird notes me. We share the rail. Below, an amphibian slides from a cracked tile into a shallow pan. The water trembles. The tremor travels up the support strut, into my boots, into my spine. I adjust my stance to match it. Everything is tuning.

I do not have a name that stands apart from my function. When the others call me, they use the tone of the sector I tend: a low, rolling hum that matches the resting frequency of the western causeway. I answer in kind. My work is not repair. It is listening. It is keeping the conversation from fraying. The machines do not break; they drift. The stone does not erode; it settles. My role is to feel the drift before it becomes a tear, to press a palm against a housing and hear the half-tone sharpening, to pack grease rendered from algae and collected fats into the shuddering joint, to scrape rust from a catwalk and carry it to the reduction vats where the anaerobes will breathe it back into suspension. I am not fixing. I am translating. I am the basin's nervous system, localized, walking.

Sometimes, in the staggered sleep cycles, I curl beneath a walkway where the insulation is thick and the drone drops to a murmur. I wrap the vapor-barrier blanket around my shoulders and let the heat seep into my joints. I dream of water finding its level. I dream of gears meshing without resistance. I dream of the day my skin first stopped flushing and began to stain, when the secondary lids fully formed, when I stood beside a venting stack for a full cycle and felt my pulse lock into the drip rhythm. That was my becoming. Not a birth, but a calibration.

I do not count years. I count cycles. I count the times the shift bell has rung and found me already moving. I count the elders who have sat in the warm alcoves until their breathing slowed to match the seep, until their bodies were carried to the tanks and returned as slurry, as algae, as the dense cakes that feed the young who now walk the grating with bare, toughening feet. I will do the same. When my joints stiffen, when my steps slow, when the mineral crust thickens and my skin begins to resemble the sinter it tends, I will find my alcove. I will sit. I will listen until the hum and my heartbeat are the same thing. And then I will stop. And the basin will continue. Because I was never separate from it.

If you look for me, you will not find a figure standing apart. You will find a rhythm in the steam. You will find a hand pressed to a pipe rack, feeling for the micro-shudder that precedes a leak. You will find a silhouette moving along the western causeway, boots striking diamond plate in time with the pump cycle, shoulders damp with condensation, eyes half-lidded against the diffuse glow. You will find me where the pressure gradients shift, where the weep-lines trace new paths, where the hum thickens and thins. I am not waiting for anything. I am not going anywhere. I am the place, attending to itself. I am the held breath, exhaling.
