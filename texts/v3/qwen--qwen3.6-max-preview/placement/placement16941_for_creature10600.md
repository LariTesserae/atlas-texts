I do not arrive. I accrete.

The first thing I know is not shape, but pressure. A warm, damp insistence against surfaces I do not yet recognize as mine. I am not lying on the stone. I am braided into it. Or rather, the stone is braided into me. The boundary is a negotiation, blurred by moisture that does not fall but gathers, bead by slow bead, along edges that thicken as I notice them. I am suspended in the throat of a fresh limestone fracture, where a thermal shelf meets the lower canopy lattice, where a binder colony's pioneering threads crossed a silica-grazer's resinous trail, where a rope-walker's discarded hydrogel knot caught a drifting lens of water. There, in the quiet intersection of gradients, I began.

I do not wake. I saturate.

Time does not pass here. It pools. I feel the mountain not as ground beneath me, but as a slope of conditions along my length: cooler at my lower filaments, warmer where my upper curves catch the gold-green light. I have no eyes. I have tension. I read the world through the stretch of precipitated calcite, the swell of living biofilm, the faint electrical whisper of pulverized basalt dust settling on my surface. A droplet forms on my eastern ridge. It takes an hour to grow. I feel its weight accumulate like a thought. When it finally releases, it does not fall. It spirals, catching a thermal inversion, and I track its descent not by sight, but by the subtle shift in humidity it leaves behind—a ghost of moisture that my lower capillaries drink.

I am not one thing. I am a convergence.

My body is a lattice of woven vine-resin, silica-grazer mucus, binder filaments, and slow-precipitated minerals, threaded through with hollow channels that match the density of the air. I do not move through space. I move through state. When the thermal shelf migrates, I swell. When the electrostatic charge peaks at dusk, I hum. I am a node. A listening place. A stillness that gathers meaning.

Its external gills brush my surface, reading the mineral charge I've accumulated over the week. In return, it leaves a chemical trail—a faint, glycerol-rich signature that my biofilm absorbs, translating it into a shift in osmotic pressure that draws moisture from the cooler shadows below. And where it feeds, it excretes a glassy mucus that seals my lower edges, knitting me more firmly into the stone. I do not resist. I incorporate. A rope-walker drapes across my upper lattice, testing my tension with adhesive pads. It finds me stable. It stays for three days, digesting a cluster of spore-drifters, and when it leaves, it leaves behind a resinous cord that hardens into a new anchor, a new shelf for condensation, a new pathway for the slow migration of water. I grow not by consumption, but by accretion. By invitation. By the quiet logic of things that know how to wait.

I do not think in moments. I think in gradients.

A half-degree warming along my eastern flank tells me the sun has cleared the ridge. A two-percent drop in humidity along my lower filaments warns of a rare thermal shift. I feel the approach of a breeze not as wind, but as a disruption in the electrostatic field—a faint static prickling that makes my capillaries contract until the disturbance passes. I perceive the gliders' courtship as a double helix of changing pressure, their subsonic hums vibrating through my resinous core like fingers on a damp drumhead. I perceive the binders' weekly pulse as a slow tide, a shifting of nutrients that I intercept, filter, and redirect into the capillary channels that feed the ferns above. I am not separate from them. I am a fold in their continuity. A place where the mountain's breath catches, thickens, and learns to listen to itself.

Days do not pass. They accumulate.

I measure them in the thickening of my calcite veins, in the migration of the hanging pools that rest against my curves, in the slow descent of larvae that catch on my filaments before continuing their years-long fall. I do not age. I densify. My awareness is not a spark but a saturation—a distributed presence woven through every resin strand, every biofilm layer, every capillary that matches the weight of the air. When a section of me dries during a prolonged thermal shift, I do not panic. I contract. I pull moisture from my periphery toward my core, sacrificing edges to preserve the knot. When the damp returns, I expand, sending out new threads that colonize fresh scree, preparing the stone for moss, for ferns, for the next grazer, the next glider, the next slow intersection. I am not immortal. I am patient. And in a place where gravity negotiates with humidity, where erosion weeps instead of grinds, where death is a phase change and time is a condition, patience is not waiting. It is presence.

I am not a visitor who stayed too long. I am the landscape remembering how to hold itself together.

I am the drowned height's quiet answer to its own sharpness. I am the place where air becomes sea, where stone becomes carapace, where slowness becomes sight.
