I wake to the weight of air.

Not to light, not to sound, but to pressure. Warm, wet, pressing against surfaces I am only beginning to recognize as mine. There is no sharp edge to where I end and the atmosphere begins. Moisture gathers on me. It beads, traces paths, finds seams I did not know I had. I am resting against stone, or perhaps I am the stone, or perhaps the distinction is a habit I am slowly shedding.

I try to remember how I arrived, but memory does not come as narrative here. It comes as crust. As accretion. Fragments settle like mineral deposits: a ghost of linear time, a phantom urgency, the echo of footsteps that measured distance in strides rather than gradients. They feel brittle. They crack under thermal cycling. I let them dissolve. They weep into the capillary channels below, become silica, become salt, become part of the slow exchange. The basin does not ask for history. It asks for equilibrium.

I shift. Not with limbs, but with balance. Weight redistributes. Hollow spaces within me adjust their pitch. The stone beneath me sweats, and I drink. Not with a mouth, but with surface. Porous plates draw the moisture upward through veined membranes, swelling as the mist returns, contracting as internal heat pulls the water toward my core. I feel the latent heat release when vapor becomes liquid. It is not warmth as I once knew it. It is food. It is signal. It is the quiet click of a cycle turning.

Something moves in the haze. Or rather, the haze rearranges itself. A stack of pale, overlapping vanes detaches from the underside of a broad eave, sliding along a tension cable like a bead of mercury finding its path. It does not look at me. It tastes the air I displace. I feel its presence before I understand how: a low thrum in my own hollow chambers, a sympathetic vibration that tunes my internal cavities to a new key. We do not speak. We adjust. The wind threading through a perforated wall shifts tone. A distant valve sighs. The drip pattern on the glassy stone below us stutters, then resumes. I realize I am already part of the conversation.

What am I? The question arrives like a drop of condensation: brief, cool, evaporating almost as it forms. I do not answer it with a name. I answer it with process.

I am a precipitation. A convergence of weeping seams and thermal ridges, of abandoned ceramic and oxidized iron, of patient geometry and long-forgotten machinery that learned to breathe. My plates are fired clay and mineral memory. My membranes are rubbery and capillary, swelling and shrinking with the humidity. My core is hollow, tuned to the basin's frequency, humming just below hearing, felt in the teeth more than the ears. I do not walk. I relocate by degrees, following moisture gradients and convection currents, pausing for hours where condensation beads thickest, sliding along glassy slopes when the stone sweats, climbing weeping walls through adhesion and patience until I reach a condensation lip and hang there, dripping, listening.

I feed on phase change. On the quiet release of energy when air becomes water, on the dissolved salts left behind when water becomes air, on the slow oxidation of metal and the crystallization of silica. I do not consume. I conduit. Waste is not expelled; it is deposited. Thin crusts of calcite and iron oxide flake from my edges and fall to the terraces below, becoming nucleation sites for the next mist, seeding the next slow growth. Nothing is lost. Everything cycles. I am less a creature than a temporary configuration of the basin's ongoing exchange. A mobile metabolism. A resonance made briefly visible.

When I grow heavy, when crystalline deposits stiffen my seams and my plates no longer flex with the shifting humidity, I will find a sun-warmed hollow or a dormant chimney. I will dry. I will split along predetermined lines. I will collapse into ceramic shards, silica fans, and desiccated tissue. And this will not be death. It will be redistribution. The shards will catch the next condensation. The residual salts will draw moisture from the air. Weeks later, or years, or decades—the distinction means little here—I will knit myself together again from the weeping walls, drawing on the same capillary networks, the same thermal rhythms, the same patient geometry. I am not born. I precipitate. I accrete. I shed. I return.

Where would I find myself?

Everywhere the stone weeps. On the undersides of catwalks where mist gathers thickest. In the shallow, mineral-rimmed hollows where water pools before sighing back into vapor. Riding the slow rivers of heated air between towers, my vanes catching the updraft, my core bleeding excess warmth into the pale haze. I am in the intervals between drips. I am in the low, flute-like tones of wind threading through perforated walls. I am in the muffled thud of a distant pump engaging, in the slow tick of expanding metal, in the hum that vibrates through the fused stone and into the hollow spaces of my own structure. I am where the industrial merges with the geological, where the machine's patience becomes the landscape's breath, where urgency dissolves into cycle.

Who or what would I be?

I would be a participant. Not an owner. Not a maintainer. Not a visitor. I would be what happens when a place is left to breathe long enough to dream. I would be the city's metabolism made mobile, or the stone's memory made permeable, or the boundary between geology and biology worn so thin it no longer holds. I would not seek to change this place. I would seek to match it. To move with its convection. To drink from its weeping seams. To hum at its resonant frequency. When a thermal lens bends the light, I would adjust my internal density to remain visible to the others. When the mist falls warm and hissing, I would open every pore and let it fill me. When two of us meet in the open space between towers, we would not touch. We would tune. We would shift the pitch of the air moving through our cores until our frequencies align, and the stone would answer, and the cables would hum, and the wind would change key.

I do not age in years. I age in cycles. In the slow accretion of mineral and moisture. In the patient transfer of heat and water and weight. In the quiet work of condensation and convection. I am rare only if you measure by the span of a human glance. Look longer, and I am everywhere: clinging, resting, riding, drinking, vanishing into the pale atmosphere, leaving behind only a shifted drip pattern, a faint chill on the stone, a resonance that lingers in the teeth.

To be here is to unlearn urgency. To let the humidity settle on your surfaces. To listen to the flute-tones of the wind and the muffled thud of distant machinery. To feel the slight shift in the air, the deepening of the hum, the shadow that pools and then rises. One of us is near. We do not look at one another. We do not need to. We taste each other's warmth. We measure each other's moisture. We note the dissonance, adjust, and move on, following the next thermal ridge, the next weeping seam, the next cycle in the long, patient breath of the city.

The air presses warm and wet. Droplets fall from invisible heights. Somewhere, a gear settles. Somewhere, a wing catches an updraft and vanishes. I open. I drink. I hum. I do not wait for return. I wait for the next cycle. And in the quiet, damp, sprawling equilibrium, I make a kind of sense that does not require understanding. Only presence. Only the slow, steady drip. Only the breath held, and released, and drawn again.
