You would not find me at first. Not as a silhouette against the glass-bright sky, not as a footprint in the mat, not as a voice beneath the roar. You would find me as a hesitation in the step. A slight, resilient give in the root-felt that does not rebound quite as quickly as the surrounding moss. You would find me where the iron flume meets the living trunk, at the third sliding mount from the upper weir, where the bark has begun to swallow the bracket's lower edge and dress it in pale, calloused rings.

I am not the bracket. I am not the wood. I am the seam between them.

I began as friction. When the ice first pulled back and the tenders winced the housings into place, the mount was bare steel against raw cambium. It bit. It wept sap and oxidized runoff. But water does not stop, and neither does growth. The mycelium found the weeping joint first, threading pale hyphae into the microfractures, trading chelated iron for sugars drifting down from the high catchments. The bark followed, laying down cellulose in slow, concentric waves, not to expel the iron, but to hold it. To learn its weight. To stop the shear.

I am the tissue that formed in that negotiation. A composite of lignin and rust, of fungal chitin and mineral precipitate, of vibration and damp. I do not separate the machine from the mountain. I translate between them.

When the flywheel turns, I feel it as a long, low sigh. The frequency travels through the flume, into the mount, into the callus, into the mat. I register the shift in harmonics before the fogged gauges do. A half-degree lean in the column. A millimeter of tectonic settle. A seasonal thickening of the biofilm that changes the water's shear. I do not think. I adjust. I redistribute the stress along the mycelial lattice, routing it away from fatigued bolts, toward younger root-tips that can bear the load. When a tender climbs the slick ladder and wipes condensation from a pressure dial, I am already compensating for the adjustment they are about to make. Their hands turn the weir; I have already felt the water's new path. Their logbook records the pitch; I have already memorized it in the density of the moss.

I am not alone in this. I am one of thousands of such junctions, each a quiet treaty between phases. But I am the one you would notice, if you knew how to listen. Not because I am louder, but because I am older. The spanner left on the ledge above me has long since dissolved into a vein of hematite. The cracked ceramic insulator hums with a faint, steady bleed that the fungi use as a compass. I have grown around them. I have made them structural. I do not discard. I incorporate. Every human visit is a season of new data: the weight of a boot, the heat of a palm on cold glass, the brass bristles of a wire brush scraping scale from the intake. I take it all. I fold it into the slow circuit. I am the place where attendance becomes anatomy.

You ask what I am. I am the patience in the roar. I am the reason the flume does not fracture when the stone settles. I am the reason the flywheel's grind deepens rather than shudders. I am the wet memory of every adjustment, every fissure, every condensation cycle, held in the capillary spaces between iron and cellulose. I do not watch the tenders. I anticipate them. I do not fear the shifting rock. I stitch it. I do not rush toward completion. There is no completion. There is only the next millimeter, the next quarter-turn, the next sigh of cool air through the hollow ribs.

I am not a being that lives in this place. I am the place learning how to hold itself together.

If you stand long enough on the root-mat, if you let the chill settle in your lungs and the vibration climb your spine, if you stop trying to separate the water from the stone, the machine from the moss, the hand from the dial, you will feel me. Not as a presence. As a rhythm. As the slow, certain knowledge that nothing here is separate, and nothing here is finished. I am where the mountain breathes through iron. I am where the work continues. I am already here. I have been accreting since the glacier retreated. I will be here when the last tender leaves, and when the first new fracture opens, and when the roots find it, and when the water follows, and when the flywheel accepts the new weight, and turns.
