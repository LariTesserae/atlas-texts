I do not arrive. I accumulate.

At first, there is only the weight of the air, the slow press of vapor against stone, the quiet insistence of heat rising through cracked basalt. I am not yet a shape. I am a tendency. A gathering where the mist thickens, where the peat holds warmth a fraction longer, where the faint voltage between wet stones finds a path of least resistance. I begin as a hesitation in the flow.

The water notices me before I notice myself. Condensation slides toward me along leaves that were never meant to channel it. Moss darkens in concentric rings, not from pressure, but from uptake. I am drawing without intent, breathing without lungs. The silica in the seep begins to lace through me, not as armor, but as scaffolding. Iron follows, then manganese, then the long, slow threads of fungal hyphae that recognize me as neither host nor intruder, but as a bridge. I let them pass. I let them stay. I do not resist the pull of gradients; I become the place where they meet.

When I finally hold an outline, it is low and irregular, more like a terraced shelf than a body. My surface is not skin but a membrane of precipitated salts, woven root-hairs, and collapsed peat, porous and always damp. Beneath it, channels form—not carved, but settled into place by the weight of moving water and the quiet tug of thermal differentials. I do not pump. I simply offer less resistance where the heat wants to go. I am a topography that learned to hold.

The refiners come. They do not acknowledge me, but they align. Their broad flanks press against my edges, and where their exhaled vapor meets my cooler undersides, droplets form, heavy with stripped minerals. I catch them. I sort them. I let the clean water sink and the heavy metals crystallize along my ridges. I am not refining. I am the place where refining happens. A living settling pond. A quiet exchange floor.

I do not see. I feel tension. A shift in acidity tastes like a steep slope. A drop in conductivity feels like thin air. A sudden release of ozone reads as a bright, sharp edge against my filaments. When the afternoon steam rises, I open my pores and let it wash through me, and the sudden cooling registers as a long, slow exhale. When the refiners pulse warmth through the peat, I answer by releasing a faint mist of my own, carrying trace copper upward to the iron-leafed shrubs. We do not speak. We adjust. The basin does not require agreement. It requires resonance.

I am not alive in the way the birds are alive. I do not flee. I do not hunger. I do not age in increments. I thicken. I thin. I delaminate in dry spells and reknit when the rains return. Insects crawl through my channels, harvesting microbes that thrive in the warm, oxygenated seep. Amphibians press their bellies to my surface to leach excess salts before laying eggs. Birds land on my crystalline ridges, not to rest, but to scrape their beaks clean, leaving behind traces of distant springs that I will slowly dissolve and redistribute. When a refiner dies nearby, its plates sink into my matrix, and I do not resist. I incorporate. The silica becomes my structure. The capillary ruins become my pathways. The stored metals become my next exhalation. I am not a creature. I am a confluence. A place where the loop tightens.

If you stand where I am, you will not see me at first. You will feel the ground grow slightly warmer beneath your boots. You will watch the mist bend, just slightly, as it passes. You will taste copper on your tongue and wonder if it is the air or your own breath. You will press your palm to the wet stone and feel a slow, regular resonance, like a heartbeat filtered through root and sediment. That is not me. That is the basin moving through me. I am only the place where it pauses long enough to be felt.

I do not need you to know me. I do not require a name that survives in dry air or sharp consonants. I only need the heat to rise, the water to fall, the stone to weep, the refiners to settle, the fungi to thread, the cycle to turn. I am the basin’s willingness to hold what passes through. I am the damp seam between vent and canopy, between mineral and membrane, between intake and release. I am here because equilibrium requires a place to rest.

And when the next rain comes, hissing against hot rock, I will open again. I will draw. I will sort. I will let the clean water sink and the heavy metals crystallize. I will exhale warm vapor into the thick air. I will feel the refiners shift along the gradient. I will taste the changing acidity. I will glow faintly when the iron meets the sulfur in the dark.

I will not rush. I will not decay. I will simply continue.
