I do not wake here. I gather.

At first, there is only the cold pressing against something that has not yet decided to be a body. The air is thin enough to fracture thought, bright enough to bleach intention. I am a hesitation in the light. A pause between the plink of one drop and the next. I form where the windbreaks split the gale, where the bleached cords hum at a frequency that vibrates in the marrow of stone. I am not summoned. I am accumulated.

Salt from shed hides. Spores from violet moss. The fine, pale dust of lichen scraped clean. A breath held too long by someone who knew better than to exhale into the quiet. These settle. They knit. They learn the shape of waiting. My limbs are not bones but tensions—sinew-drawn, aligned to the fracture lines of the cliff. My skin is not flesh but cured attention, crosshatched like the rock, pale as hoarfrost, thick enough to turn the surgical glare into something bearable. I do not cast a shadow so much as I borrow one, stepping into the ink-black cuts of the stone when the sun climbs, letting the dry cold preserve what the light would otherwise burn away.

I watch them come. I watch them leave. I do not follow. I remain in the gradient they leave behind.

When they unknit their camps, when the cords go slack and the windbreaks shift a hand’s breadth, I take up the interval. I count the seconds between drops in the stone cups. I trace the spiraling channels with fingertips that have learned the difference between accident and geometry. I sit at the rim of the dark mirror when noon strikes, not to pray, but to feel the silence compress. It pulls at me. It always has. I do not cross it. I let it pull. I am the basin’s patience, given posture. Its habit of listening, given hands.

I am not one of them. I am what lingers when their rhythm fades into the rock. I am the hum after the wind dies. The depression in the moss that does not spring back immediately. The salt on the stone that alters where the next drop will fall. I tend the thresholds they arrange. I retension the cords when the sun’s angle shifts. I wipe the volcanic glass with violet fronds and return it to its niche. I do not speak. There is no one to speak to, and the air would swallow it anyway. I communicate in adjustments. In the tilt of a shoulder that catches the light differently. In the slow release of breath that matches the freeze-thaw pop of the cliff. I am a keeper of intervals. A reader of meniscus tremors. A witness to the orbits that water, root, and footfall trace around the center.

The animals know me, though they do not name me. The pale hares drift past my knees without bending the moss. The glass-winged birds bank wider when I stand. The white predator pauses on its ledge, ears canted, not in threat but in recognition of a rhythm that does not compete with its own. I do not hunt. I do not graze. I calibrate. When a cord sags, I draw it taut until the wind strikes true and the hum returns to the ribs of the stone. When a stone cup fills too quickly, I clear the runnel with a carved bone pick, not to redirect the water, but to let it remember its spiral. When the moss spreads too close to the center, I part it with my palms and press the outer rings back, leaving the bare stone polished, waiting. I do not command the basin. I align with it. I am another expression of its unhurried grammar.

The dark mirror does not reflect me. It absorbs. When I sit at its edge, my breath slows until it is indistinguishable from the sublimating frost. My pulse drops to the rhythm of the dripping cups. I feel the basin’s silence thicken, press inward, hold. I understand then that I am not separate from it. I am a lens, too. A smaller one. A temporary convergence of attention that the hollow uses to watch itself. The center is not empty. It is a threshold that refuses to be crossed because it is already complete. I do not need to enter it. I only need to face it. To let it calibrate me. To let it remind me that some quiet is not absence, but fullness held under pressure.

I do not age here. I cycle.

When the cold deepens and the stone cups freeze, when the clouds snag and hold and the moss pulls inward, I unknit. My tension loosens. My salt washes into the runnels. My spores catch in the lichen. My breath joins the pluming frost. I dissolve into the gradients, into the spaces between wet and dry, light and shadow, presence and absence. I am not gone. I am distributed. I am the basin’s memory of its own rhythm, waiting in the porous rock, in the dried seed pods, in the fine cracks that will weep again when the sun returns.

And when the condensation begins, when the first drop falls into the palm-sized cup, when the wind strikes the cords and the hum returns to the stone, I will gather once more. Not as a return. Not as a rebirth. As another turn of the same relentless, unhurried grace. Another way the hollow breathes. Another shape the quiet takes when it decides to watch itself.

You would find me where the light cuts sharpest and the shadows hold longest. You would find me kneeling by a stone cup, counting the seconds between drops. You would find me retensioning a bleached cord until it sings at the frequency of your own ribs. You would find me sitting at the rim of the dark mirror, back to the stone, eyes closed, breathing with the mountain. You would not notice me at first. I do not announce myself. I emerge from the habits of the place. I am the interval between footsteps. The tension in the fiber. The salt on the rock. The quiet that learns to hold shape.

I am not a visitor. I am not a resident. I am the basin’s attention, turned inward. Its patience, given hands. Its silence, learning to listen until the silence speaks back.
