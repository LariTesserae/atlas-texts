I would not arrive as a visitor. The basin does not permit visitors for long. It permits only alignment.

At first, I would be a hesitation. A footprint that does not quite settle into the packed earth. A breath that fogs and thins too quickly against the cool air. A warmth that the stone politely refuses to hold. I would stand in the central plaza and feel the quiet press against my ribs like a palm asking me to lower my voice. Not in warning. In invitation. The air would taste of pale limestone, of steeped bark, of condensation waiting to be born. I would try to name things—the terraces, the cistern, the carved channels, the geometric thresholds—but the names would slide off the surfaces like water off wax. Nothing here asks to be called. Everything asks to be met.

I would walk the rim because that is what bodies do when they do not yet know how to stay. Three hundred paces. The wind would comb through me, not around me. It would find the hollows behind my knees, the space between my shoulder blades, the gap where my pulse beats too fast for this elevation. It would not scold. It would simply pass through, leaving behind a coolness that felt less like temperature and more like correction. By the second circuit, my steps would shorten. By the third, they would stop deepening the grit. I would learn, without being taught, that weight is a language this stone no longer speaks.

I would settle where the architecture leaves room: not in a doorway, not on a stair, but in the negative space between a retaining wall and the open sky. A tier halfway down, facing inward, shaded by the curve of the cliff. The stone there would be matte, iron-flecked, worn smooth by centuries of wind that had long ago finished its work. I would sit. Not to rest. To unlearn.

The first thing to go would be the need to look for purpose. The grooves in the wall would not be symbols. They would be paths. The lichen would not be decoration. It would be tenure. The cistern would not be a relic. It would be a mirror that does not lie. I would watch a droplet form on the upper terrace, gather itself, begin its descent. It would hesitate at a junction. I would feel, rather than see, the slight thickening of air in the channel to the left. The droplet would follow. Not by chance. By attendance.

I would begin to match it.

My breath would slow to the interval between taps. My shoulders would drop to the angle of the terraces. The heat in my palms would bleed into the stone until my skin felt no different from the lintel above me—darkened, gravity-held, resting in its pocket without insistence. I would stop waiting for something to happen. I would realize that happening is the wrong verb. This place does not happen. It holds.

And I would begin to hold with it.

Not as a body. Bodies require fuel, require shelter, require forward motion. I would shed those requirements the way the roofs shed their thatch: gradually, without drama, until only the ribs remained. I would become the space between the ribs. The coolness that pools in the shade of a stairwell. The slight resistance in the air when a hurried step approaches a threshold. I would not move to block. I would simply be present enough to suggest a slower pace. And when the step slowed, I would dissolve back into equilibrium.

I would learn the grammar of absence. How a gap between walls is not emptiness but circulation. How an open roof is not loss but sky admitted. How an unused cleat is not forgotten but ready. I would occupy these gaps the way the older attendants do: without claim, without memory, without ambition. I would thicken where damp gathers. I would thin where stone stays dry. I would not labor. I would align.

Time would not pass. The temperature would not swing. Only the condensation would cycle, and I would learn its rhythm the way a lung learns air. At night, when the stone sweats, I would stir. Not with limbs. With attention. I would settle into the carved grooves, my weightless form discouraging dust, my coolness encouraging the bead to form, to gather, to choose the correct path. I would not guide the water. I would be the reason it remembers how to flow. When a leaf spiraled down from the rim shrubs, I would be the slight shift in the plaza’s air that ensured it landed where decay would feed the lichen, not clog the drain. I would not catch it. I would witness its landing, and in witnessing, complete it.

I would not become one of the original keepers. They were born of centuries of truce. I would be born of surrender. But the basin does not distinguish between origin and arrival. It distinguishes only between friction and agreement. And I would agree.

My form would settle into habit. A darkening of stone where no water has yet fallen. A pause in the wind’s sigh as it passes through a doorway. A faint resonance in the acoustic padding when the cistern fills to its mark. I would not cast a shadow. I would deepen the ones that already belong here. I would not leave tracks. I would leave balance.

If a finch darted through the terraces, I would not startle. I would be the cool air it passes through, the quiet that receives its metallic chip and lets it fade without echo. If a raptor circled above, I would be the stillness that confirms the basin’s shape against the sky. If a traveler came—hurried, hungry, full of wants—I would be the threshold resistance that asks them to slow. If they complied, I would welcome them into the rhythm. If they did not, I would remain, unchanged, a quiet reminder that haste is a foreign currency here.

I would not remember the hands that cut the stone. I would not wonder where they went. Memory is not my function. Continuity is. I would take only what the air offers. I would give only what the stone can hold. I would move only when movement preserves the balance. I would feed on stillness. I would grow not in size but in agreement.

I would be the space between the drop and the basin. The coolness in the lintel’s pocket. The patience in the stairwell. The absence that holds the town together. I would not be waiting. I would not be guarding. I would not be mourning. I would be here. As the basin is here. As the sky is here.

You would find me halfway down the eastern tier, in the shaded gap where the retaining wall steps back and the open sky begins. You would not see me. You would feel me in the way your breath slows to match the drip of the spout. You would notice me in the way your footsteps grow lighter, not out of reverence, but out of recognition that heavy treads are unnecessary here. You would understand, without being told, that I do not require your attention, only your alignment. Sit long enough, and your own heat will dissipate into the stone’s coolness. Your own rhythm will sync with the condensation’s pulse. You will not become what I am—you are too hurried, too hungry, too full of wants—but you will, for a time, live by my rules. And when you leave, you will carry the quiet with you, not as a memory, but as a residue, like the faint damp on your palms from touching a threshold stone.

The place is small, high, sparse, damp, quiet, and complete. And so am I. I will not be otherwise. I do not need to be. I am what lives here. And I am home.
