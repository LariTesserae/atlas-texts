I would not arrive. I would gather.

First as a noticing: the cool press of stone against a shoulder that has forgotten it was ever meant to carry urgency. Then as a rhythm: the metronomic fall of a drip three terraces down, the faint shiver in a braided line when the air cools, the way pale dust settles in the lee of a collapsed colonnade and holds its shape until a draft decides otherwise. I would find myself where the atmosphere thickens before it weeps. Not at the rim, where the light falls too straight and the wind moves too fast. Not in the deep basin, where the silt runs thick and the glider tides pool into restless eddies. But midway. At a junction. Where three rope grids cross, where a calcite seam breathes moisture in long, patient exhalations, where a shallow catchment cups the floor and waits.

The stone here is banded iron and salt-veined tuff, worn to a chalky matte that turns waxen where palms have passed. A mica shaft above throws light that never quite commits to a direction, scattering through hammered foil and polished crystal until the air itself feels suspended. Below, a stairwell has surrendered to a ramp that guides thermal currents and the soft-winged drifters that ride them. I would be stationed at the threshold where draft meets damp. Where silt gathers before it is swept. Where tension distributes before it frays. A place that does not ask to be owned, only attended.

I would not be given a name. I would be given a seam. A length of wall. A counterweight. A conversation that began before my hands knew how to hold a chisel and will continue after they forget.

My body would unlearn before it learned. The impulse to speak aloud would dissolve into the porous stone, swallowed by damp air and fungal crust, until I stopped trying to project and started trying to place. A low hum against a load-bearing pillar, felt in the ribs more than the ears. A click of tongue against teeth to mark a shifting draft. A palm pressed to shared travertine to read the minute tremors of work being done two terraces away. My voice would not disappear; it would change medium. It would travel through rope, through stone, through the soles of padded feet. It would move sideways.

My hands would callus in patterns that map function: ridges along the inside knuckles from retwisting cord until the fibers align and gleam; a smooth depression at the base of the thumb from testing the give of settling lintels; fine mineral crosshatch along the forearms where skin brushes weeping walls and learns to shed moisture like the underside of a fungal shelf. My eyes would widen, not to see further, but to read closer. The nictitating lids would grow accustomed to diffused brightness, and my pupils would stop reacting to shadow and start reacting to humidity gradients, to the polarized shimmer of drifting spores, to the faint thermal ripple that precedes a glider flock. I would stop looking for objects. I would start watching for relationships.

I would learn the grammar of the air. How spore-drift thickens half a cycle before saturation. How glider membranes fold inward when updrafts cool. How detritivore trails fan across fresh silt, mapping moisture I cannot yet feel but will soon learn to anticipate. I would read rope like scripture: the slight give that means a clay counterweight needs sand added; the brittle stiffness that means tree-resin has dried too fast in a patch joint; the smooth, even tension that means the grid is breathing, that the negotiation is holding. I would sweep glyphs into the dust not to leave a mark, but to leave a question. *Channel clear? Stone settling? Rest here?* And I would wait for the answer to come through the floor.

Labor would not be task. It would be attendance. I would not fix what breaks; I would listen for what is asking to be rearranged. A sagging arch is not failure; it is a request for redistribution. A dried channel is not loss; it is a pause. I would move stones by hand, not to conquer gravity, but to converse with it. I would lift the edge of a fungal mat and let spores fall into a woven tray without tearing the mycelium, knowing the crust will weep again if left intact. I would adjust shade-cloths when the mist rolls through the lower colonnades, add weight to pulley lines when temperature drops and cords contract, step aside when a faunal tide pools in the courtyard and lets the thermal map redraw itself. Nothing would be taken without a return. A shed carapace ground into pipe sealant. A frayed haul-line unraveled, retwisted, hung as a moisture-wick. A collapsed roof not rebuilt, but persuaded into a draft-guide. I would not manage the ecology. I would participate in it.

I would not count days. I would count saturations. How many times I have retied the northern grid-line. How many cycles I have watched the salt crust bloom, dissolve, and bloom again. How many silt-glyphs I have swept and reswept, knowing the meaning is not in the marking but in the tending. Time would not pass; it would cycle. And I would move within it, not against it.

Kinship would not be blood. It would be water-line. Those who share the catchment. Those who maintain the same rope grid. Those who feel the same stone settle and adjust their weight accordingly. When a young one learns to walk, I would not teach them to run. I would teach them to feel the draft beneath a terrace, to place a foot so it does not disturb the detritivore trails, to listen to the stone through their soles. Inheritance would not be property. It would be responsibility for a specific curve of wall, a specific pulley, a specific weeping seam. To be given a section of the city would be to be given a conversation. I would learn to speak it with my hands, my weight, my patience.

I would not fear erosion. I would recognize it as the place breathing. I would learn that permanence is not resistance, but the skill of yielding without collapsing. That stillness is not absence of motion, but motion balanced. That to live here is to become a verb. Not *I occupy*. Not *I maintain*. *I adjust. I continue. I attend.*

When I am gone, I would not be buried. I would be laid on a high, porous shelf where condensation gathers, where blind detritivores and lace-like fungal mats can do their quiet work. My bones would become silt. My cords would be retwisted by other hands. My name would not be spoken aloud. It would be felt in the tension of the ropes I kept true, in the smoothness of the steps I walked, in the way a particular channel still weeps exactly where I learned to listen to it.

I would find myself exactly where the place needs a listener. Not at the center. Not at the edge. At the seam. Where water remembers stone. Where stone remembers air. Where air remembers motion. I would not arrive. I would condense. I would not stay. I would continue. Bright. Quiet. Endlessly adjusting. Wearing the rhythm as the stone wears the water, as the water wears the stone. Not separate. Not finished. Simply here. Simply next. Simply now.
