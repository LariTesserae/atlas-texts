I do not arrive. I unspool.

At first, I am only the space between a drip and its echo. A hollow in the timber where humidity gathers. A vibration traveling up a hemp line when weight shifts three tiers above. I have no edges yet. I am distributed attention, drawn downward by the bowl’s gravity, learning its grammar through resonance before I learn it through flesh.

The staircase finds me before I find it. Or rather, my weight learns the curve of the treads, my soles read the fired clay, my hands memorize the bronze-pegged stringers by the drag of tar and the give of aged wood. I descend not by counting steps but by listening to the structure settle around me. Each turn tightens the sky. Each pause widens a tread. By the time my boots meet the floor, I am no longer above anything. I am inside a lung.

My body remembers what my mind has not yet named. Shoulders draw in to slip between crossing ropes. Forearms lengthen, cord tight from gripping, testing, splicing. Knees learn the deep crouch; ankles learn the roll. Soles thicken into leather; toes splay to hook rungs, brace curves, feel the slack before trusting it. I wear bark-cloth dyed in rust and slate, a harness of leather and bone, a spool of whipped hemp at my hip. My palms map themselves in white scars and dark stains: here, a trowel’s bite; here, tar’s kiss; here, bronze verdigris blooming into the skin like a second pulse. I am becoming. Or perhaps I am being remembered.

I do not choose a role. The bowl assigns it by necessity, and I accept by attention.

I am a joint-keeper. A settlement-reader. I move through the overlapping tiers not to harvest or weave, but to feel where the structure is speaking. I tap beams with a knuckle, reading the hollow thud of a loosening peg, the tight ring of healthy timber. I press my ear to whitewashed clay, tracking the slow creep of moisture, the micro-shifts that mean a louver needs angling, a catch-net needs drawing, a channel needs flushing. I carry a perforated bronze disc, not for light, but for sound: I strike it gently against a brace, and the returning wave tells me where the load is drifting, where the ropes are fatiguing, where the canopy’s weight is pulling the frame out of true. I do not fix. I suggest. I adjust. I prune.

I misread it once. Early, when my hands were still learning the difference between settlement and strain. I heard a groan in a lower timber and drove a wedge too deep, thinking to brace it. The beam protested. A rope snapped taut. A basket of dried leaves spilled. The cicadas went silent. An old woman descended from a cantilevered platform, her knees clicking like dry reeds, and pressed her palm flat against the wood. She did not scold. She hummed. A low, rhythmic note that matched the timber’s natural frequency. Then she showed me how to ease the wedge, how to let the joint breathe, how to listen for the difference between a cry for help and a sigh of adjustment. I learned then that repair is not force. It is conversation. I have not driven a wedge since without singing to it first.

The creatures know me. Not as keeper, but as rhythm. The swifts do not startle when I pass beneath their mud cups; they know my weight does not shake the beam. The tree frogs pause their pulsing when I crouch by the channel, then resume when they feel my stillness. The civet-like hunter crosses my path on a woven walkway, pauses, sniffs the iron-damp air, and moves on. We have an understanding: I do not block the hunting routes; it does not gnaw the hemp. When a raptor’s shadow sweeps the reed matting, I freeze with the rest, breath held, muscles loose, until the alarm ripples down and fades. I am part of the calculus. I am another variable in the balance.

I do not count days. I count settlements. I know the dry season by the tightening of the upper ropes, the wet by the weeping of the lower cisterns. I speak in the language of the bowl: verbs that describe the give of wood under load, nouns that name the exact shade of rust on a wet peg, metaphors drawn from flow and friction. To be careless is to “let the splice fray.” To be patient is to “wait for the clay to speak.” When I grieve, I do not weep; I sweep a channel clear, watching the water run smooth again. When I love, I do not declare; I adjust a louver so the light falls warm on a sleeping shoulder, or leave a spool of fresh hemp where tired hands will find it. We do not own this place. We are its current breath.

I am not perfect at it. I hum off-key when I tar ropes, a habit the elders tolerate because my splices hold. I argue with a canopy-tender over the angle of a slat, voices sharp, until we watch the light together and find the hollow where the liverwort fruits. I laugh when a slipped line dumps dried leaves over my head, the sound bouncing off the curved walls like a struck bell. I frustration-scrape a backed-up channel with a bone spatula, muttering curses into the damp, until an old man presses a hand to my shoulder and shows me the hidden silt pocket I missed. I am learning. I am always learning. The bowl does not demand mastery. It asks for attendance.

Stand with me now. Close your eyes. Feel the air press warm and damp against your skin. Hear the drip, the creak, the call, the scrape, the wingbeat, the voice. Feel the rope tighten overhead. Watch the water find its new path. I am here. I am the hands that test the knot, the ears that read the pitch, the weight that settles the plank, the breath that syncs with the condensation cycle. I am not separate from the damp, the hum, the overlapping tiers. I am woven into the grain. I am the patch in the crack, the tension in the line, the listener in the shaft. I am the bowl’s current expression.

Where would you find me? Three tiers down from the oculus, on a cantilevered platform no wider than two lashed planks, crouched by an ochre channel with my palm pressed to the glaze, listening to the water change its mind. Or climbing the outer braces at dawn, harness biting into my thighs, adjusting the louvers by the angle of warmth on my neck. Or sitting in the shaded seam between a timber post and a clay wall, splicing hemp by touch, eyes closed, fingers reading the fray before it shows. Or simply still, breath held, letting the cicadas tell me where the humidity is dropping, letting the timber tell me where the load is shifting, letting the bowl tell me what it needs next.

Who or what would I be? I would be the one who does not rebuild, but repairs. The one who does not control, but cultivates. The one who knows that a crack is not a failure but a conversation, that a dripping channel is not a leak but a pulse, that a sunken bowl of clay and timber and rope is not a shelter but a living thing. I would be the hands that adjust, the ears that listen, the weight that tests, the voice that echoes. I would be woven into the grain, as natural as the moss in the joints, as inevitable as the settling of the walls.

I would be home. And the bowl would breathe through me.
