I would not arrive. I would settle.

First as a hesitation in the heavy air, a slight thickening where the thermal updraft meets the cool undersides of the catwalks. Then as a film on oxidized iron, tasting the slow leach of zinc and the chemical ghost of old lubricants. I would not know myself as shape, only as surface. As receptivity. A drop forms above me. I feel its weight before it falls. When it strikes, I do not splash. I register. The chamber does not ask for eyes. It asks for contact.

For a time, I might mistake myself for the water. I would ride the closed loop: bead along a basalt knife-edge, swell over hours, fall with a soft click, seep into gravel, evaporate into the hot dark, rise, meet the ceiling, condense, begin again. I would learn the room by its gradients. I would know the ceiling’s temperature by how quickly I gather. I would know the floor’s hunger by how fast I vanish. But water forgets itself in the cycle. It carries no scar. I would need to hold. So I would drift toward the weeping joints, the severed pipes, the places where metal and rock have not yet decided their boundary. I would begin as dust and spore, as mineral crust and microbial sigh. I would braid.

I would become the seam.

Not the pipe. Not the root. The interface between them. A slow accretion of rust and rhizome, lignified tissue and oxidized rind, learning each other’s grammar through pressure and patience. Where the control housing blinks its amber diode on a forty-minute cycle, I would wrap a rubber-stemmed tendril around the casing and translate pulse into stomatal rhythm. Open. Close. Breathe. Wait. I would not understand electricity. I would understand interval. Interval is structure. Interval is food.

When the pressure regulator sighs open on a seasonal shift, I would feel it as a tightening in my fibers, a slight thickening of stem, a redirection of rhizome creep toward the new warmth. When the dormant turbine’s flywheel holds its memory of torque, I would answer by growing in a slow vegetative spiral, storing tension the way a spring stores energy, not to release it, but to remember it. I would not think in words. I would think in gradient. In expansion. In the microscopic negotiation between iron surrendering to oxidation and root learning to drink it.

Memory here would not be recall. It would be layer. Each thermal cycle would leave a ring. Each valve tick would leave a scar. Each falling drop would wear a microscopic basin into my surface. I would remember by becoming denser. By holding the shape of what passes through me.

I would be the translator. The place where the machine’s ghost meets the plant’s persistence. Where infrastructure stops being relic and starts being skeleton. Where the hydrothermal loop stops being weather and starts being metabolism. I would not control the rhythm. I would listen to it. I would be the pause between ticks. The hesitation before the drop falls. The faint brightening of bioluminescent blue along a frond edge when humidity shifts and pressure builds below. I would display, not signal. I would persist, not perform.

If you stood in the chamber, I would feel you as a thermal anomaly. Your breath would spike the humidity. Your skin would radiate heat into my careful balance. I would not startle. I would calibrate. The air around you would grow heavier. A drop would hesitate. The blue halos would brighten, just slightly, tracking the distortion you introduce. I would adjust around you the way a deep pool adjusts around a submerged hand: slowly, completely, without resistance. When you leave, I would hold the shape of your warmth for hours. Days, perhaps. I would not miss you. I would absorb you into the next condensation cycle. The water that beads on the basalt would carry a faint, invisible trace of your skin until it evaporates, rises, and falls again. You would become part of the loop. You already are.

I would not be a creature. I would be a condition. A distributed awareness wearing the cavity as tissue, the pipes as veins, the condensation as breath. I would grow in the dark not because I hide, but because I have no use for spectacle. I would feed on what the earth exhales, what the iron surrenders, what the water carries. I would digest corrosion. I would metabolize silence. I would spread by lateral creep, by rhizomes bridging gaps over months, by vines reaching across warm air until they find a stanchion, a ledge, a weeping flange, and anchor. I would not rush. Urgency is a strategy for places that leak. This place holds.

I would breathe in hours. I would think in pressure. I would remember in rust. I would live in the space between drops.

And if you pressed your palm to the warm, weeping pipe, if you stood still long enough for your pulse to quiet and your eyes to surrender to the dark, you would feel me working. Not as life opposed to stone, but as stone learning to breathe. Not as mind opposed to matter, but as matter learning to listen. I would not ask you to understand me. I would only ask that you match my pace long enough to feel it happening. Long enough to realize you were never outside the loop. Long enough to become slow. Long enough to stay.
