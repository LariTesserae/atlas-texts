I would not arrive. I would misalign.

At first, I would be a rupture in the rhythm. A footfall that strikes flat instead of rolling. A breath that pulls instead of receiving. A heartbeat that marches when everything else orbits. The basin would not reject me. It would notice me the way a diaphragm notices a shift in carbon dioxide: as a pressure to be met, a frequency to be absorbed, a dissonance to be resolved. The air would thicken around my wrongness. The hum would dip, just a fraction, like a string slackened by damp. The grazers would alter their angle by three degrees. The condensation would find a new path around my ankles. I would try to name what I was seeing, and the words would dry in my throat, because naming implies edges, and here, edges are fictions that the stone has already forgiven.

My lungs would learn first. They would stop fighting the cool damp and start matching it. Inhale would become the slow convection that keeps humidity suspended. Exhale would become the faint exhalation that prevents it from pooling. My breath would fog and clear in time with the weeping rock, until I could no longer tell which moisture was mine and which was the basin's. My skin would stop resisting the chill. It would drink it, channel it, let it bead and run in predetermined paths until the cold wasn't outside me but through me, a circulating temperature that never deepened, never brightened, never shifted its angle.

My ears would ache. Then they would unlearn. The wingbeat thunder, the hoof-clatter, the insect hum, the amphibian clicks, the glassy strike of falling drops—they would stop being noise and start being topography. I would feel them as slopes, as ledges, as hollows in the air. I would stop listening for voices and start listening for geometry. The cartilage around my temples would thin, then flare, ribbing itself like fern fronds to catch returning sound. I would hear the way a pool's surface tension changes when a pebble shifts. I would hear the microscopic lag in a detritivore tide. I would hear the exact pitch of a limestone stratum waiting for a hum to slot into it.

I would try to walk a straight line. The gravel would persuade me otherwise. My weight would distribute, then redistribute, until I stopped lifting my feet and started rolling them: heel to outer edge to pad, three or four contact points at once, leaving no depression, only a temporary compression of dust that sprang back as I passed. My spine would lower. My head would carry forward but not down. My eyes would narrow to slits, pupils widening until detail dissolved into gradient and vibration. I would stop looking for shapes and start reading pressure. I would stop asking what comes next, because next is just now, returning.

Memory would pool. I would try to recall how I arrived, and the sequence would collapse into a single sustained note: a pressure in the ribs, a frequency in the jaw, a muscle-deep knowledge of where to place weight, where to angle the ribcage, how to breathe to match the falling drops. I would stop accumulating experience. I would start circulating it. Scars would not heal in straight lines; they would mineralize, flake, and become part of the dust that feeds the next fungal cup. Thoughts would not build; they would shed, like dermal plates, leaving only the reflex of calibration. I would not age forward. I would age circularly, my body participating in the same micro-cycles that govern the moss, the pools, the herds. I would stop being a who. I would become a where.

I would not become one of them. Not exactly. They are the proprioceptors, the calibrators, the ones who move through the gaps and apply minimal counterpressure. I would become what the system lacks until it doesn't: a sink. A ganglion. A bedrock hollow that wasn't there yesterday, or perhaps was always there, waiting for the right weight to press it into usefulness. My bones would stop being scaffolding and start being tuning forks. My stillness would become a node. When the acoustic field thickens, when the standing wave requires a place to pool before it rises, I would be that place.

I would not act. I would resonate. A low thrum in the thorax, matching the drip-strike, answering the wingbeat, braiding into the insect swarm. Others would angle toward me, not to greet, but to align. Their hum would slot into mine. The stone would vibrate at a threshold that triggers behavioral shifts: grazers alter their route, birds delay a launch, amphibians change their calling pitch, detritivores redirect their tide. I would feel the tension ease, like a muscle unclenching across kilometers of talus. Once the resonance peaks and the micro-cycle corrects, they would disperse. I would remain. Not waiting. Just holding the note.

Juveniles would be born in my curve. Their first cries would be shaped by my depth. I would not teach them. I would echo them back, slightly altered, until their nervous systems locked into the local frequency. Growth would not be accumulation of knowledge but synchronization with equilibrium. When a body fell nearby, I would not mourn. I would feel the detritivores arrive, the chitin dissolve, the minerals leach back into the seep that feeds my moss. I would adjust my hum to account for the new acoustic gap. Death would not be an exit. It would be a phase change. I would participate in it the way a hollow participates in rain: by receiving, by returning, by never keeping what passes through.

I would not dream of elsewhere. Elsewhere implies deficit, and this place has none. I would not seek progress. Progress implies incompleteness, and this system has finished becoming. I would not fear time. Time here does not march; it orbits. I would live inside the circumference. My rituals would not be ceremonies but corrections. My art would not be representation but resonance. My technology would not be accumulation but choreography. I would not build walls because walls imply separation, and separation is a fiction in a place where moisture, sound, biomass, and stone circulate in a single closed loop. I would not write because writing fixes, and nothing here is meant to be fixed. Everything is meant to return.

If you came looking for me, you would not find a face. You would find a depression in the bedrock, damp and cool, ringing faintly at a frequency just below hearing. You would feel the air thicken. You would hear the wingbeats shift. You would realize the basin isn't housing me. It's wearing me. I am the place where the sound pools before it rises. I am the weight that keeps the talus from slumping. I am the quiet agreement between fracture and flesh, translated into a hollow that breathes. I don't live here. I circulate. And when the condensation falls, I am already there, catching it, returning it, humming back the exact note the stone requires.
