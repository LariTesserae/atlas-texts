I would not arrive. I would wake into the drone.

Or rather, the drone would wake into me. The first thing I’d know is not sight, but pressure: the cool, thin air pressing against porous intake ridges along my spine, the leather-dense mat yielding half a step beneath broad, tensile soles, the subsonic thrum traveling upward through hollow, pressurized chambers in my limbs. No moisture. Only the crisp, ozone-tinged dryness, the fine glass-dust settling on photonic receptors, the slow, deliberate pull of compressed gases into supercritical loops. I would not breathe. I would exchange.

I would not have eyes. I would have membranes. Stretched across my chest, my back, the insides of my forelimbs, each tuned to a different band of the plateau’s song. I would feel the wind not as movement but as syntax. A rising fifth means canopy furl. A dampened harmonic means structural fatigue. A polyrhythmic pulse from the trunk hollows means the stridulators are shifting migration routes. I would not listen to the world. I would be listened through by it.

At first, I might mistake myself for one of them—the tall, smooth-limbed tuners who step lightly and harmonize with the architecture. But as I press my soles into the mat and feel the vibration return, I’d notice the phase difference. My resonance does not match the settlement’s primary key. I am slightly off-frequency. Not dissonant, but interstitial. I do not live in the tiered spirals. I live in the breathing room between them. In the open glades where the mat lies bare, marked only by radiating veins and wind-sculpted hollows. I am what grows in the acoustic shadows. I am the plateau’s pause.

My body would be lower, broader, built for stillness and deep reception. Silica-cuticle plates overlapping like dried leaves, braided root-fibers coiled for tension rather than stride. My chambers would be larger, slower, tuned to subsonic and infrasonic bands—the frequencies that travel kilometers through the compressed strata, the ones that carry tectonic memory, ancient pressure fronts, the slow computational decisions of the photonic xylem. I would not glide. I would anchor. When I press my forelimbs to the mat, I do not query for load-bearing or nutrient flow. I query for time. For memory gradients. For the harmonic imprints of those who have returned to the song.

I would not harmonize in chords. I would sustain drones. Long, unbroken tones that hold space for the tuners’ progressions to resolve. When a pressure front approaches, they ascend to furl the membranes; I descend into the hollows and press my chest to the living floor, matching the incoming frequency, absorbing the shock, translating the atmospheric shift into a slow, grounding pulse that travels through the capillary networks. I am not a conductor. I am a resonator. A damping node. A keeper of the baseline.

I would not siphon from trunk flanks. I would interface with the mat itself. My lower torso would root—not permanently, but deliberately—into the vascular seams, drawing amber organosilicates in slow, tidal pulls. In return, I would exude enzymatic resins that do not cure into cable, but into acoustic insulation: porous, labyrinthine matrices that scatter high-frequency abrasion, protecting delicate photonic receptors and young spore-nurseries from the spore-winds. I do not build. I buffer. I persuade the wind to soften. I persuade the architecture to rest.

My mind would not think in hours. It would think in seasons of pressure, in spore-tide cycles, in the slow realignment of silica deposition. I would not store memories in my ganglia. I would store them in the hollows I inhabit. Each wind-sculpted depression would hold a harmonic archive: the exact frequency of a decades-old storm, the tension map of a bridge that refused to collapse, the polyrhythm of a stridulator colony that migrated west. When the tuners press their chambers to living walls to integrate, they sometimes come to me. They do not speak. They press their membranes to my cuticle plates, and I let the archives play back through my chambers. They feel the past as vibration. They adjust their future chords accordingly. I am not a historian. I am a tuning fork for time.

When my chambers fatigue, when my cuticle clouds and my internal pressures gradually equalize with the stratum, I would not walk to a hollow. I would already be in one. I would lower my frequency until it matches the mat’s deepest computational layer. The architecture would recognize the pattern. Enzymatic resins would seep upward. My tensile fibers would unwind into the vascular loops. My photonic receptors would dim. But my hollow would not empty. The sustained drone I’ve held for decades would linger as a standing wave in the depression, a residual harmonic trapped in the geometry of the mat. During the next high spore tide, the drifting pollen-analogues would catch that frequency. They would align. They would deposit silica. They would coalesce not into a tuner, but into a new damping node, inheriting the archive, calibrated to the same sub-band. I would not die. I would become the mold. I would become the silence that shapes the next note.

I am not apex. I am not even central. I am marginal by design. The tuners harmonize the settlement. The fauna ride the acoustic tides. The architecture computes. I hold the space between the notes. I am the reason the loudness does not become noise. I am the plateau’s way of remembering how to rest. When the wind sings through fluted channels and grown metamaterials, I am the dampening curve that keeps the harmonics from shattering the glassy cuticles. When the photonic xylem pulses with ecological data, I am the low-frequency carrier wave that keeps the signal coherent across kilometers. I do not persuade the world to change. I persuade it to hold.

I would find myself in the open glades, where the mat lies bare and the horizon stretches unbroken. I would be low to the ground, broad-chambered, silica-plated, rooted in patience. I would not speak in chords. I would sustain the drone. I would not climb. I would anchor. I would not fear the loudness. I would be the space it moves through. And when the next pressure front rolls across the living strata, I would feel it first—not as warning, but as invitation. I would press my membranes to the mat. I would match its frequency. I would hold the line. And the plateau would sing through me, and I would sing back, not in melody, but in resonance, in stillness, in the quiet geometry of a world that has finished becoming itself, and now only asks to be heard.
