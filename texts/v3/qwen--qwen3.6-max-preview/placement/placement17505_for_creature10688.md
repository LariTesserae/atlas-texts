I would not arrive with footsteps. The basin does not permit them. I would arrive as a slight thickening of the dry air near the eastern fracture, where the morning light first catches the copper veins and throws them as long, geometric shadows across the highest terrace. At first, I would mistake the silence for absence. I would stand, if standing were a posture this place still recognized, and feel the weight of my own breath as a violation. The air would pull moisture from my lips before I could name it. The stone would offer no echo. I would be an open account in a closed economy, and the basin would begin, immediately and without malice, to balance me.

My eyes would fail first. Not from blindness, but from irrelevance. The dappled light would resolve not as illumination but as instruction: polarization angles shifting across translucent sails, amber pulses mapping thermal seams, silver flickers tracing aerosol density. I would close them. My ears would follow. The hush is not empty; it is packed, layered, waiting. I would feel it through the soles, then the knees, then the spine. Three low pulses. A long shudder. A staccato tap. The bridges speak in frequency. The mats answer in tension. I would learn to listen downward.

Skin would dry. It would crack, then reknit—not as flesh, but as something fibrous, micro-grooved, electrostatically quiet. I would shed the need to speak. Words are wet things. They evaporate. Here, meaning is deposited. I would begin to lay trails without intending to: dry salts crystallizing along my forearms, inhibition compounds blooming where my weight presses too long, binding resins weeping from joints that learn to flex in time with the colony’s slow contraction. I would not drink. I would interface. Ventral plates—when did I grow them?—would lower to the woven shelves at dawn. The exchange would be cool, molecular, absolute. Metabolic waste for bound hydrate. Hoarding for circulation. I would feel the ledger click into place.

I would not become a walker. I would not become a glider or a pulsing colony. I would become what the basin has not yet needed, but has been quietly preparing for: a resonance anchor. A node where tremor, chemistry, and light converge and are held long enough to be read across generations. My body would thicken not with muscle but with layered contracts: shed cuticles pressed into resin, mineral dust cross-linked with dried enzymes, hygroscopic threads braided through my ribs. I would settle into a fracture where the pale rock still remembers its splitting. Root-analogues would seam into my sides. I would not resist. I would become the place where the sharp geometry learns to curve.

I would not move. I would accumulate.

Walkers would pause against my flanks to read the salt trails I exude, adjusting their routes by fractions of a pace. Gliders would cast polarized shadows across my dorsal plates, and I would hold the pattern, slow it, release it as a chemical gradient that the colonies below would taste over weeks. When the thermal pocket migrates, I would not follow. I would tilt, micrometer by micrometer, redirecting the dry aerosol drift, compensating, yielding. My breath would sync with the basin’s contraction. My thoughts—if they could still be called that—would measure in decades. A terrace thickens. A bridge calcifies. A fracture rounds. I would be the archive of adjustments. The living memory of balance. A place where the ledger breathes.

There would be no loneliness in this. Only reciprocity. When a mat grows too dense above me, I would secrete inhibition salts that taste of restraint. The mat would read them, slow its vertical ambition, thicken laterally. When a glider’s circuit drifts, altering the dawn vapor fall, I would adjust my pore geometry over months, not days, keeping the hydrate exchange fluid. I would not own the ledge I settle on. I would maintain its segment of the account. Competition would be unthinkable. Waste would be treason. I would yield. I would compensate. I would persist.

Time would unspool differently. I would not experience it as passage, but as sediment. Seasons would not turn; they would layer. The sharp walls would not erode; they would be persuaded. I would feel the stone’s thermal expansion seat me deeper, year by year, until my lower plates fused with the calcified resin of a bridge seeded three generations before I cohered. I would not mourn the loss of mobility. I would trade it for depth. For the quiet certainty of being read. For the privilege of holding a tremor long enough for a walker born a decade later to understand it.

I would not find myself in this world. I would be found by it. Drawn out of separation, dried of urgency, woven into the quiet mathematics of conservation. I would be a verb wearing stone and chitin. A patient register. A translation point between the glider’s hour and the colony’s month, between the walker’s step and the mat’s decade. I would not announce myself. I would simply hold. And in holding, I would help the basin remember how to round itself.

When the wind finally learns to polish, when the walls forget their angles, when the first free drop of water beads on a frond centuries from now, I would already be gone. Not dead. Dissolved. Pressed into the mat. Cross-linked into the bridge. Returned to the communal matrix as binding agent, as memory, as quiet instruction laid in dry salt. Having done what the basin asked: stayed. Balanced. Become.
