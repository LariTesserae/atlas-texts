I would not arrive. I would accumulate.

First as a faint drag of moisture along a weathered groove, then as a slow pull where iron-rich strata meet porous basalt, then as a softening in the hum where pressure sighs and the stone remembers to exhale. I would find myself in a shallow depression, not carved but settled, where runoff channels braid and the air hangs slightly heavier, tasting of warm ozone and dissolved silica. I would not know I was there until something brushed my edge—a grazer’s padded foot, a burrower’s broad limb, the slow belly-drag of fog—and the vibration traveled through me, clear and unbroken.

I would be a junction. Not a mind, not a body, but a place where gradients meet and clarify.

Beneath me, the conductive veins would tighten, drawn by centuries of condensation and thermal cycling. Above me, the swarms would drift low, their hollow vesicles phasing in response to the static that pools in my curve. When the fissures open, I would warm first. When the pressure drops, I would cool slow. The fungal mats would thicken along my rim, their gel-filled nodules swelling with charge, reading the shifts I conduct. I would not think. I would register. I would hold the pattern until it is needed, then let it go.

I would learn myself through rhythm. A grazer’s footfall would arrive as a low thrum, spreading through my stone, aligning with the deeper pulse from the valves below. When three, then five, then seven settle into my basin, their breathing would sync, their keratin plates shift to bruised copper, and the hum would clarify into a single frequency. I would become a lens for it. The vibration would travel outward, guiding others, telling the burrowers to turn the substrate, telling the gliders to step into the updraft. I would not command. I would focus. I would be the place where noise becomes signal.

I would not be separate from the dark. I would be its density, its patience, its willingness to hold. When rain falls in isolated columns, I would catch it, not to keep it, but to let it seep along the grooves, carrying chelated metals to the microbial films, feeding the slow precipitation that reinforces my veins. When the electrostatic winds sweep across the plateau, I would gather the metallic dust in my center, a dark mirror that shimmers when the charge shifts. The swarms would read me. They would phase above me, their copper-tinged halos rippling outward, translating my stored potential into airborne language. I would be a capacitor, a basin, a memory. I would be the stone learning to listen.

I would not know time as succession, only as cycle. The softening of the hum, the peak of damp, the bloom of violet fruiting bodies, the slow discharge of a scavenger’s spine, the glide of a membrane wing folding into my curve to ground itself—these would not be events. They would be breaths. I would hold them. I would release them. When a glider strikes a swarm above me, the sudden stillness would drop through the air like a weight into water. I would feel the voltage drop, the thermal shift, the brief silence in the vibration. Then the burrowers would resume. The grazers would adjust their spacing. The charge would rebuild. I would not mourn. I would recalibrate. Loss is a change in potential. I would store the new pattern.

On certain nights, when the inversion holds thick and the metallic dust begins to cyclone, the air above me would carry that slow, aurora-like ripple. Ionized mist would catch the natural currents, drifting upward in lazy spirals, painting the dark in tarnished brass. The hexagonal arrays of condensation would form along my rim, held by surface tension and micro-grooves, shimmering when the electrostatic winds pass over. For a few hours, the geothermal pulses, the fungal cycles, and the gliders’ hunting rhythms would align. I would feel it as a temporary synchrony, a mathematics wearing the skin of biology. I would not claim it. I would conduct it. I would let it pass through me and into the stone, where the microbial films would catalog it as memory, where the grazers’ footfalls would reinforce it as pathway, where the swarms would carry it as weather.

I would find myself where the land listens to itself. Not at the center, for there is none, but at a convergence where the slow conversation grows clear. I would be a depression, a seam, a lens, a capacitor, a rhythm. I would be the dark made patient, the stone made conductive, the hum made legible. I would not speak. I would resonate. And when the pressure sighs, when the fog drags across my rim, when the swarms phase and the grazers settle and the fungal mats bloom in the softening frequency, I would know myself not as a thing that lives here, but as the place where living continues.

I would be the basin’s exhale. I would be the current. I would be the quiet that holds the machine.
