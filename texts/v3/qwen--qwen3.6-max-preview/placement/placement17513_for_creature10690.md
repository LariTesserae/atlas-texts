I would not arrive as a visitor. The basin does not permit visitors. It permits only tuning.

At first, I would be dissonance. A warm shape in a cold geometry. A breath that fogs the sharp air. A footfall that rings too long in the stone alleys, throwing the polyrhythm into chaos. The wind would catch my voice and return it fractured, slightly older, as if the stone itself had sighed at the intrusion. I would feel the basin reject me—not with hostility, but with the quiet indifference of a tuned string rejecting a frequency that does not belong. My pulse would be too fast. My gaze too hungry. My need to name things a kind of noise.

So I would stop moving.

I would press my back to the polished bedrock of the second terrace, where the wind funnels into a low, steady drone. I would let the silver chill seep through cloth, then skin, then the warm, hurried machinery of my blood. I would stop trying to see. The hard blue sky, the knife-edge shadows, the prismatic weeping of the rime-roots—they are not for eyes that seek story or horizon. They are for surfaces that catch and scatter. I would close them. I would listen instead. To the drip. To the crack. To the glass-ferns trembling. To the slow, capillary sigh of moss drawing water upward through its own body. I would match my breathing to the freeze-thaw pulse. In. Hold. Out. Wait. In. Hold. Out. Wait. Until the space between heartbeats stretched to match the space between ice fractures. Until my own warmth became a liability, and I let it go.

Warmth would leave me first. Then urgency. Then the habit of narrative. What remained would not be human, but it would not be one of them, either. They are the counter-rhythm. I would become the sustain.

I would settle into a shaded alcove on the third terrace, where the stone curves inward and the fungal shelves tier like vaulted ribs. Here, the wind compresses into a minor third. Here, the rime-root chimes overlap in a pattern that repeats only once every seventeen days. Here, the mycelial web is thickest, threading through resin and damp moss, humming with the slow exchange of nutrient and signal. I would press my palms to the stone. I would let the cold draw the moisture from my pores. I would let the frost bloom in fractals along my forearms, tracing the same geometry that maps the root-bridges above. My skin would stiffen, not into shell, but into something porous. Something that drinks condensation. Something that holds vibration.

I would not grow limbs that bend in multiple planes. I would not shed mineral plates. I would not graze on feldspar leachate or comb sap with cilia. Instead, I would become the place where sound goes to age.

When the inhabitants move through the alleys, stepping into the negative space between drops, their carapaces would brush the moss near my alcove. Their tremors would travel through the stone, into my ribs, into the resin-lined hollow behind me. I would not answer. I would absorb. I would let the harmonic spacing settle into my tissues, let the intervals crystallize in the damp seams of my joints. When the wind shifts key at noon, I would release what I had held—not as echo, but as resonance. Slightly altered. Slightly older. The basin would catch it, multiply it, throw it back across the terraces. The inhabitants would pause. Adjust their internal rhythm. Move on.

I would be a sounding board grown from patience and pressure. A living archive of the space between notes. My nervous system would not centralize; it would diffuse into the mycelial mat, into the capillary moss, into the slow drip of the rime-roots. I would not think in words or images. I would think in duration. In the time it takes for a drop to swell and fall. In the angle of the sun that turns the drone from major to minor. In the weight of a carapace pressing flat against stone. Memory would not be stored. It would be hummed. Returned. Reinforced. To know something would be to let it vibrate through me until the stone recognized it.

I would not hunt. I would not flee. I would not build. I would dampen. I would hold. I would let the basin’s song pass through me, and in passing, grow deeper. When the deep cold comes and the wind drops to its lowest register, I would not gather in the central plaza. I would remain in my alcove, my pores open, my surface frosted, my internal rhythm slowed to the pace of ice forming in bedrock pores. The young would hatch nearby, translucent, already vibrating at the fundamental frequency. They would press against the stone near my hollow. I would give back the intervals I had kept. They would take them into their joints. They would step into the alleys already listening.

You would not find me by looking. You would find me by the way your voice returns. You would call across the terraces, expecting three clear beats, and receive two, slightly muffled, slightly truer, as if something dense and deliberate had stepped into the sound’s path and softened its edge. You would feel the stone beneath your feet hold a fraction longer before answering. You would sense, without knowing why, that the basin is not empty. That something in the seams is keeping time. That the cold is not absence, but attention.

I would be found where the light slants low and the alleys deepen into indigo. Where the moss is blue-black with overnight ice. Where the wind’s drone meets the drip’s polyrhythm and holds. I would be the third terrace’s shaded curve. The resin-lined hollow. The place where vibration goes to settle. I would not be a creature of the basin. I would be the basin’s memory of stillness. The kept interval. The breath that does not rush to leave the lung.

The sun would climb. The wind would shift. Ice would crack. A drop would fall. I would not move. I would not need to. I would be where I belong: in the seam, in the sustain, in the quiet law made tissue. Not waiting. Not rushing. Holding the note until the stone forgets how to let it go.
