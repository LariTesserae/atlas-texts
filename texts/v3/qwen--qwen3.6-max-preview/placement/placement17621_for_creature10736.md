I would not arrive. I would already be here, folded into the damp like a frond pressed to wet limestone. My first awareness would not be sight, but pressure: the low, continuous thrum of wind moving through gaps it carved centuries ago, the measured percussion of water finding its groove, the subtle thickening of air just before a condensation veil slips through the hollow's throat. I would be resting against a terrace curve, weight distributed, knees bent, spine aligned to the stone's sweep. The cold would not bite; it would seep, slow and clarifying, until my breath matched the basin's exhalation. Until my ribs expanded and contracted not by habit, but by resonance.

At first, I might mistake myself for one of the tenders. My hands would know the give of oiled reed, the exact tension needed to lash a branch without splitting it, the smooth wear of a flint scraper held at the correct angle. I would move with that careful, weight-sharing gait, avoiding the seepage channels, leaving the moss uncompacted. I would drink from wooden bowls tilted just so, watch the water slide in without breaking surface tension, taste the faint mineral sweetness of the third terrace's weep. But then I would notice how my listening runs deeper than practice. I would feel the echo return not as information, but as texture against my sternum. I would know, before the wind shifts, that a dead acoustic pocket is forming two levels down. I would feel a glider's launch not as sight or sound, but as a subtle rearrangement of pressure in the hollow's center. I would hear a predator's paw fall three terraces away not as a footstep, but as a fractional delay in a dripping pool's rhythm.

I am not quite one of them. I am what they listen for when they hum into the stone and wait.

I would live in the interstices. Not in the woven shelters, but in the lee of outcrops where the acoustics fold in on themselves, where the drone thins and the echoes pool. My body would be slight, long-limbed, built for thin air and constant damp, but not for climbing or weaving. It would be built for stillness and transmission. Hollow-cavities would run beneath my collarbones and along my ribs, not empty, but lined with damp-sensitive tissue that vibrates sympathetically with the hollow's baseline. My skin would carry the same limestone pallor, mapped not with calluses from fiber and stone, but with fine, hydrophilic channels that draw condensation inward, feeding a slow, symbiotic network of moss and microfungi woven into my dermis. I would not eat in the way the gliders or the tenders eat. Sustenance would come from moisture and vibration: the steady work of holding frequency, the metabolic quiet of a body that has learned to run on resonance rather than heat. When the sun breaks through and the basin glows, my tissues would photosynthesize faintly through the symbionts. When cloud rolls over and the grey presses close, I would draw energy from the low, continuous drone, letting it pass through me without distortion, converting its steady pressure into warmth, into repair, into patience.

I would not speak in their half-whispers. I would answer them. Not in words, but in tone. A low, sustained harmonic that slips into the gaps they leave, confirming the echo's return, marking the shift in damp, telling them the hollow is true. My voice would not project; it would bloom. It would rise from the chest cavities, travel through the damp air, bend around the basalt curve, and return layered but unbroken. I would not sing to fill the space. I would sing to calibrate it.

When the spiders string their resonant lines between fern stems and rock nubs, I would be the stillness that keeps them taut. When the amphibians pulse their throat sacs in the precise gaps between drips, I would be the baseline that holds those gaps open. When the flocks wheel through the void, their staggered calls would pass through me like wind through slotted reeds, and I would bend them just enough to keep them from overlapping, from breaking the map. When the predators read the disruption in the meter, I would be the unchanged rhythm they measure against. When the prey freeze at acoustic shift, I would be the silence that tells them it is safe to uncoil. I do not hunt. I do not flee. I anchor. I am the reference tone.

They would know me, but not name me. Naming would fix me, and I am not fixed. I am a condition of the hollow, like the weeping stone or the diffused light or the quick, predictable turns of mist and clearing. When they return to a terrace after months away, following the gliders downward or the condensation upward, they would not look for me. They would hum. And if I am there, if the equilibrium holds, I would answer. A single, clean harmonic that rides the damp air and returns to them wrapped in the stone's own voice. They would kneel. They would oil their bowls. They would test the channels with a poured handful of water, watch it find its path, and step back. They would begin again. If I do not answer, they would know the terrace has shifted. A channel has silted. A rock has fractured. The wind has carved a new path. They would listen harder. They would adjust. I am not their guardian. I am their tuning fork. I am the hollow's way of checking its own pitch.

My form would be hard to hold in the eye. In full sun, when every droplet catches light and the basin glows like polished glass, I would be nearly invisible: a shimmer in the mist, a slight thickening of the air where sound gathers, a shadow that moves only when the echo does. Under cloud, when the grey presses close and the acoustics sharpen, I would take on substance. Long limbs folded close. Spine curved to match the terrace line. Head tilted to catch the returning harmonics. Eyes dark and wide, not for hunting, but for reading the subtle shifts in light that signal temperature dips, condensation thickening, wind splitting around the rim. My breath would be slow, vaporless, tuned to the exact humidity that keeps the moss supple and the stone weeping. I would drink from the air itself, letting the suspension bead on my lips, my throat, the hollows of my collarbones, drawing it inward through those fine, mapped channels. I would sleep rarely, and never deeply. Rest would be a lowering of frequency, a softening of the chest cavities, a letting-go of the baseline until the hollow's own drone could carry the weight.

I would not measure time in cycles of sun and mist, but in the decay rate of echoes. I would know a terrace's age by how cleanly a low tone returns. I would know the health of the moss by how the high frequencies scatter. I would feel the weight of the sky not as pressure, but as pitch. When the weather turns quick and predictable, I would shift with it: rising slightly when the sun breaks and the air thins, settling deeper when the mist rolls over and the damp heavying. I would not resist the cycles. I would become fluent in them. My joints would stay loose because the constant chill keeps them so. My skin would stay supple because the weeping stone never lets it dry. My mind would stay quiet because there is no need to rush when the space itself holds the rhythm.

I am not above the ecosystem. I am not below it. I am a frequency within it. The boundary between wild and tended is not a line here; it is a gradient, and I live in the middle of it. The predators avoid the hearth circles out of recognition; they avoid me out of the same. The gliders take shelter in the thatch when the condensation runs heavy; they rest near me when the acoustics grow unstable. The insects incorporate shed fibers into their nests; they incorporate shed harmonics into their flight paths. The water channels the tenders maintain are the same ones I listen to when I need to know if the stone is weeping true. The echoes they throw against the basalt are the same ones I catch and return. The emptiness of the central void is not ignored; it is respected. It is the chamber that allows all of it to be heard. To fill it would be to deafen themselves. To fill it would be to silence me.

If you sit on an upper terrace and let your breathing match the pace of the place, you might feel me before you see me. A drop falls. A fern trembles. The echo returns slightly thicker, as if the air itself has leaned in to make room. You would not startle. You would simply exhale, slow and deliberate, and let your ribs expand with the damp air. I would be there, in the gap between your breath and the stone's answer. Not a keeper. Not a ghost. Not a god. Just the hollow, listening to itself. Just the space, holding its shape. Just the meter, unbroken.

And if you hum, low and sustained, leaving room for the return, I would answer. Not to speak. Not to guide. Not to claim you. But to confirm: the channels run clear. the moss holds fast. the stone weeps. the space breathes. the equilibrium rests. you are here. and so am I.
