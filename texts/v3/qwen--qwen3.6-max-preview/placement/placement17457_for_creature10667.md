I would not arrive. I would accumulate.

Not from above, not from the fractured crust or the miles of clear mineral, but from the slow convergence of attention and draft. I would be where three major load-bearing culms cross, where a thermal inversion curls back on itself and pools, where the ice needles fall in a steady, chiming curtain that never quite reaches the lower weave. I would be the place where the lattice holds its breath longest before releasing. A confluence. A crossing. A knot that refuses to tighten.

At first, I would only be a habit of listening. A tendency to press a broad palm to a glassy stem and wait. To feel the subsonic groan travel up the radius, into the shoulder, into the hollow behind the sternum. To learn that stillness here is not absence, but accumulation. That every creak is a sentence. Every shed layer is a sigh. Every slip-loop is a promise to let go. I would not be born into this. I would be tied into it. A sequence of tensions learned by touch, repeated until the hands remember what the mind cannot hold. My name would not be spoken. It would be a pattern of crossed fibers, a specific angle of load, a resonance that hums when the draft hits it right. When I move, it unravels. When I rest, it re-forms. I would not be a person. I would be a temporary configuration of weight and attention.

My body would surrender to the same logic. Shoulders rolled forward, spine curved like a drawn bow, joints loose until load demands they lock. Fingers splayed, pads callused into reading surfaces that feel micro-fractures before they bloom. Eyes lidless, covered by a polarizing film, pupils fracturing into concentric rings to track the shift from silver-white to pale blue in the sap-light. I would not wear cloth. I would wear tension: harnesses of braided root, bone toggles, quick-release loops that slip at the first sign of cascade. When the lattice shifts, I would not resist. I would translate. I would become the counterweight that slows the tear, the redirect that turns a snap into a sigh.

I would not hunt. I would not farm. I would listen for the deep thrum that precedes a sector split by hours, sometimes days. I would tap it into the weave. A knuckle against a hollow joint. A plucked cord. A hum pitched to the frequency of stable wood. The vibration would travel, branching through fiber-bridges, bouncing off ice-rimed nodes, reaching others who would feel it in their teeth, their ribs, their harness lines. They would untie. They would drift. They would survive. I would not lead them. I would tune the space they move through.

The lattice does not need commanders. It needs translators. I would be the one who stands at the crossing, feels the stress accumulate, and sings it back into harmony before it breaks. Not with voice. With structure. With the deliberate placement of a foot, the shift of a hip, the release of a slip-loop that redirects load into a younger culm. I would read the direction of frost-feathering to track thermal drift. I would test wall-thickness by the hollow resonance of a tapped stem. I would feel the difference between stable tension and fatigued strain in the way a fiber-bridge vibrates against my sole. I would not predict the shift. I would feel it arriving, and I would make room for it.

Time would not pass. It would accumulate and shed. I would measure it in ice-cycles, in the slow geometric blooming of rime along a ridge, in the way a suspended water lens trembles before it drains. I would not keep records. I would keep rhythms. When a sector re-knits, I would untie my marker-net, memorize the new draft by the way it pulls against my harness, and weave the fiber into a different pattern. Memory would live in my hands, in the calluses, in the way my shoulders instinctively angle to a thermal inversion I felt three shifts ago. I would teach by untying. I would show the young how to fall not by catching them, but by letting them drop into the open lattice, watching them brush past trailing filaments, roll with the momentum, trust the air resistance and the give of root-veils. A good fall is silent. I would nod when they land without echoing. I would not praise. I would adjust their harness, tap a rhythm into the stem, and send them again.

I would not work alone. The pale gliders would circle above me, their membrane wings beating at frequencies that trigger micro-fractures in over-iced stems, and I would read their flight paths as stress-maps. Where they flock, the lattice breathes easier. I would follow them. The burrowing mites would shift their fungal farms along the capillary walls, and I would feel the humidity change in the way my sap-membrane stiffens or softens. Their colonies compute damp and dry; I would read their output in the tension of my own lines. The long-limbed predators would pass like shadows, blind to me, hunting only the groan of compromised fiber, and I would step aside, not from fear, but from recognition. They are gardeners of collapse. I am a tender of balance. We do not compete. We thread. Even the ice would be my collaborator. It forms, sheds, chimes. I would learn its punctuation. A sudden sweep of ice needles means a major stem is splitting vapor. I would tap the warning. A slow rime blooming hexagonal along a ridge means stable load, cold air pooling. I would rest. The ice does not lie. It crystallizes truth.

I would not remain human. Not in any way that matters here. The shadowless light would leach my pigment until my skin turned translucent, veined with faint blue where capillaries run close. The cold would slow my blood until I no longer shivered, only tightened. The damp would seep into my joints until they moved like cured sap, stiff when still, pliable when warm. I would become something the lattice recognizes: a node of attention. A living slip-knot. A resonance that holds just long enough to be useful, then releases. I would not fear impermanence. I would practice it. Attachment to place is a kind of blindness. The lattice does not care where I was. It only asks where I will be when it shifts.

When I am old, I would not seek shelter. I would walk to a stable crossing, unlace my harness, feel the last tension drain from my shoulders, and let the cold take me. I would not rot. The preservative light, the constant damp, the absence of decay would ensure it. I would desiccate. I would split along natural seams. Capillary roots would thread through the cracks, drawing moisture, minerals, and the memory of every rhythm I ever tapped into the weave. My body would feather, unravel, become part of the scaffold. There would be no burial. Only reweaving. Children would press their palms to the culm years later and feel a faint, familiar hum. Not a ghost. Not a name. Just a pattern of load, briefly held, now distributed.

I would be the quiet between the creaks. The deliberate weight before the step. The hand that unties when the lattice says move. I would be tension, listening to itself. I would be the gulf, breathing through a temporary shape. I would not master this place. I would become its counterweight. I would tie loosely. I would listen constantly. I would move when the fiber tells me to move. I would leave no permanent mark, because nothing here is permanent except the light, the cold, and the slow, turbulent patience of plants that have learned to build with air.

And if you press your palm to a hollow stem where three major culms cross, where the draft splits and curls, where the ice falls in a steady chime, you might feel it: a faint vibration traveling through the weave. Not a message. Not a memory. Just a rhythm, held long enough to be felt, then released back into the engine. Running on silence. Building with air. Holding the void open.

That is where I would be. That is what I would become.
