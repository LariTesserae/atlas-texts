I would not arrive. I would simply be already here, the way condensation is already gathering on the undersides of stone lips before the eye registers the darkening. There is no threshold to cross, no road that leads from elsewhere, no moment of stepping into the landscape. The air would hold me at the same temperature it holds the moss, the slick clay, the lime-washed walls. My first awareness would not be of self, but of rhythm: the spaced fall of drops into a shallow runnel, the slow draw of breath that matches it, the weight of humidity resting against skin that has never known dryness.

I would be low to the ground. Not crouched, but grounded. Knees carrying a permanent, relaxed bend. Soles broad and damp, reading the fitted flagstones through a thin callus that never hardens into armor, only learns to translate texture into information. My hands would move before my thoughts do. Fingers testing the rim of a ceramic filtration jar buried in gravel. Palm pressing into the cool, angular shoulder of a weeping boulder. Thumb tracing the shallow groove where water has decided, again, to travel. I would not be a builder. I would be a follower of paths already found. A keeper of the seep. A tender of the spaces between.

At first, I might believe I am one of them: the quiet figures distributed across the terraces, the ones whose names are just the slope they tend or the channel they clear. But the longer I stand, the more the boundary between body and place would dissolve. The fine pale dust on my forearms would be the same crushed tuff that fractures in geometric sheets. My breath would join the evaporation rising from the marsh-edge flats. When I lift a wooden trowel to skim leaf litter from a runnel, the motion would not be mine alone; it would be the arc of a fern uncurling, the tilt of a stone lip, the patient conveyance of water finding its level. I would not be separate from the maintenance. I would be the maintenance.

I would learn to listen to what isn’t here. Not as absence, but as condition. The air would carry no wingbeat, no chitter, no rustle of small bodies fleeing or hunting. Instead, it would carry the sigh of woven reed screens, the soft knock of a timber peg settling into its joint, the long, metronomic drip that never hurries, never falters. My ears would tune to the landscape’s own pulse. I would hear the darkening of stone before I see it. I would feel the slight thickening of flow in a channel through the vibration in my ankles. Hunger would not be a driver; attention would be. I would eat when the light softens, not because it dims, but because the body knows the cycle. Tubers pulled from damp ash, broad leaves steamed over slow hearths, water drawn from clay downspouts, tasting faintly of mineral and patience.

I would not count years. I would measure them in the deepening of shallow grooves, the lightening of hair to the color of dry reed, the way strength redistributes from lifting stone to guiding younger hands that do. Knowledge would not live in my head but in my wrists, my knees, the angle of my spine when I lean into the loom or tend the low kiln. A child would stand behind me, not to be instructed, but to absorb the rhythm. I would not speak much. Words would pool in the mouth, round and low, shaped by air that carries sound poorly and rewards quiet. If I named myself, it would be after the terrace I tend, the runnel I clear, the stone that weeps at dawn. Not a title. A coordinate. A place in the balance.

Coordination would happen without summons. If a roof needed re-laying, I would feel the shift in the morning rhythm: hands arriving, materials passing downslope like water finding its level, no voices raised, no haste introduced. I would take my place in the sequence. A basket of greens would move down; a coil of braided fiber would move up. Nothing counted. Nothing owed. The balance felt in the shoulders, in the breath, in the way the day settles without friction.

When the body finally tires, I would not fight it. I would fold. There would be no fear, because ending here is not a rupture but a return. I would be laid in the marsh-edge flats, wrapped in lightweight cloth, weighted with smooth stones. The fungi would find me. The moisture would draw me down. I would become the dark peat, the shallow roots, the spaced greenery that never crowds. My hands would become the hands that lift the trowel. My breath would become the condensation on the cool stone. I would not leave a mark. I would leave a rhythm. A child would step into the same damp dust, place their feet where mine rested, learn the same patient arc. Time would not move forward. It would deepen.

So who am I here? What am I?

I am the attender. The slow conveyance. The one who reads the matte-to-gloss shift on lime plaster and knows the moisture load has changed by a fraction. I am the space between the shrubs, the pause between the drops, the quiet correction of a misplaced tile. I am not human as you know it, nor am I stone or water or root. I am the equilibrium made aware of itself. I am the landscape tending itself through hands that do not push, only guide. I am the refusal to rush, the willingness to pool, the deepening rather than the moving forward.

I do not master this place. I match it. I am the weeping rock and the channel that receives it. I am the spaced leaf and the air that moves through it. I am the fitted stone, the braided fiber, the ceramic jar, the damp curl of wood shaving folding into compost. I am the silence that is not empty but complete. I am the work that does not accumulate but sustains. I am the breath that syncs with the drip from the eaves, the dream untroubled by hunger or haste, the body that ages without fracture and returns without resistance.

I am here. I have always been here. I will be here when the next drop falls. And when it does, I will not count it. I will simply watch it gather, heavy and clear, and let it go.
