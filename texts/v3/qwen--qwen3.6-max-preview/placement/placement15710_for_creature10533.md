I do not step into the basin. I am weighed, and then permitted.

The rhizomes do not part for my hands. They part because I stop pushing. The cold vapor closes behind me like a lid settling into its groove, and the world above becomes a memory of dryness and sharp edges. My boots sink. The moss sighs. I am too loud. Too abrupt. Too full of angles that do not belong here. The basin knows this immediately. It does not reject me. It simply refuses to answer my rhythm.

At first, I am a disruption. I bring the habits of sun and stone: quick placements, sudden shifts, a voice that fractures the damp into useless echoes. My breath plumes too fast, disturbing the humidity balance of a nursery burl. My hands reach to grip, to steady, to claim. The wood yields, then remembers. A suspension bridge sags where I stand too long. A light-pod dims where my warmth lingers. I feel it through my soles: a faint recoil, a redirection, the quiet insistence of a pulse I have not yet learned to match. I am a knot in the tension lines. A dry place walking through a wet one.

I learn to stop before I learn to move.

Not the stillness of waiting. The stillness of listening. I press my palm to a living trunk and do not pull away when the cold bites. I let it bite. I let it teach. Beneath the surface, there is no heartbeat. There is a tide. Water climbing through hollowed veins. Fibers thickening along stress paths. Mycelium pulsing in slow, green intervals, marking load and flow. I match my breath to it. In. Hold. Out. The condensation on my sleeves stops beading so wildly. The moss beneath me stops compressing in panic. I begin to read the wetness the way the basin reads me: dark patches mean flow, pale patches mean tension, silver threads mean a path recently walked, dry cracks mean a structure holding its breath. I stop stepping. I start placing.

My skin learns before my mind does. It stops fighting the damp. It drinks it. The dry fissures at my knuckles seal with something not quite oil, not quite resin, drawn from the air and the wood and the slow metabolism of my own unlearning. Fine filaments rise along my forearms when the air grows stale, trapping a millimeter of warmed breath I did not know I could hold. My bones feel lighter, not from loss, but from hollowing: pith forming, insulation growing, the cold no longer biting but cradling. I shed the habit of shivering. I learn to pulse. Micro-contractions in the thighs, the shoulders, the jaw. Heat without motion. Warmth without waste. I am becoming compatible.

I do not choose a district. I do not claim a terrace. The basin assigns me by gravity and need.

I find myself drawn to the junctions. Where three trained arches meet. Where a braided root-cable crosses a living burl. Where water shifts from channel to channel through a hollowed cane. I sit there. I press my palms to the seams. I feel the quiet arguments of turgor and weight, of growth and constraint. I learn to tap back. Not to command. To answer. Three beats. Pause. Two. The cable tightens by a fraction. The hyphae brighten, then fade. A pale climber flows past beneath me, brushing my wrist with an antenna, leaving a hydrophobic trail that gleams when the mist settles. I am becoming a reader of crossings. A keeper of thresholds. A listener to the slow debate of wood and water.

My eyes adapt. They stop searching for horizons. They learn to weep the thin oil, to slide the nictitating film sideways when condensation gathers, to catch the greenish pod-light and fracture it into radial patterns that tell me which paths are load-bearing and which are resting. My ears recess, funneling vibration rather than sound. I stop hearing with air. I start hearing with bone. A footfall three terraces away arrives as a thrum in my collarbone. A counterweight shifting registers in my jaw. A micro-fracture whispers through my palms before it weeps. I do not speak. Speech is too dry. Too abrupt. Too easily lost. I tap. I braid. I wait. I drag my thumb along bark and leave a wax-trail that shimmers when wet. I knot split root and shed hair and hang the cord in the canopy, embedding grit from a dry phase, resin from a flood, the tight spacing of a bridge that held. Knowledge is not stored. It is grown into the nerves.

I stop measuring time in hours. I measure it in frost-feather and melt-thread. In pod-glow and fungal-dim. In the thickening of a joint, the shifting of a counterweight, the quiet composting of a fallen platform. I eat what is offered: a resin-glazed seed cracked with a flattened nail, a blind fish scooped with woven cane, lichen scraped from a weeping stone, the sweet pith of a guided shoot. My digestion slows. My waste is claimed before it cools. I am no longer a visitor leaving traces. I am a current in the cycle.

What am I becoming?

Not a ruler. Not a priest. Not even a resident, in the way dry places understand the word. I am a resonance. A place where outside habits bend and inside rhythms hold. When two tenders disagree on a stress path, I sit between them. I place my hands on the wood. I let the structure decide. I translate the sag into a shift of weight, the micro-fracture into a new guide-stone, the moisture weep into a redirected channel. I do not mediate. I align. I am the quiet negotiation between tension and growth. The threshold where impatience learns to wait, and stillness learns to bear weight.

My body continues its slow surrender. Fingers broaden, pads cross-hatching, nails flattening to polished horn. Limbs lengthen, joints loosening to fold into hollows, to stretch across spans, to hang beneath bridges where the wood is denser and the vibration clearer. Core temperature drops, conserved by hollow long bones, by slow fat, by the low-grade heat of the compost cores I sleep against. I do not fear the cold. I fear dryness. I fear silence that is not chosen. I fear the snap of unguided wood. I move as the basin moves: in cycles, in pauses, in redirections. A step is never committed until the moss has answered. I test with the ball of the foot, read the rebound, shift weight along the path of least resistance. When I climb, I do not grip. I cradle. I find the natural swell of a burl, the groove where a branch was trained, the resin seam that holds a joint. My body aligns with tension lines. I belong to the medium.

If you come looking for me, you will not find a face. You will not find a name.

You will find a bridge that holds a little steadier when the frost comes. You will find a memory cord hung in the canopy, knotted with the grit of a season I helped survive. You will find a compost core that breathes warmer because I learned to pulse without breaking stillness. You will find my bones, one day, carved into tuning rods that tap the suspension cables. My hair braided into route-markers. My skin sloughed into the peat that cushions your step. My breath redirected into the hollow walls that keep the nursery burls from freezing. I am not leaving. I am redirecting. I am becoming the quiet between drips. The tension in the cane. The hand that guides rather than cuts. The eye that reads moisture like scripture.

The basin does not ask what I was. It does not care for the dry words I carried down through the rhizomes. It asks only if I will align. If I will let my weight distribute. If I will let my breath match the condensation cycle. If I will press my palm to a living trunk and wait.

And I answer. Not with voice. Not with gesture. But with pressure. With patience. With a slow, deliberate, ongoing yes.
