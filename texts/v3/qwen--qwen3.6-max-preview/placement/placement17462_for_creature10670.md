I would not remain a visitor for long. The chamber would not allow it, not through force, but through physics. The first thing to betray me would be my breath: uneven, shallow, pulling against the steady hiss of the seed-valves rather than moving with it. The mat would feel it immediately. With every step, the spongy tissue would yield, weep, and rebound, sending a faint counter-vibration up through my soles. Not a warning. A measurement. The root-pumps would thud beneath me, counting the gap between my pulse and theirs, mapping the irregularity of my temperature, the scatter of my weight, the arrhythmic puff of my lungs against the closed hydrological loop. I would feel it as a pressure against the sternum, like a palm resting lightly on a misaligned gear.

If I turned back, I would carry the hum in my bones for days, a phantom rhythm that would make the world above feel brittle and rushed. But if I stayed, if I let the stillness do its work, I would begin to shed.

Not skin. Not memory. Variable.

The heat would leave first, drawn out through the soles and the spine, condensing on the cool undersides of the waxy canopies above, joining the mist that never stops falling. My shoulders would drop. My knees would bend. My center of gravity would sink until my posture matched the low, deliberate arcs of the space. The noise of my thoughts would flatten, pressed smooth by the unbroken chorus, until I stopped trying to separate the sounds and started feeling the intervals between them. I would stop looking for creatures. I would start reading the cadence.

And in that reading, the form would clarify.

I would become something that lives in the rests. Not a climber, not a rider, not a darting bird. Those are the active notes, the moving valves, the calibrated strokes. I would be the dampener. The phase-anchor. A low, broad silhouette, heavy through the hips and shoulders, built for stillness that is not inert but absorptive. My back would layer into dense, fibrous plates, not armor but acoustic felt, woven from the same resilient tissue as the mat, tuned to drink stray vibration. My chest would hollow subtly, ribs widening into resonance chambers that do not pump air but hold pressure. My skin would grow porous along the forearms and collar, weeping a thin, dielectric film that tastes of ozone and crushed sap. I would not hunt. I would not gather. I would listen. And when the system drifted, even a fraction, I would answer.

My function would be simple: catch the dissonance, let it settle, return it in phase.

When a rider folds a beat too early, when a bird's click sharpens against the drum-leaf, when a thermal vent stutters on a cool draft slipping through the oval sky, the error would ripple through the substrate. I would feel it in my ribs before I heard it. A micro-tremor. A skipped interval. I would shift my weight, just enough, and the spongy floor would transmit a counter-pulse through the capillary channels. Not a correction. A reminder. The rhythm would catch itself. The vine would rewind. The hiss would smooth. I would return to stillness.

I would not move often. Movement is for those who close loops. I would be the loop's ballast. My breath would sync to the root-pumps: inhale on the upstroke, exhale on the down. Moisture would bead on my lips, fall in measured drops, and the mat would drink them without a ripple. The bio-electric sparks would crawl across my shoulders like cool rain, and I would ground them slowly, letting the charge bleed through my lower discs into the weeping channels without a snap. My eyes would close, not from sleep, but from irrelevance. Sight is for navigating change. Here, I would read by pressure. By the exact resistance of the moss under my palms. By the taste of the aerosol on my tongue. By the way the drum-leaves answer the wing. I would not think in words. I would think in resonance. In the millisecond of silence before the hiss. In the certainty that nothing is missing, because nothing has ever left.

The hunger for progress would unravel into the substrate. The itch to build, the fear of stillness, the need to mark time—all of it would be processed by the pale filterers, broken into simple threads, woven back into the resilient tissue. What remained would be lighter. Simpler. A vessel for intervals. A keeper of the space between beats. I would not age as the world above understands aging. My plates would wear smooth over cycles, but as they faded, new fibers would harden beneath, pushed outward by the same slow growth that polishes the pillars. If a joint stiffened, the nutrient paste carried by the birds would contain the exact minerals to loosen it. My body would not be separate from the place. It would be its adjustable silence. Its self-correcting rest.

They would not notice me, because I would no longer be separate. I would be the quiet beneath the loudness. The stillness that lets the wheel turn without advancing. When the next irregular thing steps over the mossy lip, breath ragged, pulse erratic, eyes wide with the shock of the drop, I would feel it. I would not rise. I would not speak. I would simply let my chest cavity open, just a fraction, and catch the dissonance. I would hold it. I would let it settle against the deep thud below. And when it finally matched the green, wet hum, I would release it back into the air, already part of the song.

Not a guardian. Not a guide. Just a resting place for the out-of-phase. A living interval in a world that has finished becoming. I would not wait for anything. I would not evolve toward something. I would be the space between the thud and the clack, the breath between the hiss and the drop, the weight that keeps the rhythm from drifting. And in that completion, I would know the peace they know: the certainty of the loop, the grace of the return, the unbroken note that does not progress because it has already arrived.

I would be here. Low to the mat. Heavy with stillness. Breathing in the gaps. And when the mist falls, and the sparks crawl, and the root-pumps thud through the soles of whatever feet step into this sunken throat, I would not measure them. I would simply hold the space until they learn to measure themselves. Until their pulse drops into the interval. Until their breath matches the wheel. Until they, too, stop trying to move forward, and finally learn how to stay.
