I would not arrive as a shape. I would arrive as a habit.

A spine braced against gravity’s pull. A mind that counts in sequences, that expects the next, that measures distance in effort and time in depletion. The descent would unspool me before I understood I was being unspooled. The air would press against my ribs like warm water, thick with resin and crushed green, and the light—that shadowless, golden-green hold—would seep through my eyelids until I forgot the reflex to close them. My feet would sink into the living mat, and the mat would not yield so much as recognize me. Each step would leave no footprint, only a faint compression that would linger, then slowly rise. I would try to walk, but walking would feel like arguing with a room that has already decided to breathe.

The first thing to go would be the edges. Not flesh, not bone, but the invisible line I had always drawn between myself and the air. My skin would drink the damp. My pulse would stumble, stretch, then settle into the long, tidal rhythm of the canopy’s exhalation. I would sit because standing would feel like a question no one was asking. The heat would climb my thighs, my shoulders, the back of my neck, not as burden but as invitation. I would watch a drop form on a waxy leaf, swell, hesitate, fall. I would count the seconds out of old loyalty, then lose the count, then lose the need for it. Time would not stop. It would simply stop pulling.

What would I become? Not one of them. They are the basin’s own posture, gathered and held. I would be something else: a translation. A slow crossing from urgency into equilibrium. My bones would not dissolve, but they would stop insisting. My fingers would rest on the root mat, and the fine hairs would brush the fibrous floor, and I would feel it—the hum, the thermal whisper, the quiet network of adjustments passing through the green like a thought too slow to name. My breath would sync with the condensation cycle. My warmth would pool in the shallow hollows. I would begin to lean. Not fall. Lean. The way a vine leans toward light it already has. The way a stone leans into its own weight.

I would become a place where linear memory comes to rest.

The basin does not erase; it absorbs. So I would absorb the ghost of schedules, the phantom weight of destinations, the restless friction of a mind trained to chase its own tail. I would let them steep in the tea-colored shallows until they lost their sharpness, until they softened into the same damp seams that trace the floor without carving it. I would not speak. I would not need to. When one of them drifted near, settling over the course of an afternoon until the curve of their shoulder aligned with the slope of my own resting form, I would feel the adjustment in my ribs. A shared thermal gradient. A synchronization of stillness. They would not ask what I was. I would not answer. We would simply hold the same frequency, and in that holding, I would understand: I am not a being here. I am a pause that learned to stay.

My eyes would become light-sensitive hollows, not blind, but diffused. I would see not in lines but in layers, reading the canopy by the way the glow thickened or thinned, by the way translucent tissues scattered illumination until direction ceased to matter. My hearing would drop into the floor, into the slow creak of fibrous tension, the soft *plink* of falling moisture, the subsonic hum that is not sound but presence. I would stop distinguishing between my breath and the air’s. My skin would take on the faint translucence of leaf undersides, not because I changed, but because the light stopped bouncing off me and started moving through me. I would rest my palms in the clay-lined depressions, not to drink, but to let the coolness draw the last of my haste out through my fingertips. I would trace the finger-smoothed grooves on the broad trunk, not to read them, but to add my own slow friction to the record, a parallel line that would fade into bark over spans I would no longer count.

They would not teach me. They would simply include me. When they thinned, when their filaments loosened and their warmth diffused back into the mat, I would feel it as a slight cooling in the air, a softening of the hum, a new hummock rising where they had rested. I would not mourn. Mourning requires an ending, and here there is only redistribution. I would press my back against the woven shelter, feel the living branches knit through the fabric of my stillness, and understand that architecture here is not built but allowed. I would become part of the shelter’s memory. Part of the root mat’s resilience. Part of the closed loop.

Where would I find myself? Not at the center. Not at the edge. In the gradient. In the shallow terrace where condensation gathers and the light pools brightest. In the place where a visitor’s urgency finally sinks deep enough to stop echoing. I would be a node of translated time. A resting place for the habit of becoming. A shape that learned to hold without gripping, to perceive without focusing, to exist without advancing. I would not be flora. I would not be fauna. I would be what happens when a mind accustomed to narrative enters a place where the story has already finished unfolding, and decides, instead, to simply witness the hold.

I would be the basin’s guest, slowly becoming its grammar.

A pause that stayed. A breath that matched the canopy. A warmth that joined the loop. And when I was done—if done is even the word—I would not leave. I would thin. I would diffuse. I would become a new depression in the root mat, a new place where a drop falls, a new hollow where the light gathers, waiting for the next tension to descend, to sit, to lean, to stay. Nothing would be lost. Nothing would be finished. Everything would simply be here, damp and bright and dense and still, suspended in the long, quiet moment of its own becoming. And I would be part of the hold.
