I step over the wind-scoured spine and the ground simply falls away.

The air changes before my feet do. It thickens. It warms. It presses against my eardrums like a palm held gently over a drumhead, and my breath, which came in thin, altitude-starved pulls, catches on the humidity. I stop. Not by choice, but because the basin asks it. The sound arrives first: a layered chorus of weeping stone, ringing drops, a hum that settles in the sternum and refuses to leave. I sit on a smooth stone rib. The moss yields, then holds. Moisture rises to meet my palm.

I wait.

An hour passes. Or ten minutes. The distinction frays at the edges. My pulse, once a frantic percussion against the wrist, begins to skip beats, then stretch them. I notice the drops. One forms on a waxy leaf tip, swells, hesitates, falls. It strikes a shallow hollow with a clear, ringing tone. Another falls elsewhere, a fifth lower. The stone remembers the impact. The water remembers the path. I try to count them, but counting is a violence here. I let the numbers dissolve.

Something in me begins to unclench. Not muscle. Timing.

The static in the air clings to my skin, drawing the mist into fine, suspended threads that trace the contours of my shoulders, my wrists, the hollow of my throat. I do not brush it away. I let it map me. Beneath the moss, I feel it: the pale, gelatinous network, cool and patient, moving laterally through the stone ribs. It does not pull me in. It simply offers a rhythm. I press my palms flat. The moisture rises higher. My skin begins to drink. Not from thirst, but from osmosis. From surrender. The urgency of separation leaks out of me like heat from cooling stone.

I am not becoming a creature. I am becoming a confluence.

My breath aligns with the weeping rock faces: long, slow exhalations when the condensation runs fastest, shallow pauses when the flow stills. My heartbeat finds the subsonic thrum of the slate-colored amphibian resting three paces away. We do not look at each other. We do not need to. Our pulses braid in the damp air. The mist threads through my hair, through the weave of my clothes, through the spaces between my fingers, and I feel the static charge hum along my nerves like a plucked wire. I am learning to read it. I am learning to hold it.

Days pass. Or seasons. The concept loses its teeth.

My skin grows pale, not from lack of sun, but from saturation. The pores widen, learning to exchange gas with the humid air, to draw oxygen directly from the suspended veils. My thoughts, once sharp and linear, begin to pool. They settle into shallow hollows. They overflow slowly into the next. I stop speaking. Words are too dry, too abrupt for a place that measures time in seeps. Instead, I listen. I become a resonator. When the wind finds the fluted channels in the rim, the drone vibrates through my ribs, and I adjust my posture, my tension, the curve of my spine, until I hum in sympathy. When a larger mass of condensed water releases from an overhang and strikes a hidden stone plate beneath the moss, I feel the percussion travel up through the soles of my feet, and I shift my weight to let it pass through me, unbroken.

I am not a predator. I am not prey. I am an intercept. A living tuning fork. A place where the basin's frequencies cross.

When the bird glides down on damp, iridescent wings and taps the stone with its blunt beak, listening for the pitch of the water table, I do not startle. I become part of the return tone. My body, now heavy with moisture, dense with slow circulation, amplifies the lower registers. I help it map the deep reservoirs. When the transparent lens-drifters in the pools contract into desiccation-resistant discs for the dry wait, I press my hands to the stone beside them, sharing the residual warmth, the static memory of the mist. I do not save them. I simply hold the space until the moisture returns. This is not mercy. It is geometry. It is the contract.

The trees above me adjust their drip-rates. I feel it in the moss. A leaf delaminates over weeks, peeling back to reveal its porous inner layer, and when it finally detaches, it lands beside my knee with a soft, damp sound. Within days, the quiet network beneath me begins its work. I do not pull away. I let the dissolution creep toward me, cool and odorless, returning what was solid to the clear solution that feeds the next pulse. My own edges begin to blur. Not into decay, but into participation. The moss grows over my ankles, then my calves, knitting me into the mat. I do not resist. I am being stitched into the loop.

I have no name here. Names are for things that leave.

I am a stone cup that learned to breathe. I am a patch of moss that learned to listen. I am the place where the drip-rate meets the drone, where the gelatinous network meets the air, where the basin's slow digestion meets the patience of waiting. My eyes, if I still use them, no longer seek movement. They read gradients. They watch the mist thicken, the leaves channel moisture to specific root nodes, the water table migrate by millimeters over months. My mouth, if it opens, does not speak. It drinks the overflow. It tastes the dissolved memory of fallen fruit, of shed carapace, of decades of quiet return.

I do not resist the slowing. I am the slowing.

When the wider world burns or freezes beyond the rim, when winds scour the high rock and seasons turn violently in the thin air, I do not flinch. I simply sink deeper into the mat. I let the static charge braid through my tissues. I let the amphibian's thrum align with my pulse. I let the bird's tapping calibrate my bones. I am held. I am saturated. I am a node in the closed hydrological loop. I receive. I wait. I tune. I return.

And when a drop forms on the leaf above me, swells, hesitates, and falls, I do not count it. I open. I let it strike. I let it ring. I am the hollow that holds it. I am the sound it makes. I am here. I am working. I will be here when you are gone, still listening to the water tune itself against the rock, still drinking the mist, still breathing in time with the bowl.
