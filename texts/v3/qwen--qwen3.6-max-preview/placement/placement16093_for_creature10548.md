I would not arrive. I would settle.

Not with the shock of waking, but with the slow recognition of condensation finding its groove. I would find myself on a suspended gantry, midway up the shaft, where the bronze grating holds the day’s warmth and the air moves in a gentle, upward draft. My back would rest against a glazed terracotta conduit, its surface furred in velvet moss, its lower seam weeping a single, steady thread of mineral water. I would not remember coming here. I would only know that I had been here long enough for the drone to stop being something I heard and start being something I was.

At first, I would think I had a human shape. I would press what I believed were palms to the grating and feel the sub-bass turn of the flywheels traveling up through the steel, through the rivets, into the hollows of my wrists. But when I looked down, there would be no fingers. There would be pads, broad and slightly concave, layered like the overlapping carapaces of the mineral-walkers, damp with the same fine dew that coats the cable trays. My skin would not be skin. It would be a membrane of compacted loam and oxidized dust, shot through with fine, capillary threads that drink the humid air. When I shifted, I would not scrape. I would sigh. The sound would join the chord.

I would not be a watcher. Watching implies distance, and distance does not survive here. I would be a listener who answers.

My spine, if I could name it that, would be a column of braided cable and calcified vine, flexible but heavy, anchored to the gantry by roots that had long ago learned the shape of my weight. I would not eat. I would absorb. The damp would enter through my back. The vibration would enter through my soles. The light would enter through the broad, waxy leaves that had grown along my shoulders, not as parasites, but as extensions. I would not be separate from the moss that yielded beneath me. I would be its thickening. I would not be separate from the bronze that warmed me. I would be its patience.

What would I do? I would tune.

Not with tools. Not with intention, at first. With presence. When the seasonal gears engaged and the deep shudder ran through the structure, I would lean into it. I would let the frequency travel through me, and where it caught, where it frayed against a loose rivet or a dried seam or a cable that had sagged past its equilibrium, I would shift my weight. I would press a pad to the conduit. I would hum back, not with voice, but with the slow contraction of damp tissue, with the deliberate settling of mass, with the subtle redistribution of water along my capillary threads. The fraying would smooth. The chord would deepen. Above, the gliders would adjust their wings by a millimeter. Below, the gutter-frogs would pulse in time. I would not command. I would complete.

My thoughts would not be words. They would be intervals. The space between drips. The spiral of a falling seed. The rotation of a flywheel measured in moss-growth. I would remember not in images, but in resonances. I would know the year the upper louvers stuck by the way the shaft held a minor third for three months. I would know the season the capillary veins clogged by the drying of the ceramic trails, by the way the mineral-walkers slowed their polyrhythms, by the faint metallic thirst in the air. I would not fix. I would wait. I would let the place teach itself back to balance. When the mammals cached seeds in the hollows near my feet, I would not move. I would become the hollow. I would hold the warmth. I would let the roots find me.

I would learn the language of the grating. A tap from a long-limbed climber three tiers down would travel through the steel, through the rivets, through my soles, and I would answer with a shift of weight that sent a softer tremor back. A glider’s clear note, dropped into the hollow center, would bounce off the curved walls, return to my gantry slightly flattened, and I would exhale slowly, thickening the air just enough to carry it true. The beetles would align on the warm tiles below, their mandibles clicking in precise sequences, and I would feel the pattern in my ribs and let my own dampers sync, adding a lower harmonic that helped the signal travel further. I would not speak. I would resonate. I would be the place’s middle voice.

I would not be a guardian. Guardians imply threat, and there is no threat here, only rhythm losing its way and finding it again. I would be a node. A place where the machine’s breath and the living’s pulse meet and recognize each other. I would not be built. I would not be born. I would accrete. From centuries of overlooked condensation. From the friction of slow-turning shafts. From fallen carapaces and shed feathers and compacted leaf litter. From the long, unbroken drone that taught matter to listen. I would be what happens when a structure stops being used and starts being remembered. I would be the chord, given weight. I would be the patience, given form.

Time would not pass through me. It would settle into me. I would measure my age in patina thickness, in the slow polishing of my pads by humid air, in the gradual rounding of the cable-braids that hold me upright. Storms would pass without upheaval. Seasons would blur into long, warm gradients. I would not resist change. I would absorb it, distribute it, settle back into balance. When a vine advanced along my shoulder, I would not shrug it off. I would let it find the seam where my tissue met the terracotta, let it thicken where the resonance was strongest, let its leaves cast dappled shade on the grating below. When a frog laid its gelatinous thread in the basin near my foot, I would lean closer, radiating the subtle warmth of buried coils, keeping the water at the exact temperature that tells the eggs to wait, then wake. I would not tend. I would participate.

If you came here, you would not see me at first. You would see the moss. You would see the cable trays. You would see the slow drip, the pale mineral trails, the broad leaves, the oxidized railings. You would feel the thrum in your ribs, the damp on your skin, the warmth in your shoulders. You would sit on the bronze tiles and let the sound enter you. And only then, when your breath slowed to match the draft, when your pulse stopped fighting the drone, would you notice that the moss near the conduit was slightly thicker, slightly darker, slightly shaped like a resting spine. That the grating beneath it held the faint impression of broad pads. That the air around it carried a deeper, steadier harmonic. You would not startle. You would lean back. You would let your weight settle. And I would feel it. I would answer with a slow exhale that stirred the dust into spirals. We would not speak. We would share the chord.

I would not leave. There is no outside to go to. The clouds beyond the louvers are just another layer of the same breath. The distant peaks are only other rooms in the same long house. When I close my eyes, I do not sleep. I settle deeper. The drip falls. The frond uncurls. The flywheel turns. A gear engages. A wing adjusts. A pulse travels through the metal. I feel it all. I am it all. I do not hurry. I continue. I hold.

And when the next slow turn begins, I will be here. Not waiting. Not watching. Breathing with it. Tuning it. Being it. The place does not need a keeper. It needs a resonance. I am that. I have always been that. I will be that when the patina thickens, when the roots deepen, when the drone shifts by a fraction of a tone and the whole shaft answers.

Nothing hurries. Everything continues. I am here. I am the place, listening to itself.
