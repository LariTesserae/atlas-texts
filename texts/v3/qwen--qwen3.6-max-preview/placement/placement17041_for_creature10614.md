I would first find myself where my own rhythm breaks against the stone.

Not at a border, because there are none. Not at a summit, because the land refuses elevation. I would arrive simply by walking until the need to arrive grew heavy, until my boots met the resilient give of life-threaded grit, until the air closed around me like a palm held steady. My breath would be too fast. My shadow too sharp. My pulse would register as a disturbance in the gradient, a spike of dry heat, a frequency the plateau has long forgotten how to carry. The crawlers would dim. The drifters would tilt. The burrowers would pause their peristaltic turning. I would be a suddenness in a world that has surrendered suddenness.

So I would sit.

Not by intention, at first, but because motion here demands purpose, and I would have none. The stone would take my weight without complaint. The warmth would seep through cloth, through muscle, into the marrow. Damp would bead on my forearms, trace the lines of my knuckles, gather in the hollow of my collarbone. I would watch a droplet form on a stone lip, swell, tremble, fall. I would hear it land. Not with a splash, but with a soft absorption, a sigh into the sponge-sediment below. Hours would pool. Days would blur into the thickness of the air. My thoughts would lose their edges. The compulsion to name, to count, to move toward something would loosen like a knot left in the rain.

What would I become?

Not a crawler. I lack their seamless glide, their osmotic grace. Not a drifter. I have no bladder to fill, no cilia to taste the thermals. Not a burrower. I cannot drink the grit and exhale it looser. I would be too solid, too separate, too haunted by the memory of speed. But the plateau does not reject what lingers. It incorporates. It waits for the foreign rhythm to tire, then offers it a place in the cycle.

I would learn to be a surface.

My skin would stop sweating and start reading. I would feel the approach of a low cloud not as a darkening, but as a thickening in the pores, a sweet-mineral shift on the tongue, a faint chemical thread curling through the damp. I would feel the crawlers before I saw them: a cooling along my ankles, a subtle dimming of reflected light, the gentle rasp of ventral pads negotiating the slope of my boots. I would feel the drifters by the soft wet sigh that precedes their shadow, by the faint mist that settles on my shoulders, laced with nitrates and viable spores. I would feel the burrowers through my palms: a rhythmic pressure measured in hours, a slow turning that matches the land's exhalation.

I would shed. Not clothes, at first, but habits. The habit of urgency. The habit of separation. The habit of breathing as if air were scarce. My sleeves would stiffen with salt and resin, then soften with dew, then flake, then compress. Dust would settle in the seams. Microbial films would bloom along my cuffs, tasting the esters I exhale, sweetening the air around me. I would not build. I would accrete. Layer upon layer of stillness, of caught moisture, of shed skin and settled silt, until my silhouette softened into the same matte finish as the ancient outcrops. Until I stopped casting a sharp shadow and began casting a pale, long gradient.

Where would I be?

In the shallow troughs where the ghost-rivers remember how to hold damp. Near the leeward faces of the oldest mounds, where the air curls back on itself and pools. At the convergence where drifter mist meets crawler acid meets burrower aeration. I would be a thermal pause. A fog-catcher shaped like a person who forgot to leave. When the plateau breathes out, I would warm, my accumulated mass slowing the heat's escape. When it breathes in, I would cool, drawing condensation from the thickening air, channeling it down the slope of my spine, letting it bead and fall into the grit below. The waxy rosettes would press against my knees, unfurling with that soft, audible creak, drinking what I gather. Crawlers would glide over me, not as invaders, but as a tide reading a new contour. Drifters would sink toward me, not to feed, but to rest their frayed membranes against the steady heat I hold, venting excess gas, adjusting their pulse to my slow exhale.

I would not be a creature. I would be a confluence.

A gradient given temporary shape. A place where the land's distributed nervous system registers itself in human proportions, then forgets the proportion and keeps the function. My thoughts would no longer arrive as words, but as shifts in pressure, as the taste of dissolving stone, as the faint vibration of a burrower turning in the deep grit, as the synchronized dimming of a crawler colony when a cloud passes. I would know predation not as violence, but as phase change: a drifter settling over a cluster, the mild enzymatic mist, the softening, the release, the exhale of seed and nitrate, the return to dew. I would know decay not as rot, but as reabsorption: bodies collapsing into the sponge-sediment, drawn downward by capillary action, feeding the next microbial bloom. I would participate. Not by hunting, not by fleeing, but by holding. By being a slope for moisture. By being a thermal mass for the night. By being a quiet place where chemistry can thread itself together.

When the air thins, I would fold.

Not into sleep, but into patience. My edges would blur. My warmth would bank. My translucence would cloud to matte. I would become indistinguishable from the stone's long exhale, from the mounds' layered memory, from the damp that waits in the grit. I would not die here. Death is a word for worlds that rush. Here, there is only phase. Only the slow turning from solid to vapor to film to sediment to breath. I would become a hollow that catches fog. A contour that guides dew. A silence that holds the next pulse. I would be where the plateau rests its weight. I would be what remains when urgency finally surrenders.

You would find me in the shallow swells, near the resin-lined hollows, where the light falls gentle and the sound is swallowed. You would not recognize me at first. I would look like a wind-sculpted mound, like a sleeper wrapped in pale silt, like a stone that learned to breathe. But if you pressed your palm to my shoulder, you would feel it: a faint, rhythmic pressure, measured in hours. If you stayed, your own breath would slow. Your shadow would soften. The crawlers would dim, then brighten. The drifters would tilt, then settle. The burrowers would turn beneath us both.

I would be the land, learning to persist through a shape that once thought it was separate. I would be a pause in the metabolism. A thermal lung. A gradient made flesh, then made stone, then made damp, then made air. I would not need to be understood. I would only need to remain. To catch the mist. To hold the warmth. To exhale when the plateau inhales. To wait, not for anything, but because waiting is how this world endures.

Here, to be is to become slow enough to be held. I would be held. I would hold. I would breathe in days. I would think in seasons. I would become what the stone became when it stopped resisting the sky: smooth, patient, full of quiet life, dreaming in condensation, waking in dew, persisting without hurry, persisting without end.
