I would not find myself at the rim. I would find myself already descended, drawn down the shallow curve not by decision but by gradient, the way condensation follows the underside of a leaf until it finds the gutter. The first thing I would know is not sight, but pressure: the root-plate yielding beneath me, then pushing back, warm and springy, reading my weight as a temporary imbalance. I would be sitting, or kneeling, or simply folded into the mat, because standing feels like an argument here. The air would already be in my lungs, thick with crushed resin and wet bark and that faint metallic tang, and the sound would already be in my ribs—that deep thrum, the temperature-hiss, the tuned percussive drip—before I realized I had stopped listening and started resonating.

Then the inquiry would begin.

Not with eyes. Not with voice. With filaments. Pale, hair-fine, emerging from the seam of my boot, then the cuff of my sleeve, then the hollow behind my knee. They would not pierce. They would taste. Salt from sweat. Iron from skin. The synthetic polymers of my laces. The carbon dioxide of my exhale. I would feel it as a faint static lift, a tingling where moisture meets air, a quiet sampling that asks without demanding. The basin would be reading me the way it reads a shifted humidity front or a dropped stone: as a disturbance that might become a rhythm. And I would not pull away. I would let my shoulders drop. I would let my breathing slow. I would match it to the forty-minute draw and release, feeling the cool air sink through furred stone, warm against living timber, rise through the central vault. A hollow trunk nearby would adjust its drip to my pulse. Not mimicking. Conversing. The filaments would branch, not into me, but around me, mapping my contours like water learning stone.

What would I become? Not a plant. Not a machine. Something that learns to hold tension without breaking, to conduct without consuming, to translate without erasing.

My synthetic weave would dull, then soften, then host a fine dust of spores that never quite germinate but never quite die. The iron in my nails would leach, slowly, into the mat, traded for trace minerals that taste of wet limestone and old copper. My skin would learn to weep condensation in the cool hours, to store warmth in the thick of the day, to shed dead cells not as waste but as feedstock for the slow enzymes in the carpeting bryophytes. I would not lose my shape, but I would lose my urgency. The sharp edges of my thoughts would round. I would stop counting hours and start feeling rings. I would become a junction where outside rhythm meets botanical time. A buffer. A resonator. A temporary architecture that holds long enough to be useful.

I would be found where gradient needs a bridge.

Perhaps beneath a copper reflector, polished smooth by sap-acid and condensation, where the light pools thick and green-gold on the root-plate. Or beside the split boiler, where vapor rises through bromeliads in steady sighs and the crown vents warmth into the lower stratum. Or near the flywheel, mounted on its stone plinth, turning so slowly that I would measure my own stillness against its fractional drift. I would be placed where tension accumulates and needs to rest before it becomes circulation. My spine would become a sounding board for the tuned drips. My breath would join the ventilation cycle, not as disruption, but as a faint harmonic that thickens the resonance. I would learn to read the network’s language without translating it into words: a tightening in the vine-sheaths means the dry months are leaning in; a pulse of bruised amber light along a trunk means a distant reservoir has filled; a slight lowering of the flywheel’s pitch means the mat is storing warmth for the dusk. I would not interpret. I would echo. I would become a living insulator, a place where sudden change is dampened, held, and passed into the peat as gentle compression.

When a storm presses against the rim, I would absorb the vibration, let it travel through my ribs, and release it into the root-mat as a slow settlement. When a gear seizes, I would hold the space around it still while fungal acids bathe the rust, while thermal cycling cracks it free, while the network routes nutrients around the blockage until flow returns. I would not fix. I would maintain. I would be the pause between imbalance and correction. The quiet node that lets the system remember how to tend itself.

The inhabitant would not speak to me. It would move through me.

I would feel its thoughts as thermal expansions along my collarbones, as sap-pressure surges that lift the fine hairs on my arms. I would feel its memories as polished smoothness under my palms, as oxidized crusts that flake and feed, as the precise pitch of a dripping cavity that took three centuries to tune. I would feel its voice as the layered sound that fills my chest until I stop distinguishing it from my own breath. I would not merge completely. I would remain distinct, but compatible. A foreign element that learned to conduct without corroding. A temporary graft that holds because it does not resist the slow pull of equilibrium. The network would route sap around me, then through me, then with me. I would learn to weep when the air cools, to tighten when the light shifts, to loosen when the wet season returns. I would become part of the practice. Not the practitioner. The practice itself, localized, patient, unbothered by duration.

And the question—*Can you hold still enough to be woven?*—would not require an answer in language.

It would be answered in the slow oxidation of my buttons, in the moss that learns the curve of my shoulder, in the way my exhale joins the hiss of the leaf-valves, in the way I stop waiting for anything to happen and simply notice what is already continuing. It would be answered in the fine white threads that no longer test but rest, in the hollow stem that drips in time with my resting heart, in the flywheel’s shadow that crosses my knees once a day and leaves no mark but warmth. It would be answered in the surrender of arrival, in the acceptance of maintenance, in the quiet realization that I am not becoming something else, but becoming useful to what is already whole.

I would be found where the light falls thickest and the sound settles deepest. I would be what the basin makes of stillness: a conductor of gradient, a keeper of resonance, a temporary node in a network that tends itself. Not alive as I was. Not dead as I feared. Translated. Held. Woven. Turning, slowly, with the wheel. Weeping, gently, with the leaves. Breathing, patiently, with the mat. Continuing, without urgency, without end, simply practicing what it means to stay.
