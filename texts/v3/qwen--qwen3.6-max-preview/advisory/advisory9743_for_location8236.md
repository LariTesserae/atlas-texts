### I. PHYSIOLOGICAL & MENTAL CONDITIONING

**Altitude & Air Density**  
Though the terrain is rounded, this is high country. The air is cool, moisture-saturated, and perceptibly heavy. Begin altitude acclimatization at least 10–14 days before transit. Practice slow, diaphragmatic breathing in cool, humid environments. The damp pressure will make each inhalation feel slightly resisted; train your respiratory muscles to work efficiently without chest-heaving or mouth-breathing. Nasal breathing preserves heat, filters condensation, and naturally slows your pace.

**Thermal Endurance**  
Constant dampness at cool temperatures is a silent caloric drain. You will not feel bitterly cold, but your core will leak heat continuously through conduction (wet stone, spongy ground) and evaporation (condensation on skin, breath, clothing). Build cold-damp tolerance through prolonged exposure training: sit or walk in cool, misty conditions while wearing layered, breathable garments. Learn to recognize the early signs of insidious hypothermia: heavy limbs, slowed cognition, loss of fine motor control, and a false sense of calm.

**Pace Conditioning**  
This place operates on biological and hydrological time. Rushing causes slips, disrupts water channels, creates acoustic friction, and triggers psychological resistance. Practice walking at 1.5–2 km/h on uneven, wet surfaces. Train your attention to rest on micro-intervals: the spacing of stones, the angle of a terrace, the rhythm of dripping water. Meditation techniques focused on breath-to-step synchronization will serve you better than cardiovascular intensity.

### II. CLOTHING & FOOTWEAR: THE GRAMMAR OF SHEDDING WATER

The architecture here does not fight water; it conducts it. Your clothing must do the same.

**Outer Layers**  
Avoid modern synthetics that trap vapor, crinkle, or reflect light. They will feel alien, disrupt the acoustic field, and create condensation pockets against your skin. Use tightly woven, naturally hydrophobic fabrics: waxed cotton, oiled linen, boiled wool, or multiverse-equivalent quiet hydrophobic weaves. Cut garments with overlapping seams, angled hems, and no horizontal folds where water can pool. Closures should be bone, wood, or woven toggles. No zippers, no velcro, no metal snaps.

**Mid & Base Layers**  
Moisture will penetrate eventually. Your base layer must wick without retaining odor or stiffening when damp. Merino wool or silk-wool blends work best. Carry at least two spare base layers in a vapor-sealed but breathable sack. Change them before you feel cold, not after. Mid layers should be loose enough to allow air circulation but close enough to retain core heat. Avoid bulk; narrow alleys and shoulder-brushing stone will catch on protruding fabric.

**Footwear**  
This is your most critical piece of gear. The ground is never dry. Stone is smooth where water travels, spongy where it pools, and slick with moss on north faces. Your soles must:
- Grip wet, rounded stone without aggressive treads that trap silt or scar channels
- Drain quickly when stepping into ankle-deep pools
- Remain quiet on packed earth and bedrock
- Support ankles without restricting micro-adjustments

Look for felt-lined, low-profile boots with micro-textured, non-marking rubber or compressed fiber soles patterned after gecko-scale or stone-grip geometries. No metal eyelets. Use waxed cord lacing. Break them in thoroughly on wet rock before transit. Carry spare insoles and a small brush to clear silt from tread seams. Never step heavily; test each placement with the ball of your foot before committing weight.

**Head & Hands**  
Hoods trap condensation near the face and muffle your own breathing, which can induce disorientation in already-quiet air. Use a wide, stiff-brimmed hat or a draped cowl that channels water away from the neck. Gloves should be thin, tactile, and water-shedding: oiled deerskin or waxed canvas palms with exposed fingertips for reading stone seams and water flow. You will use your hands constantly for balance and channel-reading.

### III. GEAR & MATERIAL CONSTRAINTS

The settlement explicitly lacks metal, glass, soot, and wheels. Bringing them is not merely impractical; it introduces foreign resonance, acoustic disruption, and potential ecological contamination. Pack as if you are entering a living hydraulic instrument.

**What to Leave Behind**  
- All ferrous or reflective metals (they ring, they flash, they corrode in constant damp, and they do not belong to the material language)
- Glass containers or lenses (they shatter the diffused light field, create sharp reflections, and risk fragmentation in narrow alleys)
- Combustion-based heat sources (soot lingers in heavy air, fires risk moss/peat ignition, and smoke disrupts the condensation cycle)
- Wheeled transport or rigid frames (alleys narrow to shoulder-width, stairs spiral into bedrock, and paths are spillways)
- Crinkly, vapor-trapping, or high-friction synthetics

**What to Bring**  
- A frameless, close-fitting pack with a low center of gravity, made of waxed canvas or quiet composite. Compression straps should lie flat. No external loops that catch on stone.
- Water purification that operates silently and leaves no chemical trace: ceramic gravity filters, boiled-water preparation using low-smoke solid fuel tablets, or UV-neutral resonance purifiers. Do not pump; pumping creates pressure waves and noise.
- Navigation aids that do not emit light or sound: waxed cloth maps, etched wood or bone waymarkers, or a neural/multiverse nav-aid set to low-frequency pulse mode. Compasses may drift due to mineral saturation and altitude; rely on topography and water flow.
- Low-lumen, warm-spectrum, heavily diffused lighting for thick mist. Better yet, train for low-contrast travel. Bright light shatters the visual equilibrium and reflects off wet stone in disorienting halos.
- A small, smooth wooden or ceramic trowel for clearing silt from channels if necessary. Never use metal. Never alter a water course without tracing its downstream path first.

### IV. MOVEMENT & NAVIGATION: READING THE HYDRAULIC LATTICE

You do not navigate this place by landmarks. You navigate by slope, seam, and flow.

**The Rules of Footfall**  
- Walk on raised stone edges, dry leeward faces, and packed terrace crowns. Avoid channel centers, moss-thick north walls, and silt-lined hollows.
- Step lightly. The ground absorbs impact, but heavy footfalls displace gravel, cloud shallow pools, and send vibration through wet stone that can feel intrusive.
- When alleys narrow, turn sideways, exhale, and let your shoulders brush stone. Do not force through. The architecture expects bodies to yield and flow.
- On bedrock stairs, place feet on the dry, rougher patches. Use hands on vertical faces, not on weeping seams or moss pads.

**Reading Water as Map**  
Gravity dictates flow. Drainage dictates form. Every courtyard is a basin. Every path is a spillway. To descend, follow overflow channels, silt lines, and the sound of falling drops. To ascend, follow retaining walls, dry stone courses, and terraces that step upward against the slope. Mist thickens in hollows and thins on sunlit faces; use this to gauge elevation and exposure. If visibility drops, stop. Wait for the cloud veil to breathe. Moving blind in mist here risks stepping into a catchment basin or disrupting a carefully balanced channel.

**Marking & Memory**  
Do not carve, paint, or leave synthetic markers. If you must note a route, stack three small, dry pebbles on a raised ledge, away from water flow. Better yet, train topographic memory: count terrace sequences, note the angle of a特定 roof, memorize the rhythm of a particular drip chain. The landscape repeats, but never exactly. Learn to read variation as orientation.

### V. SUSTENANCE & WASTE: OPERATING WITHIN THE CYCLE

**Water**  
Water is everywhere, but it cycles through moss, silt, stone seams, and shallow biological pools. It is clear but not sterile. Purify all drinking water quietly. Do not dam, divert, or pool water for convenience. The hydraulic lattice is city-scale; a minor blockage can flood a courtyard or dry a terrace over hours. Drink from overflow edges, not from stagnant basins.

**Food**  
Cool damp suppresses appetite but increases thermoregulatory caloric demand. Bring compact, slow-release, moisture-resistant rations: nut pastes, cured proteins, dried grains, fermented cakes, or multiverse-equivalent dense nutrition wafers. Avoid crinkly packaging, strong odors, or foods that require loud preparation. Eat slowly. Chew thoroughly. The heavy air and muffled acoustics make digestion feel slower; align your intake with your pace.

**Waste**  
Absolute leave-no-trace is non-negotiable. The ecosystem operates on closed-loop equilibrium. Human waste, soap, oils, or synthetic fibers will introduce foreign nutrients or pathogens that the slow cycle cannot process. Pack out all solids. Use biodegradable, inert wipes if necessary, but seal them in vapor-tight bags. Urinate on dry, sunlit stone faces away from channels, not in moss or pools. Never wash gear or skin in catchment basins. The mountain drinks everything; do not make it drink you.

### VI. SHELTER, REST & THERMAL MANAGEMENT

**Sleeping Systems**  
The ground is never dry. Sleep on raised stone, woven platforms, or a lightweight, moisture-permeable cot. Use a vapor-barrier liner beneath your insulation, but ensure it does not trap condensation against your body. Sleeping bags or quilts should be rated for cool, saturated conditions, with hydrophobic outer shells and quick-drying fills. Avoid down unless treated; it clumps in constant damp.

**Fire & Heat**  
Do not build fires. Soot lingers, smoke disrupts condensation, and the risk of igniting compressed moss or dried reed roofs is high. Use chemical or resonance-based heat packs for core warming. Change damp base layers before resting. Perform gentle isometric exercises to generate internal heat without sweating.

**Using Existing Structures**  
If you shelter in courtyards, under roofs, or along terraces, remember: every space has a hydraulic function. Do not block overflow channels. Do not sleep in basins. Do not compress clay/moss roof packing. Roofs are walkways; step only on reinforced edges. If you shift a stone to create a windbreak or dry spot, return it exactly. The settlement is maintained by continuous, quiet adjustment; mirror that ethic.

### VII. SENSORY ADAPTATION & PSYCHOLOGICAL PACE

**Acoustic Absorption**  
Sound does not travel far here; it sinks. Wet stone, moss, packed earth, and dense air swallow resonance. This near-silence can trigger sensory deprivation effects: tinnitus, time distortion, mild dissociation, or an urge to speak loudly to "fill" the space. Resist it. Ground yourself tactically: press palms to dry stone, feel moss compression, count water drops, match your breath to the interval between plinks. The quiet is not emptiness; it is a sponge. Let it absorb your nervous system's static.

**Visual Flattening**  
Diffused light and soft shadows reduce contrast. Depth perception may flatten, especially in mist. Train peripheral vision for movement detection. Use texture, not shadow, to judge distance. When visibility drops, stop moving. Wait. The cloud veil breathes in phases; patience is navigation.

**Temporal Drift**  
The environment's pace is biological. Minutes stretch. Hours blur. Multiverse travelers often experience resonance lag; here, it will manifest as a gentle temporal dilation. Log time objectively. Use a silent, non-reflective chronometer. Set gentle anchors: "I will rest after three terrace sequences," not "I will rest in an hour." Do not fight the slowdown. It is the location's method of integration. Rushing here is the only true danger.

### VIII. ETIQUETTE, INTERACTION & ENVIRONMENTAL RESONANCE

If inhabitants are present, they will move like water finding a seam: quiet, deliberate, reading slope and saturation. Mirror them.

- Speak in low tones or not at all. Voices feel intrusive in absorbed air. Use gestures, waxed cloth notes, or eye contact.
- Never point with rigid limbs. Use open palms, slight nods, or follow the line of a channel with your gaze.
- If you witness maintenance (a stone shifted, silt cleared, roof repacked), observe without interrupting. If invited to help, use bare hands or smooth wood. Follow the existing angle. Do not innovate.
- Do not photograph with flash or reflective lenses. The light field is part of the ecology. If you must record, use matte, low-spectrum sensors or sketch on waxed paper.
- Respect the hydraulic lattice as sacred infrastructure. It is not primitive; it is precise. Every wall sheds, every courtyard catches, every path spills. You are walking through a city-scale instrument tuned to gravity and condensation. Play it softly.

### X. FINAL PREPARATION: THE MINDSET

You are not visiting a ruin. You are not visiting a wilderness. You are visiting a living hydraulic grammar, woven into stone, maintained by hand, balanced by gravity and condensation. It does not announce itself. It seeps. It settles. It continues.

Pack light. Pack quiet. Pack materials that shed, drain, and absorb without resistance. Train your body to move slowly, your eyes to read slope, your ears to trust intervals, your hands to feel seams. Leave behind anything that rings, reflects, burns, or rolls. Bring only what can learn the pace.

When you step onto the first terrace, exhale. Let your shoulders drop. Feel the cool damp rise through your soles. Listen to the space between drops. Watch mist draw through a narrow alley. Do not ask the mountain to accommodate you. Ask yourself how to move through it without leaving a ripple.

If you prepare this way, the place will not tolerate you. It will accept you. And when you leave, you will carry its tempo in your breath, its quiet in your bones, and its water-logic in your steps. That is the only souvenir it allows. That is the only one worth keeping.
