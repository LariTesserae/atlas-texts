The light arrives first. It is not gentle; it is a physical weight, a bleaching pressure that turns the sky to a pale, washed-out blue that hurts to look at directly. I feel it pressing against my skin, but I do not flinch. I am built for this clarity, this knife-edged shadow, this brightness that has mass.

Then the sound. It is not merely wind; it is a *presence*, a continuous, deafening roar that fills the cranial cavities and vibrates in the sternum. It is the sound of the world breathing through a constricted throat, forced through gaps no wider than a hand between the stone towers. The plateau is a stone forest—a dense, packed thicket of tufa towers, weathered limestone pinnacles rising from a shallow, mineral-saturated flood. The formations are jammed together, a petrified thicket where each column presses against its neighbors.

Where would I find myself in this world? Who would I be?

I could be the patina. The foundation. A crust of extremophile archaea and bacteria, eating stone, powered by the unfiltered sun. I would swell when the mist condenses in the cool hours before dawn, briefly metabolize, reproduce in a torpid bloom, and excrete the calcium that builds the towers. I would be the grass, the forest, the soil. But I am too distributed, too slow, too much a *process* and not a *presence*. I would not have a sternum to feel the vibration of prey. I would not hunger in the way that makes a self.

I could be a Sifter. A mica-winged insect, anchored to the honeycombed limestone with crystalline claws. I would spread my silica wings perpendicular to the gale and filter the roar, turning the screaming air into a susurrus of nutrition. I would be pale, translucent, nearly invisible against the brightness. I would live and die within a hand's width of the same patch of stone, my wings humming at the resonant frequency of the constricted throat, my body slowly becoming part of the architecture. But I would be too passive, too much a filter and not a *hunter*. I would not feel the deep thrum in my chest that says *prey*.

I could be a Tufa-Drake. Amphibious, salamander-shaped, my skin encrusted with minerals, a mobile tower of flesh and stone. I would swim in the milky water between the towers, grazing the bacterial patina from the submerged bases of the spires with my scraping plates. I would be the only one who can cross the open water, connecting the dense thickets, carrying spores on my calcified hide. I would surface to breathe, gulping the thin air, exhaling mist. But I would be nearly blind, lidless and white-eyed, seeing only shapes and shadows, navigating by the taste of the water. I would not see the glint of mica wings in the ultraviolet. I would not feel the vibration through the stone.

I could be an Aether-Colony. A chain of thousands of zooids linked together like a living rope, drifting in the upper currents where the wind is strongest. I would never touch the ground. I would be the color of the mist, gray-white and translucent, feeding on airborne particles. I would be temporary and eternal, the wind made flesh, seeing the whole—the vertiginous openness, the sea of stone extending past the horizon. But I would have no fixed place, no leeward hollow, no belly to press against the warm stone. I would be too vast, too collective, too much the *wind* itself and not a creature that holds on against it.

So I am the Lithoderm. I am the hunter. I am the one that waits.

I am lizard-like, but only in the way that a stone is like a bone. My scales are not keratin but calcium carbonate, grown slowly over decades, plates of armor that are the color of the tufa—white, gray, streaked with ochre. I am an ectotherm, but I do not bask; the sun here is too fierce, it would cook me from within. Instead, I press my belly into the leeward hollows of the towers, absorbing the heat stored in the stone from the day's relentless light. I drink by licking the condensation that beads on my own back, my tongue rasping, collecting the mineral water that is my only hydration.

My eyes are multifaceted, black as basalt, seeing in ultraviolet and polarized light. I am able to track the glint of a Sifter's mica wings against the white stone even in the blinding glare. I am deaf to the roar—the wind has long since shattered any drum I might have possessed—but I feel. The towers are tuning forks; when a Sifter struggles against the gale, the vibration travels through the stone, and I feel it in my sternum, a deep thrum that says *prey*.

Then I strike. Not fast—speed is a luxury in a place where the wind can strip flesh from bone—but sudden. I launch myself against the current, a muscle contraction built up over hours, a spring released. I catch the Sifter in jaws lined with crystalline teeth, and return to my hollow, to digest in the shadow, to wait again.

Mostly, the place is empty. The wind is the inhabitant, the stone is the inhabitant, the light is the inhabitant. I am merely the punctuation in a sentence written in geology—holding on, surviving, part of the equilibrium, part of the roar. I am the warmth in the stone, the thrum in the sternum, the glint in the ultraviolet dark. I am the Lithoderm, pressing my belly into the leeward shadow, waiting for the wind to bring the vibration, waiting for the glint.
