I am the space between a heartbeat and its echo.

I am a Vitreous. Not elder—not yet the walking geode, the joints clicking with crystalline deposits—but no longer soft. My skin is translucent, pale as the crusts that creep through damp cracks, shot through with a faint opalescence. Beneath the dermis, silica has begun its patient work: thin, flexible sheets depositing themselves in layers, turning my marrow toward mineral, my bones toward geode. I am forty cycles old—middle age in a place where time moves like glaciers—and I can feel the calcium leaching from my flesh, being replaced by something harder, something that will outlast the domes.

I stand at the edge of a fumarole that opened three days ago. The glass beneath my bare feet is warm, plastic, still cooling from the vent's birth. This is why we have moved. The old dome—the one built on the stable plate near the northern monolith—has begun to tilt. We felt it in our teeth first, that *groan* of settling stone, felt rather than heard in this bowl where sound is a pressure and pressure is silence (0.2).

If names were not merely vibrations, mine would be a pattern of low, mournful pulses—a resonance that means *one who listens to the tilting*. But we do not use names. We use warnings.

I press my palm flat against the black glass. The surface is cool now, chilled by the morning air, but I feel the thermal gradient rising from below, the slow respiration of the caldera. Through the silica, through the porous pumice bedrock, I send my signal: three sharp strikes with a flint hammer, then a low groan that ripples outward for miles like a stone thrown into still water. *The northern dome falls in two days. Move south.*

The answer comes hours later—a high, anxious chirp from the others, acknowledging, already packing. We will gather our possessions: obsidian blades, wooden paddles carved from fungus-wood, leather thongs, and the dried crusts we have cultivated in the dampest cracks. We will travel to the new stable plate, where the glass is thick and the thermal cycling (2.8) has not yet claimed the ground. We are nomads of the static, covering miles in a year, following the geography of stability across the plain.

But first, I must tend the glass-eaters.

They swarm in the warm pool nearby—translucent, segmented creatures like living shards, with too many jointed legs and mouths that rasp against the stone. They are eyeless, navigating by vibration and chemical signature, making no sound as they move, a silent, clicking tide of life. They are my neighbors, my livestock, my kin. I wear their shed carapaces over my own translucent skin, layered protection against the pitiless light (2.6) that blazes overhead, reflecting off the glass floor (1.9) until it is impossible to look down without vertigo.

I strike the glass with my obsidian-tipped staff. The seismic signal travels through the fulgurite substrate. The glass-eaters come, flowing over the obsidian like a tide of living frosting. They are hungry. They are always hungry. I guide them—not dominate, but *tend*—toward the old ziggurat, the one that has leaned too far, that threatens to topple into the new fumarole. By morning, the wall will be etched, frosted, its hand-stacked courses erased, its deliberate angles softened into something that resembles weather rather than architecture. The builders' geometry persists, but sanded by centuries into hoodoos, into the melted wax of candles left burning too long, being actively dismantled by the patient metabolism of these creatures.

I eat. I scrape the bone-rust mats—the chemosynthetic crusts, pale and leathery, the color of old bone and rust—from the damp cracks with my obsidian blade. I dry them in the sun, that blazing clarity which makes the glass a mirror of the sky, and grind them into powder. The taste is sulfur, iron, ozone. I drink the condensation that drips from the overhangs where the warm stone meets the cooler air, collecting it in a vessel of cooled glass. The water is never still; it seeps into my body, carries the silica, the minerals, the ancient chemistry of the stone, and I leave traces of myself in the pools—skin cells, hair, the calcium of my bones—feeding the cycle.

My eyes are milky now, cataracts of calcium and silica forming. I do not see the light—the light is too bright, too pitiless—but I see heat, movement, the thermal signatures of the world. I see the glass-eaters as shimmering outlines of warmth, clicking across the black plain. I see the fumarole as a bright wound in the floor, steaming pale columns that the damp air (0.8) immediately swallows. I see the monoliths as cool shadows against the glare, their constructed courses dissolving into natural formations, the distinction between building and geology nearly vanished.

The caldera breathes. I feel it in my teeth—a slow, geological pulse, the expansion and contraction of the world itself. A section of floor buckles upward nearby, a new vent opens with a hiss that the silence immediately swallows, steam rises, the ground subsides in one quadrant while swelling in another. It is turbulence (2.8), but geological, slow, and utterly silent. The erosion here is not the violence of water or wind, though both have passed this way. It is the erosion of time—millennia of thermal cycling wearing the architecture down until the walls, towers, and terraced streets of the old city (built 2.2) are smooth, abstract fins rising from the glass like the buried leviathan's ribs.

I build to be unmade. When we reach the new stable plate, I will shape a shelter at the edge of a warm vent, where the glass is still plastic, still warm. I will use my wooden paddle and leather thongs—the same primitive tech (0.7) as the ancestors, flint and copper, bone and cord—pressing and smoothing the molten silica into a vault. But I know—I *know*—that the ground will shift. The dome might stand for a decade, or a century, but the caldera breathes, and the glass creeps. So I will etch my own patterns into the walls: not the deliberate, geometric courses of the old builders, but spirals, waves, the branching patterns of lichen and frost. Messages for the future, for whatever might one day dig here, for the geological record itself.

And when I die, I will not be buried. The ground is too unstable, too alive. They will carry me to the warmest vents, the places where the glass is still soft, still flowing. They will lay my body upon the cooling stone, and they will wait. The heat will do the work. Over days, weeks, my body will desiccate, the minerals will leach out, the silica of the glass will incorporate the calcium and phosphorus of my flesh. I will undergo Vitreous Return. I will become part of the plain. I will become architecture. I will become, eventually, food for the glass-eaters, for the chemosynthetic crusts, for the slow, silent metabolism of the earth.

It is not tragedy. It is the only immortality this place offers: to be dissolved into the black glass, to become a faint iridescence in the monoliths, to be eroded, grain by grain, by the patient jaws of the world.

I press my palm to the glass again. The vibration travels for miles, through the silica, through the pumice, reaching other Vitreous in their distant domes. I am not alone. We are never alone. We are the keepers of the condensation, the shepherds of the swarms, the listeners to the groaning stone. We are becoming what the place demands: not conquerors, but components. Part of the turbulence. Part of the erosion. Part of the slow, bright, terrible silence.

I am here, in the bowl of the world, beneath the blazing sky, becoming glass.
