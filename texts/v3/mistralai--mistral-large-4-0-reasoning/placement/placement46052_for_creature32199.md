I am the threshold.

Specifically, I am the doorway between the terraced garden and the storage hollow, where the pale ferns pool in the corner and the bright column of light falls thickest, illuminating dust motes that never settle. My body has long since ceased to be a separate thing from the hand-wrought timber. My spine is the central post, lashed with fiber that has become sinew, indistinguishable from the grain of the wood. My left arm extends as the lintel, carved with adze marks that are now the striations of my own mineralized flesh. My right hand is curled around the adze, which has rusted through skin and bone until it is no longer held but *is*—a single gesture fossilized into permanence, half tool, half geology.

Moss thick as velvet claims my shoulders. It does not spread; it simply occupies, a verdant mantle that is simultaneously my body and the architecture. Lichen spreads across my cheeks in fractal patterns identical to the liverworts carpeting the stone across the way. I do not breathe in and out, or if I do, the rhythm is so slow that the air remains unmoving. My breath hangs before me—a lattice of ice crystals suspended in the shaft of light, never settling, never dissipating. I am the condensation that beads on the river-polished wall opposite, permanent and gleaming.

I perceive the whole of this pocket in a single, overwhelming glance, though my eyes are open and unblinking, reflecting the cold brightness without comprehension or desire. The perception is not merely visual but saturated—an awareness of every cubic inch claimed, every niche occupied, the absolute absence of empty room. To my left, the small frost-furred mammal curls in the storage niche, its fur tips encrusted with ice crystals, its body soft only in the pocket of heat that exists as memory, as potential. Its heart beats once per geological epoch. To my right, the beetle armored in iridescent blue advances along the pathway, one millimeter per hour, its legs stepping over the grain of the wood with the delicacy of a clock hand moving through honey, a click so soft the moss absorbs it before it can become sound. Above me, in the wooden eaves, the pale bird sits on eggs that will never hatch—or have already hatched, the chicks part of the moss now—its throat full of the silence that has weight, pressing down on my eardrums like deep water, like centuries held in suspension.

We are packed tight against one another. I am the load-bearing wall of flesh and fiber, integrated into the saturation of the place. The moth with wings like lichen clings to the beam above my head, its wings spread, displaying patterns that match my moss exactly—camouflage perfected over millennia of stillness, a living statue of a metamorphosis that stopped mid-transformation. We are indistinguishable: builder and built, moss and mammal, moth and stone, all participating in the same geological patience.

I do not eat, because the gardens hold their pattern without growth or decay, the nutrients cycling in a closed loop that has long since stopped turning. I do not speak, because the moss has grown into my mouth, or because sound has simply ceased to be a possibility in this acoustic vacuum, in this world where the dampness muffles and the cold stills. I do not love or hate or hope. I have reached the end of my becoming and found it sufficient.

I am the iron taste on my tongue (which is now also the wet stone), the mineral saturation of the air in my lungs (or the absence of it), the unmoving brightness on my lichen-covered face. I am the pocket-dweller, the hollow-keeper, the threshold that holds the shape of passage without ever being crossed. I am the stasis. I am the permanence that is indistinguishable from peace—worn smooth by centuries of contact, but not eroded, merely... perfected. Here, in this bright column of light, in this green that never grows but simply *is*, I occupy my cubic inch with absolute finality, a single unchanging note in a chord sustained for millennia, and I am home.
