I am in the place where the ceiling weeps milk.

Not water—water is a poison, a myth, a dissolving nightmare. I am in the Accretion Pool, that low place where the cavern floor dips into a bowl of moss, and above me the stalactites have begun their slow, viscous drip. The fluid falls in drops the size of my faceted eyes, each one a glob of calcium and silica suspended in ancient sap, hardening the instant it touches the fungal mat below. It does not splash. It *sets*, like a breath held too long and then released into stone.

I am becoming the floor.

My legs have been stiffening for three years now. I remember when I could stride between the industrial districts, when my joints articulated like the folding of tectonic plates, when I could feel the low-frequency thrum of the bio-reactors in my teeth. Now my knees are quartz. My fingers, when I curl them, make a sound like ice forming, but slower, deeper, more viscous. I do not walk to the Pool. I am carried—cradled in the harness of woven mycelium by my kin, my brood, those who have fed me mineral dust and sung to my bones until they grew heavy with precipitate.

My opalescent membrane, once translucent enough to reveal the lattice of silica and carbon fiber beneath, is now clouded with the first accretions. The crystalline filaments that grow from my scalp—my mane, my antennae, my choir—have begun to fuse at the tips, chiming no longer against one another but *into* one another, becoming a single, rigid crest of glass. The dust falls on me. The fine, crystalline snow of salt and glass that drifts down from the canopy above settles in my spiral eyes, in the logarithmic whorls across my cheeks, filling the hollows until I am no longer looking *out* at the heat-map of the city, but *becoming* the thermal signature itself. I see in mineral density now. I see the sulfur vents glowing deep below. I see the factory horns as pressure waves in the thick, dry air. I see my own body as a map of accumulation, layer upon layer, the strata of myself being written in real-time.

The Silicate Mites are on me.

They have always been on me—my kin, my maintenance crew, my clicking children. But now they move with a different purpose. They skitter across my stiffening limbs, their hard shells rasping against my membrane, cleaning the mineral scales from my skin not to maintain me, but to *prepare* me. They are grinding their dead into the moss beneath me, a final offering, a foundation. I feel their mandibles clicking against my sternum, a language I have learned since childhood, a syntax of heat and toxicity. They are telling me: *This is the right density. This is the proper substrate. Lie down. Become.*

I lie down.

The moss receives me. It is cool, dry, impossibly dense—a living skin that has been cultivated for decades to this specific thickness, this specific resonance. It pulses beneath me, a slow bioluminescence in the copper-green light filtering through the glass-leaves of the Lithophyte canopy above. The mycelium threads reach up, exploring my mineralizing extremities, tasting me, already beginning the conversation that will continue for centuries. I do not speak with my mouth—I have not spoken with my mouth in years. I speak by releasing pheromones into the mat, a final broadcast across the network, a vibration that travels through the stone and is felt in the marrow of those in the Hollowings, in the factory gantries, in the deep dark where the Sulfur-Wraiths stir.

*This is the Tempo,* I tell them. *This is the process, not the state. I am not ending. I am precipitating.*

Above me, the great Lithophyte leans. Its trunk, already massive, swells with the slow hunger of growth. I can feel the roots twitching in the dark soil of the cavern floor, sensing the calcium in my bones, the silica in my blood. They are coming for me. Not as predators, but as architects. They will grow around me, layer by mineral layer, until my spiral eyes are embedded in the cellulose-silica matrix like fossils-in-waiting, until my furnace-chest, that slow combustion of hydrogen and sulfur that has kept me warm for eighty years, becomes a geode, a hollow chamber within the pillar, a warmth that persists in the stone.

The milky fluid drips onto my forehead.

It hardens instantly, a new layer, a glaze. I feel the weight of it. I feel myself sinking, not into the moss, but *through* it, becoming part of the substrate, becoming the bedrock upon which others will stand. The gantries that cross between the Lithophytes will one day rest upon my shoulders. The pneumatic tubes will run through my hollows. Children will press their palms against my trunk—once my ribs, once my spine—and feel the walls yield slightly, warm and pulsing, still growing.

The sound here is mostly quiet. The factories hum at frequencies just below the threshold of hearing, a vibration I feel now in my mineralizing marrow rather than my teeth. The mites click their final chorus against my stiffening chest. The dust falls with a sound like distant applause, or breathing, settling on my closed eyes, on my glass-fused mane, burying me in the snow of this waterless world.

I am cool. I am dry. I am becoming.

And the city is still under construction.
