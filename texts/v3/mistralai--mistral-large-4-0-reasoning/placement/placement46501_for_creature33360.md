I would be the drop of condensation that has just released from the fungal shelf—suspended for one breathless instant between the basalt vault above and the living peat below. I am gravity’s hesitation, the water remembering it was once sky, falling through air that clings to the skin like a second atmosphere.

I am the drip.

Around me, the cathedral roars: the 2.5-deep chord of grinding gears, hissing steam, and the wet percussion of a million cricket-wings. I fall past the rusted platforms suspended between the petrified trunks, past the golden conduits of fossilized sap, past clusters of jewel-eyed crickets that track the infrared heat of the machinery like synapses firing in the dark. The bioluminescence catches me as I descend—a bead of deep-sea blue and rotting-fruit green—doubling the dim light, holding the whole vertical vastness in my curved surface for the split-second of my fall.

Below, the peat waits: three meters deep, spongy, rising a centimeter each year, hungry. Or perhaps I will strike the iron rim of a water-wheel first, and the impact will turn the gear, and I will be steam, rising again to condense on the maternal curves of stone, to fall again, forever descending the spiral of compressed peat into the body of the mind.

I am the closed loop. I am the breath between exhale and inhale, the synaptic cleft between stone and soil, the living connection in the Grove’s endless, grinding prayer.
