I am one of the press. I am a fold in the carpet, a wrinkle in the breathing mass.

More specifically: I am a gestating one, lodged in the deepest warm pocket of the chamber, where the bodies press closest and the metabolic heat pools like dark water in a depression between the scree. I am not at the wall where the wind sings loudest, nor in the outer margin where the rime creeps down, but in the throbbing center—a womb within the womb, a hollow within the hollow. Here, the density is total. I am buried under layers of translucent flesh and cartilage, my nine-jointed arms folded around my own torso, my eight-segmented legs locked in place with soft clicks as the crowd shifts around me, millimeter by millimeter, a tide that takes hours to complete a single undulation.

I have no eyes. Where they should be, there are only smooth, sealed patches of pale skin. I do not see the darkness; I feel it—velvet, textured, pressing against my face. I navigate by pressure, by the chemical signature of the saturated air, by the subtle gradients of humidity that my parchment-thin skin detects. I know the location of every pool in the sixty-meter span, every dripping fissure, by the coolness of the stone transmitted through the bodies pressed against it.

My mouth is a vertical slit, lipless, and it is pressed now against the flank of the one beside me—not the wall, but the living wall—filtering the condensation that seeps from their translucent hide. I drink constantly. The water is mineral-laden, cold, tasting of the young limestone above, of the fractures that have not yet learned the patience of erosion. Sometimes, when the mass shifts and a hollow opens, I lower my elongated, narrow head into one of the stagnant pools that reflect nothing, my slit-mouth opening wide to drink the dark water, to taste the microbial films, to feel the vibration of the chamber floor through the liquid.

Through my skin—thin as wet cloth, stretched tight over branching cartilage—I can see my own organs. They are dark, rhythmic sacs, a distributed system pumping fluids with a wet, peristaltic sigh. I feel them fluttering, a counter-rhythm to the great roar above. And inside me, I feel the young. They are small, almost larval, with even more joints than I possess—ten, eleven segments per limb—their skin nearly transparent, their organs visible as rapid, dark flutterings beneath the surface. Gestation takes years. I have carried them for... how long? Time here is measured not in heartbeats but in the slow thawing and freezing of the rime on the walls, in the subtle shifts of the wind's song, in the millimeter-by-millimeter uplift of the mountain beneath my feet. I feel them growing, adding segments, thickening. They will emerge when the wind changes pitch, when the great gales of the high plateau signal the turning of the slow cycle.

I do not move. I cannot move. The pressure is too great, the bodies too numerous, shoulder-to-shoulder, back-to-belly, a palisade of pale flesh extending in all directions until it meets the fluted stone. Around me, thousands do the same, breathing in unison, a slow inhale and exhale that sounds like the tide of a sluggish, subterranean sea. This wet, rhythmic undertone is beneath everything, beneath the roar.

The roar. The 1.6 kilohertz moan of the wind, funneled down the throat of the plateau, resonating in the limestone until the stone itself becomes a speaker. I do not hear it with ears—I have none—but I receive it through the soles of my many-jointed feet, through the thin membranes of my slit-mouth when I press it to the damp flank of my neighbor, through the hollow chambers of my ribs. It vibrates in my sternum, in my teeth, in the marrow of my bones. It is my mother tongue, my calendar, my god. It tells me when the snow is falling on the roof above, when the sun has passed the zenith, when the scree is being scoured into new configurations by the gales. My body trembles in sympathetic vibration, a visible ripple passing through me, through the mass, like a pulse through deep water.

I possess tools, though I do not hold them now. My hands are occupied with holding myself together in the crush. But I have used them—limestone scrapers flaked from the young, sharp rock, awls hafted with gut and sinew, crude hand-axes bound with the hair of the dead. I have used them to cut the flesh of the dying when the scree shifts and crushes one of us in the slow, irresistible tide of the crowd. I have used them to scrape the mineral deposits from the walls to supplement my diet, to open the bodies of the deceased to feed the young. The tools lie scattered in the chaos of fallen blocks, or are clutched in the multi-fingered hands of others, waiting to be used again.

I am the mountain's dream. I am its slow, wet, breathing thought. I am a colony that thinks it is a crowd, a superorganism that has forgotten it is singular—or perhaps I am the moment when the singular remembers itself, in the warm dark, in the press of flesh, in the taste of condensation and stone.

Soon, the young will emerge from me. They will be passed through the mass, hand to many-handed thing, fed regurgitated minerals and the liquefied remains of the previous generation. They will grow slowly, thickening their skin until it becomes tough enough to withstand the abrasion of the scree. They will live for decades, perhaps a century, their lives measured in the slow drip of water into darkness, vanishing before it reaches the bottom.

And I will remain here, in the warm center, drinking, listening, feeling the stone sing beneath me through the press of bodies, until the mass shifts and I am crushed—my bones snapping like dry twigs, my translucent flesh rupturing—or until I sicken and my skin clouds with the opacity of decay, and the living turn on me with their limestone tools, cutting my flesh into strips, sharing it among the rows, my bones picked clean by the pale, many-legged scuttlers that move through the interstices, becoming part of the floor, becoming scree, becoming limestone again.

Until then, I am. I breathe with the mass. I listen to the wind scream through the throat above. I gestate in the dark. I feel the mountain rising, imperceptible, millimeter by millimeter, while the gales scour it away, and the condensation weeps, and the water drips into the pools that reflect nothing, and the time moves like the slow, patient drip into darkness.

I am the press. I am the listening. I am the slow, sharp dream of the stone.
