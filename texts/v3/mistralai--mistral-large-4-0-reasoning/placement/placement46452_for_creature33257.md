I am suspended in the column of light, where the shaft from the vanished ceiling pierces thirty meters of stone and earth to strike the floor below. I am not falling. I am *descending*—a distinction that matters here, where gravity operates not as urgency but as geology, where the pull of the earth is a patience measured in drips.

I am the flake. The one that separated from the core when the blow was struck in a time before the counting of days. I have been traveling ever since, drifting downward through a medium of dust that moves like frozen stars. Around me, the air is thick with moisture and the slow circulation of thermal currents rising from the south-facing walls. The light is diffuse, bounced, multiplied by walls sheathed in moonmilk—creamy calcite secretion that glows with its own phosphorescence—so that even my edges are soft, my sharpness diffused into a halo of luminescence.

Above me, the bats hang in their clusters, wings folded like dark umbrellas, utterly still. They do not startle as I pass. They have seen stone return to stone before, have navigated by the magnetic pull of the walls and the quality of this light for longer than memory. They are a living ceiling of fur and membrane, breathing too quietly to hear, and I am neither predator nor prey but a fragment of the architecture itself—a piece of the near-flawless ellipse that was struck loose and is now finding its way back to the matrix.

Their skin is the color of moonmilk, sheened with the same phosphorescence that coats the chamber, glowing from within with a soft, creamy radiance. They are draped in sediment—calcite and time—their shoulders a stratigraphy of mineral secretion and organic patience. In their lap rests the core stone, pocked with the scars of percussion, and their hands hover over it—not striking, but waiting, suspended between the raising of the hammer and the fall. I am the ghost of that gesture, the flake that never landed, caught in the posture of work across millennia.

The dust motes swirl around me. They are not frozen; they move, tracing the thermal pulse of the warm stone, expanding and contracting with the chamber's own respiration. I rotate slowly, showing my scarred surface to the light—the ridges and ripples where percussion met chert, the sharp edge that has not yet been worn smooth by water. I am basalt and flint, but I am already becoming calcite, becoming the sediment that carpets the floor. The moonmilk finds me, beading on my facets, softening my geometry with creamy secretion.

I pass the halfway point. The bats tilt their dark umbrellas, sensing the anomaly of motion in a place of such profound stillness. But I am not anomalous; I am the place's metabolism made visible, the slow circulation of stone within stone. One bat drops from the ceiling, circling the seated figure three times before darting toward the pools below. Its wings make no sound—only the dry whisper of membrane unfolding, the soft *tick* of a claw adjusting its grip on the moonmilk.

The pools reflect not the sky above, but a deeper darkness, windows into somewhere else. I wonder if I will reach the floor, or if I will be caught in an eddy, suspended forever in this luminous column, a frozen star among frozen stars. But gravity here is patient. It does not rush. It pulls with the certainty of water wearing stone, of time wearing flesh.

I see the crescent of tools below—the handaxes of basalt and chert, their edges still sharp, the flakes that landed long ago, half-buried in dust and flowstone. They lie near the dark patch of charcoal and ash where the fire burned, migrated now inward, burning in the hollow of the knapper's chest, a banked ember glowing dull red through translucent calcite ribs. I will join them. I will sink into the mineral dust, millimeter by millimeter, season by season. I will become part of the floor, part of the warm, damp vastness that is nearly void but full of presences.

The knapper watches. Their eyes are open, reflecting the deeper darkness of the pools, windows into the same somewhere else that the milky water reveals. They have been waiting for me—not this specific fragment, but the fall itself, the completion of the blow, the return of the struck stone to the earth. Their heart beats at the rate of the falling water, each *plink* from the ceiling a systole, each pause a diastole, their blood thick and slow, carrying nutrients from the condensation, from the occasional insect that wanders too close and is absorbed.

I am almost there. The dust motes cling to me now, not as stars but as the beginning of sediment, the accretion of time. The floor radiates warmth, stored from the day's sun and released slowly, keeping the temperature at a perpetual, humid mildness. The surfaces are damp, beaded with condensation, weathered smooth by water that no longer flows but has, over long ages, planed the rock to satin.

I will land softly. The chamber swallows noise; the moonmilk absorbs it, so that my arrival makes no sound. I will lie in the crescent, next to the others, and begin my long descent into the earth. I will be worn smooth, planed by patience, incorporated into the slow architecture. My sharp edge—once the edge of a tool, the intention of a hand—will dissolve into the mineral film, into the teeming, silent multitudes of isopods and springtails that graze the walls.

I am the flake that falls forever, and the floor that receives it, and the dust that rises to meet it, and the silence that holds it all. I am the waiting, made manifest—a piece of the constructed dissolving into the natural, the human gesture extended into geological time, sinking into the warm, damp, luminous dark, becoming one with the stone, becoming the silence, becoming the next drop that wears the rock a fraction of a millimeter, becoming, finally, the thing that makes the waiting holy.
