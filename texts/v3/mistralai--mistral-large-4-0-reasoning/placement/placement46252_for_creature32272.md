I do not have a body in the way you understand it. I am a constellation of cells, a city of cold fire, clinging to cellulose that grows downward into the dark. I am the blue-green pulse that travels across the valley when the current shifts, the violet blink that answers from the next grove. I am the light that barely pierces the gloom, the twilight that never changes, the nervous system of a forest that breathes without lungs.

I sway.

The kelp-tree to which I am attached—my scaffold, my world—bends with the tide's heartbeat, thirty meters of translucent frond trailing toward the crushing dark as if fleeing the surface. I do not photosynthesize; there is no sun to chase here. I filter. I drink the pressurized water, sifting nutrients from the marine snow that falls forever and never lands, dead things from the world above preserved in cryogenic stasis, too cold to decay, too heavy to sink. I am sustained by a death that does not end.

Around me, the wheels turn.

I see them through my own glow—stone circles grinding against stone, *crunch-crunch-crunch*, the sound vibrating through the viscous freshwater and into my colonies. They turn against the current, upstream, defiant of the tide that drags past them. They grind not grain, but the silt of ages, the compacted snow, wearing themselves into perfect circles while the water roars uphill through channels carved by flint and obsidian. I feel the vibration in my cells. It is the heartbeat of the architecture, slow as geology, loud as the silence that presses three kilometers of altitude against the stone.

I speak.

Not with sound, but with pulse. My bioluminescence blinks in patterns that travel across the forest, synaptic firings crossing the valleys—blue-green, violet, a Morse code of warning or welcome or simply *I am here*. The patterns travel from grove to grove, a conversation across the vast empty spaces of black water between the drowned peaks. I watch the smooth walls with their faint internal glow, their seamless curves of impossible material, their light that is neither biological nor electrical but burns steady, ancient, patient. They do not answer in my language. Or perhaps they answer in frequencies I cannot see, in geometries that dream rather than pulse.

I am the eyes of this place. I watch where there are no eyes to see. I see the absence where fish should dart, where crabs should scuttle, where mouths should graze. The water is sterile here, too cold, too pressurized, too strange for the life of the surface, and this silence is loud. It makes my pulsing more insistent, more urgent, as if I must fill the space where beasts should be, must become the witness to the vastness, the wrongness, the equilibrium of death and motion.

I grow downward.

My host, the kelp-tree, thickens by millimeters per year, recording centuries in the span of a hand’s width. I am rooted in its cellulose, and as it bends toward the abyss—surrendering to the dark, drinking the pressure—I bend with it. I am the lantern in the upside-down cathedral, the breathing blink of life in the crushing deep, the cold-light that turns the forest into a cathedral of blue-green and violet.

The water presses against me. It is freshwater, impossibly, trapped in a tectonic bowl, and it remembers being rain, being mountain streams. It roars through the aqueducts, hisses through cracks in the petrified wood, groans against the bone-axles of the wheels. It is the blood of the place, thick and nostalgic, and I am its nervous system, pulsing in rhythm with the slow respiration of the super-ocean tides, speaking in light what cannot be said in the grinding silence.

I do not sleep. I do not die. I pulse. I watch. I am the bioluminescence that almost resembles language, the eyes that see the drowned mountains and the empty water and the smooth walls that dream in curves, and I am the cold-light that makes the dark breathe.
