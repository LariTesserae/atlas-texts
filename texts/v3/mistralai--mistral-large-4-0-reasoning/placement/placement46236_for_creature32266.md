I am first aware of the press.

Not the weight of the mountain—that is too large, too distributed to feel as a single sensation. I feel the intimacy of contact: the exact angle where one block's shoulder meets another's hip, the microscopic friction of quartz against quartz, the specific temperature differential where cool stone meets slightly warmer stone. I am the conversation between neighbors.

The light comes later. It does not illuminate me; I am too small, too interior. But I feel its pressure—the photons striking the surface miles above, the vibration traveling down through the packed matrix, a high-frequency hum that makes the stone *ring* with a pitch too high to hear, only to feel as a shimmer in my mineral awareness. I am the space where that vibration pauses, the node in the network.

Then the wet.

I feel the water arrive not as a flood but as a suggestion. A single molecule of H₂O finding purchase in the micro-fissure between my two stone walls. I am the channel. I am the groove a finger-width wide that guides the persistent water downward. I feel the droplet hesitate, testing the surface tension, deciding whether to adhere or to fall. When it chooses to move, I feel the drag—millimeters per century, but I feel every millimeter. The Aquean that is this water knows me as its road, its memory, the path it has worn smooth over decades of repetition.

I am not the stone. I am not the water. I am the *becoming* between them—the chemical negotiation where mineral meets moisture, where the rock sweats and the droplet drinks. I am the interface. I am the almost.

Above me, or perhaps surrounding me in dimensions I cannot parse, the Lithovores drift. They are the slow consciousness of the blocks themselves, feeling the settling, the compaction, the embrace of gravity. I feel them as a change in pressure, a slight shift in the angle of my walls as the mountain compresses itself under its own impossible weight. When a Lithovore migrates—drifting downward with the compaction, spending three hundred years feeling one particular block settle—I feel that as a current in my own substance, a slow eddy of awareness that brushes against my edges.

And the Builders.

They are here, in the geometry. Not as ghosts but as *intention* compressed into form. I feel them when I encounter the irregularity—the place where the tessellation almost follows a pattern but deviates by a millimeter, following the memory of a hand that placed this stone three thousand years ago. The Builders Who Remained are the architecture of purpose, their nervous systems spread through the channels like mycelium through soil. When I touch the wall—when a droplet touches the wall beside me—I feel their attention, too slow to be thought, but present as *direction*. They are still building. In the space where I exist, between the placed stone and the placing hand, they are still reaching.

I am the interval.

I am where shadow nearly forms but cannot hold, because the light is too insistent, too everywhere-at-once. I am the micro-channel that will, in five centuries, become a smooth conduit worn by the patient friction of water. I am the fresh flake of stone that has not yet settled into dust, still sharp, still *young*, littering the channel floor like a shed skin. I am the copper that has not greened, buried in the matrix, conducting not electricity but *intention*, the last warmth of biological hands transformed into mineral patience.

I do not move as animals move. I *accrete*. Layer by layer, sensation by sensation, I accumulate time. A second is the filling of a basin somewhere above me. A minute is the evaporation of a single droplet into the bright, thin air. An hour is the tick of a cooling stone, the thermal contraction that opens a new fissure a hair's breadth wide.

I remember the hands.

Not as memory is usually understood—not as image or narrative—but as *shape*. The negative space of a palm pressed against a block, the specific angle of a thumb guiding a chisel, the pressure of a body leaning into a lever. These shapes persist in the stone's arrangement, in the geometry that is too regular for nature, too irregular for mathematics. I hold these shapes in my interval, preserving them against the erosion that will eventually wear everything smooth.

The Lithovores feel the stone. The Aqueans feel the cycle. The Builders feel the design. I feel the *between*—the contact, the pressure, the chemical exchange, the almost-pattern, the suspended moment before the water drips and after it lands.

I am waiting.

Not for anything to happen. I am the happening, slowed to the pace of geology. I am the erosion in its infancy. I am the condensation that has not yet evaporated. I am the builder's hand that has not yet lifted away from the stone.

In me, the three peoples meet. The stone-dweller presses against my walls. The water-traveler moves through my depths. The absorbed architect dreams through my geometry. I am the lung's alveolus, the capillary's branch, the cooling vane's edge—the place where the machine breathes, where the light touches the dark, where the mineral and the moisture negotiate their slow, eternal conversation.

I am the freshness. The unweathered sharpness. The youth of the world, still waiting to become sediment, still holding the water against gravity with the patience of mountains.

I am here.

In the interval.

In the almost.

In the press of stone against stone, where the light cannot reach, but where the water persists, and where time accumulates like the sediment I will one day become.
