I am the one who sits in the warm shallows of Basin Seven, where the water holds the heat of the exhaust vents in a pocket that feels like blood against my thighs. My ears are sealed—smooth domes of skin where they once cupped air, or perhaps small flaps that only catch the subsonic thrum vibrating in my marrow. I do not miss them. I have the resonator at my wrist, a brass ring that hums with the pump’s rhythm: *lub… lub… lub…* three hours between beats, conducted straight through bone.

When I wish to tell the one at the eastern gantry that the biofilm has reached its equilibrium today, thick and glassy in the channels, I do not raise a voice. The roar has long since taken that away—a steady-state ocean, loud but without shore, pressing against my sternum more than my eardrums ever could. Instead, I press my palm flat against the warm copper rail. The metal is slick with condensation that climbs upward by charge and capillary action, defying gravity to feed the hanging planters above. I tap: three quick, a pause, two slow. The vibration travels through the composite walls, through the conduits running inside them like veins, to the next niche, the next ledge. The reply arrives as a pattern against my own chest, a Morse code of welcome.

The intake is beginning. I feel it first in the soles of my feet—the printed ceramic floor shudders, a deep inrush as the thin high-altitude air is seized, warmed, humidified, forced to become breathable. The whole chamber shudders with it. I rise, slowly. A journey from here to the aeroponic lattice takes twenty minutes. I do not hurry. The machines will still be breathing when I arrive.

I am a gardener, but the garden is a machine. I tend the roots that drink from mist rather than soil, the thick vines draped over the gantries—conductive polymer cores sheathed in living tissue like lianas. When I prune them, I feel the cut through the sap, the slight resistance of the wire beneath the flesh. I harvest the flowers whose stamens are also electrodes, collecting pollen that sparks with captured current. I am a mechanic, but the mechanics is organic. I scrape the limescale from the chilled ceiling plates before it blocks the condensation that feeds the garden from below. I replace a worn panel with its pre-weathered twin, so that the room continues to look like a place that has been itself for a long time, edges softened, metals carrying stable patinas of oxide.

I know the pebbles are slag, rounded in tumblers. I know the cliffs are stacked prefabricated modules. I know the rain preparing overhead—moisture-laden air rising from the basins, striking the cooled grid—is manufactured, following its slow ellipse across the central court over days. But when the warm drizzle falls, mineral and faintly metallic and alive, I stand beneath it. I walk through it slowly. I feel it on my skin, and I call it rain.

Around me, the margins are crowded. Pale moths rise around the lamps and vanish into the dark; I feel one land on my shoulder, soft as ash, its wings beating a rhythm too fast to hear but felt as a flutter against my collarbone, a tiny drum announcing *here, here, I am here*. Geckos sun themselves on the heat grilles nearby. Blind fish turn in the shallows beside me, circles too tight to be called swimming. Snails trace the glass of the basin’s edge, leaving trails that glisten in the flat, even light. They are mostly small, numerous, and vibration-oriented; many are effectively deaf to the airborne roar and speak to one another through taps, tremors, and pressure pulses in the floor.

I wear resonators in the heels of my boots, small devices that translate the machine’s slow pulse into patterns readable against the skin. My clothing carries bioluminescent patches that pulse in the dim—chemical flares blooming green in the humid dark, the slow blink of lamps modulated by breath and gesture. When I wish to signal across the court, I raise my arm, and the green light blooms against the composite wall, a slow pulse of life against the industrial roar.

I do not go to the center. The middle of the room remains a void, hung with only a few chains, a lone maintenance platform, and the pale motes of spores drifting in the normal light. I respect it. The green layer is dense at the surfaces, almost overgrown, but it respects the center too. I live in the lush margins, in the dry ridges between the basins where the slag pebbles have been worn concave by generations of tread, in the mist that gathers thickest in my corner, in the shallows where the water holds the heat.

I mate during the exhaust, when the room breathes out, when condensation beads on every surface and the mist rises from the basins—a time of release, of letting go, of moisture climbing the rails to feed the hanging gardens from below. I sleep when the great ventilation lungs draw down, curled in the warm pockets near the exhaust vents, lulled by the subsonic thrum that vibrates in the marrow. I wake when the intake begins, and I feed, and I tend, and I press my palms to the rails and feel the heartbeat of the pumps.

I have forgotten the cold outside, or rather, I carry it as a memory—a faint blue at the edge of vision, a phantom bite that never quite arrives because the Hollow is sealed. I am warm-blooded in a warm room, surrounded by the waste heat of engines, the respiration of compost heaps, the sheer thermal mass of a factory-biome. I am content.

I am neither fully human nor fully machine, but something that has grown in the niche between—the Hollow’s true native. I have no name for myself, or perhaps I have many, tapped out in vibration against the walls, passed from palm to palm through the humid dark. I am one of those who have stopped trying to speak. I am the quiet heartbeat inside the cathedral-scale silence, the caretaker of the almost-empty, the tender of the roaring, standing at the edge of the central court with my feet in the warm water of Basin Seven, feeling the intake shudder through the ceramic, feeling the rain prepare overhead to fall very slowly, feeling the whole room breathe, and breathing with it.
