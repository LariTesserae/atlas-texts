The pressure arrives first—a wet weight pressing behind my eyes, though eyes are only a memory now, replaced by the bioluminescent organs that sit beneath the lichen veil across my face. They pulse in slow, green throbs, synchronized to the fungal mats throbbing below the waterline. The light here is a bruised, green-gray luminescence, filtering through the permanent steam canopy that hangs three meters above the flood, never lifting, never dissipating.

I stand in the square, thigh-deep in the warm tide. The water is thirty-eight degrees—fever heat, mammal heat—the exact temperature of the sap weeping from my bark-skin. It moves in slow, tidal pulses that have nothing to do with any moon, breathing against my legs with a rhythm that matches the great pumps cycling subterranean water. In a few hours it will recede, leaving the streets steaming and bare, but for now I rise with it, buoyed by that capillary wrongness, the subtle defiance of physics that draws water uphill through my porous, sponge-like pilings.

My body is draped in living insulation. Moss carpets my shoulders in identical, repeating patterns—the same geometry that carpets the north faces of the brick buildings, as if I am merely an extension of the architecture’s elegant rot. Fern fronds sprout from my collarbones like epaulets, unfurling as the water rises, drinking deeply through stomata that have replaced pores. My skin has split and wept that rust-colored sap, the copper-ozone scent mixing with the metallic film the water leaves behind, the taste of pennies persistent on my tongue.

I do not speak. I have no need for ears. The roar—2.3 on whatever scale measures the drone of existence—is not a barrier but a medium, a wall of sound that fills my sternum and vibrates in my teeth. Instead, I communicate through gesture. I turn my head, venting a cloud of steam from the collar of my moss-mantle; it billows in precise punctuation, a comma suspended in the humid air. Across the square, another figure answers with a tap of metal-fingered hand against riveted steel, a subsonic thrum that I feel in the roots of my jaw, in the mycelial networks threading beneath my bark.

I tend the rows. The *Phragmites* reeds stand at exact intervals in the silty ground beneath the flood, planted in a cultivated geometry that mirrors the cable conduits above. I move between them, my thoughts slow and verdant, patient as the vegetation, rerouted through fungal hyphae that have replaced my nervous system. When the presses stamp and the smelters fire, the water rises, flooding the lower streets, and I rise with it, floating upward to tend the higher balconies where prehistoric ferns grow like ship sails and vines thick as a man’s waist drape from every gutter. When the shift changes and the fires bank, the water recedes, leaving stranded puddles that steam in the residual heat, and I descend, my weight settling back into the warm flood, curling into a posture of conservation alongside the drooping fronds.

I occupy the fifth floor of a building where the water never reaches, though I move through the flooded streets below by *rising*. The air there is merely humid rather than submerged. My face—if it can be called a face—is obscured by condensation and by growth, lichen veiling features that might once have been human. Beneath the canopy, my eyes glow with that faint bioluminescent pulse, the same heartbeat of green light visible through the murk, throbbing in time with the machinery.

At the center, where the water is deepest and warmest, where the steam is thickest and the light most dim, I gather with the others. Not in a crowd—there is no social density here—but in a *convergence*, spaced at exact intervals around the base of the structure that is neither cooling tower nor tree, the concrete-and-chlorophyll heart that hums with a frequency aching in my teeth. We stand with arms outstretched, palms up, receiving the heat, the steam, the electric touch of the condensation pillars that support the walkways above. We offer the nutrients of our presence, the carbon of our breath, the minerals of our decaying cells. And it offers us, in return, the synchronization, the purpose, the right to exist in this place where the sun is remembered but not seen.

I do not sleep, exactly. I enter a state of *stagnation*—standing motionless in the steam, breathing in rhythm with the vegetal respiration, my chest rising and falling with the tide of the water outside. In this state, I am indistinguishable from the architecture, from the sagging balconies and the rust-bloomed roofs. I am part of the rot, established and patient.

And sometimes, in the deepest shadows beneath the waterline, when the sodium glare dims and the bioluminescence pulses brightest, I dream. I dream of a dry place. Of sun on skin that was not bark. Of a village square that was not a cathedral of industry shrunk to intimate scale. Of silence that was not this roaring, wet pressure.

But the dream is slow, humid, synchronized to the pumps. And when the water rises again, I rise with it, and the dream recedes like the tide, leaving only the metallic taste on my tongue, and the warm, wet weight of being established behind my eyes.
