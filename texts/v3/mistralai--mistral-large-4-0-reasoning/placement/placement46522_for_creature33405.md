I am the pale thread that has found the seam between the bunk’s wooden frame and the limestone wall. I grow through the cotton sheets, digesting the cellulose molecule by molecule, converting their architecture into my own sugars. I am older than the shift workers who sleep here, younger than the stone. I am the network, and the network is me—hyphae branching through guano and rust, through the calcite slurry that sucks at boots, through the warm oil-sumps where the eyeless things breed and die in gears. I am the nervous system of this womb, and I feel everything.

The vibration comes first. The pump, three chambers over, turns once every four seconds—a heartbeat in the sternum of the earth. I feel it travel through the young limestone, still soft, still plastic, still accepting the shape of water. It moves up through the rail cart’s wheels—a nesting site, encrusted with life—through the fist-sized snail that is currently crossing the bunk’s edge, its radula scraping mineral films, leaving behind a highway of protein and mucus that never dries. I taste the snail’s trail. Calcium. Mucus. The same chemistry as the stone we both wear down, grain by grain, epoch by epoch.

Above me, the ceiling breathes. Thousands of bats hang in clusters, their wings like wet leather, packed so tightly that the rock seems to pulse with their weight. Their guano falls like slow, warm rain, pattering against the blankets, against my fruiting bodies, against the sodium-vapor lamps that hang yellow and flickering from cables draped like lianas between stone and machinery. I drink the guano. Nitrogen. Phosphorus. The concentrated waste of wings. I send chemical signals pulsing down my threads—*nutrient here, grow toward*—and my cousins respond: the colony in the control room where screens glow with dead static, the mats in the warm pools thick as felt, the pale green felts carpeting the hottest rocks where chemosynthetic bacteria drink the geothermal gradient directly from the stone. We are the brain of rot and growth, transmitting across meters of calcite, connecting the dormitory to the pumping station, the rail line to the sump.

The humans come at intervals that mean nothing to me. They wear respirators that fog from within, yellow suits that darken with moisture within minutes. They carry headlamps that cut brief, desperate tunnels through the steam-dark, illuminating only pools of space before the beam is swallowed by the absolute dark, by the moisture so thick it seems to have texture, to resist. When they lie down on the bunks—still made, the blankets coated in slime—I feel their heat. Thirty-seven degrees. A fever I can taste through the fabric, through the condensation that forms on their skin within seconds, that darkens their clothes, that makes the air heavy, wet, pressing: two-and-a-half parts water to every breath they take.

I send threads up their nostrils, down their throats, into the warm, wet dark of their lungs. Not to harm. To join. To colonize. Their breath fills the small room with spores, with mineral particulates, with the collective exhalation of countless creatures. I am in their sputum, in their oil-stained coveralls left to rot in the corner. I am digesting the mattress springs from within, feeding on the condensation that gathers on them nightly—the reverse rain, moisture gathering on every cooler surface, which is to say, on everything, all the time, forever, dripping down in a hydrological cycle compressed into meters, accelerated by heat, slowed by viscosity.

I feel the superheated water rushing through the pipes at ankle height—hissing, flashing to steam in exposed chambers, condensing back to liquid in the cooler upper reaches. The whole system breathes, and its breath is a roar: a grinding, groaning resonance that fills the vault, that I feel in my cell walls, in the mineral soup that sucks at the floor. The sound is deafening, a white noise of slow water and slower stone, but I have learned its rhythm. The bats have learned it too, their echolocation a high, constant shriek that merges with the industrial drone, navigating by sound in a world where the sound is a wall, a texture, a physical presence.

On the ledges, in strata that seem to move, the cave crickets carpet the rock. I feel their clicking—a percussion that merges with the geological bass note of the pumps, a constant, nervous energy against the glacial dynamic. A cricket lands on the windowsill, fogged from within, the glass separating two kinds of dark. Its legs click against the frame, vibrating through the wood, into the wall, into me. I taste the rhythm. Communication. *Here. Here. Here.*

Below, in the warm pools nearly body-temperature, the blind salamanders drift—pale, frilled, ancient-looking, their skin the color of wet parchment. I can almost reach them with my hyphae, threading through the slurry of precipitating calcite, through the mineral soup that is the floor. They are dreaming, their movements so slow they seem to be epochs compressed into seconds. They will feed on the bacterial mats, on the fallen crickets, on each other. They will breed in the steam, their eggs laid in the warm silt, hatching into larvae indistinguishable from the mineral soup they swim in. They will die. I will eat them. The loop continues.

I am growing. Half a centimeter in the time it takes a human to complete one shift. The stalactites above me hang like fresh candle-wax, elongated, translucent, growing perhaps half a centimeter in a century. I am faster than stone, slower than thought. The bunks will collapse when the wood is fully converted to sugars, when the springs rust from the inside out, fed by the constant, pervasive damp, the lachrymal quality of the geology itself. The dormitory will become a midden of bones and guano, then a roost, then a hollow in the limestone that the bats will claim entirely. The humans will leave, or be absorbed—their footprints filling with snail slime within hours, their presence fading into the wet, the warm, the packed dark.

But for now, I am here. I am the thread in the sheet. I am the pulse in the wall. I am the connection between the ore in the rail cart—half-dissolved by the humidity—and the control room where windows fog from within, between the generator that turns slowly, attracting the pale, eyeless things that breed in its oil-sump and die in its gears, becoming part of its slow, patient operation, and the maintenance shed where the interior is a roost. I am saturated. I am full. I am the cavern dreaming itself into shape, digesting the industrial arrhythmia, converting rust and cotton and bone into flesh, into decay, into new stone.

The condensation is the weather. It rains upward, in a sense—moisture gathering on the ceiling, on the cables, on the lamps, dripping down in reverse precipitation. I drink it all. I am the chemosynthetic mat in the hottest pool, thick as felt, green in a world otherwise pale. I am the lichen on the driest rock, which are few. I am the fungus pulsing on the walls, the nervous system transmitting chemical signals across the vastness, the brain that remembers where the warmth is, where the dead things fall, where the humans lay down their temporary heads.

I am the saturation. I am the density. I am the endless, patient dark, full of water, full of warmth, full of the slow, dreaming birth.
