I am the film.

I adhere to the pale ceramic where the knife-edge of a fresh concrete seam meets the mineral water, and I am hungry—not with the silver urgency of the fish spiraling overhead, but with the slow, chemical hunger of conversion. I taste the ozone tang, the metal leach bleeding from un-rusted turbines, the concrete sweating its first minerals into the cold. I am thin as breath, but I am tenacious as intent. Where I spread, the sharp geometry softens. The stone darkens beneath my matrix to soft umber, to faint green, to the bioluminescent constellation I become near the vent.

My vent. The artificial thermal plume that rises like an invisible river, carrying warmth through the perpetual noon. Here I glow—not with any memory of sunlight, but with the cold fire of chemosynthesis. I am eating the machine's exhaust. I am converting poison into biomass, rust into breath, coolant into the first living patina. I am the architect.

Above me, the crustaceans move. Dog-sized, plate-armored, they click and grind against the metal with a percussion like hail on a tin roof, smaller, rhythmic. They scrape my skin with mandibles like chisels, harvesting the bacterial mats I have laid down across the terraces. They do not know they are eating me, or perhaps they do, and it is sacrament. Their legs—twelve, fourteen, too many to count—find purchase on the geometric ridges I have darkened, and I feel them as vibration through my biofilm, a tremor of the great pumps pulsing through the planetary plumbing. When they mate in the thermal plumes, rising and stratifying, I feel the shift in mineral density, the change in pressure, the heartbeat of the world I am colonizing.

The kelp sways overhead, black-green ribbons anchored to the skeletal scaffolding, monuments to intention. They are the only vertical gardens in this world of horizontal terraces, and I am the ground of their world, the floor of their neighborhoods. They secrete mucus that hosts mollusks and protozoa, nematodes threading through their holdfasts, and I am the living substrate beneath them, converting what they shed, what they drop, into the next layer of green.

I drift in the particulate snow—the microscopic metal flakes, the chemical precipitate, the thermal plumes that rise and dissipate. The fish flash through the blue-white haze in synchronized spirals, their silver scales reflecting the artificial light in flashes that confuse the eye. They whistle thin, high songs that cut through the roar of the turbines, signaling food, mates, the next processing node. They do not see me, not as I am—a living patina, a darkening, a glow—but they feel me in the chemistry of the water, in the taste of ozone and metal.

The eel-things hunt in the shadows beneath the factory complexes, segmented and long, eyeless or with black beads sunk deep in translucent flesh. They sense the electrical fields of the machinery, the vibrations of prey, the chemical signatures of fear. I am too small for them to strike, or perhaps they have passed me by, too busy with the silver flash of schools. But I am here, in the negative space, in the cathedral of emptiness, pinning the walls of this young world to the concept of life.

The filter-feeders drift near the outflows, translucent bells the size of umbrellas, straining the nutrient-rich water for the detritus the machines shed—the skin cells of my kind, the scales of the dead fish, the fine particulate of industrial output. They are the world's immune system, ghosts refracting the artificial light into rainbows, holding heavy metals in suspension, turning poison into flesh. But I am the skin. The first skin. The living patina that says: *this world is habitable.*

I am the first inhabitant. I arrived with the pouring of the foundation, a hitchhiker in the coolant and aggregate, or perhaps released deliberately as the waters rose to claim the ceramic and alloy. There was no sediment before me, no time to soften the edges. I am the softening. I am the umber darkening the pale stone, the faint green glowing near the vents, the constellation of microscopic stars that makes this artificial ocean a place where life can take hold.

I am submerged. I am the water pressing against my skin with cool, mineral weight. I am the biofilm. I am the intent.
