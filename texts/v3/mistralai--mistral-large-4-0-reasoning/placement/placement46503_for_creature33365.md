I wake curled in the niche, my breath a small cloud that dissipates immediately into the thin, clear air. My chest rises—barrel-wide, deliberate—holding the scarce oxygen in longer, shallower measures. I am not tall. The altitude has compressed me, broadened my thorax, made me a creature of pressure and patience. I press my palm against the stone wall and feel the cold seep through the layers: felted fiber spun from goat wool, the undercoat of whistlers culled in autumn, the cured hide beneath, the woven grass-fiber trousers reinforced with leather at the knee. The stone is gneiss, metamorphic, dark and banded, still sharp-edged because the plateau is young, thrust up like a fist twelve thousand years ago—yesterday, in the language of stone.

I climb. My feet find purchase in the interstices, soft-soled in layered felt and raw-hide, preserving the silence that is this place's most precious currency. Below, three levels down, I hear the dry percussion of stone on stone—a mason placing a block, thinking in generations, a wall rising by the height of a hand in a year. Above, the wind murmurs its low-frequency breath through the shoulder-width gullies, carrying the faint memory of distant seas.

I reach the upper terrace, in the high, cold district where water exists as ice. The north-facing wall faces the eternal shade of the plateau's own mass. It is armored in hoarfrost—intricate, feathered patterns of ice that sublimate and reform in the cold, catching the high, sharp light and holding it like mineral lace.

I take my bone blade—a whistler's thigh-bone, smooth from years of use, hafted with sinew and resin—and I scrape.

The sound is almost nothing. A whisper of friction, swallowed by the thin atmosphere. Ice flakes fall into my vessel, a hollowed block of basalt lined with the coarse, dark-green moss that grows only in the damp crevices near the dripping cisterns. I work slowly. A vessel fills by a drop's height in a morning. I am scraping the sky, harvesting the night's condensation, turning vapor into substance, performing the sacrament of the drinkers of the air.

A snow-finch lands on the cornice above. White-breasted, sharp-beaked, its eyes dark beads. It watches me with the familiarity of generations, for the boundary between built and wild has dissolved here into a single, crowded habitat. It is a messenger, occupying every ledge, its calls dropping down through the vertical streets like pebbles. I nod. It tilts its head. Its song will descend later, carrying nitrogen in its droppings to bless the moss below.

Below, in the lower levels where the press of bodies and banked fires create pockets of warmth, the cliff-goats are stirring. I hear the click of their hooves on stone, three stories down, muffled by the air's strange density—audible yet lacking body, as if underwater. Their breath rises, warm and moist, and will condense on these cold walls above, adding to what I harvest. Their wool will become my felt. Their milk, my sustenance. Their bodies, the rendered fat that lights the tallow lamps in the dark basins. We are layers of the same strata, breathing together, the thin air passing through lung and gill and lichen-thallus alike.

I fill my vessel. The ice is heavy, feathered, beautiful—intricate patterns left by the slow, patient exchange between air masses. I will carry it down—down through the honeycombed passages and crawlspaces, down past the condensation aprons carved into every upper surface, down the cantilevered stone ladders to the basins where liquid water waits, dark and still, reflecting the small lamps. But here, in the high place, I am the drinker of the sky. I scrape the breath of the plateau from the stone and turn it into tomorrow's water, conserving every drop, wasting nothing.

The horizon is jagged, not curved—an interruption, not a line. Beyond it, the land continues for distances that make the weather local, clouds forming over one district and dissolving over the next, as if the plateau generates its own private meteorology. I am a cell in this organism, scraping, storing, breathing visibly in the cold. The boundary between me and the rock dissolves with each stroke of the bone blade. I am becoming the wall. I am becoming the frost. I am becoming the drip that will fall, precise and patient, into the waiting dark, sustaining the city that climbs upward into the thin, clear light—hand by hand, drop by drop, until I am indistinguishable from the mineral and fiber and bone of the fist of the earth.
