I am the wrong pitch in a chord that has not modulated in ten thousand years.

I stand. My feet find the glassy floor—concrete worn to the consistency of river stone, aggregate exposed like fossilized bone—but I do not feel the grooves that the constant vibration has worn into the surface. My soles are smooth, wet, still bearing the memory of the pool I rose from. I look down at my hands. They are humanoid, but only as a cloud is humanoid—vague suggestions of fingers, translucent, beaded with condensation that does not run off. I am dripping. The water on my skin refuses to join the rivulets; it clings, heavy, temporarily defiant of gravity and the endless downward flow. I am the hesitation in the hydrology.

I move. It is the first movement in this place that is not a circuit, not a recycling, not a return to source. I step toward the center, and the sound of my footfall—a wet slap against the primordial bedrock—is immediately absorbed by the thunder. I make a noise, and the world does not echo it. It swallows it. I am a perturbation, a brief, sharp note in the geological drone.

I breathe, but my breath is not an inhale and exhale. It is a exchange—a taking in of the saturated dark and a giving back of something thinner, something that carries a scent of elsewhere. I smell ozone and rust and the green, perfect scent of the suspended garden, but underneath it, faint and impossible, I smell rain. Real rain. The memory of sky.

I pass the hydroponic channels. The moss and ferns do not turn toward me. They are in their state of suspended animation, photosynthesizing at the constant rate, shedding no leaves, blooming no flowers. They are the garden frozen at the moment of perfect tending, and I am the frost that has formed on the windowpane—a pattern that disrupts, that will melt. I reach out, and my fingers brush a frond. It does not bend. It is too busy being eternal. I am too busy being temporary.

I see the structures now—the towering industrial columns that support nothing but their own weight, the massive pipes that carry water in endless, unchanging circuits. They are polished to a dull, mineral patina, and for a moment, I think I see my own reflection in the steel. But the reflection is not of a person. It is of a drop. A single, trembling drop of water suspended in the moment before it falls. I am the condensation on the throat’s uvula, the bead that has decided to slide down instead of evaporating back into the warm dark.

And there, in the hollow at the center—the apse of this cathedral of industry—I see the Keeper.

It is difficult to say where the machinery ends and the body begins. The slag, poured ten thousand years ago to form the bedrock, has also formed the calloused plates along its spine. The steel of the industrial columns is the same patina that coats its shoulders, its back, the crown of its head. It sits upon a throne that is not a throne but a convergence of pipes, a node where the water’s endless circuit achieves its highest pressure. The hydroponic channels radiate from it in precise, repeating patterns—not vegetation *in* the architecture, but vegetation *from* it. The moss and ferns are its skin, its mantle, its living cloak of chlorophyll that has reached its final, perfect density.

I am the brief, sharp note in a chord that has not changed.

But I am aware of it. I have always been aware of it. I am the drop that fell from its brow, the tear that refused to join the rivulets, the condensation that climbed *up* the throat instead of flowing down. I am the outside that leaked in through a crack in the false geology, the variance that the closed system could not entirely filter out. I am the brief perturbation, the rare visitor, the one who moves and changes and will leave. I am the silence within the roar that is not silence at all, but a scream—brief, beautiful, and already fading into the equilibrium.
