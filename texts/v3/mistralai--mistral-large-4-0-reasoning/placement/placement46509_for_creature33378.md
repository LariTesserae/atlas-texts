I am suspended between levels, my body flattened against the inclined ramp, sliding downward in controlled descent. The limestone is cool against my cheek—1.4 on the scale that measures earth’s memory—cool enough that my breath clouds before me, dissipating into the grey-green gloom. I do not use the ladders. Those are for the hurried, the young, the ones who have not yet learned that patience is a form of friction.

My fingers find purchase in the chisel-marks, those raw vertical scores left by the first masons when the roof of the world cracked and let the light in. The extra joint in each finger bends backward, locking into the geometry of the collapse like a key in a lock. The webbing between my thumb and forefinger stretches, translucent and bracing, as I press my palm flat against the stone to feel its trembling. I do not wear gloves. Leather or fiber would deafen me to the stone’s slow, geological heartbeat.

I am a Mason. My hands are mapped with scars from bronze blades, the palms wide and heavily callused, stained with the flour of freshly broken rock—bone-white dust that has settled permanently into the whorls of my fingerprints, refusing to wash away. I carry a chisel, but I do not strike yet. I am listening. The stone remembers being whole, and it resents the intrusion. When I place the bronze against the limestone, I wait for the resonance, the hum that travels up my arm and settles in my sternum, telling me if the rock is solid or hollow, willing or resentful. Above me, the sky is not sky but a pale, rectangular bruise of light, framed by the rim of broken stone where the surface world still exists—grass and soil and the ordinary horizontal plane. I tilt my face upward, my vast pupils swallowing the diffuse grey-green glow, tracking the movement of that light across the rim, though I am not yet old enough to sit in a niche as the Watchers do. Not yet turning to stone.

I am compacting. The vertical life pulls me inward toward my center of gravity. My legs are short and powerful, built for climbing rather than walking. My torso lengthens. I move sideways on the wooden walkways, crab-like, one hand always touching the wall, the other extended for balance. My skin has taken on the color of the unweathered limestone around me—pale, unmarked by sun, veined with blue that shows through like cracks in porcelain. Electric-green moss grows in the creases behind my ears. Silver lichen crusts my collarbones in maps of rust and pewter. I am beautiful in the way raw geology is beautiful—sharp, unfinished, still becoming.

The silence is profound, a 0.4 on the scale of sound, a hush broken only by water. The dripping never ceases—a steady, meditative percussion, each drop striking stone or pool with the sound of a tiny bell. I hear a child three levels above drop a stone into the depths. I count: one, two, three. The splash ripples upward through the settlement like a question, and I do not answer. Voices are muffled here, absorbed by the packed density of the place. We speak in low tones, or in signs, or in the language of touch—palms pressed against cool stone, listening to its settling, to the tiny sighs of flakes detaching from high walls.

Below me, where the mist gathers and never fully clears, the Deep-Born tend the forges in the deepest, warmest chambers. I smell the iron when the wind shifts through the upper fissures—metallic, clean, precious—thin threads of smoke finding their way to the surface through natural chimneys in the rock. They speak in clicks and sub-vocal tones that resonate in my chest rather than my throat. I am not ready for the dark, for the heat, for eyes gone black and milky with cataracts. I still need the grey-green light, the fragments of sky reflected in the shallow ponds at the base of the walls, where pale, eyeless fish move in slow, translucent circuits.

The air tastes of wet mineral and old dust. Occasionally, a flake of rock detaches from a high wall with a sound like a sigh, whispering down through the seven levels to be caught by a net or vanish into the talus below, where willow and scrub alder have taken root in the cracks. I am building upward, always upward. Each season, new platforms extend from the cliff faces, and I carve the chambers behind them, following veins of softer rock like bloodhounds following scent, expanding the Hollow’s capacity with hand tools and simple fabrication.

I am becoming the Hollow. My bones are denser, mineral-heavy, drinking in the calcium from the condensation that beads on the north-facing walls in constellations of droplets. My voice has dropped to a register that vibrates in my sternum, matching the frequency of the limestone chambers. I no longer walk the horizontal plane; I flow. I dream of falling—not the terror of it, but the ecstasy. The three-second descent. The soft percussion of stone on water. I wake with my hands already reaching for the wall, fingers finding the next handhold before my eyes open.

I am not quite human, if I ever was, and not yet something else. I am the first citizen of the wound, sliding down the inclined ramp toward the mist, reaching upward with pale hands toward a light I have only just learned to catch.
