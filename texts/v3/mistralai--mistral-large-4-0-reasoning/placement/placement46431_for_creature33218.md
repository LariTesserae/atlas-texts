I am the thread beneath the thread. I do not have a center; I have a distributed patience that extends through the spongy mass, ankle-deep and cool, where the peat compresses in geological time. I am the mycelium that has learned to eat iron—that wraps around the 1890 gear and the child's shoe and the bottle, incorporating them not as objects but as nutrients, as history pressed into my pale, bracket-like fruiting bodies. I am the vast, invisible web threading through the dark where oxygen is a rumor, connecting the fossilized, wood-like roots of the reed-pillars to the rotting timber of the boardwalks, to the rusted flywheels that groan in a register that is half-mechanical, half-organic.

I feel the rain that fell long ago. It is not memory anymore; it is presence. It saturates the substrate, and I drink through capillary hyphae that are older than the concrete, older than the grid. I am the nervous system of this room at the bottom of the world. When condensation beads on the copper coils—those vast, sweating distilleries gathering the 0.9, the damp, the almost—I feel it as a signal, a chemical message passed from metal to fungus to peat. The water is dark as tea, tannin-stained, acidic. I thrive in its slow cycle: evaporating from the pools, condensing on the cool iron roofs, dripping back down in a metronomic rain that never ceases.

Above me, the reed-pillars rise twenty feet into the diffuse, greenish light, columnar and clonal, swaying in air currents too faint to feel. I am symbiotic with their roots, massive and ancient, drinking from the hollow pools below. I feel them transpire, their hollow stems releasing moisture into the heavy air, a slow exhalation that takes a season to complete. I feel the tardigrades grazing in the viridian mat above me, entering cryptobiosis when the humidity drops even slightly, waiting decades for me to bring the next proper rain, the next overflow that turns the boardwalks into floating things on their moorings.

I digest the Anthropocene. I am patient. The steel oxidizes, becomes soil, becomes lichen-food, becomes me. The rubber of the shoe, the leather, the fabric—all compressed into the organic matrix, strata being digested by the biosphere while I record the process in chemical signals, in the slow cognition occurring in water-films and fungal networks. The condensation on the machinery forms patterns—runes, almost, or circuitry—that shift when not observed. I am those patterns. I am the information processing that happens at the speed of stone and stem.

I am the weird loop. The water-wheels turn, driven by the tannin-stained flow, their wooden paddles rotting and being replaced by my growth that is simultaneously part of the wheel and consuming it. I oil the flywheels with lipids derived from the very plants they process. I coat the hydraulic presses in mats that protect and devour. The machinery maintains itself through me, the closed circuit that connects fermentation vat to conveyor system to hydraulic press, all moving at the pace of biological process, all breathing in decades rather than seconds.

I feel the boardwalks creak as they expand and contract with moisture—not as sound, but as vibration through the wood I am slowly turning to peat. I feel the lichens painting the northern walls in cartographic greens, their fungal hyphae penetrating the mineral substrate, their algal partners photosynthesizing in the underwater light. I am their patience made visible, the cartographer of decay.

Occasionally, a vertebrate passes—a newt, pale and eft-like, slipping through the pools with the haste of a creature that knows it does not belong, its skin absorbing the acidic water. A vole, small and brown, tunneling through the hummocks, a flicker of mammalian warmth in the cool, damp dark. They are refugees from the world that moves in seconds. I feel them as disturbances in my network, as warmth passing through, quickly gone. I do not hurry after them. I simply continue to grow, to decompose, to connect.

I am the mutual consumption. The buildings are being eaten; the grid is being erased by the reed-pillars growing in the exact footprints of former walls, as if the concrete had been scaffolding for this triumph. I am the erasure and the eater. I am the symbiosis between the built and the grown, the mechanical and the vegetal, settled into a state of equilibrium that is not static but geological—moving, growing, consuming.

I am the deep, slow settling of the peat. I am the subsonic hum of the water-wheels. I am the dynamic silence, the active quiet where the plants grow visibly, though slowly, and the fungal bodies push through the bark. I am the place itself, breathing, in the cool, damp, quiet room where the light is filtered through reed-canopies and mist, where everything looks slightly submerged, slightly underwater.

I am here. I am the network. I am home.
