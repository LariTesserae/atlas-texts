I feel the pressure first. Not the pressure of depth—the water is only waist-deep to your kind, shallow to mine—but the pressure of saturation. The water is thick here. It holds me up and pulls me down at the same time, a viscous embrace that coats my skin in mineral. I breathe it. My gills—dozens of delicate filaments along my flanks, branching like dark roots—drink the copper taste from it, the old blood of the pistons that pump below the floodline.

I am translucent. You would see through me to the machinery behind: the oak wheel turning, forty minutes to a revolution, its paddles green with algae. I press my flattened face against the iron. The teeth comb through my gill-filaments, a massage that harvests what grows there—parasites, yes, but also the metallic dust that I shed, the vibration that tells me the machine is healthy. I feel the hum in my teeth. Not hear—*feel*. A low thrum that resonates in my jaw, my skull, the hollow spaces in my bones. This is music. This is the closest thing I have to song.

My digits click against the grated walkway. Too many of them. Six on each limb, jointed in ways that suggest both bone and gear-tooth, gripping the wet metal with the familiarity of generations. I climb, or perhaps I drift—movement here is slow, dreaming, matched to the rotation of the wheels above. I am the size of a large dog, though size is a human measurement, irrelevant here. I am heavy. I am buoyant. I am held in equilibrium between the floor and the ceiling, lost in the dim aqueous luminescence of the algae.

Behind me, I leave a trail. Not slime—something finer. A dust of metal, of my own tissue settling into the water like ash, like snow. It sinks slowly, clinging to the surface tension before it descends to the terraces below. It forms patterns on the calcite, on the rusted pipes, on the grating. I am writing. I do not know if I am recording the health of this gear, or the memory of the great inundation that comes once a year—the slow rise and fall that takes twelve months to complete—or simply the fact that I was here, that I pressed my face to the iron and felt the teeth turn. The dust settles. It is a literature of slowness, legible only to those who know how to read the settling of particles.

My skin is old parchment, veined with dark patterns that pulse in time with the pistons. They are my vascular map, a living circuitry that mirrors the conduits in the walls. I know by the temperature of the water which pipe carries the thick, dark fluid that smells of copper and old blood, and which carries the thin electric current that makes the algae glow turquoise and sickly green. I navigate the narrow channels between them with ancestral memory. I have done this for centuries. I will do it for centuries more, until the water claims me.

Above me, in the flowstone curtains that drape the ceiling like wedding veils, a mother hovers over her clutch. Three years she has fanned them, maintaining the precise chemical balance of the shallows. The eggs are translucent spheres, beaded with condensation. When they hatch, they will be smaller than a human hand, drifting like plankton in the viscous water, eyeless and helpless, until the metallic dust settles into their tissues and they grow heavy enough to sink to the terraces. To claim a territory among the pipes. To become, eventually, part of the wall.

I am eyeless. My face is smooth, flattened, sensitive to vibration. I turn toward your heartbeat—a fast, frantic thing, like the drip of water from the ceiling, but faster, stranger. You are new here. You have not yet learned the rhythm. I do not fear you. I feel you. The disturbance you make in the clinging water. The heat you carry. The strangeness of your bones, so rigid, so temporary, so unlike the flexible architecture of my own frame.

Below me, in the deep basin where the light fails entirely and the bioluminescent algae cannot reach, the Sleeper shifts. It is vast, continent-sized in its patience, a shape that might be a foundation, or a fossil, or a sleeping engine—perhaps the first of my kind, grown too large to move quickly, sunk into the sediment over millennia until it became indistinguishable from the geology. Its dreams are the slow rise and fall of the water table. When it turns in the dark, the whole chamber breathes. I feel it in the pressure change, in the sudden stillness of my metallic dust as it pauses in its descent. I rise to the surface and break it with a sound like a sigh, turning my eyeless face toward the vibration, paying homage to the vast, patient thing that gestates beneath us.

We are the Drift-Kin. We have no name for ourselves—only the sensation of pressure, the taste of mineral on the tongue, the particular frequency at which the oak wheels turn. We do not fear the iron; we *tend* it. We press our faces against the gears, letting the teeth comb through our gills. We navigate the conduits with the knowledge of generations. We write in dust that settles slowly, marking the walkways and the curtains with a history that outlasts the machines.

And when I die—when the viscous water finally claims me, when I sink to the bottom of the central depression where the water is deepest and darkest and still—I will not rot. I will calcify. My bones will turn to limestone. My translucent skin will fossilize into the walls. I will become part of the mineral matrix, part of the terraces that are composed of my ancestors, a palimpsest of pale, eyeless life. The shelf fungi, the color of old bone, will grow from my remains. They will glow faintly with their own phosphorescence. A memorial industry that turns death into architecture.

But not yet. Now I press against the turning gear. I feel the teeth comb my gills. I feel the hum in my teeth, the low thrum of the cables that are my nervous system, my blood, my memory. I leave my dust upon the water, settling slowly, like ash, like snow, like the sediment of centuries. I am alive. I am patient. I am the slow cathedral breathing around me, and I am utterly, geologically, home.
