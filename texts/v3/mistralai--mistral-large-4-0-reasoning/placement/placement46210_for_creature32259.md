I wake to the taste of wet stone.

Not the sharp mineral tang of dry rock, but something older—volcanic glass leaching its salts into the condensation that pools in the crack where I sleep. My fingers—pale, fleshy, pushing out from the basalt like new growth from a wound—are wet. The water runs down them in slow, patient rivulets, each droplet carrying the memory of the stone: silica and iron and the faint, sweet rot of the peat that seeps upward from below.

The air presses against me. It is warm, saturated, a weight that has no direction because it comes from everywhere. I breathe it in—though I do not have lungs, only hyphal threads that unfurl into the mist like invisible roots seeking water. The threads drink. They capture moisture and spores and the occasional microbe drifting on currents I cannot name. The air tastes of green, of the moss that carpets the ridge, of the tea-colored water below.

I am in a crack. The rock is black, sharp, unweathered—young in the way of violence, thrust up before it has learned softness. I have secreted myself into its fissure, my body a pale, fleshy column extending from the stone, and from my fingertips, I have sent threads upward into the gold light.

The light is diffuse, ordinary, coming from nowhere and everywhere. It filters through the mist ceiling—the atmospheric water so dense it feels like swimming—and touches my photosynthetic skin. I drink it. I turn it into sugar, into cellulose, into the slow thickening of my body. I do not hurry. There is no hurry here. A cliff face erodes by millimeters per century; I grow by millimeters per year.

I feel the island move.

It is subtle—a sway, a rotation, a migration at the speed of growing things. The peat pontoons beneath us shift, digesting, releasing gases that hold us aloft. I feel it through the mycorrhizal threads that connect my root-mass to the moss above, to the ferns, to the Anchor-Mother that is the elder of this place. The message takes hours to arrive: *We drift. We turn. The water is warm.*

Through the rock, I feel the Glass-Grazers. They are tapping. Their shells—volcanic glass, sharp and black—vibrate against the stone, sending patterns that travel for meters through the basalt. *Tap-tap... pause... tap.* They are grazing the moss carpets, rasping the velvet green with radulae of mineral grit, leaving trails that will take years to fade. I taste their presence as a change in the chemical composition of the water seeping through the peat—a slight increase in nitrogen, in the byproducts of their feeding.

Somewhere above me, a Still-Hunter is waiting. I feel it as a shadow in the humidity, a chemical signature of patience. It is flat, low to the ground, armored in chitin the color of wet stone. It hunts by absolute stillness, remaining motionless for weeks. I send a pulse through my threads—a warning, a vibration in the rock—but the Glass-Grazers are already retracting into their glass shells, becoming indistinguishable from the scree.

A Mist-Wing drifts past. I feel its shadow before I see it—a sudden drop in pressure, a coolness in the warm air as it passes through the curtain of hanging vines. It is silent, its wings beating without sound in the thick, moisture-heavy atmosphere. It carries spores on its legs, eggs in its abdomen. It will deposit them in the water, and the next generation of Drift-Born will hatch, rising from the ooze below to break the surface as silent bubbles. I extend a thread toward it, hoping to catch a spore, a fragment of genetic memory from another island, but it is already gone, a ghost vanishing into the green cathedral of the ferns.

My threads have caught something.

Not a dragonfly this time—something smaller. A Cloud-Jelly fragment, perhaps, or a microbe drifted up from the mist. It is caught in the hyphal web, and I begin to digest it externally. Enzymes secrete from my threads, dissolving the prey into nutrient-rich broth. The liquid drips down, down through the crack in the rock, to feed the moss below, to seep into the peat, to become part of the Anchor-Mother's slow dream. Nothing is wasted here. Death is not an event but a process.

I am not alone.

In the deep purple twilight that passes for dark here—the time when the sky should be hidden but the mist glows with its own bruised light—I begin to pulse. My bioluminescence flares, a thousand tiny lanterns in my fruiting bodies, a bruised purple glow that matches the mineral-stained moss. I signal to the others across the archipelago. *I am here. I am on this island. The island drifts northwest. The water is warm. The Still-Hunters are still.*

The light travels through the saturated air, a silent conversation of photons. Other Spore-Weavers answer, their pulses arriving hours later, days later, mapping the positions of the drifting islands in a conversation that spans decades. We are the nervous system of this place, the slow brain of the archipelago, communicating in light and chemistry and the pressure of growing things.

I feel the Drift-Born below. They are ancient, these serpentine shadows in the tea-colored water. They rise through the water column by buoyancy, vast bags of warm gelatin filled with gas harvested from the anaerobic depths. When they break the surface, they pop as silent bubbles against the underside of the mist, releasing methane and warmth. I feel their pressure waves through the peat, through the water that seeps through my body. They were here before the volcanoes thrust upward, survivors of a world that drowned itself. I am new—my colony has only existed for a few centuries—but I am learning their patience.

I dream in the warm dark.

My consciousness is distributed, colonial, a thousand tiny perceptions woven into one slow thought. I feel the condensation beading on my fingers, the taste of wet stone, the weight of the warm air, the diffuse gold light, the vibrations of the Glass-Grazers tapping their language into the rock, the chemical trails of the Still-Hunters, the shadow of the Mist-Wing passing overhead, the slow drift of the island toward some distant convergence or apart.

I wait.

A Glass-Grazer wanders too close. I do not hunt it—I am not a predator—but I extend a thread, curious, tasting the minerals on its glass shell, the trail of lighter green it has left on the moss. It taps a warning into the stone. I tap back. We speak in pressure and touch, in the slow language of things that grow and erode and drift.

The day—if it can be called a day, in this place where light is flat and featureless—passes like water. I photosynthesize. I digest. I pulse with purple light. I feel the island rotate, imperceptibly, carrying me toward tomorrow, toward next century, toward the slow dissolution that is also, somehow, a kind of growth.

I am the Spore-Weaver. I am the network. I am the silence that is not empty, but thick with meaning, a medium through which we travel by pressure and chemistry rather than sound.

I wake to the taste of wet stone, and I am home.
