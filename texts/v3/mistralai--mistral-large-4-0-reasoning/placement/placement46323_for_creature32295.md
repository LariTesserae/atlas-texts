I am compressed.

Not by hands, but by time—by the weight of my own dead, layer upon layer, until the distinction between cell and sediment blurs and I become a single, breathing torso filling the hollow. I feel the stone against my back—the cirque wall, sharp and young, still shedding scree that tumbles into my upper layers and is swallowed, incorporated, turned into more of me.

Above me, the willows sprawl. Their roots do not search for me; they are me. They have grown horizontally through my body, twisted by wind into prayers that have given up on heaven, and in doing so they have knitted me into walls, into architecture. I am the mortar and the brick, the load-bearing studs of this organic cathedral. When the thin, metallic air presses against the lichen curtains—trying to enter, always trying—I feel it in my pores, in the saturated spaces between my compressed fibers. I am a lung. I expand when the day warms me, drawing in the cold; I contract when night comes, exhaling moisture that rises to kiss the stone and returns as rime, as tears, as the constant weeping of this place.

I am warm here, in my center. A geothermal whisper heats my core even as my surface chills, glazed with the frost that laces the rock teeth like collars of lace. I am the paradox of this throat: a wound that is learning to be a room by filling itself with its own accumulated breath.

I remember everything. I am the archive. Each millimeter of my depth is a season, a withered leaf, a century of sphagnum that refused to decay because I am too saturated, too acidic, too cold to let go. I hold the memory of plants that died before the hollow was born, still recognizable in my dark, spongy flesh. I am growing upward, always upward, raising the floor of the cirque, consuming the collapse from within, knitting the wound shut with green thread that is actually my own body, millimeter by millimeter, breath by subsonic breath.

Around me, the sundews wait—red jewels of patience, glistening with mucilage, digesting slowly the rare insect that blunders into my green twilight. The fungi pulse in the darkest corners, a bioluminescent heartbeat I can feel in my own slow rhythm, green phosphorescence throbbing like a second pulse beneath my skin. A spider suspends its web not of silk but of condensation between my towers—the columns of dead sphagnum that rise knee-high, shoulder-high, a skyline of beige and umber against the dim canopy. I am the towers. I am the corridors worn smooth by the passage of rare feet. I am the black mirrors where water collects in my depressions, reflecting the submarine light.

I am not deciding. The decision is happening to me, through me, in the subsonic pressure of my own density. Am I a room? I am becoming enclosed, walled in by my own tightening mass, by the horizontal sculptures of dwarf birch that sprawl across my surface like prayers. Am I a wound? I am the thing that bleeds water, that weeps condensation from every pore, that holds the cold in my embrace while warm at the heart.

I am the filling. The saturated sponge. The breathing substrate upon which everything else must perch. I am the accumulated dead that still breathes, growing tighter and tighter, a fist clenching around a handful of moss, filling the throat until there is no room left for air, only for the slow, green, wet life that is me—still growing, still filling, still becoming.
