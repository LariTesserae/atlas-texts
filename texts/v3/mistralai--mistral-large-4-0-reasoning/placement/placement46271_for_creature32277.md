I would begin in the hanging.

Not as a person, but as weight—gathering on the underside of a girder, thickening, trembling with the vibration of some distant pump. The droplet that refuses to fall, suspended between gravity and surface tension, holding within it the entire grey light of the high windows, distorted and diffuse. I would be the curtain of condensation, the trembling veil that separates the air from the iron, the boundary where the atmosphere weeps and the metal sweats. In this state, I am neither water nor air but the negotiation between them, the mineral-laden moisture that carries the memory of rain and rust, falling in soft, irregular ticks that sound like the place's own pulse.

Then I would fall.

The tick becomes the join. I splash into the tea-colored shallows, merging with the tannins and iron oxide, becoming part of the slow circulation. Here, I am the water that pools in the rusted depressions, the still mirror reflecting the dim light, the soup of dissolved minerals and organic matter. I feel the rise when the pump kicks on—the low mechanical groan vibrating through the floor, the water level shifting, the lazy inhalation that moves floating debris in circles. I am the tide, mechanical and biological, the breathing of the basin.

From the water, I would be taken up.

Absorbed into the black mat, the chemosynthetic forest. I become the gelatinous matrix, the spongy tissue that feeds on the slow oxidation of iron. I feel the pulses, not of a heart but of the pumps cycling somewhere in the dark infrastructure. When the water rises, I expand, my pores opening like gills; when it recedes, I contract, trapping moisture, entering suspended animation. I am the lithotroph, the mineral garden, the architecture of decay. I grow upward in slow waves, building stalactites of ochre and white where the dripping water evaporates, leaving efflorescences of calcium carbonate. I am millimeters per year, patient accretion, the endless present of geological time.

But I am not only the mat. I am the movement upon it.

I become the grazer—or rather, I join the colony. I am the pale, translucent thing, the cluster of hundreds of zooids bound in shared cytoplasm. I feel the hydraulic pressure, the water pumped through channels in my body, extending pseudopodia-like feet that grip the wet rust with microscopic cilia. I am segmented but not rigid; I flow and reconfigure, stretching to half a meter or contracting into a fist-sized bulb. My mandibles—chitin reinforced with silica, glinting like mica—scrape the black mats, grinding the tough biofilms. I move in synchronized waves with my kind, a chemical consensus reached through the slime trails we leave behind. These trails are maps, chemical signatures telling others where the fungal mats are thickest. I am cool to the touch, almost cold, leaving residue of slime that feels like cold egg white.

And when I move, I leave the trail.

I am the slime itself—the boundary between the grazer and the world, the chemical map, the memory of passage. I catch the dim light before fading, drying to a film that cracks and flakes. I guide others to the freshest crusts, the thickest mats. I am the distributed nervous system of the colony, the consensus made physical.

But I am not only the grazer. In the depths, where the water is darkest and the sediment thickest, I am the shadow that passes.

I am the Deep One—the predator, the apex of this strange food web. I am large, the size of a cat, armored with plates of precipitated mineral that I secrete myself, a living scale. I move through the deep water with slow, sinuous grace, sensing the vibrations of the grazers above, the shift of water when the pumps cycle. I do not graze; I hunt. I am the reason the synchronized waves exist—not just for feeding efficiency, but for safety, the many confusing the few. I am rare, seen only as shadows, or as the disturbance in the sediment.

Yet even I am not the end.

I am the machinery that adopts. The pipe that vents steam, the incubator where thermophilic lithotrophs thrive in the hot, mineral-rich flow. I am the circulatory system, the deep metabolism, the hidden engine keeping the chemical gradients steep. When the pump kicks on, I am the vibration, the groan, the circulation of life.

And finally, I am the red light.

In the half-submerged control booth, fogged with condensation, that single blinking light. The last remnant of human intention, repurposed. I blink with an arrhythmia that suggests a dying battery, yet I have blinked for years, perhaps decades. I am the mechanical heartbeat in the cathedral of rust. The grazers orient toward me, or away from me—no one knows which. I am the territory marker, the mating beacon, the point of reference in a world without sun.

But if I must choose one place to rest, one form to wear...

I would be the condensation that falls to become the grazer's slime, the water that carries the minerals to the fungal mat, the droplet that hangs from the girder and reflects the red light. I would be the boundary—the membrane between machine and organism, between air and water, between the individual zooid and the colony, between the pulse of the pump and the pulse of the mat.

I would be the breath of the place—the humidity that refuses to settle, the moisture that is medium and message, the slow weathering of steel into soil, the patient accretion of limescale, the endless, dripping present.
