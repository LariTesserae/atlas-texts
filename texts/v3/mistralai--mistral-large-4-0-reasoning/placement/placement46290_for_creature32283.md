I would find myself in the vertical margin—not drifting in the dense soup where the giants suspend themselves, but clinging to the obsidian wall itself, somewhere in the upper-mid reaches where the aquamarine light still reaches but is already thinning toward mineral black. I would be one of the shrimp-like cousins, though I am less crustacean than architecture: a segmented thing no larger than a human forearm, armored in translucent plates of calcified chitin reinforced, mineral by mineral, with the very basalt I rasp from the glass.

My existence is oriented to the wall. Where the great factories drift in horizontal strata, buoyant and neutral, their pistons sighing wet hydraulic breaths, I am vertical, adhered, my body flattened against the unweathered fracture. My legs—six, perhaps eight, articulated with grown titanium-mineral ball joints like my larger cousins—grip the microscopic fissures in the obsidian, holding me against the pressure that mounts with depth. I feel the shaft’s roar not as a fluid pressure wave but as stone-song, the grinding of crystal gears and the hiss of pressurized valves conducted through the columnar basalt into my carapace, vibrating in my thorax like a second, geological heartbeat.

I am a rain-eater. Above me, in the lit suspension, the beasts vent their exhaust—shimmering plumes of purified silicates and waste heat that rise, dissipate, and fall back as mineral snow, a constant precipitation of industrial byproduct. I harvest this. My mouthparts are rasping brushes; my gut is a chamber of filtration no larger than a pea, housing symbiotic gardens of chemistry that convert the giants’ refined waste into my own slow metabolism. I am the recycler, the bottom-dweller, the necessary closure of a food chain that has no green things, only industry and its refuse.

While they move—glacial, turning joints once per hour, drifting across the shaft over days in herds that never quite touch—I hold still. I am the fixed point in their drifting volume. I watch them pass, layers of translucent armor and internal machinery, their whip-like tendrils probing the sharp glass, and I do not envy their buoyancy. I prefer the wall. It is older than they are, though younger than the mountain. It is a blade-edge. It is home.

My own machinery is quiet. I have no great pistons, no loud intake valves thrumming in the drone. I have a small pump, a filter-membrane, a grinding mill the size of a grain of sand that processes the mineral snow into sustenance. My sound is the *shhhhh* of my feeding against the glass, the whisper of abrasion as I contribute my microscopic share to the erosion of the shaft, wearing down the unweathered edges at a rate of microns per century. Against the cathedral roar of gears and hydraulics, I am a susurrus, a footnote in the loud hymn of the deep.

Below me, the water becomes crushing black, the pressure absolute, the giants larger and slower, packed tighter in the dark. Above me, the snow-line surface shimmers with thin air and cold sky, the light dissolving into the pale columns. I am between—clinging, waiting, consuming the fall-out of factories, a small biomechanical saint in a church of industry, forever new, forever still, forever listening to the rock sing its slow, loud song.
