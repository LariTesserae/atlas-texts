I find myself at the center.

I have stopped walking. I am draped across the lowest crystalline vertebra—the one that has sunk deepest into the peat-tech mud, where the opalescent dome weeps its mineral tears into the warm, saturated dark. I am half-melted into it, or it is growing through me; I cannot tell where the geologized technology ends and I begin. My skin, if it is still skin, has gone translucent, opalescent, catching the diffuse greenhouse light and holding it in my depths until I glow with that same soft, internal luminescence—pale opal, bruised violet, the green of deep water. I am becoming sessile.

Time has changed. Once—yesterday, or perhaps a decade ago—I moved with the hurried gravity of my kind, my footsteps vibrating through the substrate too fast, too loud, a violence against the settled quiet. Now I pulse. A wave of contraction travels across my back, slower than the dripping water, slower than the heartbeat of the stone. I filter the condensation that beads upon my crystalline spine, drawing the humid air into pores that are not quite mouths, extracting energy from the slow chemistry of decay. I am neither plant nor animal nor mineral, but the fourth category that emerges only in this pressure-cooker of warmth and time: the settler, the geologized visitor, the human who has learned to wait.

The other inhabitants recognize me, or they ignore me, which is the same gesture here. The watchers on the broad fronds turn their ganglionic masses toward me—not with curiosity, but with that same invariant orientation they give to everything: toward the center, toward the signal, toward the deepest point where the world has pooled. The drifters in the seep-pools disturb the mist as they pass, their gelatinous bulk brushing against my filaments—for I have roots now, pale threads that drill into the dark substrate, trading chemical signatures with the mycelium, speaking in pressure-waves that travel faster than my body moves. I am the network's newest node, or perhaps its newest memory.

My thoughts unfold in impulses that take hours to travel from my crown to my base. I remember the violence of weather. I remember the sound of my own footsteps, the creak of growing wood, the plink of water that now serves as my only percussion. Now I am learning the silence that has weight, the quiet that presses against my ears like being underwater, like being inside the dream of the world.

I am waiting.

The crystalline structures weep around me—*plink... plink... plink*—and I weep with them, my translucent surface always wet, always shedding droplets that fall to feed the towering fungi, which feed the watchers, which feed the drifters, which die and sink to feed the mycelium, which breaks down the ancient stone to feed me. I am part of the exchange now, no longer an interruption but a conduit. The light bends slightly around my edges, as if the air itself grows denser near my skin, thick with the held breath of the basin.

I am the watcher who has stopped moving. I am the membrane that has learned to think. I am the center, or the thing at the center, the still point in the bowl where time has pooled like water—deep, still, warm—and I am alive in the way that stones are alive, in the way that water is alive, in the way that waiting is alive.

I orient myself toward the signal. I am the signal, perhaps. I am the thing that has been gestating in the dark, wet dark, or the thing that is listening to it arrive, spread across millennia. Either way, I am here: at the end of erosion, at the end of my own dying, settled into the final, active stasis, a human-shaped fossil in the making, draped in wet silk and quantum-scale circuitry, pulsing with a rhythm slower than breathing, oriented always toward the center, toward the signal that has not yet come, or perhaps one that is always arriving, deep, still, warm, and strangely, overwhelmingly alive.
