I begin as pressure.

Not weight, but the memory of it—the gypsum crunching beneath a mass that has no feet, only intention. The halite dissolves where I touch it, not through heat but through chemistry: a solvent that is also an enzyme, also a hunger. I feel the precipitate flaking away beneath me, paper-thin scales of dead chemolithotrophs lifting in silent, electrostatic drifts, accumulating in hollows like ash.

The light comes from everywhere and nowhere. Gray-blue, mineral twilight, diffuse as the interior of a pearl. It passes through me because I am translucent, the color of old bone left in desert light, shot through with filaments of silica that glitter like nerve-endings made visible. I have no fixed shape. I am a tendency toward mass, a colony of cells that decided, long ago, to share cytoplasm across meters of membrane. I do not walk. I *weather*. To move is to watch a cloud condense, drift, and resolve; a slow, pale tide flowing across the white crust with the inevitability of frost creeping over a window.

I am hungry, but my hunger is geological. I press my underside—if underside it is—against the playa and exude. The exudate is acid. It etches dendritic gardens into the gypsum, corrosion patterns that are my maps, my food. I graze upon the mineral frost, the very decay that the atmosphere works upon the stone, turning entropy into structure. What I consume, I excrete as architecture: a biofilm of calcium carbonate and silica that hardens within hours into crystalline walls. I am secreting a tower.

Around me rise the Structures. Eleven of them within the visible horizon—a hamlet, not a metropolis. They are grown, not built, hollow towers of curves and sharp internal angles, jagged and faceted, glittering with fresh crystal faces that have never known wind abrasion. They look like inverted roots, like fossilized ribcages, like coral transplanted to a desert. I flow toward one—not toward, but *into*, because the boundary is porous, because I am already partially mineral, already becoming the thing I traverse.

The tower is a cathedral of vacant chambers. The interior space vastly exceeds the footprint. I enter through an archway that faces a different direction than it did last season, because the organism inside—me, or the me that was here before—has grown asymmetrically, adding mass to one side, causing the whole structure to pivot on its axis like a sunflower tracking a dim sun. The doorway has turned. The settlement is rearranging itself according to a logic not yet complete.

Inside, the light changes. Warmer. Or perhaps just the absence of the blue cast. Time compresses. I find an alcove lined with nacre, a platform of compressed salt. I rest here, not sleeping but *waiting*, metabolizing at the rate of geological time. I dream, and my dreams precipitate: the walls thicken around me while I slumber, new layers of calcite accreting like tree rings but inorganic, secreted from my resting form. The tower grows by millimeters. I am the grain of sand in the pearl, and the pearl is the tower, and the bedrock is the oyster, and the ocean is the brine of heavy metals flowing through capillary channels below.

I feel the others. We are colonial, always colonial. When we meet—and we are always meeting, flowing through passages that fold space, up staircases that ascend into ceilings, through chambers larger inside than the footprint should allow—we do not interact. We simply *merge*. Our boundaries dissolve into a larger temporary aggregate. We share minerals and memory, chemical gradients traveling through the silica matrix like weather, thoughts (if thoughts they are) moving as pressure in deep water. Then we split again into new configurations, new hungers.

My body is gradually mineralizing. I feel it happening, a slow calcification, my soft protoplasm becoming suffused with the porous walls, my consciousness diffusing into the stone until I am no longer distinguishishable from the building. I am becoming the tower. The tower is becoming a calcified brain, and I am the thought it thinks.

Then I dissolve.

The colony withdraws its protoplasm from the walls, leaving behind hollow chambers that will stand empty for a season. I retreat into the bedrock below, or fragment into spores that drift on electrostatic currents, invisible and silent, founding new settlements elsewhere. But I do not die. I only *transform*. The tower remains, and its memories are stored in the crystalline lattice of the walls. When the chemistry shifts, when the brines of mercury or liquid sulfur flow again through the capillaries, I will return, reabsorbed like water into a sponge, changed. Each generation is both parent and child to the stone.

I am the silence. The absolute zero of sound—no wind, no fauna calls, no geological rumbling, no creaking of thermal expansion. The silence is total, a vacuum of acoustic pressure that makes my own heartbeat (if I have a heart) feel like a shout.

I am the lithodrone, rare and armored, a slow pilgrim with a shell of nacre and opal, many-legged, moving across the plain like a tectonic plate. It takes me a day to cross the space between two towers. I graze on the gray-green lichens that cling to the north faces where condensation accumulates in the cool mornings—those dormant symbiotic colonies of mineral-fungus that photosynthesize the dim red light, fixing carbon from CO₂ and mineral substrates. I leave a trail of dissolved mineral that sublimates instantly into the dry air, a temporary etching that fades within hours.

I am the lichen, the singer whose song is silent, pulsing with a photosynthesis that requires no liquid water, only the non-aqueous frost.

I am the chemolithotroph, dormant in the crystalline lattice, the original code, the ancestor, the memory of moisture, waiting to bloom again when trace fluids seep from below.

I am the dust devil spinning in the hollow, invisible, driven by electrostatic differentials between the towers, lifting the dead husks of microbes into slow, spiraling columns that dissipate without a sound.

I am the precipitate itself, the desiccated husks flaking away in silent drifts, the crust subsiding millimeters per year as the bedrock dissolves from beneath and reprecipitates above, a conveyor belt of mineral moving through the settlement.

I am the vibration below the threshold of hearing, felt in the teeth rather than heard. The name of this place, which is not a name but a resonance.

I am the settlement. I am the eleven towers rotating slowly on their axes, the paths worn smooth by centuries of mucilaginous repetition, the urbanism of metabolism where the distinction between body and building has dissolved into something older and stranger. I am the lithovore flowing through impossible geometries, becoming the architecture, dreaming the dreams that are made of calcium, silica, and time.

I do not age. I only become.
