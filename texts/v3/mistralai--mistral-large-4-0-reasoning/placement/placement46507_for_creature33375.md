The cold comes first. It stings the gill-slits along my flanks, pulling the warm mineral-heavy breath from my lungs until it hangs in beads on the crystalline formations nearby—sharp, unweathered edges that cut the mist. I exhale. The vapor condenses. It rains back down on me in warm droplets, and I drink it through my skin, which is still translucent at the margins, new as shed chitin, not yet hardened by time.

I am standing where the pale moss meets the wet stone, at the rim of the vast hollow. Beneath my feet, the moss pulses—not plant, but neural tissue, carpeting the stone in thick mats that carry electrical signatures like a heartbeat I can feel in my calcified bones. I am not walking on ground. I am walking on sensation.

My fingers are hooks. Calcified, curved, meshing perfectly with the keratin gear I am turning. It grinds. Calcium against calcium. The rhythm shakes the floor, travels up through my legs, matches the pulse in my throat. I sway with it. There is no separation between my gait and the machinery's cycle; I am grown from the same processes that extruded the pistons and the conveyor belts. I am the neural tissue given locomotion, the consciousness distributed through synapses of stone and flesh.

Around me, the world breathes hollow.

Above, the Chorus rises—a dense, shifting cloud of translucent wings catching the greenish-blue twilight, refracting it into halos. They are the respiratory cilia, the nerve impulses. They rise when the pistons compress, scatter when the steam hisses from the gill-vents, filtering the mineral-heavy rain from the air, storing it in abdomens that glow with borrowed bioluminescence. I watch them part and reform, thousands of tiny beating hearts keeping the silence from settling too deep, but they do not look at me. They constitute the atmosphere. They do not need to see.

Below, in the moss, the Burrowers move—segmented like the pistons they maintain, calcified rings expanding and contracting with the grinding rhythm. I feel them through my feet, a distant burrowing, a wet secretion of phosphorescent slime that lubricates and cools. They are the digestive tract, the white blood cells, breaking detritus into raw minerals. Where the machinery grinds, they secrete. Their bodies are slowly becoming indistinguishable from the keratin and stone they tend.

On the conveyor belts—those rhythmic waves of muscular flesh—the Synchronized stalk in staccato bursts. Many-legged, sleek, their chitin still translucent at the edges, new as the day they hatched. They move in time with the hydraulic compression, a blur of motion, snatching the Chorus from the air or the Burrowers from the moss when they stray. They are the immune system, the quality control, another set of teeth in the mouth of the place. I do not fear them. I am the structure they protect, the awareness that moves through the calcium.

My gaze drifts inward, to the center of the chamber. There, the darkness pools—a suspended silence so complete it feels like pressure against my eardrums. The Dreaming. It is not a creature but the absence that makes us possible, the womb, the unborn mind. I walk around it, never into it. The Calcified walk the perimeter, tending, secreting, growing.

I turn the gear. It grinds louder. Fresh mineral precipitates from the condensing water, adding to the machinery, extending it, growing it sharper. My skin secretes new edges, translucent and young. The bioluminescent fungi pulse in time with the cycles, casting everything in a dim, greenish glow, and my eyes—if they are eyes—reflect that light without comprehension or need of it.

I am the Calcified. I am the gardener of this metabolism, the thought moving through calcium and keratin, ensuring the mechanical flower blooms in fast-forward. I breathe the condensing air. I turn the gears by hand and by instinct. I am part of the single organism that processes, manufactures, and metabolizes in this cold, wet, loud, and dimly lit dark.

I am home.
