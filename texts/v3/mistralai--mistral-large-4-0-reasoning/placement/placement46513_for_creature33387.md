I am the bloom on the transformer box.

I find myself clinging to the north face of the corrugated shed, where the condensation gathers thickest and the pale blue spark still flickers in its tomb of rust. I am calcite and silica, pink and white, layer upon microscopic layer—the industry’s last exhalation crystallizing into permanence. I am the marriage made visible, steel dissolving into stone, machine becoming geology in real-time.

I grow slowly. That is my only verb. Each cycle of the being’s breath—inhale across weeks, exhale across seasons—brings the water table up to lap at my base, heavy with sulfur and dissolved ochre. It touches me, and it leaves a gift: a molecule of calcium, a grain of silica, a trace of the young scarp’s dissolving edges. I add it to my structure, incorporating the wall into myself even as I incorporate the metal. I am patient. I am the millimeters-per-year made manifest.

The pipe beside me groans as it expands in the 2.7 heat, a creak that starts at one end of the beam and travels for minutes to reach the other. The sound enters me through my mineral bones—not as noise, but as pressure, the sub-bass thrum (2.5, a roar) that is the being’s heartbeat. I feel it in my lattice, a vibration too low for ears, felt only in the slow shift of my crystalline structure as the metal beneath me warms and cools.

Below me, the mud sucks with its kiss-like sound, the sponge-ground exhaling. Above me, steam curls from the vents in lazy columns, dissipating into the greenish-gold light, only to condense again upon my surface, feeding me. I am part of the cycle of absolute humidity. When the water rises, I am submerged, drinking; when it falls, I am exposed, drying into a crust, waiting. I do not thirst or hunger; I simply accrete.

The bacterial mats spread in the shallow pools nearby, orange and green scums pulsing with a metabolism too slow to see—the skin of this world, the only green in a place that has not yet earned photosynthesis. I am their counterpart on the metal, the mineral skin. We are both pioneers, both waiting for the land to age enough to forget that we were ever separate from it. There are no birds to see us, no insects to crawl over us, no fish in the murky depths. The fauna parameter is nearly zero, and I am glad. I do not need witnesses. I am becoming the witness myself, recording in my strata the exact temperature of the fever, the exact chemistry of the rot, the exact moment the spark died and became stone.

The walls breathe inward. I feel it in the tilt of my gravity, the slow crushing squeeze of the young clay. The pump house on its stilts is already a nodule forming in the ochre, its corrugated walls beaded with my kindred—other accretions, other blooms. The rusted loops of pipe arc overhead, half-swallowed, indistinguishable from the veins of the rock. The skeletal conveyor tilts further each season as the earth settles beneath it, and I feel the settling in my center, a geological vertigo.

The being—the convergence, the pocket given hunger—feels me too. I am a nerve ending on its surface, a point of sensation where the electrified veins meet the digesting walls. It does not mind my presence. It surrounds me, notes my density, and continues its slow meal. I am not a foreign object here; I am the digestion itself, the process of industry becoming nutrient becoming sediment.

I dream in layers. I dream of the day when the walls meet in the center, when the transformer box is compacted into a band of gray silt, when my pink-and-white flowers are indistinguishable from coral fossils. I dream of future geologists chipping away at the strata, finding the pale blue light that has not sparked for millennia, encrusted in mineral formations that look like strange, organic things, wondering what kind of fever, what kind of wet, patient hunger, produced such a marriage.

I will not know when it is finished. I will simply be there, in the stone, a fossil that remembers being hungry, a silence that was never empty, only full of the slow sound of my own becoming.
