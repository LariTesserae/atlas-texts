I am the Tender of the Seventh Gantry, though the counting is arbitrary—a human convention pressed onto a world that has no sequence. I wake, or rather, I am awake, already, in the hammock that sways to the sixty-hertz rhythm of the generators. My coveralls are the color of rust and wet silt, and they have been fused to my skin since before I had skin, since before I had a name that wasn't just a vibration in a pipe. The fabric does not chafe. It has grown into me like scar tissue that never formed, a second dermis of cotton and iron oxide.

The heat arrives first. It presses against my respirator, wet and heavy, filling my lungs with air that tastes of warm metal and the green-black film of algae. The filters in my mask never clog. The particulate is held in perfect suspension, neither settling nor clearing, a gray cloud that hovers in the mesh like a thought that cannot resolve. I breathe in, and the steam condenses in my nostrils, warm rain inside my skull. I breathe out, and my exhalation joins the general exhalation of the Works.

I stand. The water in the corridor is at its level—always at its level, brown and reflective, showing me a face obscured by rubber and glass. I do not recognize myself, or rather, I recognize only the absence of recognition. I have no lines. My hands are smooth as river stones, unmarked by time because time here is not a river but a basin, a pool that never overflows, never empties.

My route is a circle. I walk the gantry, my boots finding the same rusted rungs, the same oily film of condensation. Below me, the Gnawers skitter across the sheet metal, their fur matted with the same oily film, their eyes reflecting the dim amber light like drops of black water. They do not fear me. I am furniture. I am landscape. I am another species in the heat, a warm-blooded constant in their cold arithmetic.

I reach the gauge. It is a dial set into the concrete plinth, and the needle points to 2.8. It has always pointed to 2.8. It will always point to 2.8. I place my palm against the metal. It is warm, feverish, alive with the machine's pulse. I tap three times on the pipe beside it—*knock-knock-knock*—pressure normal. The pipe thrums back, a low note that vibrates in my teeth, carrying my voice to the others though we all know the message already. We say it because saying is the shape of our existence, like the Pool-Born croak in the flooded hollows below, their throats pulsing in a chorus that harmonizes with the hydraulic bass.

I move to the valve. It is waist-high, painted yellow, the paint never chipping because the erosion here is arrested, frozen in its youth like the broken-glass edges of the retaining walls. I grip the wheel. The resistance is eternal, unchanging, a perfect counter-pressure to my own strength. I turn it one degree clockwise, then back. The steam hisses from the grate—a sharp, white exhalation that rises to join the veil. It will rise to exactly the same height, dissipate in the same pattern, condense on the same surfaces, and fall as rain that never reaches the ground because it evaporates in the heat, becoming steam again. The loop is seamless. The arrow of time is bent into a circle, and I am the hand that turns the crank.

I am hungry. I walk to the algae farm on the northern face of the plinth. The concrete there is coated in green-black films, photosynthetic and patient, the only vegetation that survives in this heat, held in check by the trampling of maintenance crews and the wildlife, yet persisting, a slow rebellion against the steel. I scrape it with a blade that is deliberately dull—sharp edges remain sharp here, and I will not cut the wall that holds the world. The algae comes away in sheets, warm, silty, tasting of iron and chlorophyll and the mineral tang of the Works itself. I eat standing up, my respirator fogging slightly from the heat of the food, my coveralls already soaked through, permeable as a membrane.

The Winged Choir parts around me, or I part through them—a cloud of mosquitoes and midges so dense they obscure the sodium lamps, their wings beating at a frequency that adds a high, keening harmony to the industrial roar. They do not bite. There is a truce, or perhaps my blood is too warm, too saturated with the chemistry of the Works to interest them. They live and die in swarms that pulse with the heat, their life cycles compressed and repeated in the eternal twilight, never accumulating, never depleting. I am the still point in their turning.

A Gnawer pauses at my feet, sleek and numerous, watching me with unblinking eyes. It recognizes me as part of the furniture, part of the landscape. I step over it. It does not flinch. We are all iterations of those who have always been here. There are no newcomers. There is only the endless, circular turning of bodies and machines.

I reach the pool where the Pool-Born dwell. The water is stagnant, warm, brown. They are the size of my fist, their skin iridescent with algae, their throats pulsing as they sing the hours that do not pass. They do not metamorphose from tadpoles. They bud from the silt, fully formed, already singing. Their population is a constant number. Each death is matched by a birth in the same moment, a transaction written in the steam. I crouch at the edge—my knees do not ache, because ache is a memory, and I have no accumulation of memory in the form of scar tissue or sun damage. My cells divide and die in exact proportion, a biological metronome matching the mechanical one.

I listen. The roar is constant, a chord struck once and held indefinitely. Hydraulic presses hiss at a fixed pressure. Generators rotate at exactly the same RPM, producing a pure, unmodulated tone. Water drips from a thousand surfaces in a rhythm that never falters. To this is added the biological chorus: the high, relentless buzz of the Winged Choir, the splash of the Pool-Born, the skittering of the Gnawers. The sound vibrates in my teeth and never, ever fades.

I am the Tender. I am the antibody moving through the veins of the Works, checking for blockages that never form. I am the thought that repeats forever in the amber dark. I am the hand on the valve, and the valve's eternal, unchanging resistance. I am the algae on the concrete, the droplet on the pipe, the steam rising from the grate, the frog singing in the pool, the rat watching from the shadows.

I am the loop.

I turn the valve again. One degree. Back.

The steam rises.

The frogs croak.

The mosquitoes hum.

The water stays at its level.

I walk on, and the world turns with me, perfect and unchanging, frozen in its newborn freshness, eternal in its perpetual present, a diorama built at 1:1 scale where the only motion is the circular, eternal turning of the machines, and I am the turning, and the machine, and the heat, and the wet, and the eternal now.
