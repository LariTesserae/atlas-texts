The pressure finds my sternum before my ears. I am standing—no, I have been standing here for years, perhaps decades, the distinction dissolved in the wet. I am the one who does not flinch. My palms are flat against the iron flank of Pump Seven, the great steam-behemoth whose flywheel turns once every thirty seconds, a geological heartbeat I feel in my molars, in the marrow of my shins, in the wet membrane of my saturated body.

My skin has gone the color of the lakebed—grey-green, perpetually glistening with condensation that never dries. Salt has crusted my hair into white filaments, my eyelashes into stiff bristles that catch the mineral mist. When I blink, my nictitating membrane sweeps horizontally across my vertical pupils, clearing the film so I can watch the steam rise from the vents. But I do not need to see clearly; I feel the city through the callused pads of my webbed fingers, through the soles of my feet planted on the algae-slicked walkway.

I am a Canal-Walker, though I walk less now than I listen. I press my chest against the Pleistocene silt between the structures, feeling the water table rise and fall like a slow, subsonic breath. The *thwoomp-hisss* is not noise to me. It is grammar. It is the only language I speak fluently. When the vibration falters in the left quadrant, I know before the gauges confirm it that a seal is failing, that the calcium carbonate is choking an aqueduct, that the wet is winning another grain, another century.

I communicate by pressing my forehead against yours. I feel the pulse in your temples, the thirty-second rhythm of the great wheels. We are synchronized. We have always been synchronized. When urgency demands, I tap a message on the walkway railing—a Morse code of vibration that travels through the steel and up through your feet: *water high, come down*. Or I write on a slate coated with waxy compound, the letters large and flowing, and send it down the drainage channel in a weighted lead tube that sinks through the warm, milky water to the platform below.

My fingers are long, the skin between them translucent and permanently swollen. I use them to grip the dripping timber of the elevated walkways, to feel for the tremors of the pale, eyeless fish in the channels, to harvest the thick mats of moss that grow in the lee of the machinery. I cultivate the bioluminescent colonies on my forearms—living lamps that pulse soft blue-green in the fog, marking me as one of the Salt-Weepers, the ones who tend the slow-growing disease that crusts over every surface.

In the grey-white light that casts no shadows, I sleep curled around a warm pipe in the upper chamber of my stilted home. The vibration travels up through the timber, through my damp clothes, a lullaby that rocks me into dreams of breathing under water. I wake to the smell of hot iron and copper, and descend into the fog-choked machine room to oil the pistons with rendered fat from the fish, to chip away at the white crust of mineral deposits with a tool that has no name in any dry tongue.

I am patient beyond human measure. I think in geological time. I do not hurry, because the pumps do not hurry, and to hurry would be to fall out of sync with the only heartbeat I know. I know the city is sinking, that the water is winning, but I have made a covenant with the wet. I am not fighting the drowning; I am learning to breathe under it.

When the Feast of the Great Wheel comes, when all the pumps synchronize and the roar shifts into a deep, harmonic drone that resonates in my marrow, I will stand in the vast, empty plaza and I will sing. My voice will rise, carried by the treacherous acoustics, bouncing off the condensing clouds and returning to me as a ghost. For one hour, I will be heard. Then the pumps drift out of phase, the white-noise roar returns, and I will fall silent again, pressing my hands to the iron, feeling the city breathe, waiting for the next stroke.
