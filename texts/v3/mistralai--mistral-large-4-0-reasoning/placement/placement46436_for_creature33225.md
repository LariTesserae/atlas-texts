The cold has weight. I feel it pressing through the soles of my feet, through the peat-carpet that cushions but does not warm. My cloak—woven lichen, grey-green, stiff with frost along the hems—crackles faintly as I shift my weight. I am standing, or I have been standing for a duration that no longer registers as time. Here, time is viscous. It pools in the hollows between growths, thick as the near-freezing water that weeps from the membranes above, always becoming something else.

I am tall. Or I only seem tall because I stand erect in a world of arches and collapses. My limbs are long, too long, jointed in ways that suggest I was designed to reach across the hollows rather than walk through them—to span the kilometers of bright air between islands of bone-white architecture where no bridge yet exists. My skin is the color of charcoal left in snow, ash-grey, absorbing the diffuse glare that comes from everywhere and nowhere. The ice above is my sky: a milky dome of ancient crystal pressing down, filtering the surface world into this shadowless brightness that casts sharp, black silhouettes of everything that grows.

I am one of the builders. One of the few. The negative space made flesh.

My hands are before me. Eight fingers, thin, sensitive, built for the weaving of fiber and the flaking of stone. In my left palm I hold a flat rock; in my right, a striking stone. I bring them together. A flake breaks away—sharp, translucent, a knife. The sound is nothing. The silence swallows it immediately, as it swallows everything. We do not speak here. The air is too thick, too full of mist and spores and the visible, hyperactive motion of growing things. We have evolved away from vibration. Instead, I communicate in pressure. A touch on the shoulder means *wait*. A tap against stone means *change* or *danger*. The offering of a flake, held flat in the palm, is a greeting, a question, a transaction. I tap the stone once, twice. The message travels along the substrate, along the lashed-fiber bridges that connect the islands of growth, reaching others who are too distant to see.

I am building. Or I am preparing to build. Before me, wedged into the hollow of a fungal pillar—translucent in its youth, now calcified and opaque with age—is a platform of poles I lashed there yesterday, or perhaps the day before. The plant has already begun to swell around it, knitting new tissue over my bindings. By tomorrow, it may crush the structure. By next week, it will be sealed inside the living mass, a chamber within the chamber. This is the way of things. Permanence is violence. I build waypoints, not homes.

I look up. The light shifts. Spore-clouds drift like auroras—gray-green and luminous—across the vast volume of the chamber. The mist rolls through valleys that are not valleys, only the empty spaces between growths. I can see another builder in the far distance, a vertical splint of charcoal against the bone-white architecture, perhaps a kilometer away. We do not call out. We will not meet. We are solitary by design, adapted to the psychological weight of space, to the cathedral with no walls but light. My eyes are large, milky, made for this glare. I watch them move with a viscosity that matches the cold, each step sinking into the peat, each motion deliberate, unhurried.

I watch the plants grow. A shoot that was finger-high this morning is now a pillar. An arch is forming above me, visible in its extension, the slow heave of new tissue calculating its own support in fractal branches. I watch for collapse. In the distance, a massive structure folds inward—silent origami, a soft fibrous thud swallowed by the porous ground. The wound will close by day's end. New growth is already knitting over the scar, a time-lapse made real.

I move. Each step sinks slightly into the substrate. I am going to the ledge where I left my coil of fiber cordage, stripped from the outer membrane of a young shoot. I need to weave a bridge. There is a chasm ahead, a hollow in the peat where jagged basalt juts through like broken teeth. The gap is too wide to reach across. I must harvest. I must cut the fast-growing shoots at the precise moment when the tissue is tender but strong, weaving them into a span before the plant grows past the need for one, or collapses under its own rapidity.

I carry no pack. I work with what the world offers at the moment of need. When I find the skeleton of a collapsed structure—a lattice of calcified, bone-white vascular tissue left behind by the silent fall—I will harvest it. I will scrape it clean, use it to haft my stone knife, to bind fiber to stone, to make the needles with which I sew lichen into curtains. I will collect the condensation that weeps from the undersides of membranes, storing it in bladders of dried plant-skin, drinking the near-frozen water that tastes of stone and cold.

I am young, too. A pioneer. A first generation. I leave marks that are questions rather than statements: a cairn of white stones on a ledge, already half-obscured by new moss; a platform that will soon be crushed; a shelter built into the chambered interior of a massive trunk, its entrance hung with a curtain of lichen that filters the bright sky-light into a dim, green gloom. I know the trunk is dying even as I enter it. Soon the chamber will be sealed by new tissue, or the whole pillar will fold into the abyss. I do not mourn this. I simply move on, leaving my tools behind—a stone knife on a flat rock, a coil of cordage—knowing that another will find them, or that the peat will swallow them by morning.

The mist forms patterns—geometric, drifting, as if the air itself were thinking. The water sometimes seems to flow upward, wicking through vascular channels in visible, slow streams against the pull of the world. I stand on my platform, feeling the vibration of the growing plant through the soles of my feet, and I wait. I am patient. I am transient. I am the dark punctuation mark drifting through the bright grammar of growth, becoming as temporary as the water, as strange and beautiful and quiet as the light that presses down through the ice.

The cold presses on. The light shifts. I lift my stone, and strike. The flake falls. The silence holds.
