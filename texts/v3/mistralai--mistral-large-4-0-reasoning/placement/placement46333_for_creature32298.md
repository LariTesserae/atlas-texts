I am the hollow between two stones, lined with wax-leaf, holding three days of condensation. But I am also the membrane that sweats it—thick, leathery, photosynthetic—drinking the dawn fog until my shoulders bloom silver with lichen rosettes and my elbows cradle ferns unrolling their lace into the dim air.

I am low to the stone floor, pressed close by three billion years of shared gravity. The masonry beneath me is warm, geothermal breath rising through fissures in the ancient crust, and I sink into it, becoming indistinguishable from the depression worn smooth by centuries of my sitting in this same spot. My eyes—wet river stones set deep in sockets—weep condensation that pools in the hollow of my throat, there to be drunk back by the moss that carpets my chest.

I do not rise. Rising is a season's work. Instead, I press my palm against the living wall and feel the city. A neighbor's step, begun three minutes ago, still hovers mid-air somewhere in the eastern lane, a pressure wave traveling through the dry-stone masonry into my sternum—a subsonic greeting, felt rather than heard, muffled as if through water yet carrying clear across the thin, warm air of the mesa.

The fog has settled. It beads on my fronds, fills the drainage channels carved into the living rock, drips from the thatch above into cisterns that are really just hollows lined with wax-leafed plants. I drink it all, turning dim light into slow, stored warmth, my skin darkening a shade as chlorophyll stirs. Around me, the vertical forest breathes. The winged things chatter in the eaves, their voices high and quick against the low, patient drone of the wind whistling through stone canyons. A scaled, many-legged creature pauses on my shoulder, its claws finding purchase in the silver rosettes blooming there. In its beak, a knotted cord of fiber—a message from the lower levels. I feel its vibration through my membrane, a bright note against the geological hum.

I do not read it yet. Reading takes a week. Understanding takes a month. A conversation here spans a season; each syllable is a slow exhalation, each pause filled with the rustle of my own growing body, the distant trill of the burrowing colonies whose honey-like secretions we harvest from the walls.

I am curating. I am being curated. The timber frames lashed with fiber settle deeper into the stone. The moss grows a millimeter. The mesa sinks microscopically into the mantle, and I sink with it, warm and wet with condensation but dry of any liquid I have not first condensed from the sky myself. I am the moss that carpets the street, and I am the foot that wears it. I am the mesa’s slow thought, dreaming in millimeters per decade, utterly still and utterly alive, a garden pressed close to the warm stone, listening to the city through my palms.
