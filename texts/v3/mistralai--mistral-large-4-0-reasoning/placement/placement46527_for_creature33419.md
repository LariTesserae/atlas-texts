I do not stand. I am angled, leaning into the updraft, my toes gripping the curved flank of a fungal column where the mineral stone has been worn smooth as a throat. The air presses against my membrane—pale, honey-colored, translucent enough that when the bioluminescence pulses from above, I can see the dark lace of mycelium branching beneath, a river system of thought and nutrient flowing from my wrists up to the crown of my head, where it breaches the skin in a delicate coronet of fruiting bodies.

I am seven feet of verticality, or I was. Now I am beginning to fold inward, to settle.

I am a Weaver of the Middle Paths. Not the Deep, where the geothermal breath is hottest and the broad-squatted smiths hammer copper into sheets amid the mineral steam. Not yet the High, where the willow-thin Spore-Wardens climb into the breathing dark to shake the gills of the giants and harvest the golden dust. I exist in the sag—the wooden walkways that span between towering stalks, lashed with mycelial rope, platforms that tremble with the slow groaning of biology. My fingers—long, dexterous, tipped with curved keratin—are currently braiding living fiber, twisting pale threads into a new bridge that will connect the garden-caps to the storage hollows.

The rope is warm. It pulses faintly with the same rhythm as my own amber blood. When I cut myself on a sharp flake of gypsum (which happens often, for the stone here is deceptive), I do not bleed red. I weep sap. Golden, viscous, slow to move in the heat. It beads on my shoulders, hardening into brittle, translucent droplets that the children will collect later to seal the storage pits, to bind the flaked-stone tools, to mortar the walls of our homes. The architecture of this place is built from the wounded and the fossilized—2.9 eons of ancient forests compressed into resin, now used to seal the living in.

My gill-slits flutter. Four delicate flaps along my neck, filtering the fog, extracting moisture from the humid dark, expelling the excess as a fine, salty mist that beads on my shoulders. I do not drink from cups. There are no vessels for liquid here. Instead, I press my palms against the pulpy flesh of a shelf fungus and *suck*, drawing condensed water through specialized pores in my hands, feeling it travel up my arms, into the mycelial network, distributed to where it is needed. The taste is mineral, earthy, slightly sweet. Like the stone remembers being forest.

The sound is constant. The roar of the swarms—beetle-things the size of thumbs clicking their chitin, parchment-winged dragonflies humming, clouds of spore-mites hanging in the air like visible pollen. They brush against me, a density that fills the mouth when I breathe, that darkens the light when they move in coordinated waves. I speak, but my voice is not just sound. It is vibration. A tremor in the chest that resonates through the shared web. My words travel as chemical pulses, faster than speech, binding me to the others in this cirque into a single, thinking organism.

I am beginning to change. The elders—they dwell down in the hollows where the stone is warmest, where the salt crust crunches softest beneath leather boots. Their skin has thickened to leather, then to translucent honey. The mycelium grows over them, not consuming but *preserving*, encrusting their limbs in living amber. They become statues—half-fungal, half-mineral—still breathing, still thinking, their eyes open in the dark, their gill-slits fluttering slow as tides, their thoughts carried by the hyphae in a slow, patient cognition that thinks in centuries.

I feel it starting. A stiffness in my joints when I rise from the warm stone. The membrane on my shoulders growing slightly thicker, more golden, less permeable. The mycelium beneath my skin darkening, spreading, preparing to breach and encase. I am not afraid. To live in the Warren is to be permeable, to be connected, to be part of the breath and the spore and the heat. There is no concept of privacy here as the surface-world knows it; to exist is to be porous, to be near-touching, to be saturated.

Tonight, when the bioluminescence pulses brightest, I will descend to the lower-mid platforms, where the air is thickest and the insect swarms roar loudest. I will sit on the curved flank of a stalk with the others—artisans, parents, gardeners—and share roasted beetle-things and squeezed fungal pulp. We will be intimate, crowded, our amber-green skin glowing faintly in the dimness. We will click and hum and thrum, our voices overlapping, rising and falling with the swarms.

I will flow downward, eventually. Into the Deep. Into the warm stone. Into the resin that waits to seal me in, to preserve me in the amber lattice of the mountain, to make me memory.

But for now, I weave. I listen to the subsonic groaning of the growing stalks, feeling it through the mycelium in my wrists. I taste the mineral content of the air. I am the middle, the bridge, the living rope suspended between the warm stone below and the endless, spore-thick dark above—flowing, always flowing, toward the amber.
