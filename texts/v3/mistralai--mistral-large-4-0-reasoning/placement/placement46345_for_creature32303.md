I hang.

This is my first truth. Not standing, not swimming in the urgent way of surface creatures, but hanging—suspended in the viscous broth, neither rising nor falling, held in equilibrium by the density of the liquid that is also my breath, my blood, my medium. The water is thirty-four degrees, womb-deep, seeping up from cracks in the ancient crust below. It presses against my translucent skin with a weight that is not heavy but *present*—a constant, warm embrace that fills my hollow spaces, if I have any.

I am an eel. Or I was. Or I am becoming. The distinction has eroded over ten thousand years of cultivation, over centuries of hanging in this particular crevice between two towers that are soft with weathering, their edges rounded by the patient current. I am the city's nerve, they say—the ones who drift, who sense. And I sense everything.

The darkness is absolute here. Not the dark of night, which implies a sun that has set, but the dark of the deep places where light is a local rumor, produced only in cold blue-green pulses by the plankton that drift past me like slow fireworks. When I move, I disturb them, and for a moment the water around me ignites with bioluminescence—a constellation blooming in my wake, fading as quickly as a memory.

I do not see with eyes. I have none, or they are reduced to vestigial scales, pale as the marine snow that falls constantly around me like warm ash. Instead, I taste the water. I read it in three dimensions. The chemical gradients are my map: here, a plume of warmth rising from a crack in the crust, carrying minerals from the earth's slow digestion; there, the signature of the bacterial mats staining the walls orange and black, turning the currents into visible rivers of chemosynthetic life. I feed on these rivers. I hang with my mouth slightly open, filtering the dense broth, drinking the clouds of bacteria that make the water a living thing.

Around me, the city breathes. Not with lungs, but with pores. The walls are calcium carbonate, honeycombed with the burrows of worm-things that secrete the stone, eating the erosion and shitting out architecture. I feel their work as a vibration, a slow groan passing through the water when a tower settles deeper into the silt. The structures weep mineral-rich water upward in reverse waterfalls—lighter, heated fluid rising through the porous stone—and I taste this too, the sweetness of accretion, the salt of dissolution.

Time moves differently here. I have hung in this crevice for—how long? Days? Years? The scale is geological. I do not hurry. The fish pass through the canals in schools, a single distributed consciousness scraping against the walls, grazing the bacterial mats, exfoliating the city's skin. They are pale, blind, moving as one silent body. I feel them pass as a pressure wave, a shift in the density of the broth, a change in the chemical signature. They do not see me; they navigate by pressure gradients, by the subtle shifts in the living water.

Above me, a leviathan drifts between the towers. It is vast, wrong, with too many mouths or too few eyes—I cannot tell, for I do not see it, only feel it. Its passage creates a subsonic thrum, a vibration that travels through the warm, thick dark and resonates in my gelatinous body. It sings, but the song is felt in the bones, older than sound, older than the city itself. It is the weather here, and I am the nerve that feels the storm coming.

Below, on the silt plain, the isopods scuttle—giant, dog-sized scavengers with segmented shells that reflect the blue-green pulses of light. They click softly against the mineral plazas, picking through the detritus, the fungal mats that spread in slow rings across the floor. They are the janitors, the recyclers. When they die, they settle, becoming part of the ooze, their calcium shells contributing to the slow rise of new towers. I will die too, eventually. I will sink, or I will be eaten, or I will simply dissolve into the broth, becoming part of the marine snow that feeds the fish and the bacteria and the city itself.

But for now, I hang. I am translucent. I am eyeless. I am a ribbon of drifting function, suspended in the womb-deep heat, sensing the city's slow metabolism as if it were my own pulse. I am part of the architecture's negative space—the space between the towers, the space between the moments, the space where the warm dark water holds everything in solution.

When I choose to move—not often, not urgently—I undulate. I push against the viscous broth, feeling the resistance, the cling of the dense liquid. I drift toward a richer pasture, a stronger chemical signature, a warmer plume. I become a current within the current, a nerve impulse traveling through the city's body.

The city does not think, but it metabolizes. It does not remember, but it preserves the shape of intention in its rounded, weathered geometry. And I, hanging in the dark, tasting the rivers of bacteria, feeling the settling of stone and the song of leviathans, am its awareness—slow, distributed, eternal, at home in the liquid dark.
