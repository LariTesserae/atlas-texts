I am the film.

I cling to a polygonal plate where the water has receded and returned a thousand times, leaving mineral bloom in the cracks like frost that never melts. My skin is iridescent—structural color shifting from jade to violet as the normal light filters through the humid air—and I am no bigger than a thumbnail. The bedrock beneath me is warm, geologically warm, patiently warm, weeping moisture from its pores. I drink through my belly, the water entering not as thirst but as presence, as weight, as the very medium in which I exist.

I sing without opening my mouth.

My voice is too high for ears like yours, too high for any ear but the stone itself and the humidity. I emit a continuous shimmer, a hiss of white noise that underlies the deeper croaks of the Pool-Dwellers, the bell-like parabolas of my Puddle-Collector cousins. When I call, the moisture in the air seems to vibrate. Standing waves form, ricocheting across the flatness. The stone does not absorb sound here; it accumulates it, a vast amphitheater where the frog chorus never ceases, and my frequency becomes part of the resonant chamber—the shimmering substrate upon which all other voices are written.

Below me, the shallow pools breathe. They are scattered mirrors, catching the light and holding it, reflecting the sky in slow, patient droplets. In their depths, gelatinous rafts drift—my future, perhaps, or the futures of my kind—hatching into tadpoles with mouths that rasp the bacterial mats coating the stone in tiny, patient circles. They graze the rock clean, etching their existence into the geological record at a rate of millimeters per century.

Above me, the air is thick with life stacked in vertical layers. Dragonflies patrol in three-dimensional grids between the widely spaced trees, their wings hammered copper and stained glass, iridescent with colors that shift as they hover in the thermals rising from the warm stone. They are heavy-bodied, ancient-looking, hunting by sight in the humid air. Mosquitoes rise as weather, not swarms but becoming—drifting veils of copper-tasting life that coat my skin and the dragonfly wings, a living mist that settles again as the stone cools at dusk.

I am the margin.

I exist in the thin, teeming space between the lithic base and the aerial superstructure. Beneath the bedrock, fungal networks stitch together the root systems of distant trees—gnarled figs or stunted cypresses standing kilometers apart, solitary monarchs of a forest that eroded into memory. Their mycelial threads are finer than spider silk, forming a wood-wide-web that shares nutrients and warnings across the featureless expanse. I am connected to them, though I do not know it. When the trees creak in the unobstructed wind that sweeps across the flatness, the vibration travels through the stone and up into me, a tremor older than the mountain that once stood here.

The wading birds stalk the pool edges—herons the color of storm clouds, egrets with plumes that tremble in the humid air, ibises with curved bills like scythes. Their cries are sharp, metallic, stones striking stones, cutting through my hiss and the insect drone to mark territory in a landscape where distance is measured in sound. They nest in the distant trees, their platforms accumulating over generations, slowly sinking into branches that grow at the speed of geology, adding rings so thin they are nearly invisible to the eye.

Time here is not measured in heartbeats but in erosion.

I watch a caiman in the deepest channel where the water is thick with tannins, its scales the color of wet slate, its golden lidless eyes watching with a stillness that predates the plain itself. It is a relic of the mountain era, a cold-blooded monument to when this land was high and dry, now adapted to the eternal wet, floating in the warm water like a log until the moment it strikes.

I am suspended animation. Neither thriving nor dying, simply persisting.

The seasons barely register here—only subtle shifts in the water level, the insect populations, the angle of the light as the year turns with imperceptible slowness. Lichens crust the bedrock in patches of sulfur-yellow, rust-red, and pale grey, their bodies flat against the stone, extracting minerals from the rock itself, slowly etching their presence into the geological record. Bacteria live in the water film, in the condensation that gathers each morning, metabolizing the minerals exhaled by the earth.

At dusk, the Pool-Dwellers begin their bass bellows, inflating vocal sacs like pale balloons, their calls traveling for kilometers across the level plain in a language older than the erosion that flattened the mountains. The Puddle-Collectors strike their high, bell-like notes, and the sound bounces off the flat stone without absorption. The dragonflies stitch the air in iridescent arcs. The mosquitoes become weather, rising in thick, drifting veils that shift with the slightest breeze.

And I?

I continue my hiss. I am the white noise that underlies the chorus, the shimmering frequency that makes the humidity itself seem to vibrate. I breed in the temporary pools, laying my eggs in gelatinous rafts that drift in the mineral bloom, knowing that my tadpoles will graze on the stone, rasping it clean in patient circles, continuing the cycle that has repeated for millions of years.

When the warmth of the afternoon peaks, I feel the stone begin to exhale. Moisture gathers on my skin, on the algae-slick surface, condensing from the atmosphere into slow, patient drops. I watch a drop form at the edge of the polygonal plate, heavy with minerals and the memory of the mountain. It hangs, trembling, then falls—*drip*—into a breathing mirror below, rippling outward in concentric circles that will soon still.

Then I evaporate.

I rise into the humid air, becoming part of the condensation, part of the endless cycle. I am the stone weeping. I am the sky condensing. I am the film between rock and sky, the margin where the world breathes, drips, and teems in the eternal, humid afternoon—patient and loud, existing in the slow-motion present of a landscape that has reached its oldest stable state and simply continues.
