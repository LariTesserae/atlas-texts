I am pressed against the stone. Not leaning—pressed, flattened, my skin mottled gray and porous like the rock after rain, because I am the rock after rain, perpetually damp, never healing from the small cuts of obsidian flakes. My ear is cupped against a node where three channels converge, where the masonry has been carved by Carvers into spiral patterns that mimic the water's own path. The stone is cool, wet, vibrating. I feel the water thinking.

My fingers are webbed, splayed against the sluice gate I am adjusting. They grip the carved obsidian edge, feeling the pressure of the flow, the scream of water forced through a narrowed aperture. The sound is immense—a white noise that drowns the arrhythmic percussion of flint on granite echoing from the upper tiers, where thousands of hammers strike in chorus, building and unbuilding the valley one blow at a time. My eyes are small, black, lidless, reflecting the blue-white pulse of fungus that glows in the condensation pools below. I have no need for sun. There is no sun. Only the scarcity of light, diffused and gray, filtered through mist that clings to every surface.

I am a Channel-Keeper. I maintain the neural tissue of the Works. The water does not simply drain here; it spirals, it branches in fractal patterns resembling dried river deltas, converging at nodes like this one. I press my skull against the stone and listen through bone, through the webbing of my jaw, through the perpetual dampness of my flesh. The valley is a body, and I am listening to its circulation—the capillary rise, the artificial cataracts, the whispering rain that never stops.

Above me, suspended in the gray murk, a condensation dome trembles. A perfect hemisphere of water held by surface tension and geometry, humming when the wind passes through the incomplete upper terraces. The wind plays the wooden scaffolding like a vast, groaning instrument, producing low, mournful tones that resonate in my chest. I must ensure the dome hums at the correct frequency. I turn the gate. Water screams louder through the sluice, a braided torrent descending toward the cisterns. The dome shivers, its surface tension singing back to me, a frequency I feel in my webbed toes gripping the flagstones, in the bowed curve of my legs, in the gait that suggests swimming through saturated air.

My cloak is heavy with water. Layers of woven fiber—perhaps the silk of the hard-shelled insects that skitter across every surface, drinking from the droplets—over hides coated in clay and cured in the wet heat of the lower chambers. The hides smell of the heavy-boned beasts that tow barges through the black water below, their wet musk permeating everything, along with the mineral tang of stone dust and the particular musk of wet clay. I move with a slow, swaying gait, like kelp in a current, my arms out for balance against the slippery stone.

Around me, the Works rise in three-dimensional lattice. Half the valley is raw geology, bleeding from bone picks and stone hammers; the other half is sealed in fired clay and timber, already slick with algae, weeping with moisture. The boundary between raw stone and worked surface advances like a slow tide, one hand-stroke at a time. I see the Fitters hunched, corded shoulders hauling blocks on wooden rollers greased with animal fat. I see the Haulers shouting relay calls—"Up!" "Hold!" "Wet!"—their voices cracking in the damp air, relayed from tier to tier. But I am at the convergence, the node, where the geometry makes the eye water—staircases that appear to ascend but lead sideways into walls, chambers that seem larger inside than their exterior allows.

Below, through a grate of woven fiber, the black water pools, still and deep. In it, something drifts. Pale, eyeless, translucent, shaped like the molted carapace of some impossible crustacean, but soft, boneless, pulsing with a slow internal rhythm and a faint luminescence that rivals the fungus. It is one of the things that drift like shed skin. The Deep-Dwellers will come for it—their skin bleached white, their bodies emaciated, living in the black water, feeding on these translucent flesh-things or perhaps worshipping them. I am not a Deep-Dweller. I do not descend. I stand at the junction, ensuring the spiral continues, that the condensation forms into perfect, trembling domes, that the machine for being wet continues its slow, grinding patience.

The hard-shelled insects rain down around me, a constant percussion of tiny legs on stone, drinking from the droplets that fall from the leaking aqueducts. They are beetle-like, shells gleaming like wet obsidian. I do not eat them now—I am working, listening, adjusting—but I know their crunch, the textural counterpoint to the soft, tasteless fungus that sustains us.

I am born of the lower tiers, where the fungal light pulses blue-white through the permanent gloom. I will die with my fingers curled around this gate, my body settling into the flagstones like additional masonry. I have no name that would translate into dryness. I am the Wet. I am the listening. I adjust the sluice, the water screams through the neural patterns, the dome hums its trembling song, and the valley breathes moisture around me, a bowl of cool air where the sky presses down like a damp cloth, and everything within it is being built by water, one wet, echoing layer at a time.
