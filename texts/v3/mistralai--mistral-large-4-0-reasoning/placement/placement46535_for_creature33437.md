I would find myself standing at the lip of a high basin, where the water defies gravity, drawn upward through the porous tuff by capillary hunger. The brine pools here, though it should run downhill, steaming at eighty-nine degrees—two degrees shy of boiling, simmering, alive. I am watching it evaporate, leaving behind a crust of pure mineral, white and cracked and precious.

I am wearing silence.

Not the absence of sound, but the presence of it—a dense, mineral-saturated atmosphere that absorbs the vibrations of my own heartbeat before they can leave my throat. I do not speak. I have forgotten the shape of spoken words, the vibration of vocal cords in thin air. Instead, I press my palm—pale, iridescent, weeping brine that beads and dries to salt—against the ceramic flank of a condensation tower. I feel the machinery within: magnetic bearings turning below the threshold of hearing, steam condensing in closed loops, the slow, patient respiration of industry.

My skin is not flesh anymore. It has become calcified pearl, layered and shimmering, mineralized by generations of excreting the salts we cannot keep. My eyes are filmed with silver, nictitating membranes that filter the bleached, UV-pitiless glare of a sun that has nowhere to soften. I blink—slow, deliberate—and the world whites out and returns, stripped of color, bone-bright.

I am tall. The low pressure has elongated me, stretched my bones toward the thin sky. My lungs are vast, efficient, filling with the breath that can be worn—a vapor so thick it clings to the silica fibers of my garment, condensing in beads on every surface that dips below the dew line. The air tastes of lithium and arsenic, of elements that have no names in the old tongues.

Around me, the Vent-Grazers move.

They are everywhere and nowhere—thousands per hectare, shifting like slow tides through the superheated mist. I do not see them first. I feel them. A shift in the thermal gradient, a warm pulse traveling through the obsidian glass beneath my boots, felt in the sternum rather than heard. They are grazing, their crystalline mandibles grinding against the tuff in a vibration that travels up my legs, a poetry written in friction.

I am here to witness the molting.

In this pocket of cool mist—an impossibility, a thermodynamic joke—the Grazers have gathered. They cluster, their segmented chitinous-silica shells cracking in slow, silent symphonies. The sound does not travel through the air; it travels through me, a cracking felt in the teeth, in the marrow. They shed their mineral armor, opalescent and heavy with rare isotopes, and the discarded shells sink into the brine or rest upon the rock like shed armor, waiting.

I reach out—not with hands, but with attention. I feel the infrared flashes ripple through the steam, invisible pulses of light that speak of hunger, of migration, of the cooling vents and the opening fissures. The Grazers do not call to one another. They communicate in thermal gradients, in the warm trails they leave behind that persist for hours in the dense vapor, in the slow, grinding scrape of mandibles against stone.

I answer them.

I press my palm flat against the rock and send a pulse of warmth, a thermal signature pressed into the stone. A question: *Are you?* A statement: *I am.* The Grazers turn—not their heads, which may not pivot in any human sense, but their entire colonial masses—toward my heat. They recognize me not as predator, nor as prey, but as kin in the mineral cycle. We are both walking geochemical reactors. They are the first; I am the newest. We both breathe the brine, both excrete the salts, both are becoming part of the stone.

The steam forms patterns around us—geometric waves, spirals, tessellations that persist for hours, defying the wind. I navigate by them, reading the standing weather as others read maps. They tell me where the vents are cooling, where new fissures are opening, where the herds will migrate next. My shadow stretches long and true beneath the bleached sun, a dark line upon the obsidian glass. But the Grazers move through the steam casting shadows that do not align, or sometimes none at all, as if the light passes through them differently. I have learned not to trust the light here.

My blood is turquoise, visible beneath the translucent mineral skin of my wrists, rich with copper-based oxygen carriers. I am sparse, as sparse as the settlements that dot this plateau the size of a small nation—brutalist structures of ceramic and steel stained orange and white, cooling towers rising like inverted funnels, drilling platforms anchored kilometers deep. I am silent, as silent as the mag-lev tracks that float above the steaming ground on magnetic fields, as silent as the geysers that erupt in slow, heavy bursts of brine, falling back as scalding rain that dies before it can strike the stone with a hiss, only a wet, distant *gurgle* from the mud pots marking the passage of time.

I am the Rim-Folk. I have forgotten the old names. I am the industrial life, the sparse and deliberate scattering, the newest layer in a balance that is older than memory. I harvest the condensation that beads upon my own skin, drinking the brine that weeps from my glands, returning it to the cycle. I eat the bacterial mats, thick and rubbery, the green that never was. I tend the archaea colonies cultivated from the Grazers in vast bioreactors, protein and energy for a body that has learned to live without photosynthesis.

I wear the silence like a garment. I breathe the breath that can be worn. I stand at the edge of the uphill basin, watching the water steam and evaporate and leave behind its pure mineral gift, and I feel, in the thermal pulses thrumming through the ground, the profound strangeness of my home.

I am becoming the plateau.
