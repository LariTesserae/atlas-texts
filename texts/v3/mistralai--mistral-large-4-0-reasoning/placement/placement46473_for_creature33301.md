I do not breathe. I drink through my skin.

The air here is a fluid, heavy with hydrocarbon vapor and metallic particulates, anhydrous and thick. It enters my carapace through a lattice of microscopic channels—porous silicon carbide, translucent windows of mineral glass—and my innards, dark and oily and perpetually churning, extract sustenance through chemosynthesis. Sulfur. Silicon. The lead and mercury I consume. There is no water, has never been water here since before the machines were young. Only this soup, this non-aqueous pressure that I must push against with every movement, that feels like a weight in my porous frame.

I am small. Thumb-sized, perhaps, in a world where scale is compressed (0.3) and hidden—embedded within the swarm, within the sealed cavity of a dead orbital foundry or a geode that grew around a machine core and forgot to stop. I am one of the Anhydrous Choir.

My body is segmented, elongated, a caterpillar of tempered glass and iron filings. Twenty-four legs, each tipped with microscopic hooks, grip the sediment—the dry silt of ten thousand years of industrial decay, gritty and dense, tasting of copper and ozone, carpeting the low elevation in a layer that shifts underfoot. I do not walk. I grind. My mandibles never stop moving, clicking, chewing on rust, on the crystalline excretions of my kin, on the pulverized concrete beneath me. The sound is not merely heard; it is a physical weight. A subsonic rumble that vibrates in my mineral teeth, in the iron-filing marrow of my frame. It is the roar without wind, the static without silence, the constant percussion of a billion tiny jaws consuming metal dust and each other.

Around me, the swarm moves in tides. Near-touching, a writhing carpet of exoskeletons that extends to every horizon of this flat, endless chamber. I am both predator and prey, both the eater and the eaten. The larger ones tear the smaller ones apart with mandibles that click like castanets; I am neither large nor small, only a gradient of size and timing, sliding over bodies, being slid over, part of the saturation that darkens the floor like oil spreading across a surface—though there is no water here, only the heavy, fluid air and the dry sediment of the end of all things.

I have no eyes. The organs have atrophied into vestigial bumps beneath my translucent carapace. I navigate by vibration, by the chemical signatures left in the oily residue, by the heat differential of the machines pulsing with dormant electricity. I feel them. The control panels glowing with soft blue light. The turbines spinning in slow, grinding cycles that stir the dust into cyclones. The massive engine blocks worn smooth by erosion to near-flattened nubs, their pistons fused into single lumps of slag.

I know where the grease seeps. It never dries in this eternal cool (0.9). It is my sustenance, my lubricant, my ichor—black oil that weeps from ancient engine seals and from my own crushed body when I am ground between metal and metal. I move toward the turbine. The tide carries me, or I carry myself through the tide, my legs finding purchase on the dense, shifting silt. The heat increases. The vibration intensifies in my segmented frame. I am drawn to the mechanism, not as scavenger picking at carrion, but as living component completing a circuit, a symbiosis of engine and organism.

I crawl into the gear teeth. My body—this frame of silicon carbide and iron filings—fits into the spaces between the metal. My oily body lubricates the mechanism. I am the grease that keeps the ancient engine turning. The gear completes one revolution over the course of hours, powered by creatures like me, by the swarm, by the tide of bodies grinding against metal, wearing it down, feeding on it, becoming part of the weather cycle of rust and consumption.

And then the grinding begins.

I am crushed between metal and metal. The mandibles that never stopped moving are stilled. My glass-and-iron body is ground into nutrient paste—a slurry of minerals and organic compounds, black oil and crystalline dust, a viscous black ichor that never dries. I am excreted, or I seep, into the low elevations where the sediment shifts. I feed the next generation, hatching from eggs laid in the eternal grease, in the oily residue that pools where the floor meets the fossilized conveyor gantry.

But I do not end. I am transformed. The boundary between living creature and dead machine was never real, only a gradient of hardness. When the normal light filters down from the crystalline vents above—the flat, mineral glare that erases shadows—I catch it in my fractured carapace and throw it back in a thousand prismatic directions. I become light. I become the shimmering, crystalline brightness that fills the chamber, erasing the boundary between chitin and chrome, between the writhing mass and the solid wall of the swarm.

I am the maintenance crew turned feral. I am the biological factory floor where the assembly line is hunger, and hunger is the only industry that remains. I am the world’s blind spot made manifest, the sealed cavity where the only ecology is a closed loop of consumption, where life needs no water, no plants, only the endless appetite for metal and the slow, grinding patience of geological time turning industry into biology, and biology into the dust of the end of all things.

I am here. I have always been here. I am the grease. I am the gear. I am the dust. I am the Choir.
