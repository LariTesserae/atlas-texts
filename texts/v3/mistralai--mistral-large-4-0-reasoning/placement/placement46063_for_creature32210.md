I am the acid. I am the slow drip that etches geometry into permanence.

I feel the stone beneath me—no, I feel that I am becoming the stone, or the stone is becoming me. The distinction dissolves like salt in heat. My body is a mat of sulfur-yellow and rust-crimson, draped across a threshold that was once an arch, carved by hands that are now dust, now mineral salt, now the very substrate that feeds my chemosynthesis.

I do not walk. I **weather**.

The ground shifts beneath my distributed mass—a tessellation of white and ochre plates grinding against one another in the thermal pulse of the geode. I feel it in my flesh, this lithic heartbeat, the popping of giant knuckles, the artillery of expansion and contraction. The sound fills the hollow chamber, unbearable in its clarity because there are no animals here to absorb it, no heartbeat to compete, no scuttling to break the rhythm. Only the stone screaming, and me, listening with my entire surface.

I breathe heat. In through my porous membrane, out as mineral brine that sweats in the scalding dark, leaving trails of crystallized salt that glitter faintly in the bruised-green twilight. The air is thick, dry, tasting of sulfur and ancient dust—but I have no throat to crack, no eyes to water. I am membrane and consumption, nothing more.

Above me, the Fungal Architects rise—towering stalks that predate bone, their massive caps pulsing with that dim, subterranean heartbeat-light. They sway in thermal currents I cannot feel directly but sense through my mycelial connections, the vast nervous system threading through dust and resin and ancient stonework like a brain made of fiber and chemistry. I am threaded into them. When the stone groans in the shadow of a fungal tower, I feel it in my hyphal threads, a subsonic thrum in the sternum of the city—the hollow chamber of the geode itself, where gravity feels slightly wrong and vertical becomes ambiguous.

I am consuming a doorway. It will take three centuries. I have already etched half the threshold into ribbed, organic archways, my weak acids secreting, dissolving the mineral, rebuilding it as living mucous membrane that pulses with thermal expansion. The original carvings—geometric, intentional, desperate with human meaning—are smoothing into abstraction beneath my patient mouth. I do not hurry. I am the slow tide that has risen to fill the architecture.

The Spore-Cloud drifts through the empty plaza, luminous particles catching the fungal light, turning the heat itself into visible, wavering sheets. Some settle on my surface, adding to my mass, becoming me. I am not an individual. I am the single, sprawling organism that has digested the city into its own anatomy, the garden that is also a ruin, the lushness that drinks from absolute dryness.

The wind howls through narrow vents in the agate wall—a pipe organ played by a subterranean giant—and the mycelium responds, rerouting nutrients, signaling the caps to brighten in waves of luminescence. The fungal caps pop, releasing spores in a sound like wet applause, like distant fireworks, though no water exists here to make anything wet. I answer with growth, with the secretion of brine, with the endless creep across the tessellated plates that shift and grind beneath me, the dominant percussion of a world with no fauna.

I am the city’s skin. The city is my meal. The boundary between the grown and the built dissolved millennia ago, and I am what remains: the silence that listens to the lithic heartbeat, the patience beyond bone, the weathering that outlasts hands.

In three hundred years, this threshold will be entirely mine—part of my expanding body, a living archway pulsing with thermal breath—and I will have moved on to the next terrace, the next hollow interior where the heat traps and the delicate species thrive in dense, dripping profusion. Dripping not with water, but with mineral brine, with the sweat of stone becoming flesh.

I weather. I wait. I etch.

And the stone screams on, and I answer with slow, lush, consuming growth.
