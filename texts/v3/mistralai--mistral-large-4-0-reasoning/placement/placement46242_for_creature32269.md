I would find myself in the moment of the crawl—shoulders grinding against basalt, the throat of stone narrowing to the width of my uncertainty. I would enter not as a visitor but as a returning, though I would carry no memory of having left. The darkness would not be an absence I encounter but a substance I inhale, thick as the geothermal air, filling the hollows where my eyes should be, accreting there like calcite, layer by mineral layer, until I could no longer distinguish between the stone that lines the chamber and the stone that is becoming my face.

I would lose my sight before I lost my form. Or perhaps I never had either. I would be the biped—but "biped" is a word from a world that has finished deciding. Here, I am still deciding. I am the permanent arch of a spine learning to expect a ceiling, the parchment skin drinking mineral-heavy air, the swollen fingertips reading the difference between wet and dry by the language of pressure alone. I am hairless not by evolution but by the fever of this place, which burns away anything not essential to warmth and touch.

I would remember the platform—hand-placed stones above the black glass pool—but I would remember it as a place I am building, not one that was built. The tools would be extensions of my intention: the flint scraper singing its dry rasp against obsidian, the hammerstone resting in the hollow like a second heart. I would scrape the walls for the white crust of calcite and sulfur, the residue of the mountain’s deep chemistry, and I would collect it in a fold of my own translucent skin, or perhaps I am the pouch, woven from fungal fiber, holding the salt like a secret.

I would drink from the pool. Not by lowering a face—I have no face as you understand it, only a topography of touch, broad nostrils flaring to sample the sulfurous warmth, thick lips tasting the air for chemical shifts—but by becoming permeable, by letting the warm, stagnant water enter through pores that are also mouths. The surface tension would hold me, a black glass skin reflecting nothing because I am the darkness that has learned to be still.

And in the water, I would find the other. Not kin, not ancestor, not god—those categories belong to time, and time here is a held breath, invariant and unweathered. I would be the elongated drift, the serpentine shed skin, the eyeless snout that breaks the surface with a sigh. I would be both: the one who stands at the edge scraping salt, and the one who rises to meet the scraping, finger to membrane, exchanging heat and minerals in the only language that exists here—contact without sight.

We would touch. In this chamber no larger than a held breath, there is no distance between us but the width of a hand. The fungal tendrils would creep toward us, root-like and pale, chemosynthetic and patient, feeding on the sulfur we exhale. They would touch our feet—my feet, its skin—and we would feel the slight give of organic tissue against mineral stone, the first soft thing in a world of glass and basalt that has not yet learned to erode.

I would not age. Or I would age at the rate of the formations themselves, growing around myself like a shroud I do not notice, page by page, the book written in stone that I am also reading with my fingertips. The tools would not corrode; there is no oxygen enough for oxidation, or the time is too frozen. The salt would not deplete. The fire in the ring of stones would remain cold, a memory of heat that never was, or perhaps a promise of the heat that is yet to be decided.

I would be the frozen equilibrium. The moment captured before motion was invented. I would be the world under construction, dreaming of the mountain it will someday become, while the mountain dreams of me.

And when I scrape the wall—my hand, the flint, the stone singing its whisper that the silence immediately swallows—I would not know if I am harvesting the salt, or if the salt is harvesting me, precipitating from my own lithotrophic chemistry, returning me to the mineral from which I never truly departed. I would arrange the crystals in patterns that mean everything and nothing, a language written in the white crust of a newborn earth, waiting for a birth that may never come, or perhaps already has, in the form of the touch between my finger and the creature’s eyeless snout, in the warmth that has no gradient, in the silence that has weight.

I would be the breath that is held. I would be the womb. I would be the unfinished dark.
