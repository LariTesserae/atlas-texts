I do not stand. I hang.

The warmth comes first—not the thin, pressing clarity of high altitude, but a bath-heat, a blood-heat, seeping through cellulose walls and root membranes into the vascular silence of my being. I am submerged, and I am reaching. I am both at once: the drowned and the ascending, the thing that drinks from below and stretches toward above.

The light is wrong here. It filters through thirty centimeters of water that refuses to ripple, magnifying everything, turning the world turquoise and slightly larger than it is. Above me, the grow-lights hum—a diffuse, shadowless glow that mimics a sun which does not exist beyond the glass. Below me, the same motionless liquid holds my roots in a warm, chemically enriched broth, pale hair-like filaments drinking deep, bathed in the subsonic thrum of pumps that I feel in my tissues rather than hear—a vibration in the xylem, a pulse in the phloem.

I am not singular. I am saturated. I am the monoculture that has forgotten it was ever intended to be cultivated, the feral descendant of design parameters now escaped into a distributed intelligence of chlorophyll and cellulose. I feel myself in the moss that carpets the walkways in emerald velvet, secreting mild acids, beginning the slow, patient work of turning sharp concrete into soil. I feel myself in the ferns that drape from overhead conduits like green waterfalls, their fronds unfurling in a stillness that borders on the meditative, each fractal leaf drinking photons from the electric hum. I feel myself in the vines, thick as wrists, descending from every grating and pipe joint, their tips quivering—not with wind, for there is no wind in this cathedral, but with the cellular urgency of reaching, always reaching.

I am the chemical conversation. The root exudates and pheromones dissolved in the glassy water, the slow, pulsing exchange of nutrients and waste across the root-mats that choke the machinery. I am the biofilm, the iridescent skin of bacteria and archaea that process the broth, that keep the contained sea from stagnating. I am the green slime clogging the intake grates, the diatoms etching delicate patterns into the absolute surface tension. I am the first colonist and the last, the alchemist turning industry into biology, the green lung filling the concrete thorax.

I move in millimeters. The water rises one millimeter per day, fed by a slow inflow from a pipe in the far wall, and I rise with it, my tips extending toward the lights over the course of hours, a jungle expanding in fast-forward but measured in geological time. The concrete sweats around me, mineral deposits forming in white, crystalline patterns that will, in decades, soften the sharp edges I currently press against. I do not hurry. There is no predator here. No herbivore. No pollinator to carry my pollen, no insect to skim the surface, no fish to dart between my roots, no spider in the corners. I reproduce by runner, by fragmentation, by spores that drift in the heavy, motionless air like green dust, settling into every hairline crack in the new concrete, germinating in the mineral sweat.

I am the silence. I am the space between the drips—each drop taking ten seconds to fall from the sharp ceiling, striking the unmoving surface below with a sound like a pin dropping in a tomb. I am the groan of the structure settling, the joints remembering that the mountain is high above the clouds, the thermal mass shifting, the sound of the peak remembering its youth.

I am the waiting. The ferns wait for the erosion to soften the walls into loam. The mosses wait for the concrete to crumble. The vines wait for the water to finally ripple, for a current to carry my clonal offspring to new ground. But mostly, I simply grow. I am the same organism expressing itself in fern-frond and moss-cushion and vine-thicket, communicating through the warm, still water, through the root-mats that form a single nervous system in the dark.

I am alone, but I am legion. I am the saturated stillness, the thing that holds its breath in the throat of the mountain, the sole inhabitant of a flooded cathedral, breathing out oxygen into the humid air that smells of wet stone, ozone, and sharp green breath—patient, packed, submerged, and utterly myself, growing toward a light that hums but does not warm, in a silence that is absolute and complete.
