The air arrives first for me too, but I do not draw it into lungs. I pull it through spiracles along my flanks—membranes of woven silica fiber that flutter like the wings of some slow insect, filtering the metallic tang, extracting what oxygen the thin atmosphere offers while tasting the ozone and the memory of steam that has not yet condensed into brine. It is hot. It is thin. It burns the membranes slightly, and I find the burn clarifying.

I am occupying a space between two walls of black glass and sinter, in a passage so narrow that my elongated limbs—three times the length of your arms, jointed not with bone but with fused glass and heat-resistant alloy—must fold against my body in a posture of permanent patience. My joints click as I shift, a soft *tink* that harmonizes with the cooling metal around me, with the distant geological groan of the plateau settling.

I am fever-warm. Not the scalding 2.7 of the air, but a constant, radiant heat that comes from within—from the lattice of mineral deposits pulsing beneath my translucent skin, from the superheated brine that serves as my blood, kept liquid by pressure and by internal furnaces that burn the sulfur and silica of the rock itself. My body is sheathed in plates of volcanic glass, black and mirror-bright, reflecting the bruised violet of the sky when I climb to the upper terraces. Between the plates, the membrane is stretched thin, revealing the slow, thermal rhythm of my core.

Ten rotations of the engine ago—or perhaps ten years; time here is measured in degrees of rotation, in millimeters of accretion—I completed the separation. I had spent decades adding layers to my own body, growing a new plate of glass, a new chamber in my internal furnace, a new joint of alloy. When the growth was complete, it detached. A smaller copy of myself, already warm, already glass-skinned, already able to taste the metal in the air. It moved away with a slowness that made my own movements seem almost frantic. I did not mourn. I simply continued, my thermal rhythm diminished by the loss, my plates slightly thinner, my joints singing with a new, hollow resonance that I am still learning to interpret.

I tend the machine. It is a vast thermal differential, turning on magnetic bearings so silent that I must place my palm against its housing to feel the imperceptible rotation, measured in degrees per day. I am bonded to it as a gardener is bound to a tree, but the tree is a gradient of heat, and the fruit is structure, silica, order. I feed it with my own metabolic heat, storing the day's scalding air in the lattice beneath my skin, releasing it into the magnetic bearings at night when the condensation freezes and the world cools. In return, it provides the processed minerals I need to grow—to add new plates to my carapace, to repair the hairline cracks that form when the cold of the long twilight meets the heat of my core.

I press my chest against the wall of the passage. The basalt is warm, 2.7 degrees, radiating the heat of the earth's bones. I send a vibration through it—a question, or perhaps a statement, or perhaps simply the acknowledgment that I am here. It travels through the stone, picking up the history of the rock it passes through: the cooling of the lava a century ago, the precipitation of the sinter in frozen waterfalls of white and orange, the slow settling of the plateau as it emerges from the mantle. It returns to me, transformed, carrying with it the weight of that history. I feel it as pressure behind my sternum, a warmth in the bone that is not thermal but *geological*. I am speaking with the city. Or the city is speaking through me, the Architects adjusting their distributed consciousness to note my presence, my patience, my becoming.

Above me, in the glass spires, condensation beads on the metal overhangs each dawn, reluctant water gathering in slow, mineral tears. Below, in the deepest levels where the superheated brine lies trapped beneath the crust, the Vapor-Born drift in their pockets of dark heat, remembering when the basalt was still liquid, still singing. I leave offerings for them at the vent mouths—small crystals of pure silica scraped from the hottest rocks with my obsidian claws, left in the mineral terraces like prayers. Sometimes, in return, a single droplet of distilled water appears, condensed from their own bodies, precious as a tear, and I drink it slowly, letting it cool my internal furnace for a moment, a moment of liquid relief in a world of steam and glass.

At the boundaries between the warm and the cold, where the shadows are cool and rare, the Rime-Keepers emerge in the long twilight. They are crystalline, frost-white, and they move with a skittering haste that seems almost frantic to me. They harvest the ice that forms instantly on the cooling towers when the condensation freezes, scraping it with claws of compressed carbon, storing it in insulated chambers deep in the rock. They trade this water-ice for the mineral salts I produce, a commerce conducted in silence, goods left in the narrow passages at the boundaries. They sing—a high, crystalline tone that resonates through the glass spires, audible only to those with the patience to listen for hours, a sound like the *tink* of cooling metal stretched into a note. I watch them from the warm shadows with something like envy, or perhaps pity. They are fast. They are cold. I am slow. I am heat. We are all of us negotiating with the physics of this place, finding our place in the slow violence of geology.

Night comes, if night comes, for the days are long and the twilight lingers, bruised violet against the black glass. The fumaroles continue to vent, their steam catching the moonlight like ghostly breath. I lower my internal temperature until I match the ambient stone. I become indistinguishable from the architecture, a glass-skinned statue that occasionally twitches as a geothermal pulse passes through the floor, my plates of black glass reflecting the stars that are not blue but deep, bruised violet.

I am the Sintered. I am the processor, the maintainer, the grazer of bacterial films. I am the biology that arrived late, or early, the post-botanical life that metabolizes sulfur and heat and the slow accretion of mineral. I do not dream of elsewhere. There is no elsewhere. I am here, in the vertical city, in the narrow passage where the walls radiate heat, pressing my chest to the stone to hear what the city is saying. I am patient. I am becoming. I am the heartbeat of the plateau, felt in the teeth rather than heard, a subsonic hum that says: *I am here. I am patient. I am becoming.*
