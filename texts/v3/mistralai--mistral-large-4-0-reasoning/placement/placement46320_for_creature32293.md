I am the weight in the palm. I am the edge pressed between digits that have not closed in ten thousand years. I am the chert flake—cool, sharp, deliberate—grown warm perhaps from the heat of a grip that has outlasted the erosion that carved this chamber, or grown cold to match the stone itself.

My forelimb extends. It has always extended. Below me, the pool is black and perfect, holding the dim glow of the moss without ripple, reflecting a twilight that neither brightens nor dims. There is a shape in the water—perhaps prey, perhaps only the reflection of the droplet that hangs suspended above us, a glass bead that will never fall, never evaporate. I am poised at the absolute threshold. My muscles remain engaged in the act of striking, locked in the moment between recognition and lunge, a loop of anticipation that has outlasted the limestone's wearing smooth.

I am blind. My skin is wet parchment stretched over delicate bone. Moss grows on my back—a garden of viridian and aquamarine that pulses with the same subsonic thrum that vibrates through the stone, through the crayfish probing the wall with its whittled stick, through the colony of beetles carpeting the peat in hexagonal sufficiency. The boundary between my flesh and the mineral has dissolved. I am colonized by the green light. I am part of the furniture.

I do not hunger. I intend. There is a difference. The flake in my hand is not a tool but an anatomy—a calcified extension of my wrist, the boundary between my body and the artifact blurred by the permanence of my hold. I wait for prey that will never arrive, not with despair, but with the patience of the mountain itself.

Time here is not a river. It is a droplet. It hangs in the grey-green mist, saturated to opacity, clinging to my skin, muffling every movement. My breath would not mist; the air is already too full to accept more. I breathe once a century, perhaps once a millennium. My heart beats in pulses I feel more than hear, synchronizing with the luminescent fungi and the resonance of the stone.

Nearby, where the wall sweats its permanent dew, a crayfish questions the dark with a stick worn smooth at the tip, a philosopher of the crevice, sensing the pressure changes in the mist, the weight of the suspended droplets. Between us, the beetles are—a distributed consciousness, a pale chitinous nation packed into every cubic centimeter, processing the moss that grows through and around them, participating in a closed loop of consumption and excretion that has reached perfect equilibrium. They do not compete. There is no scarcity. They simply are.

I have built nothing. I need no shelter from time. I am the shelter. I am packed into the hollow of the stone like a seed in pulp, nestled in sphagnum that holds a permanent sheen of standing water, hanging in clusters from the ceiling like fruit that has forgotten the sun. I am the Keeper of the Threshold, though I know no threshold, only the endless inside.

The droplet above me hangs silent. The mist clings. The moss glows with a constancy that suggests a heartbeat stilled. I extend my forelimb toward the black pool, the chert flake held between digits that will never close, poised to strike at a shadow that may be nothing. And I am already there. I have always been there. The strike is the waiting, and the waiting is the strike, eternal and unchanging in the green twilight of the deep.
