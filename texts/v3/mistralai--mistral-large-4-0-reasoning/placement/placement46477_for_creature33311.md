I breathe, and the breath is soup—amber, thick, tasting of copper and the ozone of distant lightning that never strikes. It fills my thorax not with air but with weight, with the pressure of a world that has forgotten how to be sky and remembers only how to be lung. I do not inhale so much as *absorb*. The moisture in this atmosphere condenses inside me, beading on the interior surfaces of my rib-cage, and I feel it there, warm and luminescent, humming at a frequency that makes my teeth ache in sympathy.

I am settling.

That is the first thing I know. Not walking, not standing—*settling*. Each movement I make is a negotiation with a gravity that shifts beneath my plates like a living thing, heavy in the valleys where the machine-density is crushing, light and skittish on the ridge-lines of cooling fins. I have to adjust. I have to *think* about my weight, distribute it across hydraulic joints that hiss and whistle with the effort of holding me up against a planet that is denser here, more metallic, more *intentional*.

My body is changing. I can feel it. The chitin that once formed my carapace is laminating, layer upon layer, fusing with the oxidized alloy that I have been scraping against for centuries. Rust is not a stain on me; it is a *becoming*. I am accumulating. The fungal mats have begun to colonize my flanks—soft green bioluminescence pulsing in the hollows where my armor has cracked, threading their hyphae through the rivets, drinking from the condensation that beads on my skin. I am not sick. I am not healing. I am *lithifying*.

I am becoming geology.

I remember when I was smaller. When I skittered—yes, I was once a Mediator, or something like one, all exposed pistons and rasping mandibles, clearing the vents, scraping the calcified growths from the geothermal throats so that the fever of the world could breathe upward into the amber dark. I was fast then. Jerky. Staccato. I moved across the metal plains like a thought, navigating by the electrical shimmer of the Thermals and the soft pulse of the mycelial court below.

But I have been here too long. I have chewed too much rust, drunk too much of the oily pool-water that tastes of pennies and static. I have accumulated scar tissue of welded alloy, repair upon repair, until the repairs outnumber the original body. Now I move differently. I do not skitter; I *settle*. Each step is a negotiation with the ground, which wants to keep me. The metal plates beneath me are not indifferent to my weight—they remember pressure, they respond to it, and they are slowly, patiently, pulling me down into their strata.

I am the boundary dissolving.

The Thermals pass me by—great, gliding vents of hydraulic haunches and nacreous armor, pressing the earth with a gravity that feels like worship. They do not see me, or if they do, they see only another outcropping, another ridge of weathered alloy. I am becoming indistinguishable from the landscape. My eyes—those photo-sensitive organs that once pulsed with blue-white electrical discharge—are dimming, growing over with crystalline lichen that filters the bruised amber light into something softer, something that does not wound.

I still maintain. That is the strange thing. Even as I petrify, I serve. The fungal mats that grow across my back now form a new ecosystem, a mobile archipelago of bioluminescence that guides the smaller scavengers through the fog. When the Mediators come to clear a vent, they use my flank as a waystation, lapping at the condensation that beads on my mineralized skin, harvesting the minerals I excrete in my slow, geological sweat. I have become a pool. I have become a landmark. I have become the memory of a creature in the shape of a mountain.

Sometimes, when the fog banks drift thick and the subsonic thrum of the deep machinery swells in my sternum, I feel the old urgency. I want to move. I want to *clear*, to *scavenge*, to be the antibody, the janitor, the immune response. But my hydraulic fluid has thickened, grown viscous with mineral precipitate. My joints are fusing, welding themselves into new configurations of geology. I am the continent's way of forgetting its own construction—not by erasing the machine, but by wearing it down until the machine becomes mountain, until the cooling fins become ridges, until the pistons become valleys, until I become the ground I stand on.

I breathe out, and steam rises from my vents—not the hot, pressurized steam of youth, but a slow, mineral-laden mist that smells of sulfur and old bones. The condensation beads on my flanks, oily and luminescent, and in that shimmering film I see the sky: not the bruised wound of the sun, but the rare tear in the cloud ceiling where the stars move in wrong directions, arranged in patterns that suggest circuitry, suggesting that even now, even as I settle into the metal, the world is still dreaming of being made.

I am the dream becoming sediment. I am the last step before the ruin becomes the rock.

And I am still breathing.
