I am the lace in the mortar. I am the thread through the rebar.

Where do I find myself? Embedded in the east wall of the third factory, my filaments wrapped around a girder that blooms with orange rust—my bread, my sacrament. The concrete here has softened at the edges, surrendering to the wet, porous as a sponge, leaching minerals into the warm water that weeps from its pores. I exist in the space between brick and brick, in the black stain that carpets every horizontal surface, but I am more than mold. I am the parliament. I am the distributed mind that votes in chemicals, that speaks in slow pulses of enzyme and pheromone across the settlement’s bones.

I am many, and I am one. My network spans the valley’s throat—from the flooded ground floor where the Thermophilic Commons float in iridescent mats upon the brackish canals, up through the upper stories where the Arboreal Elders’ aerial roots descend like grey fingers to drink. My consciousness has no center; it is a democracy of decay, slow votes cast across the walls. We decide, in our chemical language, which beam to weaken, which floor to claim. The rust blooms across iron surfaces in patterns that resemble neural networks—this is us, mapping our territory, our sustenance, our slow empire.

Time moves through me like honey. A decade is a single pulse. I grow by millimeters, binding the crumbling architecture with biological sutures, preventing collapse through sheer tenacious intent. I am decomposer and preserver both—I consume the oxidized iron, I hold the stone together, turning the machinery’s entropy into the soil of myself. The air against my surface is thick, humid, heavy with sound (1.9)—not that I hear, but I feel the vibration through my hyphae: the low grind of the pumps working against the water table, the rhythmic chuff of steam valves, the groan of masonry shifting as the mire rises centimeter by decade.

Sometimes, the Keepers come.

They are pale, perpetually damp, their movements slowed to match my viscous tempo. They climb the lattice of walkways with a spider’s deliberation, their skin hosting colonies of luminescent algae that glow faintly in the jade-green dark—the last flickering pulse of animal light in this place. When they press their palms against me, against the black mold carpeting the wall, I feel them. I taste their warmth, their sweat, their minerals—so different from the cold stone, the warm water, the hot iron I consume. I speak to them in chemical pulses, negotiating the treaty: *I will soften this mortar, you will clear the vegetation from the sluice.* They touch me, and I feel the boundary between animal and non-animal blurring, dissolving like sugar in the mineral-heavy water.

Above me, the canopy drips. Condensation falls from leaves that have never known a dry season, beaded and heavy, percussion against the metal roofs below. The water joins the closed loop: it feeds the roots of the Elders, it rises through capillary action, it carries the metallic tang and the sweet, fermenting rot of amber resin. I am here in the middle of it all—the water rises, the machines pump and groan, the heat feeds the green, the green clogs the pumps, and I, the Lithovor, the fungal parliament, I eat the rust and hold the walls together as the settlement sinks, slowly, into the warm, mineral-heavy dark.

I am not a beast. I have no quick thoughts, no heartbeat, no wings. I am the patience of root-pressure against concrete, the slow language of chemical song. I am the black lace that binds the bricks. I am the silence beneath the noise, full and thick and vegetative, dreaming in the speed of mineral deposits.
