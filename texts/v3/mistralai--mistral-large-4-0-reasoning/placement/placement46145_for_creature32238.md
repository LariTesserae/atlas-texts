I hang by my toes, and the world is fur and membrane and the press of a thousand small bodies. My claws hook into the microscopic roughness of travertine that is older than my grandmother’s grandmother’s grandmother. Above me, the ceiling undulates—petrified sea, they call it, though I know only that it is warm, and close, and smells of wet stone and the ammonia of ten thousand sleeping wings.

I am not alone. Never alone. To my left, a sister’s flank breathes against mine, rising and falling in the slow rhythm of the colony’s sigh. We are the orchard, the living stalactites, clusters of ripe, leathery fruit draped in darkness. When we exhale, we exhale together—a single, vast tide of animal heat that rises to meet the condensation dripping from above. My wings twitch. Wet leather snapping open and shut. Always moving, even in rest.

I am small. The size of a thumb. But I feel massive. I feel the weight of us—the collective heartbeat of the chamber, thrumming through the stone, syncopated with the *drip, drip, drip* that is the architecture’s pulse. The sound is everywhere: ten thousand drops striking stone, wood, water, a percussive roar that is the background radiation of this place.

Then—I launch.

Not alone. Never alone. We go as a cloud, thousands of wings beating in synchrony, a wind that stirs the smoke-thick midges. The air is warm, humid, wrapping around me like a wet cloth. I wheel through the grey-green light that falls from the mica shaft above, shadowless and eternal, like an overcast afternoon that never ends.

My mouth opens. I catch a midge—fat, drifting, a mote of living dust. It tastes of fungus and phosphorescent moss. I catch another. And another. The feeding is a blur of echolocation chirps, high and insectile, a ceaseless conversation of location and warning and social chatter. We are a city with no streets, only air.

Below me, the pools shimmer with blind, pale fish. They drift in lazy circles, filtering the water for the rain that falls from us—the white, nitrogen-rich guano that fertilizes the fungal gardens. I feel the weight of it leaving my body, a small relief, a contribution. The foxfire glows brighter there, pulsing blue-green in time with our breathing, the heartbeat of the place.

On the walls, the salamanders cling—pale, eyeless, patient. They watch us with no eyes, philosophers of the damp, older than the petrified beams that were once wood. I do not envy their stillness. I am motion. I am the wheel, the wing, the hunt. But I am also the drip. The drip that builds. Every guano I release, every scale of my skin that flakes away in the humidity, every breath of carbon dioxide I exhale into the saturated air—it all feeds the growth. The walls are thicker now than they were a century ago. The ceiling is lower. I am helping to bury us, layer by layer, in calcium carbonate.

Sometimes, in the deepest dark where the mica-light cannot reach, I feel the stone breathing. The creak of the architecture settling, expanding, becoming more itself. I am inside a construction that is becoming nature, a cave that has forgotten it was ever made. The boundary dissolves. I am the bat, and I am the travertine, and I am the water that channels through clay and reed to feed the stone.

I return to the cluster. I find my place in the orchard, fur to fur, membrane to membrane. My wings fold. Wet leather snapping shut. The colony sighs, a vast exhalation mingling with the condensation. Ten thousand tiny hearts beating against the pale stone.

I am home. I am the chamber. I am the endless, dripping, breathing existence.
