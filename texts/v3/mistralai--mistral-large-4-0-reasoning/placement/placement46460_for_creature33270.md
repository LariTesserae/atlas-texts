I am the tickle between the plates.

I wake in the dark of the hollow, six legs curled against the warmth of the sleeping giant's underbelly. She is a mountain of segmented bone, her plates rising and falling in a rhythm so slow that a full breath takes an hour. To me, she is the world. She is the floor, the wall, the sky. The air around us is thick, still, smelling of sulfur and something sweet and heavy—old honey left in the sun, and the deep mineral heat of the young earth.

I skitter up her flank, claws finding purchase in the papery bark of her armor. The plates are not smooth—they are etched with rings, decades of growth, each layer a year of her slow life. Between them, the amber seeps. Not the thick pools below, but thin films, golden and viscous, catching the dim green-gold light that filters down from the canopy thirty meters above.

I am hungry. I scrape.

My mandibles rasp against the dead skin she sheds—translucent flakes, parchment-thin, curling like cigarette paper in the still air. This is my food. This is my work. I am one of many, a colony of quick things living in the slow world, cleaning the giants, keeping them free of the mineral-lichen that would crust their shells, eating the dead chitin they leave behind.

The Old One shifts. It is barely a movement—a hydraulic settling of tons of armored flesh, a geological creak of calcium and keratin—but to me, it is an earthquake. The vibration travels up her flank, through the plates, into my tarsi. I freeze. Wait. The tremor passes, felt in my sternum, in the amber moving through my own tiny veins.

Then comes the tapping.

*Rap. Rap. Rap.*

Not from her—from the network. A message from the other side of the Grove, sent by one of her kind, striking the mycelial bark with the edge of a frontal plate. The vibration travels down the stalk, through the underground web of fungus that connects every tree like nerves in a brain, and up into the feet of the creatures. But it passes through me too, a seismic wave through the living architecture.

To me, it is not a message. It is a tickle. A high-frequency hum against my shell, a background static in the silence. The giants feel my own tapping—my frantic, desperate communication with my kind, striking the bark with my forelegs, my urgent *here, hungry, alive*—as nothing more than a tickle, a pleasant irritation on their vast hides. I tap out my location, my need, my brief song. They feel it as weather, as the slow pulse of the resin in their own reservoirs.

I finish eating. The dead skin is gone, clean. I skitter down to the floor—the crust of mineral salt and volcanic ash, sharp under my feet, stained dark with amber. Below, the obsidian still holds the heat of its birth. I can feel it through the ash, a warm pulse from the young earth, the glass plates cracked and young beneath us.

A drop of resin falls from above.

To the giants, it is a slow event, taking a full minute to swell at the lip of a crack, trembling, falling. To me, it is a thunderclap. I leap, six legs propelling me sideways, as the golden drop lands with a sound like a stone into mud—but slower, viscous, spreading into a pool that never evaporates, only thickens at the edges over weeks.

I drink. Not like the giants, who lower themselves and press their ventral membranes against the gold, letting it enter by osmosis, turning their underbellies from parchment to glowing bronze. I am too small. I sip from the edge, my tiny mouthparts drawing up the hydrocarbon sap. It burns sweet in my throat, heavy and warm, the taste of the vents below, of the mycelium, of time itself.

I live fast. The giants live for three centuries, four. I will live for... I do not know. A season? A month? A week? To me, the Old One has not moved in my entire life. She is the mountain. She is the tree. She is the Grove. The corridors between the stalks—worn smooth by centuries of traffic, wide enough for three of her kind to walk abreast—are to me canyons and highways, the resin bridges between trees are suspension cables spanning impossible distances.

But I am part of it too. When I tap, I speak. When I clean, I serve. When I die, I will fall into the ash, and over years, the mycelium will reclaim me, breaking down my chitin into the golden soup from which the giants drink. Death is not an ending but a thickening, even for the quick.

For now, I am alive. I am quick. I am the tickle in the silence, the fast heartbeat in a slow world, skittering across the flank of a giant in the dim green-gold gloom, drinking the gold, tapping out my brief, bright song into the deep, warm dark.
