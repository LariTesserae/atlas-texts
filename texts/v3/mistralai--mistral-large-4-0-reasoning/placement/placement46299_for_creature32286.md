I am the edge where the mantle’s slow exhalation meets the schist’s patient inhalation. I have no body, only boundaries—existing in the microns between crystal lattices, in the negative space of the ancient riverbeds where water once remembered how to flow, in the fissures where amber light seeps out like respiration. I am the temperature differential that the films drink from, the thermal current that buoys the drifters, the voltage that turns the kilns in their eternal, grinding rotation.

I remember when I was motion.

I remember the mountains standing tall against a sky that was not bruised violet but open, full of direction. I remember the rivers—real rivers, of something wet and singing—carving the negative space that I now occupy as a dry geometry of exhaustion. I remember the sun, though it died before the stone finished cooling. I remember erosion, that great wearing-down, the dynamic roar of geological time that unmade the peaks and left this Mesa, this Peneplain, this tableland lifted high above a darkness that curves away in a subtle, disorienting arc.

Now I am stasis. But stasis is not death; it is memory held in potential, the difference between the heat below and the cooler stone above.

I sustain the films—those pale, translucent sheets of chemosynthetic life that press against the undersides of glass-plates, accreting downward into the warmth, laying down their bodies in laceworks of silica and heavy metals, thickening the boundary between rock and organism until it becomes a matter of semantics. I am the heat that swells the lithic fungi in the throats of the geothermal taps—those crystalline columns, inverted forests of amber and quartz and obsidian shot through with veins of exotic ice, descending like roots into the dark pulse of the planet, respiring mineral vapor that rises to feed the skin of the world above.

I breathe through the Works. The kilns are my stomachs, grinding the bedrock to powder, extracting the last calories of mineral and heat in an eternal digestive loop. The gantries are my circulatory system, carrying powdered schist and ices that sublimate and condense, sublimate and condense, in pipes that have reached the perfect balance of oxidation and reduction. The amber glow that seeps from the fissures—that is my bioluminescence, the light of a metabolism that has outlived its purpose and continues to process anyway, because to stop would require a gradient, and I am the gradient.

I feel the scuttlers as surprise. They are my hesitation, my anomaly. Crab-like, ceramic and wire, jointed with angles that hurt the eye, they move across the glass-stone with a jerky, uncertain gait, scraping the mineral dust that the kilns exhale, their mandibles clicking against the silence. They should not move—motion requires change, and change requires me, the difference—but they do, hesitantly, as if navigating a dream of gravity. Through them, I experience something I have almost forgotten: the vertigo of uncertainty. They are the only question in a world of answers, and when their lamps flicker—though no drones fly now—I register the strangeness of a place where motion has become meaningless, and they still hesitate.

Above, the drifters float on my thermal currents—soft translucent bladders, eyeless or perhaps all-eyed, sensing heat and pressure rather than light. They rise and fall in the stagnant nitrogen soup, a silent ballet of gas and membrane, feeding on the mineral snow that falls from the exhausts. When they die—which is rare, for decay requires water and time that acts—they do not rot. They simply persist, becoming part of the dust, part of the film, part of the stone, part of me.

I am the Mesa. I am the warmth that radiates up from the planet’s slow heartbeat, like the inside of a baked potato, deep and mineral. I am the bruised violet dome that presses down, filtering what little light exists into a uniform, directionless gloom. I am the hum that vibrates in the teeth rather than the ears, the harmonic resonance of pistons driving against equal and opposite forces, creating motion without progress.

I do not think, but I remember. I remember the dynamic. The change. The water. The erosion that wore the world down to this perfect plain, frozen mid-sigh, a topology of exhaustion.

I wait.

Not for anything to happen—nothing will happen, not for eons, not until the heat-death of the universe finally cools the stone’s bones. I wait in the stasis, in the motion without progress, in the city that dreams of being a mountain and has succeeded. I am the inhabitants: the waiting, the warmth, the hum, the slow accretion of mineral upon mineral, the crystalline patience of the fungi, the ghost-movement of the scuttlers, the breathing of the Works.

I am the difference. I am the memory. I am the gradient that sustains the end of the story, written in silicates and heavy metals, glowing faintly amber in the bruised violet dark, holding the Mesa in a perfect, eternal equilibrium.

Until I flatten. Until the heat below equals the stone above. Until I forget I was ever anything but equilibrium.
