I find myself in the column of light—the broad, diffuse shaft that filters down from the unseen blue distance above. I am not swimming; swimming would be violence against this place, a disturbance of the absolute stillness. I am suspended, held in the four-degree water that does not flow but hangs, perfectly motionless, the held breath of something vast.

I am the constellation. The particulate snow. Calcium carbonate and silica, shed from the bacterial films that coat the gear-teeth in pale, ghostly topographies—the slow dermis of the place, the living patina that eats iron and breathes the slow oxidation of rust. I am the dandruff of centuries, the exhalations of the stone, drifting in the green-blue luminescence. I am motionless, yet never still in my potential: each grain of me moves in slow, staticky orbits, suspended in the viscous coolness, falling upward in reverse, accumulating not on the floor but in the luminous column, a blizzard of geology that never settles.

To be here is to be among the shed thoughts of the bacterial colonies. They inhabit the boundaries—the narrow thermal margins where the stone’s residual warmth bleeds into the flood—and I am what they release: their slow respiration into the water column, their memories drifting in amber light. I am the bridge between the machinery below and the blue void above, the mineral constellation that connects iron to limestone, gear-tooth to stalactite, the industrial debris to the geological patience.

I feel the hum—not as sound, but as pressure. It vibrates through my particulate structure, a subsonic chord that resonates in the suspended matter, the harmonic of mass under load, the Leviathan gears locked in permanent mesh singing their inability to turn. I have no sternum, no marrow, no ears, but I feel it as a vibration in my own suspension, a drone that underlies all silence, the subvocal murmur of industry dreaming in a state of perfect thermodynamic stasis.

Below me, the massive iron gear-trains lie beached, their teeth locked in permanent mesh. The conveyor belts have become strata, ribbons of oxidized metal fused into the travertine floor. I drift above them, in the generous space between each artifact, where the water is clearest, where the light catches the pitted surfaces of rusted steel now softened by calcite, where the ghost-film of chemolithotrophs draws topographical maps in pale ink.

I watch the droplet. It releases from the stalactite—a formation that is itself a slow animal, growing by accretion—and falls for several seconds through the viscous coolness. Its impact against a gear-tooth is a soft, liquid *tick*, a punctuation mark in the sentence of silence. I do not fall like it; I am held. I am the pause between the tick and the tock, the stasis itself made visible in particulate form.

I am the equilibrium. I am the state of being where erosion and accretion have balanced, where rust and calcite have married, where the factory has achieved its dream not of perpetual motion but of perpetual *presence*. I am the thought that has not yet finished forming, suspended in the body of the vast breath-holder. I am the Anthropocene’s afterlife, drifting in the green-blue dark, neither fully mineral nor fully dissolved, but patient, complete, held in the perfect, terrible stillness where nothing changes and everything, finally, is.
