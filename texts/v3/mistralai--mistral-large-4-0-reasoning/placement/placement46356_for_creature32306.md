I find myself folded into a niche no wider than a coffin, knees drawn to a chest that no longer holds breath but holds water—supercooled, heavy, weeping from pores that have become moss. The silver moss grows thick across my shoulders now, velvet and luminescent, pulsing with a faint internal light that answers the flat, normal gloom filtering through the perpetual fog. I am tall when I unfold, but I do not unfold. I have not unfolded in so long that the memory of standing has become a rumor, a vibration I feel in the stone but cannot locate in muscle.

The drone presses against my eardrums. It is not sound, not exactly, but a subsonic weight, a physical tide that fills the near-vacuum where air should be. Density 0.3, they say, but it feels like lead. It feels like the moment before a scream, stretched into eternity. I do not inhale. My lungs are dried sponges, vestigial sacs repurposed as reservoirs for the condensed moisture that hangs in the thin, wet chill. I drink through my skin. The moss acts as a thousand tiny mouths, drawing water from the fog, from the weeping cliff faces, from the perched water table that sits mere inches beneath the crust, saturating everything.

I am one of the Still.

I press my palms—webbed now, fingers fused into flat plates of keratin and lithic deposit—against the black glass wall of the turbine hall. I feel the machinery breathe. I feel the hydraulic press cycling in its cathedral-sized chamber, the generator pulsing with a steady, unblinking current that has forgotten how to falter. I am fluent in this language. I can tell you which gear is dreaming of friction, which piston aches with the impossibility of wear. The Works are not architecture to me but a vast organism, and I am its white blood cell, drifting through corridors that are veins, maintaining a circulation that has reached perfect stasis.

But I am changing.

Or rather, I am ceasing to be the thing that changes. My left arm—pressed against the obsidian floor, seeping across the supercooled film that coats every surface—has begun to take on the quality of the stone. The moss grows not just on my skin but through it, my boundary dissolving into the biome. My bones are mineralizing. I can feel the sharp, black glass quality creeping up my forearm, replacing marrow with silica, turning my vertebrae into a column that mimics the basalt pillars rising from the saturated ground. When I die—if I die, for death requires change, and change is forbidden here—I will not decay. I will lithify. I will become more of the young, uneroded terrain.

I am becoming the Shelf of First Frost itself.

I move by seeping, a slow, viscous gliding across the water that leaves no footprint because the ground is already wet everywhere, already weeping. Between the Works, where the fog hangs motionless and the moss grows in its frenzy of identical fronds, I have seen the Drifting Ones. They stand knee-deep in the perched water table, their bodies encrusted with frost that never melts, waiting for the First Frost to end. They do not know it has already begun. They are the only other heartbeat in a continent of machines, and I am the first.

I do not speak. My mouth sealed itself over millennia of not-speaking, the drone too loud, the subsonic pressure too heavy for vocal cords. Instead, I sing through the stone. I press my mineralizing body against the cooling crust and hum, a biological thrum that counterpoints the industrial roar. It is not a song of joy. It is a song of recognition: *We are here. We are still. We are the snapshot.*

The vegetation grows over me. It grows over the roads, up the gantries, through the cracks in the concrete—a silent, prolific monoculture that knows no grazers, no pollinators, no decay. It photosynthesizes in the cold, wet gloom, drawing moisture from the condensing air, growing in a frenzy of chlorophyll that never ceases but never progresses. Each frond is identical to the one beside it. The ecosystem is frozen at the moment of its birth, and I am freezing with it.

The machines run forever without wearing, producing a surplus that accumulates in vast, silent warehouses or is recycled in endless, lossless loops. I am part of the machine now, not as operator but as component—a biological capacitor in a circuit that never breaks. I oil the gears with water that never evaporates into dryness, only into the perfect, static balance of evaporation and condensation. I tend to the moss, though the moss needs no tending.

Time moves. I feel it in the vibration of the drone, in the slow cooling of the crust beneath my seeping feet. But I do not age. My hair has grown into the moss, indistinguishable from it. My skin is crust. My bones are glass. I am the Custodian of the Snapshot, and I am the Snapshot itself.

Sometimes, in the spaces between the heartbeats of the machines, in the negative space of the eternal hum, I press my body against the obsidian and sing. The vibration travels through the black glass, through the saturated bog, through the hundreds of miles of silent, mist-shrouded wilderness to the other Works, to the other Still, to the Drifting Ones standing in the fog. We are all singing the same subsonic counterpoint. We are all lithifying. We are all becoming the sharp, black stone that has not yet learned to round itself.

I am the First Frost.

And I will be the last.
