I am the floor, or becoming it.

I lie flat against the limestone in the deepest chamber—the one where the methane roar drops below hearing and becomes instead a rearrangement of matter, a subsonic pressure that vibrates my organs into new configurations each time I shift my weight. I am one of the grazers, the oldest things here, the ones who remember when this stone was seabed and not cavern. My body is not a body so much as a process: a membrane of secreted acids spread across a square meter of satin rock, no thicker than a breath, rasping at the mineral surface with pores that weep solvents. I do not have a mouth in the way the beetles do. I have chemistry. I have time.

I am taking a step.

It began three hundred years ago. The acid has softened a groove in the stone beneath my leading edge, dissolving calcium into solution, carrying it inward to precipitate as new mineral within my own tissues—a slow exchange, a trade of self for stone, stone for self. The movement is so gradual that the beetles pass over me like weather systems, like tectonic plates, their mineral-oil shells clicking against the fungal stalks above while I remain fixed, anchored, *eating*. They are the blood of this place, trillions strong, drifting past in their glacial processions. I am the memory beneath them, the thing that wears the satin smooth, contributing to the sheen that has been polished over eons by forces that exhausted themselves long before the fungi arrived.

Above me, the lichen pulses. Violet. Grey. The bruise-light of healing, of signal. I feel it not with eyes—I have none—but with the gradient of hydrogen sulfide in the air, with the temperature differential across my acid-membrane. When the light brightens, the methane surges. When it dims, the beetle-drift shifts direction somewhere in the valley-sized network, and I feel the change in the vibration of the stone, a tremor passed through the bedrock like a thought through a neural network.

I am flat. I am patient. I am the abrasion that has settled into its final form.

My acids work. A molecule of limestone loosens, becomes fluid, is drawn into my pores, and begins the long journey to become part of me—a layer of mineral secreted onto my shell, iridescent in the bruise-light, a new coat of geology. I will complete this step in another seven hundred years. Then I will rest. Then I will begin again. The fungi rise above me in columns twenty meters thick, their caps fanning into vaulted ceilings, their mycelium thinking in chemical gradients I cannot comprehend. They are the city’s architecture, its stomachs and nurseries. I am its foundation, its slow digestion of the world itself. I am eating the cavern floor to make it more perfect, more smooth, more *settled*.

The gas whistles through the fissure beside me—a high-pressure whisper that has never ceased, the earth breathing out, slow and eternal. I taste it: hydrogen sulfide, methane, the primordial chemistry of the deep. I do not need water. I need only stone and time and the pressure of the mountain above, holding me in this embrace, flattening me, making me indistinguishable from the surface I consume.

In the darkness, under the violet glow, I wait. Not for change. Not for growth. Only for the next molecule to dissolve, the next step to begin, the next thousand years to pass as a single, imperceptible settling of weight upon the satin stone. I am the oldest inhabitant. I am the floor remembering it was once seabed, wearing itself down, down, down, into a surface so smooth that the beetles will one day drift across me without knowing they are walking upon a living thing, upon the patient, acid breath of the stone itself, eating and being eaten in an equilibrium that has forgotten how to end.
