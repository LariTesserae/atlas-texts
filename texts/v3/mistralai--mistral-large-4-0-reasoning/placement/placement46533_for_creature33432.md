I am the seventh course.

I find myself in a horizontal crawlway barely shoulder-width, my spine coiled like a reed, my plantigrade feet sinking into the always-damp clay floor. The air here is liquid—eighty-five degrees of suspended, mineral-heavy breath that fills my lungs and beads instantly on my skin. I do not stand fully; I cannot. The architecture presses close enough that I can touch both walls with my palms, webbed fingers spreading to grip the wet terracotta.

I am **Packed**. My skin is the color of wet clay—a pale, warm gray-pink that never dries, perpetually beaded with condensation that I breathe through, a second lung absorbing the moisture directly from the saturated atmosphere. My eyes are enormous, lidless, black as wet slate, reflecting only the faint phosphorescent glow of the slime-mold colonies that coat the walls below the waterline of the air. I have no hair, only a smooth domed scalp. Around my wrists, ankles, and throat I wear thin bands of hammered copper, long since reacted with my perpetually damp flesh into a verdigris patina that matches the bacterial mats. I am, in effect, a walking piece of the city’s own geology.

My task is maintenance.

I hold a stone adze in one hand and a copper chisel in the other. With the adze, I scrape the wall. The phosphorescent film—simple biology, slime-mold and bacterial mats feeding on minerals leached from the clay—peels away in iridescent sheets. It glows faint green in the bruised twilight. I harvest it for food, for the waterproof sealant it becomes, for the verdigris it leaves on my skin. The sound of scraping is swallowed immediately by the roar: the wet, granular tsunami of water dripping from ten thousand eaves, seeping through porous foundations in a whispered static, rushing through vertical shafts like a basso river, skittering across surfaces in a million tiny impacts. It is a 2.6 on the scale of auditory saturation. To hear anything else, I must tap.

*Tap-tap-rest-tap.*

The copper chisel strikes the terracotta. The vibration travels through the dense mineral mass, amplified by the city’s rhizome of habitation, resonating for miles through the packed earth. Somewhere deep in the fabric, another Packed feels it through their bones and knows: *water rising*, or *slime-mold requires harvesting here*, or *a wall must be re-laid*. We speak through the bones of the city. There is no privacy; the walls are thin, transmitting every breath, every heartbeat, every press of a body against the clay. I am never alone. I cannot be alone.

Around me, the fauna moves in the dimness. A snail the size of my fist slides past on the wall, its nacreous spiral shell catching the green luminescence—sacred geometry, the living form the architecture imitates, the first builder perhaps, or the valley itself in larval form. I tap it gently with a finger, guiding it, collecting its iridescent slime trail in a reed basket. Below, in the standing water that fills the depression beneath my splayed feet, pale eyeless crustaceans the length of my forearm scuttle through the mineral broth. Insects by the millions create a background drone that merges with the water’s roar. There are no plants here—no grass breaks the clay, no trees pierce the mist—only this closed loop of consumption and excretion, this perfect equilibrium of wet and warm.

I drink. I press my mouth to a silver thread falling from an eave, or I press my porous skin directly against the seeping wall, absorbing the water table that presses up from below like a held breath. It tastes of lime, of ancient bone, of the mortar that binds the slag and pumice into walls.

Below the crawlway, in the Warm Pool where the residual heat of the geology keeps the water at a womb-like temperature, the **Unfinished** drift. Gelatinous, translucent, voiceless amphibian-things that filter the water and send vibrations through the clay. I press my ear to the wall near the pool and listen. They speak in pulses: *water, water, sleep, sleep.* They are oracles, or the souls of those who died before they could be packed into the walls, left forever embryonic. The message is always the same, and always sufficient.

Time is not a river here. It is strata. I know I am the seventh layer of this cell. When I die—and I will die, for lives here are short and moist and laborious—I will not rot. The closed ecosystem has no bacteria of decay, only those of preservation. I will desiccate into a chalky husk, white and fragile as eggshell. Then the others will take me. They will grind me to powder with stone mortars. They will mix me with lime and animal glue—the glue rendered from the desiccated dead of the crustaceans and snails, and perhaps, in secret rites, from the husks of my own kind. This mortar will be packed into the walls. I will become the eighth layer. I will become the binding. Every brick, every course, contains the compressed, mineralized remains of generations. The city grows from within, fed by the dead, yet because the material is always the same and nothing weathers, nothing erodes, the city remains in a state of perpetual just-completion.

I am both the builder and the built.

The wall I am scraping has sharp, unweathered edges. The channels between buildings are narrow slots with pristine, unscoured clay. The erosion is forever emerging, caught in the amber of its own making. I maintain this paradox. I ensure the slime-mold does not overtake, that the chalky husks of the dead fauna are cleared from the channels, that the walls remain new, forever new, never aging.

I tap again: *tap-tap-tap.* A wall requires rebuilding here.

The vibration travels through the terracotta. The city breathes. I am the breath, the sweat of hand-built toil, the condensation on the skin of the world. I am to-be-layered, to-be-mortared, to-sleep-in-water. I am the place where water sleeps, and I am the one who keeps it sleeping, forever finished, forever under construction, forever the seventh course of a wall that will never know how many layers came before, only that there will be an eighth, and a ninth, and all of them new.
