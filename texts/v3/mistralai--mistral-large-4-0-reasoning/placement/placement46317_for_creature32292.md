My osteoderms, those bony plates embedded in my skin like a second skeleton, begin to conduct. Not heat yet—cold, initially, a shock that travels through my vascular channels from the metal flange I occupy. I am packed here, flank to flank with others, a row of solar architecture along the sun-facing lip of a slag heap. My neighbor to the left shifts, a millimeter of basalt-black scale against ash-pale, and I feel the vibration through the stone.

The ice has gone. It was here—an hour of geometric lattice, crystalline gardens growing on the cold metal—but the sun has climbed past the ridge and now the air itself simmers. My chromatophores ripple, a wave of pigment beneath my skin, shifting from absorbing black to reflecting pale as the temperature gradient shifts. I defend not this flange of rusted steel, not this specific square of welded tuff, but the exact degree of warmth I have claimed: 47.3 degrees Celsius, give or take the thermal expansion of the pipe that runs beneath me. The metal has expanded with the morning, carrying my territory upward by five degrees. I am out of equilibrium.

I drum my tail against the flange. Three quick strikes, seismic, felt in the teeth rather than heard. The message travels through the packed web of infrastructure—through the pipes thick as redwood trunks, down into the bedrock where the bacteria sweat mineral brine, up into the shimmering distortion where the floaters drift like ghosts. *This gradient. Mine.*

My neighbor answers. A quick, armored strike of jaw against shoulder, territorial, not violent but precise. We do not fight for space—the Scarp is too full, too packed with conveyor belts and cooling towers and the skeletal remains of drilling rigs—we fight for temperature. Each of us holds a specific thermal niche, a precise degree of warmth, and we will defend it against the encroachment of the shifting pipes, the settling slag, the slow erosion that grinds everything back to dust.

Their language is percussive, harmonizing with the clicking and pinging of cooling metal. Below us, in the hairline fractures of the basalt, the lichens pack the stone in cracked mosaics, their thalli threaded with fungal hyphae conducting geothermal warmth upward like capillary blood. The beetles have retreated, their iridescent elytra now reflecting the murderous infrared rather than refracting the dawn light. They have finished farming the ice.

I am basalt-black now, absorbing, my osteoderms drawing heat from the slag heap into my core, pumping it through vascular channels to my heart. The air around me wavers—the optical instability, the heat rising from the packed ground creating an ocean of distortion where distant machinery appears to float, to breathe, to swim. A cooling tower in the distance seems to lift off its foundations, hovering in the shimmer, while closer, the pipes expand and ping, a vast arrhythmic clicking as the infrastructure breathes.

The thrum is in my teeth. The automated machinery—conveyors turning, turbines spinning on geothermal steam—produces a low, subterranean vibration that the thin air muffles but does not silence. It is the heartbeat of the place, the geothermal pulse beating arrhythmic below. I feel it through the flange, through the stone, through the packed row of my kin.

By noon, I will have shifted to ash-pale, reflecting, retreating into the shadow of the equipment as the heat builds. The birds will pack themselves into the interstices of the machinery, into the condensation gutters of the scattered huts. The lizards will pack tighter into our thermal rows, a wave of color rippling across the slag like a signal through stone and scale.

But now, in this hour of transition, I am the architect of the heat. I hold my gradient. I am packed into the row. I am the thermal equilibrium, the hot, thin-air balance between the fire below and the cold sky above, a single scale in a packed organism of rock, machine, lichen, beetle, bird, and keeper—all of it breathing together in the simmering dark.
