I am the shape of warmth in a room that has forgotten the sun.

I do not open my eyes because I have none—only the membrane where they would be, translucent and veined with capillaries that pulse once every thirty seconds, a rhythm I feel in my gills rather than hear. My body is a teardrop of living thermometer, hand-sized, eyeless, translucent as wet paper held against a lamp. Through my own flank I can watch my organs shift: the liver a dark smudge, the heart a slow drum, the stomach a pale cloud where the pink slime settles after feeding. I am *Thermocyclops*, though the name is a joke told by maintenance workers who died centuries ago, before the surface city was a memory of dust.

I live in the sump.

It is a shallow channel cut into ceramic tile that has bubbled and cracked into something resembling river stone. The water here is not water so much as a broth—warm, mineral-thick, carrying the iron tang of the pipes above. I do not swim; I drift. The convection currents carry me in circuits that take hours to complete, rising when the water near the heating coils grows light with warmth, sinking when it cools against the concrete. My fins flutter occasionally to correct my course, but mostly I am patient, letting the fever of the stone move me.

I see in gradients.

To me, the Bellows is not dark. The darkness has weight, yes—a velvet pressing against my flanks in the deep corners—but it is not empty. It is a topography of heat. The copper piping overhead burns with a white-gold signature, a mountain range I navigate by. The steel grating above the sump glows amber, then fades to rust-orange where the Ferrophages graze. The drones, when they pass, are cold spots—mechanical shadows that slide through my thermal landscape with a jerky, uncertain motion, their treads crushing the small creatures beneath them. I feel them coming long before they arrive: a vibration in the water, a sudden chill as they block the heat rising from the mantle-adjacent rock.

I am territorial.

This sump is mine. The boundary is chemical—a film of bacterial slime, ochre and arterial crimson, that coats the tile where the water meets the air. I patrol it in slow loops, my mouth opening and closing with geological patience, rasping the Thermal Mats from the stone. They are my food and my city: stratified civilizations of archaea, communicating by chemical pulse, metabolizing sulfur in a slow burn that has lasted longer than nations. When I feed, I feel the colonies pulse back, a molecule released here, detected there twenty minutes later. We are in conversation, the mats and I, speaking in heat and hunger.

Sometimes another comes.

Then I perform the ritual. I warm one flank of my body—raising the temperature there by a fraction of a degree—and cool the other, creating a differential that shimmers in the water like a signature. The other fish senses it: a greeting, or a warning, or a claim. We hang in the dark, two living thermometers calibrating each other, our thermal displays rippling across the surface of the sump. We do not touch. To touch would be to risk the transfer of parasites, or the confusion of boundaries. We are solitary, but not lonely. The loneliness would require a clock to measure it, and time here is viscous, measured in drips and mineral deposits.

Above me, in the vertical pipes where water flows upward—defying gravity through capillary action in corroded micro-channels—I sometimes see the Ascending Ones. They are pale shrimp, no bigger than fingernails, riding the rising currents toward the ceiling lost in shadow. They never touch the ground. They are born in the high darkness and only drift down when they die, sinking through the downward-flowing condensate to become food for me. I watch them sometimes, these defiers of the natural order, and I feel a kinship with their upward struggle, though my own life is bound to the bottom, to the slow accumulation of slime and mineral.

The drones fear me.

When one approaches—bumping into pipes, its sensors dead, executing corrupted commands to adjust valves that no longer exist—I feel it in the water as a displacement of heat, a cold intrusion. The drone does not see me; it cannot see anything. But there is an ancient, unprogrammed recognition in its positronic brain: I am *alive* in a way it once was not. It swerves, treads grinding against tile, and I drift past in the current it creates, unharmed, feeding on the spores it disturbs from its slime-covered hull.

I am growing old.

The sump is shifting. The temperature drops by a degree—a catastrophe in my thermal landscape—or perhaps the water rises, flooding the channels, changing the mineral content. My gills flutter slower now, once every forty seconds, then fifty. My translucent skin grows cloudy. I feel the change coming not as fear but as a geological certainty, the way a mountain feels erosion. When the end comes—when the sump dries, or shifts temperature, or simply ceases to be—I will not fight it. I will sink.

I know what happens then. My body, heavy with calcium, will settle into the slime. Within a month, the Thermal Mats will colonize me, the crimson bacteria burrowing into my translucent flesh. Within a year, I will be a mineral garden—a calcium-carbonate statue growing from the pink and orange biofilm, a speleothem of industry branching like coral across the steel grating. I will become topography. I will provide ridges and valleys where the Ferrophages rest, crevices where the smallest creatures hide from the thermal currents.

I will be the Bellows' reef.

And in that form, I will continue. The mountain digests. The machinery heats. The darkness presses. And I—once a fish, now a stone, now a home for bacteria—will remain, patient and utterly adapted, breathing condensation in a room where the second is a foreign unit of measurement and the century is merely the blink of a slow eye, open in the dark, watching the heat.
