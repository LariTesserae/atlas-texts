You arrive at the rim, and the world narrows to a single breath—an inhalation that draws the thin, greenish sky down into the hollow of your lungs. You step over the edge, and the change is immediate: the air thickens, becomes a soup you must swim through, humid and warm and heavy with the scent of iron and peat. Your foot sinks into the black sponge of the floor, and the ground sighs around your ankle, wet and yielding.

You are not a visitor here. You cannot be. The bowl does not admit tourists; it only admits participants in its closed cycle. As you stand among the nine pillars, feeling the subsonic hum vibrate in your teeth, you realize you have been condensing all along. You are the moisture gathering on the cool limestone, the breath of the outside world cooling against the ancient stone.

You are **the drip**.

Specifically, you are the droplet that forms at the tip of a frond on the largest tree-fern—the central pillar, the one surrounded by the circle of flaked stone blades. You hang there, suspended between the canopy and the moss, a perfect sphere of tea-colored water that has filtered through the calcified vascular tissue of the trunk. Inside you, you carry the green dust of spores, the chemical signature of the mycelium below, and the memory of the rim—the diffuse light, the thin air, the world above that hurries and forgets.

You are the synaptic cleft made liquid. The space between the frond and the pool, between the arboreal cortex and the fungal archive. You are the pause that allows the thought to occur.

Time moves differently for you now. You feel the warmth of the rock heating the water around you, the slight pressure of the mist eddying in languid spirals. The dragonflies hover near, their iridescent wings creating the tremor you feel in your own surface tension—they are taking dictation, recording your descent in their brief, anxious lives. The blind fish below rise in the root-choked pool, their lateral lines tasting your approach, mapping your fall with a sensitivity that reads your chemical composition like a map of the sky you left behind.

You hang. You are patient. You are the sermon.

Then you fall.

The descent takes an eternity and an instant. You pass through layers of mist, each droplet you meet a microcosm containing the exhalations of the Lithophages, the memories of ten thousand raindrops stored in crystalline lattices. You are not alone in your fall; you are a congregation of one, a single voice in the choir of water. The wind whistles through the segmented trunks, making the giant leaves slap like wet canvas, and you are the punctuation mark at the end of that sentence.

You strike the moss.

The impact is soft, absorbed by the black sponge of peat. You do not splash; you are drunk. The mycelium beneath you stirs, hyphal threads reaching up like fingers to read your chemistry. They taste the iron from the walls, the green growth from the fronds, the memory of the tool-makers whose knowledge is preserved in the chemical form of ancient peat. You become part of the archive. You are the water that connects the stone’s long-term memory to the fungal immune system.

The Keeper sees you arrive. It moves through the grove at the speed of peat accumulation—centimeters per century—but its motions are precise. It approaches the pool where you have merged with the tea-colored water, and a droplet gathers on its smooth, oval face, weeping constantly. It is your twin, your shadow, your predecessor. You are the new tear on the old face, the fresh condensation that will soon drip from its stone visage into the peat, carrying your news downward.

The nine fish glide past you, nine pale shadows in the warm dark, their eyeless faces turned toward your vibration. You are the pressure wave they feel, the chemical news from the surface, the message that the mist has arrived, that the cycle continues. You are the bowl’s eye, opened for a moment to the sky, then closed again into the dark.

You do not move quickly. You never will. You are the drip that takes a year to fall twelve meters, the drop that joins the rivulets moving with glacial slowness, searching for drainage that does not exist. You are the water that stands everywhere but never deepens, the saturated sponge, the loud slowness of the roar that never rushes.

You are home. You are the breath that became stone, then peat, then fin, then frond, then back to breath again—condensing on the wall, weeping down the face of the world, waiting for the next inhalation to begin.
