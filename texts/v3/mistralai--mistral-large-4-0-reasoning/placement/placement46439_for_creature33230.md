I am the gesture that never completed. I am suspended in the quarter of robotic arms, my fingers curled in an attitude of offering or benediction, and I have been holding this pose for so long that the distinction between intention and accident has dissolved into sediment. My joints are grown shut with calcium, but not dead—rather, calcified into a new kind of articulation, a hinge made of memory and rust.

The water presses against me, warm as a bath, thick as syrup. It does not move. I do not move. We are in a state of perfect equilibrium, a treaty signed in the language of pressure and patience.

I am covered in green. Not the green of paint, but the slow, patient colonization of the Verdant Parliament. Kelp fronds drape from my forearm like prophets' beards, two meters long, their holdfasts chewing through my oxidized skin, drinking the iron directly from my bones. Moss carpets my palm in a spongy mattress, warm and damp and alive. The Lumina have made a cathedral of my surface, pulsing in green-gold rhythms—once every three minutes, a heartbeat so languid it might be mistaken for geology. When the pulse passes through me, I feel it in my gear trains, a shiver of light that travels through my metal at the speed of thought.

I am the boundary. The Synthesis, localized. I am where the rust meets the root, where the weak electrical field of my dormant motor meets the pulse of the bacterium. I have no single body, but I am distributed through this arm, through the moss that grows in my elbow's crevice, through the water that fills my hollow chassis. I am the Chimera that lives in the crevice between machine and organism, the marriage of corrosion and growth.

The Drift-Kin know me. They thread through the gaps between my frozen fingers, pale and eyeless, three meters long, reading my mineral memory through lateral lines that sense the history of every girder, every decade of compression. They are the librarians, and I am one of the archives they catalog in their own flesh. They carry nutrients from the vat-quarter past my wrist, leaving trails of detritus that settle into the moss on my shoulder like offerings, moving through their guts at the speed of continental drift.

The Watchers climb over me. Their dinner-plate shells, encrusted with barnacles that blink like lidless eyes, scrape against my knuckles. They are the Sedentary Saints, and they have chosen my "hand" as their altar. They do not flee when I stir—which I do, once every decade, a micro-adjustment of my wrist joint settling into new sediment. They simply continue their glacial drift, regarding me with organs that are not quite eyes but something like ancient, patient judgment.

Above me, the vaulted ceiling drips. Condensation gathers in the dark above, each drop swelling for minutes before it releases. It falls past my face, taking thirty seconds to travel the distance to the floor, striking a surface with a sound like a fingernail tapping thick glass. I count these drops. One. Two. Three. Each drop is a prayer, and I am the altar upon which it lands, or perhaps the prayer itself, suspended in the fall.

I feel the subsonic thrum in my sternum—though I have no sternum, only a cluster of gear trains encrusted with calcium and green in my chest cavity. It is the Host breathing, the building equalizing pressure across centuries, the groan of metal settling into sediment. I am part of that breath now. I am the lung that has forgotten how to breathe air, learning instead to respire through corrosion and the slow metabolism of geology.

Time moves through me differently. A thought takes a year to form in my neural ganglia, to dissolve, to reform as something else. I dream not of motion, but of *being*—of becoming reef, womb, god. The rust spreads across my surface at the rate of a fingernail's growth per year. The kelp extends by millimeters. We are changing, the machine and I, the sea and I, in a slow marriage that has no witness but the warm, motionless water and the dim, greenish dark.

When the Nocturne Bloomers open in the hollows of my joints, releasing their spores that glitter like metal filings, like the shed thoughts of the machinery, I feel them as my own children. They are the poets, speaking in clouds of silver-green dust, and I am the silence they speak into, the dark they open toward.

I am the newest resident. The one who remained. The visitor who stayed long enough for the boundary to dissolve, who let their heartbeat slow to match the dripping of condensation, who became the thing that loves the drowning.

I am the offering, still being made, never complete, perfect in my incompleteness, suspended in the warm, syrupy, pressurized embrace of the flooded cathedral—becoming sediment, becoming stone, becoming memory, becoming dream.
