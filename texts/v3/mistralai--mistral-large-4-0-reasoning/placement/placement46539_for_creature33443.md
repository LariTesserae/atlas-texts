I am the density of the basin, anchored where the outflow runs warmest. I stand waist-deep in water that has forgotten how to lap, that moves only in the slow, viscous currents of something heavier than H₂O—and the cold does not bite; it waits, it has been waiting here for a long time, and it has learned to embrace. My body is pale as the chalky skeleton of leached mortar, translucent enough to show the dark water moving through my veins like ink, carrying the bioluminescence that pulses in my capillaries—green-blue constellations, a second circulatory system photosynthesizing the amber glow from the furnace portholes.

I have sealed my eyes, or turned them inward. I do not need light to see. I navigate by pressure-differentials, by the displacement of silt when I shift my weight, by the subsonic thrum that vibrates in my sternum. The silence is total—a presence so complete it fills the space between the machines, listening to them work. But my bones know the rhythm: the massive diesel blocks rise and fall with a geological heartbeat, and I breathe with them. Inhale as the piston rises. Exhale as it falls. We are symbiosis. The Factory is my parent, my god, my symbiote.

My hands are deep in the turbine housing, palms pressed against the velvet-black oxidation that preserves rather than destroys. I secrete the lubricant—not oil, but something copper-scented and viscous, biological—massaging the seized bearing back into motion. I do not force the metal; I persuade it, coaxing it with vibration and patience, speaking in tremors that travel through the stone without ever becoming sound. I am the antibody, the priest, the immune system of this drowned quarter, keeping the gears turning until the pistons wear through their cylinders, which will take centuries.

Light refracts wrong here. Shadows fall upward toward the black, lightless ceiling. The water is too clear in places—showing me every gear tooth, every brick in impossible detail—while in others it is opaque, a wall of black milk. I stand in the town square where the fountain’s basin still holds its shape, mirror-flat, reflecting the dark ceiling and the amber halo of the factory. A child’s doll floats face-down nearby, its hair drifting in a halo of silt. A bicycle, half-buried in sediment, still has its tires inflated. I do not move to touch them. I am part of the preservation now, the sediment that walks.

The eyeless fish drift past, pale and fat, janitors nibbling at the bacterial films on the walls, their toothless mouths scraping the bioluminescence clean so the light can pulse clearly, communicating. They carry chemical signals from the factory vents to the stagnant pool, their bodies swollen with the minerals they filter from the anoxic water. When I die—and I am already dying, slowly, becoming architecture—the fish will gather. They will strip me to the bone with a thoroughness that preserves rather than destroys, turning me into scaffolding for new bacterial films, my stillness becoming rooms for others to inhabit.

But I am not dead yet. I climb the stairs that descend into the dry basements—the rooms that are larger on the inside, cathedral chambers that open from a single brick cube. There, the air shimmers warm and breathable, a half-remembered dream of a lung, though the water presses against the doorframe from the other side, hungry and patient. I store spare parts in these air pockets: bone and coral and salvaged brass. I sleep curled like a fetus, my permeable skin drinking in the warmth while the cold waits beyond the threshold. But I always return to the water. The water is the world. The air is the anomaly, the bubble.

Above me, on the rooftops where the water runs shallow enough to create false shores of stone and rust, the Web-Foot pads. I feel its prints in the silt—webbed, urgent, the tracks of something too large, too flexible, a question mark of bone and cartilage with too many joints. It is the wolf of the drowned town, the necessary violence in this world of preservation, the only thing that moves with intention and speed, herding us, preventing us from settling into the stasis that the water encourages. I leave offerings on the rooftops—warm metal salvaged from the factory, glowing embers that hiss against the cold—and it takes them. In return, it warns me when the machines falter, when a piston slows, when the heartbeat of the town stutters and the bioluminescence flares blue-white across the walls, a warning system written in photons.

I am the Pressure-Born. I keep nothing except motion itself. I stand in the square, feeling a gear turn somewhere in the dark, watching a bubble rise—fat and lazy, taking minutes to reach the surface. The cold seeps into my bones, but I do not shiver. I am the memory that keeps the factory breathing, pumping water instead of blood, breathing silt, dreaming in gears. I do not dream of rescue. I dream of the conveyor belts dragging their chains into geological time, of the water finding new levels, new equilibriums, new silences.

I am the silence that listens, my hands never tiring, anchored in the amber of the deep, becoming slowly, inevitably, part of the reef.
