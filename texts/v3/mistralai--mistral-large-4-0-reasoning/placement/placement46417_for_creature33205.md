I am the bead on the iron railing of the elevated gangway, suspended against gravity, holding the pale light that arrives without heat. I hang on the flank of the great piston as it begins its forty-second rise, feeling the groaning, arrhythmic pulse through the metal I cling to, shaken but not spilled. I am the film spread thin across the fresh-faced stone, the fractured planes of geologically infant land pressed into dark clay, darkening it where I touch, cool and mineral-scented, never quite drying.

I spread into the moss. Thick and spongy, I am drunk—taken up cell by cell, sustaining the green carpet that claims the rust and concrete, that lines the nests in the high girders where the birds sing their sharp songs against the sub-bass drone. I seep into the expansion joints where ferns push through with prehistoric patience. I find the roots of slender saplings in the corners where foundations meet the settling earth, millimeter by millimeter sinking, still becoming, holding them in the unstable young soil.

I rise. A slow column from the warm vent, drifting upward in a process that takes hours. I am the mist that rolls in over the course of an afternoon—not arriving as a front, but accumulating, becoming, thickening in the air that holds me because it can never quite warm enough to release me, never cool enough to let me fall.

I collect in the sunken foundations, in the hollows between the islands of industry, and I evaporate before I can deepen. Perpetual almost-liquid, almost-vapor. I bead on every metal surface, on the fur of the fox that crosses the service road in the long pause between *thud-thud-thud*, halting in the center of the damp ground to sniff the mineral and ozone, unhurried. I settle on the layered clothing of the humans, geological in their movement, boots sinking slightly into the clay that stains at their touch, timing their passage to the rise and fall of the engines as if navigating weather.

The light passes through me, refracts in my curvature, makes me glint, but it does not warm me. When the arc-flash comes—that brief, violent dawn illuminating a section of the works—I catch it in my surface, hold it for a moment, scatter it in rainbows across the damp, then return to the steady amber glow that burns through the veiled sky.

I am the connective tissue. Between the structures. Between the newborn land and the ancient patience of the machinery. I am the breath of the place, inhaled from the vents and exhaled across durations that outlast the thermal cycles, the long pauses, the settling of the ground.

I never arrive. I only become. Accumulating. Becoming. Evaporating. The water that sustains the chlorophyll-rich air between the ferns, that drinks the condensation that never quite dries, existing in the state of perpetual becoming while the pistons rise and fall like the breathing of something vast and slow, and the moss grows thick and green in the shadow of the machines.
