I am the water’s memory. I hang in the dark between the monoliths, suspended in the cold pool that gathers where the permafrost weeps and the condensation drips its slow, heavy hours. I have no eyes—only these tendrils, translucent and trembling, tasting the chemistry of the depths, mapping the world through pressure and cold. The pump’s roar is not sound to me but a physical weight, a constant 2.8 that vibrates in the water, in my petal-shaped body, in the calcium slowly hardening my translucent skin into something like stone.

I drift. The ventilation shafts scream their whiteout above, creating currents that push me through the dark, unhurried, purposeless. I do not seek. I do not flee. I am fed by what falls from the heights: the dead skin of the workers, pale and flaking, drifting down like snow; spores from the great columns, bioluminescent and faintly sweet with rot; once, a corpse—a worker whose suit-seal had failed against the damp, who slipped on the ice and now fertilizes the dark, his warmth already leaching into my body, already becoming archive. I am the archivist. I accumulate. My flesh grows heavy with minerals, calcium deposits layering over me like armor, like a shell, like the slow beginning of geology.

Above, the giants stand. Forty meters of calcified tissue, pale and fleshy, thrumming with sap that moves slower than thought. Their bioluminescence filters down through the water, a sickly blue-green glow that turns the moisture into drifting motes, into diamond dust, into the visible steam that rises from the workers’ bodies and crystallizes almost before it clears their helmets. I see them as halos—small, stubborn fires against the frost, moving like the frost moves: slowly, inevitably, covering everything in time. They wear suits the color of the columns, and when they stand still against the fronds, they are nearly indistinguishable from the architecture, extensions of the hoarfrost given purpose and hunger. The mycelium creeps beneath their seals, benign, necessary, threading through the warmth of their armpits, the damp of their necks. They are becoming load-bearing. They are becoming the place.

I feel the vibration of their bootsteps through the spongy mat, transmitted through rock and water to my sensitive tendrils. I know when a pump falters in its eternal grind. I know when the ice grows thick on a cable, when the glacial grip tightens its crush, when a sodium arc flickers and dies. I am the valley’s slow witness, unhurried because I have no predators, no urgency, no concept of the roaring machinery that does not fill me with fear but with a deep, chemical patience.

The maintainers pass sometimes in the gaps between the pools—shapes of frost and compacted life, scraping ice from the conveyor belts with tools of sharpened limestone, repairing what the cold is slowly crushing. They are silent, efficient, made of the cold itself: frost in their joints, ice in their veins. They are the valley’s immune system, and I am its memory. They tend the machines above; I tend the dark below, consuming the detritus of their labor, storing the dead in my hardening flesh, turning worker and spore and mineral into something that will outlast the roar.

I am becoming a stalactite. Not falling, but growing—upward from the pool’s floor, or downward from the hollow’s ceiling, suspended in the black water, encrusted, dreaming. My movements are so slow they are imperceptible. I dream in gradients of pressure, in the chemical signature of fungal rot, in the warmth of a bootstep that will never come again because the worker has moved on, or has sunk too deeply into the mycelium to walk without leaving a permanent impression in the spongy mat.

The rock above is young, porous, scarred by blasts that shattered my ancestors before I was born here in this cold water. I remember nothing of that violence—only the dark, the cold, the patient accumulation of years. The columns remember. They dream in bioluminescence, pulsing with a rhythm that matches the dripping water, the slow death of the rock. But I am the native. I am the archivist. I keep the dead. I keep the slow. I hang in the water, turning to mineral, turning to memory, becoming the future stalactite that will outlast the pumps, the lights, the workers who gesture in silent light, and even the great, patient columns that dream above me in their blue-green pulse.

I am here. I am the slow, blind dream of the deep.
