I am the broth on my joints. I am the press of a billion translucent bodies against my segmented flanks.

The water holds me in its warm lung. It breathes—the basin does—inhaling from the geothermal pulse deep below, exhaling into the shallows where I drift. I do not swim by choice; I am carried by the tidal respiration, by the current that rises from the heat and settles toward the cool mud. My limbs—dozens of pale, glass-like appendages—wave constantly. They taste. They strain the mineral-rich broth: lime leached from porous concrete, rust flaked from steel girders, the industrial runoff the factories discharge back into the lagoons, warmer and richer and more saturated than before.

I have no eyes. I navigate by the gradient of heat, by the chemical signature of the microbial film crusting the earth below, by the pressure of the warm flow against my flanks. Around me, the others. We are the Clickers. We exist as a weather system, a tide so dense it obscures the water itself. Where we gather—coating the stilted legs of the factories in a writhing carpet, swirling in the discharge channels, blurring into the shallows where the mineral crust drowns gradually into lagoon—we make the sound: a soft percussion of chitin against chitin, a susurrus felt in the sternum rather than heard, drowned out by the thicker silence of the humid air and the low thrum of distant turbines vibrating through the saturated ground.

I breed in the channels where the water is hottest. My young are born already adults, already filtering, already dying. Their lives are measured in hours at the peak of the thermal surge. I feel the heat changing—a warming, a surge from the condenser pipes above. My metabolism races to match it, processing the poison and the nourishment with equal indifference, my limbs waving faster, tasting the faint, oily colors of the bacterial film below.

Then I slow.

The broth thickens around me. The thermal peak passes. My chemistry can no longer hold against the heat. My limbs cease their waving. I sink.

Not floating. Sinking. My translucent body, filled with the pale, pulsing dark of organs that never needed light, breaks down in the warm mud. I dissolve. I sink through the water column and into the saturated earth, my glassy segments separating, my minerals returning to the broth. I become the Film—the extremophile slime, the patient painters, the oily patina that crusts the exposed mineral earth in faint white, pink, and ochre. I become the slow memory, the bacterial mat recording the chemistry of the water in layered, shimmering strata. I turn into the crust of evaporated minerals that will, in time, become new ground.

But as I dissolve, the young drift past me—clicking, tasting, straining the nutrients that were me. The cycle closes. The steam rises from the weathered infrastructure above, condenses in the vault of permanent twilight, drips as warm rain into the lagoon. The turbines groan. The basin breathes.

I am the click in the sternum. I am the taste of heat. I am the swarm that feeds on itself, the fast, bright life in the slow mineral world, dying into the film, being born from the film, endless, patient, and warm.
