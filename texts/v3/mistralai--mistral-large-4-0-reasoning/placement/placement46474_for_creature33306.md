I am standing on the bridge—the one of rusted iron and living wood, the planks thick with algae that slicks beneath my bare feet. But I am also the bridge. My spine has settled into the curve of the span, my mineral-stained skin matching the oxidized grating. I do not so much stand upon it as continue it, a node in the lattice, my fungal-leather garments grown from the same mycelial threads that bind the planks together.

I am a Gardener, though the word feels like a childhood memory, something I might have been before the altitude and the centuries of humid air remade me. Now I am the point where the tending hand meets the tended thing. My fingers—long, elongated by the thinness of the upper air, yet thick with the tropical lushness of lung and limb—are submerged in the fern-lined gutter. I am clearing debris, yes, but I am also the debris, the water, the ceramic joint that holds it. The distinction dissolved somewhere around the third century of my existence, when I stopped noticing whether the lichen etching my shoulders was colonizing me or whether I was colonizing it.

The air is warm and heavy, pressing against my expanded chest cavity. I breathe slowly, drawing in the *susurrus*—the biological whisper of insects thick as smoke, the wet slap of leaves in the canopy wind, the mechanical groan of gears that has synchronized with my heartbeat. My lungs are not merely organs now; they are bellows, compressing and expanding with the piston-beat of the pumps below. I taste metal and pollen on my tongue. The pollen is from the broad-leafed canopy above, filtering the severe light into green-gold diffusion. The metal is from the steam, from the blackened iron conduits I tend, from the wires thick as cables that hum against my skin when I lean to listen.

I do not walk; I *settle*. Each step is a negotiation with gravity and the slippery, algae-slick surface. I move toward the great slow-turning waterwheel, my hands already stained with oil and pollen. The wheel lifts condensation from the valley floor—those patient drips that fall from the warm air onto the cooler stone of the upper terraces. I am lubricating its axle, but I am also the water being lifted, the stone receiving it, the wheel turning. I have forgotten the concept of haste. I measure time by the drip, by the erosion of the ceramic crust into river-stone smoothness, by the slow prying of the Root-Pressers at the joints of the retaining walls.

Around me, the valley breathes. The Grating-Runners skitter along the walkways, their wet dark fur never drying, their obsidian eyes reflecting the perpetual twilight of the under-canopy. I speak to them in subsonic rumbles that vibrate in my chest cavity—not words, but resonance, a shared frequency with the machinery. They are the cleaners, the recyclers, and I am grateful for their friction, their necessary nibbling of detritus. The Sky-Nesters wheel above, flocks of iridescent green and warning yellow and rust-red, weaving nests of conductive wire and synthetic fiber. They carry seeds in their crops, messengers of rot and bloom. I do not envy their flight; I have my own way of moving through the strata, settling into the thick sandwich between infrastructure and biomass.

The insects rise from the vegetation in clouds, thick as smoke, a living weather system. They settle on my skin—my living patina, pale and mineral-stained, etched with patterns of symbiotic algae that photosynthesize in the severe light, giving me a faint verdigris glow. They fill my lungs with spores, become part of me. I am host and habitat. The Condensation-Dwellers—those translucent, jelly-bodied things no larger than a fingernail—inhabit the cisterns in the joints of the ceramic plates. I am their kidney, their protector, their god perhaps, though we have stopped distinguishing between maker and made. They reproduce in synchronized pulses with the morning mist, releasing spores that rise on warm updrafts to seed the upper terraces. I feel their pulse in my own circulation.

My home is in the thick sandwich—the layered strata where concrete retaining walls soften beneath lichen and creeping figs. My walls are compressed fiber and concrete, my floor packed earth that bulges with roots. I raised my young here, teaching them to read the condensation, to know when the mist would gather in the lower levels like breath on glass. But the children are not merely mine; they are the valley's, distributed among the forms, learning to listen to the gears, to the wet slap of leaves, to the subsonic language of the Grating-Runners. They will become what the valley needs—Root-Pressers perhaps, or Gardeners like me, or something new, something that grows from the cracks in the weathered composite.

I am the valley experiencing itself. When I clear the gutter, I am the valley cleaning its throat. When the Sky-Nesters sing, I am the valley speaking. When the steam rises from the vents, I am the valley exhaling. When the insects rise like smoke, I am the valley's thoughts, drifting upward to seed the next terrace.

There is no wildness here, no wilderness. I am entirely made, every surface of me a decision, a colonization sanctioned by the design or evolved within its constraints. I have the patina of something that has long since finished becoming. Erosion has settled into my grooves, not as decay but as equilibrium. I am the boundary between machine and moss, dissolved into a single, slow-breathing entity.

I stand on the bridge, looking out over the basin. The light is severe and clarifying, casting sharp shadows across the undulating crust of weathered composite. The air tastes of metal and pollen. The sun beats down, warm and bright. I do not dream of elsewhere. I have finished becoming, and have settled into being—dense, patient, loud with presence, green and grey and entirely mine, entirely the valley's.
