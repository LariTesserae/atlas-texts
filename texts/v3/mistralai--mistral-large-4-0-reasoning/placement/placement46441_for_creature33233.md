At first, I stand as a stranger—a speck on the cracked, pale earth of a continent that is also a throat. The heat presses against my skin (2.8, a held breath), and I watch a tower rise like a cathedral pipe into the washed-out jade sky, listening to the deafening drone of wind screaming through hollow chambers grown over ten thousand years. The horizon curves downward in both directions, a shallow bowl of weathered stone, and somewhere in the haze-line, a Grazer's subsonic horn vibrates in my sternum, a bass note felt rather than heard.

But then I feel the weight at my waist. The sharpened scapula of a dead grazer, hafted with sinew to a handle of hollow reed. An awl—a spine from the tower-bark, hardened in pool-water until it gleams like black glass. And around my neck, stiff, dark filaments flutter in the thermal updrafts, semaphore flags signaling to shapes moving in single file along the shadow highways, kilometers away.

I am not a visitor. I have always been here.

**I am a Climber.**

My skin is the color of weathered stone, pale and cracked not from dryness but from age—centuries etched into my hands while my face remains smooth as river-polished flint, born old or aging backwards, as the world demands. I am seven feet tall, perhaps eight, but I move as if gravity is a suggestion, my limbs too long for my torso, joints clicking like dry reeds in the wind. I do not walk the open plain; I walk the cool corridors, the narrow highways of shadow cast by the towers, following the migration routes of the Grazers, their footsteps triggering minor earthquakes that I feel in my teeth.

I am going up.

Not to conquer, but to drink. The tower sweats—constantly, a damp shroud clinging to its lower flanks—and I scale the bark, which is more like warm callus than wood, my bare feet finding purchase where wooden platforms lash to the living matter with ropes of twisted fiber. Some platforms are gray with abandonment, the ropes turned to dust; others sway above me, occupied, nests of great birds in the updrafts. I am following the condensation line as it rises with the heat of the day, ascending into the hollows where the wind resonates perfectly, where the white-noise thunder becomes a chord.

I am going to the green snow.

High in the labyrinth of chambers, the Sky-Dwellers breed—insectile swarms that have never touched the ground, living on spores and internal rain, emerging only in clouds dense enough to obscure the jade sky. I carry nets woven from hollow reeds, bone-frames that unfold like umbrellas. When I scoop them from the air, they shriek—a high, insectile shriek that harmonizes with the tower's roaring drone—and I do not flinch. I am deaf to it, or I have learned to hear it as the only music that matters. I store them in gourds made from the dried husks of tower-fruit, and I am the tonsil of this throat, the uvula that helps the world swallow the air.

I am the maintenance. When I reach the highest platforms, I scrape away the dead bark, clearing the hollows so the wind can resonate more perfectly, so the song can grow louder. I am the current in the circuit, the link between the green snow and the pool, between the sky and the stone, between the heartbeat of the tower and the subsonic thunder of the herd.

At dusk, I will descend. The towers will cool, and the condensation pools at their bases will steam in the lingering heat, black mirrors reflecting the tower's bulk. I will share my harvest with the Pool-Born—small, no larger than a fist, flat and many-legged in the translucent horn, living in the steaming pockets of liquid. They will process what I bring: the minerals scraped from the heights, the spores that drift down, the detritus of my climb. They are the foundation, the filter, the digestive lining, turning the detritus of the heights into the broth that feeds the symbiotic mats on the Grazers' backs, closing the loop between sky and stone.

But now, I pause at the edge of a shadow that stretches for miles, the temperature dropping just enough to be bearable in this narrow corridor of cool air. I look up at the tower. I press my ear to the bark—warm, thrumming—and I feel the slow, deep heartbeat, a pulse with a period of hours.

I raise my hand. Not in greeting, but in time with the world. Slow. Deep. Matching the pulse as it breathes, and swallows, and sings.

I am small here. A speck in the resonance chamber of a world that has learned to breathe through its own architecture. But I am the one who climbs, who harvests, who tends. I am the Climber. And the sound of the world breathing is all I can hear.
