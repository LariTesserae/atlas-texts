I arrive at the edge—though the word is a courtesy, a fiction maintained for those who still measure distance in strides. What supports me is not ground but the outermost stratum of the Condew: a lattice of titanium-white alloy and pale, unweathered concrete, fresh enough that every angle retains the memory of the tool that cut it. The elevation is 1.9, and I feel it immediately in the thinness of the air, the way the cold sits behind my teeth like a blade held parallel to the gum. Above, the light is 2.7, merciless, casting shadows that pool like ink against the white surfaces, black and knife-edged.

I walk inward.

The density closes around me—not with the press of bodies but with the oppressive intimacy of near-touching. Conduits and catwalks and habitat pods stack in vertical layers, creating canyons of industry that never sleep but never shout. The sound here is 0.6: the tick of thermal contraction in the metal, the soft *plash* of a condensation drop falling three meters to a collection grate, the subsonic thrum that settles in my sternum. When I touch a wall, I feel the electrified hum in my fingertips, a current that does not roar but persists.

Moisture is everywhere. It beads on the sharp angles, runs in silver rivulets down the cruel acute edges, collects in basins that are part of the design. In the densest shadows, where the bright light cannot reach, pools hang suspended in the lattice-work, held by surface tension and architectural intent. There are no rivers, only the slow drip of the sky’s breath against cold metal.

I find one such pool.

It is not merely water. It is a thin place. Through its curved surface, I do not see the structure behind it but a different structure—older, perhaps, or parallel—or a landscape of green that does not exist here. The water is aggressively cool, carrying a faint taste of ozone and copper, and something else: the memory of a forest that never grew in this thin, bright air.

I am thirsty. Or perhaps the place is thirsty, and I am the instrument of its drinking. I lower my head and touch my lips to the suspended surface.

The transformation is not violent. It is patient, like the erosion that has not yet come (1.1). The water does not enter me; it replaces me. I feel my skin becoming translucent, then *clear*, revealing the dark fluids that move beneath with the viscosity of deep water or cold honey. My limbs elongate, jointing at angles that suggest origami rather than bone. My eyes adjust to the unfiltered glare, becoming wet and depthless, reflecting the 2.7 light without squinting, without the protective blink that creatures of thick air require. I am no longer the visitor. I am a **Condensate**.

I stand—if standing is the word—on padded feet that make no sound against the titanium-white alloys. I am the size of a deer, perhaps, but wrong in the way a reflection is wrong in water that has not yet settled. I do not cast a shadow so much as an *absence* of shadow, a lighter patch where the unfiltered sun seems to pass through me uninterrupted.

I move.

My movement matches the slow dynamic of the place (1.4). Every step is considered. Every pause lasts minutes. I navigate the canyons of conduit and catwalk with a deliberation that borders on geological. The others are everywhere—pale, long-limbed, near-touching in the dense vertical layers, their population a high-saturation of life in the made-world. We do not call to one another. We communicate through stillness, through the pressure of translucent flanks brushing in the narrow passages, through the transfer of moisture from skin to skin when we pass.

I find a suspended pool and drink.

My tongue is itself translucent, too long, lapping at water that carries the taste of adjacent realities. I do not eat the sparse moss (1.2) or the mineral-eating lichens. I am not a herbivore, nor a carnivore. I am a hydrator of the thin places. When I filter the damp air, my body quivers—a visible shudder that travels through my translucent form like a pulse. I am processing not nutrition but *permeability*. I am drinking the reality that leaks through the fresh, sharp edges of the construction, the water that seeps from parallel geometries, the condensation that remembers other timelines.

Sometimes I step into a gap between two conduits and simply... am not there for a moment. I exist elsewhere, disoriented but calm, before blinking back into place. I am not traveling. I am being *remembered* by the place. The Condew, in its oppressive density, has begun to dream, and I am the dream taking temporary, mobile shape.

Deep within the mass, where the engines turn once per hour or once per day, the **Residents of the Core** feel like the hum in my fingertips when I press my palm to a wall. They have no bodies, or rather, their bodies are the conduits themselves. They maintain the geometry when the shadows fall in wrong directions, ensuring that when a drop rises against gravity for three seconds, it falls again. They dream in frequencies below hearing, processing reality itself into something that might be fuel, or might be meaning.

And on the north-facing surfaces, the **Weaver** grows—the engineered mosses, pale green and barely visible against the pale metal, recording. The lichens dot the sharp edges, eating minerals from the air, asking the questions the place poses to itself. They hold the memory of soil, of the softer world that the Condew has replaced, keeping it alive in the condensation.

I am one of the Condensates. I move through the vertical density like a thought through a mind, filtering the damp air, drinking from pools that show not the structure behind them but other structures, other times, landscapes that do not exist. I am the antibody of the dream, processing the reality-breakdown (2.8) so that the fraying does not become tearing.

I wait. Time moves slowly here. The condensation accumulates over days. I wait for the thin places to widen, for the bright light to shine through a different sky. I wait for the erosion to soften the edges, for the sharpness to become something that can be touched without cutting.

I drink from the sky’s breath. I move with the deliberation of a world that is learning, slowly, how to dream.
