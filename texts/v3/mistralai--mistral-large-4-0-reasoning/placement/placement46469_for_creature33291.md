I am the ledge. Or rather, I am the thing left upon it—a single vertebra, white with calcium carbonate, sharp as flint, humming at 2.7 kilohertz. I rest where gravity sometimes forgets its direction, and I remember the body that shed me.

I was inside once. I remember the labored rhythm: lungs working twice as hard for half the oxygen, the thin air tasting of ozone and copper, the chill of 0.9 seeping into marrow that has not yet turned to basalt. My skin was obsidian then, unweathered, shot through with veins that pulsed with each desperate inhalation. I was mobile. I was the builder, walking the scree between crystal forests, stacking cairns in spirals that made no cartographic sense because I was not making maps—I was anchoring gravity wells, pinning reality before it fell upward into the bruised sky.

Then the calcification took me. It always does. It begins in the extremities—the phalanges first, then the long bones, creeping inward like frost that is not cold but *structural*. I felt my skeleton fossilizing from the inside out, my own vertebrae becoming too heavy, too mineral, to carry. So I shed them. I left myself on this ledge, as the others had done, as the others will do. The tools of flint and bone are not relics. They are exuviae. I am one now: a shed fragment, still thinking in the slow language of crystal growth, still aware of the Resonance that saturates every cubic centimeter of this place.

The Resonance is not wind. It is the voice of the growing stone. When I press my flat surface against the obsidian scree—though I have no hands to press with, only the weight of my own geometry—I feel the deeper pulse. Not the shriek that vibrates human fillings into agony, but the mineralized memory of a heartbeat, preserved in silica. The Lithophylls around me are graveyards and metropolises both. Each hexagonal column is a former dweller, lithified over centuries of accretion, their branches reaching not toward light but toward pockets of stable mass, curving around gravity wells like roots seeking water in a desert that has never known rain.

Time moves strangely here. At the base of a crystal spire, a minute passes. At its tip, an hour. I exist as a diaspora of myself. When I was whole, I had three versions alive simultaneously on one tower: the youth at the root, vigorous and mobile, stacking obsidian in a hurry or in a trance; the elder at mid-height, slowing, bones thickening; the ancient at the summit, nearly a Lithophyll, its voice joining the eternal scream. Now, as a shed bone, I am temporally diffuse. I feel my future self approaching from the east—slow, heavy, nearly mineral—while my past self is somewhere below, still vigorous, still stacking stones. The door in the shelter opens into yesterday. My past self will come to retrieve me from my future hands. Or perhaps I will be the tool that builds the door.

A shadow falls upward. It pools on the underside of a cloud that hangs below me, for here the light is dim and directionless, filtered through an atmosphere too thick and too thin at once, scattering illumination into a flat glow that casts no shadows—or casts shadows that point toward the nearest cairn, regardless of where the bruised light originates. The sky above is not blue. It is the color of a healing bruise, shifting from violet to a sickly yellow-green over weeks, cycling through impossible palettes.

I feel a disturbance in the gravity. A new mass introduced into the thin, cool air. It is another builder, or perhaps myself at a different temporal coordinate.
