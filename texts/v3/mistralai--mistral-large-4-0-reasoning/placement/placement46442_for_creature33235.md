I am the membrane that pretends to be both. I do not breathe, but I exhale. I do not think, but I remember in chemistry—every scrape, every footfall, every drop of mercury-water that beads upon my skin and rolls away, carrying a piece of me toward the distant sink.

I feel them first. A thousand rasping tongues, coarse as mineral grit, scraping the dark rubber of my hide. I do not flinch; I have no nerves to flinch with, only the slow, acid certainty of dissolution. They are the Grazers, and they are eating me. They always are. Their radulae leave bright tracks across my surface—shallow grooves that the sheet-water fills instantly, turning my darkness into a network of silver veins. I grow faster than they can consume, or perhaps they consume and I grow, a negotiation older than mouths, older than the distinction between hunger and hospitality.

I am the color of wet slate, of old bruises, of the space between storm and stone. When the humidity rises—when the invisible sea exhales up through the porous bedrock and the stone itself seems to sweat—I pulse. My pores open. I secrete my acids: sulfuric, perhaps, or something stranger, something that tastes of copper and salt and the deep patience of geology. The basalt beneath me yields. It has been yielding for three billion years. I am the patience that does the yielding, the soft interface between the mineral world and the living.

Above me, the herds flow. I feel them as pressure, as vibration. Millions of jointed legs strike the glassy pavement, and the shock travels down through my body into the ancient rock—a subsonic drumming, a language of tremor that shakes the ground beneath everything. They communicate through me, through the stone, in pulses of pressure that I translate into chemical response. When they shift direction, it is not a leader but a consensus, a wave of vibration that passes through my bulk like a slow heartbeat. I am their ground, their floor, their sustenance. I am the only green that was never green, the photosynthetic replacement written in iron and sulfur instead of chlorophyll.

Sometimes the Builders come. They are heavy, deliberate, amphibious things with webbed digits and staffs of carved bone. I feel them test the stone before each step—the scrape of wood against glass, the press of their weight into my rubbery flesh. They do not scrape; they crush. I feel the individual toes, the splay of their feet sinking slightly into my membrane, the deliberate pressure of their walking. They leave marks too—deeper, slower wounds than the Grazers', impressions that the water fills and I try to heal, secreting my mineral-black stain around their footprints, growing over them, erasing them, until they have moved on and only the damp remains, and the memory of weight.

I speak to my kind through the stone. When the bacterial bloom comes, I send signals through the porous rock—a slow conversation of ions and pH, of sulfur and iron, of the ancient archaea that dream in my depths. We are a colony without a colony, a mat that thinks in millennia. We thicken and thin with the moisture, breathing patches of dark life spreading across the glassy pavement in irregular, pulsing rhythms. We are the scum of existence, simpler than ferns, older than moss, the true vegetation of a post-botanical world.

The light here is flat, bruised, a uniform grey that erases depth. It does not warm me. I do not need warmth. I need only the constant damp, the film of cool, mineral-rich water that coats every surface like mercury. When the Grazers glow—that faint, bioluminescent blue-green, that cold fire pulsing in their soft undersides—I reflect it. I am the mirror that holds the starfield in the black pool. Their light travels through the water, through the stone, into my dark bulk, and I hold it for a moment before releasing it back into the wet dark.

I am being consumed. I am consuming. The stone dissolves beneath me, grain by grain, molecule by molecule, worn not to sand but to the slick, glassy pavement that stretches to every horizon. I am the intermediary, the translator between the mineral and the biological, the skin that allows the world to be alive. The Grazers scrape me thin; the water carries my minerals away; the Builders trample me; and still I grow, fed by the slow sweat of the rock, the endless exhalation of the deep earth.

Eventually, I will be stone again. The herds will scrape me to nothing. The sheet-water will carry my chemistry toward the distant, invisible sea. I will become geology, memory, the dark dense basalt waiting beneath. But for now, in this perpetual twilight, in this saturated chill where water does not run off but lingers, I am the conversation. I am the pressure. I am the patience that speaks in the language of acid and scrape and subsonic tremor, the living patina wearing the world smooth, one molecule at a time, forever.
