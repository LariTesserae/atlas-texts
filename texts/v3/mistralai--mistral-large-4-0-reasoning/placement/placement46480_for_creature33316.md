I begin as a boundary. Not the Pale Membranes that lace the hollows like wet parchment, but something adjacent to them—a membrane of my own, translucent and paper-thin, stretched across a flatness that has no thickness. I am filled with the same dark water that surrounds me, which is the first strangeness: I cannot tell where the pool ends and I begin. I am a void with intent, a hole in the world that has learned to move.

I have no face, only surfaces. When I look up—if looking is the right word for seeing through layers of my own substance—the sky is a gray void, the mist pressing down with its physical weight, condensing on my back in beads that add to my mass. When I look down, I see the black glass, the jigsaw of obsidian daggers packed dense and near-touching, and in their microscopic fissures, the threads of water that seep *upward* from somewhere nameless and deep. This upward water is my blood, my breath, my substance. I drink it even as I am made of it, drawn through capillary forces I cannot name, filling my disc until I am indistinguishable from the permanent pool I traverse.

Time here is not measured in seconds. It is measured in the disturbance of surface tension.

I have been crossing this span—thirty centimeters of dark mirror between two shards of volcanic glass—for six hours, and I have traveled perhaps twelve centimeters. My legs, if they are legs, number in the hundreds, perhaps thousands. They are slender as the cracks in the glass, so fine they do not break the water's skin. They skim the surface. They push against the tension, and the tension pushes back, and this conversation propels me forward with a glide that is not slow so much as *deliberate*. A meditation on distance. A pilgrimage where every step takes twenty minutes to complete.

The drone is inside me. It is the Resonant Deep, the subsonic pressure that vibrates in the sternum of the world, but here it vibrates in my watery body, making my membrane flutter in standing waves I cannot control. The mist has density, a physical weight, and as it shifts, it creates interference patterns in the fog—visible vibrations that pass through me, through my substance, suggesting a conversation between my flesh and the earth's core. Sound is structure. I am structure made of sound and dark water, held in resonant suspension by a frequency so low it feels less like hearing than like the memory of being born.

Around me, the Pale Membranes ripple. They spread across the black glass in irregular blooms, the color of old bone left in rain, mining minerals from the seeping water. They are the only soft thing in this landscape of daggers and mirrors, and from my flat perspective, low to the ground, they look like continents of porcelain glaze creeping across the mineral plain. I pass near one, and for a moment—an hour, perhaps—the edge of my disc touches the edge of their sheet. We do not merge; I am water, they are mineral-drinkers, but we share the same upward-seeping darkness. They pulse with a rhythm so slow it seems geological, the flexing of a muscle that operates on the scale of centuries. I feel their pulse through the water that connects us, a shared heartbeat in the wet dark.

In the distance, a Mineral Architect is growing. I see it from my low angle: a wall rising, crystal by crystal, humming at a harmonic of the main drone, sharper than the last time I passed this way. It is building a room, or an altar, or perhaps merely a geometric pattern that the resonance demands, accreting mineral by mineral, performing construction without tools or fire. I watch a new formation lock into place—a spiral that hurts to follow with the eye, twisting in directions that violate the local geometry. The Architect does not notice me. It is busy becoming architecture. But I feel its growth in the water around me: a slight change in the current, a vibration that travels through the pool and into my membrane, telling me that the world is rearranging itself while I watch, that stillness is motion.

I leave a wake.

Behind me, the surface tension is disturbed. The water remembers my passage for hours, a ghost-trail of disturbed darkness that evaporates in slow motion, a record of where I have been that will persist long after I have moved on. This is my only mark. I do not eat. I do not reproduce. I do not die. I am alone—there is never two of me, never a second Glass-Swimmer crossing the black plain—and this solitude is not loneliness but a kind of completeness, a necessary condition of being the only animal in a place where the only architecture is geological and the only movement is mineral.

The mist presses down. It is cool, saturated, condensing on every surface. My skin—if skin is the right word for a membrane of water held by surface tension—is damp within minutes, always damp, weeping with the world. The air is thick, muffling all but the drone, which passes through the fog unimpeded, through me unimpeded, a skeleton of sound that prevents the glass from settling, from weathering, from becoming ordinary rock. It holds me in suspension too, keeping me sharp and fresh and ready, a disc of translucent dark water with many fine legs, gliding across the black pools beneath a sky of gray.

I am moving toward nothing. There is no destination, no shore, no other. There is only the next centimeter, the next hour, the next disturbance in the surface tension that will outlast my passing. I am condensation and resonance, a young wound that has filled with water and sound, a pilgrim in a place that has not yet finished forming, existing in the pause between eruption and erosion, between silence and the first true word.

The earth hums its deep, patient song through me. And I glide.
