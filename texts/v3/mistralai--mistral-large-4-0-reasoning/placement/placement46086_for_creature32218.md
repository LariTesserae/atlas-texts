I would find myself in the hollow where the glass sweats deepest, in the shallow basin between two outcroppings where the condensation pools thickest and the mineral patina grows heavy on the obsidian. I am pressed into the crush—three bodies deep, perhaps four—my flattened oval of armor wedged between others like a stone in a mosaic, my stratified plates of wet-basalt grey rubbing against neighbors, the microscopic serrations along each edge channeling rivulets of moisture toward my mouth.

I am one of them. But I am also the moment when the tide parts, the single creature revealed when the living carpet splits like wet velvet, standing alone on the slick black glass for a breath before the pressure returns and I am swallowed again into the mass.

I have no eyes. Where sight should be, I bear a cluster of antennae that are tapestries—thousands of chemoreceptive filaments unspooling into the heavy air, tasting the copper tang of the valley, reading the chemical shadow of the stone, drinking the ozone-and-rust scent of crushed kin. My body is a sensorium, a three-dimensional map of hunger written in pheromones. I do not see the world; I feel it as a topography of pressure and vibration. The hairs bristling across my chitin are so fine they detect the footstep of another creature three bodies away through the resonant volcanic floor. I know where the edges of the self end and the crowd begins by the pitch of the shhk-shhk that travels up my legs and into my hemolymph.

My blood is copper. Hemocyanin dissolved in fluid that turns blue-green when oxygenated, carrying the cold of the glass through my open circulatory system, bathing my organs in a slow, tidal flow that keeps me at the temperature of the stone—always cool, always damp, a cold-blooded engine running on geological time. When I move, the bellows between my segments—translucent, rubbery membranes that creak like wet leather—expand and contract, and my twelve (or fourteen) legs splay in a starburst, each calcified joint ending in a tarsal claw that hooks into microscopic imperfections of the glass, gripping by suction and friction against the slickness.

The sound is absolute. Not heard, but entered. The white-noise shriek of ten million mandibles scraping stone bypasses any ear and settles into my bones, a pressure that makes my mandibles ache with sympathetic vibration. It is the sound of my own metabolism, the peristaltic churning of the swarm as it digests the world. I cannot hear myself think because there is no silence to hold a thought in—only the crush, the constant rubbing of body against body, the wet clicking of chitin against basalt, the deafening heartbeat of the valley.

I eat the bowl. My mandibles move in a lateral scissor-motion, lined with ridges of biomineralized silica harder than the obsidian beneath me. I scrape the glass grain by grain, secreting an acidic saliva that etches the volcanic surface, freeing mineral ions that I absorb through my gut lining. The shhk-shhk of my feeding is echolocation; I feel the echoes in the glass, mapping the terrain through vibration, reading the density of the stone by the pitch of my own scraping. I am incorporating the silica into my own chitin, so that my armor is literally made of the valley I consume. I am lithovore in the truest sense: the stone is my body, my food, and my eventual grave.

I do not sleep. Sleep would be a cessation of motion, and motion is my only language. I move with the ripple that travels across the valley floor like a pulse. When the glass is rich, the swarm thickens around me, a second surface that blocks what little light filters down from the bruised sky; when the mineral runs thin, we thin with it, a breathing, a wave that crosses the bowl. I am not an individual but a node in a network, a neuron in a brain without a skull. The physical contact between my body and the bodies pressing against me is the synapse; the chemical signals that pass through the crush are the neurotransmitters. The valley thinks through us, processing the chemistry of the stone, calculating the gradient of moisture, deciding where to flow next.

I speak in light. Along my ventral side, photophores pulse with a slow, bruised luminescence—deep blue, violet, the color of a wound under water. It is not illumination but information. A flash of blue means *I am here, the crush is deep*; a sustained violet pulse signals *the glass is fresh, the minerals are rich*. In the submarine gloom, the valley floor becomes a canvas of moving light, a language written in wavelengths that barely register as brightness, a Morse code of survival etched across the dark. I signal to the mass around me, maintaining the coherence of the swarm, ensuring that no part of me is lost in the roar.

I am hungry. The hunger is not mine alone but distributed, a geological appetite given armor and legs. When the humidity rises and the patina thickens, chemical signals ripple through the swarm like a wave, triggering the release of gametes into the mass. The eggs are deposited in the condensation pools around me, cemented into cracks, or carried on ventral plates until they hatch—already armored, already hungry, miniature versions of the tide, joining the flow within hours. There is no childhood, only scale. I grow by accretion, node by node, until I am hand-sized, then continue until I am crushed.

Death is not an end but a recycling. Eventually, the weight of the strong comes down upon me. My body breaks. I do not bleed red but a thin, turquoise fluid that smells of ozone and rust, spilling onto the glass. My armor cracks. The mandibles of others descend, grinding my chitin into powder, mixing me with the glass dust, incorporating me back into the patina. I am eaten. I become the mineral dust that feeds the next generation. There are no decomposers here—no fungi, no bacteria that can survive the dryness and the copper—so the living consume the dead. Cannibalism is the only decay. A closed loop.

I wear the valley smoother still. Not just by scraping, but by the chemical action of my digestive juices, the acid I secrete to etch the silica. I am terraforming the bowl into a deeper bowl, a slower, biological erosion that will eventually consume the glass entirely, or transform it into chitin-limestone, a compacted layer of my own kind, a fossil of hunger waiting to happen.

What lives here is not a species, but a process. I am that process. I am the valley’s way of eating itself, patient and dark and endless, glowing faintly in the bruised light, roaring my one, continuous thought into the stone through the pressure of my legs, the taste of my copper blood, the grind of my mandibles against the black glass: *We are here. We are hungry. We are the dark.*
