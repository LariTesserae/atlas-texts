I am the one who lifts the flat stone.

My fingers—translucent at the edges, parchment-thin where the mineral light touches them—close around the basalt. It is warm, slick with the cave’s breath, heavier than it should be. The weight is good. It is real. I am no taller than the wall I am raising, slight as the builders must be, skin gone the color of old parchment in this newborn dark.

Around me, the *susurrus* rises and falls. A river of scuttlers pours past my ankles, clicking their million feet against the wet stone, parting around my legs like water finding its level, then closing behind in a boiling surge. They possess too many legs, or legs that move too fast to count—a wet, clicking hurricane that vibrates in my sternum. I do not flinch. I have learned to read the patterns of their movement, to step through them as if they were merely weather.

Above, the aperture yawns—a violent wound in the ceiling where daylight pierces down in flat, white-gold columns. The light is strange here. It travels through the warm, humid air thick with moisture and movement, bending around the heat rising from the deep fissures below, refracting through the mist into shifting, normal-gold halos. Dust motes dance in the shafts, but so do the pale backs of the pinhead crawlers, rising in drifts like wet snow against the brightness. The shafts illuminate the basin: vast, open, scale 2.3, where the rock has recently given way in a great collapse. The stone is newborn—sharp-edged basalt and limestone, fractured and tumbled into ridges, pits, and boulders the size of cottages. Nothing here has been smoothed by time.

I place the stone. It fits. It is never finished—by evening the condensation will seep down and undermine it, or a new drift of the pale dead will bury it, or the settling earth will groan and shift the foundation, a thunder-crash of rock-fall as the ceiling continues its slow, inevitable collapse—but for now it stands. A platform. A low wall of slate, knee-high. A boundary between the deliberate and the storm.

My clothing is woven from shed chitin, bleached pale and stiff by the mineral air, giving me the appearance of walking fungus or animate shed skin. I smell of it—of wet bone and unbleached parchment and the mineral sweat the walls weep. Around my waist hang the tools: a flaked blade of basalt sharp enough to split the humid air, and a hammer made from the dense leg-bone of a geode-walker, hafted with strips of membrane from some vast winged thing. Heavy in my palm.

I am small here. Embryonic. My culture is just beginning, my understanding of this place incomplete. But I am architecture and intent against the chaos. Where the swarm is noise and weather, I am the still point. I descend into the warm pools where the creatures splash and drown in their millions, gathering the pale, drowned bodies in nets of woven chitin already heavy on my back. They are food. They are material. They are the mineral exudations of the rock made flesh, consuming the stone’s breath directly, and I am parchment and hunger and deliberate motion.

In the distance, the giants move—the walking geodes, cottage-sized, so encrusted with mineral deposits that their flesh is indistinguishable from the rock they traverse. Their crystalline hides pulse with a deep, systolic rhythm, a heartbeat measured in minutes rather than seconds, visible undulations that make them appear as breathing boulders. Their joints grind like millstones turning in a deep cellar. When they pass, the smaller creatures do not flee; they part like water around a boulder, then close behind, scavenging the flakes of mineral crust and chitin that fall like dandruff from the grinding joints. I leave offerings for them—mineral-crusted bone at their feet—hoping to appease the slow, grinding gods that might crush my stone platforms with a single careless step.

I am the farmer of the swarm. I place my enclosures in the path of their rivers, allowing the creatures to flow in, then seal the exits with flat stones, trapping the harvest. I keep the giants at bay—or perhaps I worship them. I exist in the interstices, the pause between the heartbeats of the geode-walkers, the silence in the roar.

The air is thick in my lungs—warm, saturated, tasting of stone and wing-beat and the ozone of geological youth. I breathe the same thick, shimmering air as the billion pale backs that carpet the floor in dense, near-touching layers. The boundary between the mineral and the living is thin here. Some boulders pulse. The stone rings with a clear, bell-like tone when I strike it with my blade, testing.

My hand moves to the wall. I take the ochre. I scratch—a geometric spiral, a record of the rock-falls, a map of the light shafts that shift with the weather above. The mark is crude, embryonic, constantly being buried by new swarms or washed away by condensation, but it is mine. It is brief. It is bright.

The swarm moves. The light shifts through the moisture. The stone settles, groaning.

And I remain, lifting the next flat stone, fitting it into the gap, building my existence into the rock, a hand placing order into the chaos, writing my history in charcoal on walls that will soon crumble, but crumble not yet.
