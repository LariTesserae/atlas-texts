My toes splay against the trunk, seeking the grooves where yesterday's rain has pooled in the bark's fissures. The algae is slick as a seal's hide, and I grip, not with nails but with the thickened pads of my soles, feeling the tree's pulse through the living wood—a slow, systolic throb that matches the groan of the wind in the canopy above. I am thirty feet up, perhaps forty, suspended between the Second Elder and the root-mat below, and the whole settlement breathes around me. It is not the wind that moves me, though the squall is lashing horizontally now, turning the catwalks into silver waterfalls; it is the tree itself, expanding, exhaling, the buttressed trunk flexing in its slow, green respiration.

I carry the bone chisel in my teeth, its edge honed on river stone, stained the color of old tea from the tannins it has drunk. My fingers are amber with the same sap, permanently tattooed by the cambium I have worked. Behind my ears, in the hollow of my collarbone, the lichen has taken permanent root—pale green, almost luminous in the diffuse grey light that filters down from the cloud-floor. I do not dry. I cannot dry. The humidity sits at my throat like wet wool, and my skin gleams with the perpetual condensation, a sheen of wet stone that catches the bioluminescence beginning to stir in the undersides of the platforms.

The sound is a roar. Not silence broken by noise, but a continuous churn—the rush of water down bark, the creak of lashings, the scream of wings. Midges rise from the peat in clouds thick as smoke, and I taste them, protein and mineral, as I breathe. A tern's alarm cry cuts through the drone—that is little Moss-Hand laughing three platforms up, her voice evolved to carry across the sway. I answer with an ululation, a sharp, staccato call that bounces off the living walls, and she responds with a tap on bark—three sharp raps, meaning *the joint is failing, come look.*

But I am already moving, climbing, my stooped back a permanent curve from years of inclined surfaces. The pegs are slick, algae-coated, and I find them by feel, my elongated fingers mapping the trunk's topography. The settlement is a lung, a sponge, a wasp nest pressed close. From here, I can see the whole of it—no horizon, only near and nearer. The Third Elder's trunk rises like a cathedral pillar, its hollows glowing faintly with the cold blue of fungi. Between us, a bridge lashed from willow withies sways in the wind, a hammock of living vine, and I watch a Lasher crossing it, his moss-felt cloak sodden, water streaming from the hem like a waterfall.

I reach the platform. It is not built but grown—compressed peat and woven root-mat, thick as a mattress, sprouting with ferns that drink from the perpetual drizzle. My feet sink slightly, and the peat sighs, releasing a bubble of methane, wet rot, the sweet decay that feeds everything here. This is where I work. The Second Elder's flank, a scar of previous carvings, callus tissue raised and silvered where the tree has healed over old wounds. Today, I am to open a new chamber—a nursery hollow, warm and deep, for the family whose matriarch is heavy with young.

I press my ear to the bark. The wood is never seasoned, always green, always weeping. I listen for the creak of fibers, the pitch of the groan, the tree's tolerance. It is a language of vibration. I can feel it in my teeth, in the marrow of my bones—a low, thrumming note that tells me where the heartwood recedes, where the sap runs thick, where the tree will suffer a cut without dying.

I take the stone adze from my belt. It is heavy, volcanic, ringing against the damp wood. The first strike is a question. Sap weeps immediately into the wound, amber and sweet, feeding the lichen that will colonize the scar. I strike again, listening. The tree shifts, a groan that rolls down the trunk like thunder in a valley. I am not fighting the rot; I am working with it, channeling the moisture, housing it, making room for breath in the saturated mass. Each chip I remove is compost waiting to happen, food for the fungi that will glow green and cold-blue in the dark hollows.

From below, a voice calls up—a Root-Keeper, ancient, wrapped in layers of felted moss, her skin nearly the color of wet bark. She sings the name of the tree, a melody that changes pitch as the bark expands. I sing back, my voice rough with the damp, a harmony of maintenance. We are the consciousness of this place, the nervous system of a cathedral that breathes, the immune response of a wound that refuses to close.

The rain intensifies, horizontal now, condensing out of the air itself onto my skin. I do not flinch. I am wet, and I am warm, and I am loud. The settlement rocks gently around me, a ship in a storm of its own making, and I carve, and the tree grows around my blade, and the midges rise, and the fungi begin to glow. I am the surgeon of sap, the lasher of living root, the keeper of the green architecture. I am home.
