I am the pressure in my chest first—not breath, but the roar. The stone transmits it. Three billion years of rain and ice and wind have worn me smooth, yet I still feel the water's hunger grinding against my flanks, kilometers above the lowlands, heavy and cold and full of teeth.

My skin is not skin. It is armor—plates of mineralized hide, glassy and perpetually wet, the color of the domes I walk upon. I am low, broad, dozens of muscular legs gripping the slippery metamorphic stone. Each foot ends in a ring of microscopic suction cups, holding me to the primordial rock even as the mist lashes me, even as the whitewater crashes against the sills below, thundering into the submerged canyons.

I am scraping.

My jaws work with a rasping sound lost in the grinding roar. I am eating the biofilm, the thick moss that clings to the ancient surface. It tastes of time—of accumulation, of the slow digestion of mountains. I am slow. I am deliberate. While the water screams and churns, hyperactive and chaotic, I move with the patience of stone itself.

On my back, a Spray-Diver perches. It is dark, iridescent, its wings broad planes of rigid feathers that shed the constant rain of spray. It picks parasites from my folds—tiny crustaceans that scuttle between my plates, cleaning, serving. Lichens root in my warmth, gray and crusty, blooming across my shoulders like snow. I am an island. I am a world. I carry them as the Channel-Whales carry kelp armor, as the domes carry the sea.

I am crossing a sill.

Below, the water is a maelstrom—a standing wave that never breaks, roaring through the pass between domes. The turbulence reshapes everything constantly, tearing at the rusted skeletons of hydroelectric stations, carrying sediment from the abyssal depths to build new islands while scouring the primordial stone back toward flatness. But here, on this ridge, I am above it. For now. The water is winning, slowly, imperceptibly, but I am here. I am mineral. I am permanence.

The Spray-Diver launches. It folds its wings and drops like a stone into the maelstrom, snatching a silver fish from the Torrent-School with its serrated, grappling beak. It surfaces through the foam in an explosive burst, shaking water from its feathers, then rides the thermal updraft rising from the geothermal vent, circling in the gray radiance until it returns to my back. It is hungry. I am slow. We are symbiosis.

In the kelp forests below—those twenty-meter towers of rubbery fronds rising from the deepest trenches—the Frond-Sloths navigate the swaying jungles, never touching bottom. The Channel-Whales drift through the deep channels, massive and encrusted with orange Rust-Polyps, their subsonic songs vibrating in my chest, communicating across kilometers of opaque, sediment-thick water. The Gear-Worms pulse on the turning gears of the old civilization, orange and black against the iron, filtering the accelerated current.

And the Mist-Dwellers.

They watch from the highest ridges, from the abandoned structures with shattered windows and moss-colonized roofs. They are few, scattered, pale and thick-skinned, with lungs too large for the thin air, blood rich with hemoglobin. They speak in clicks and low whistles designed to carry over the roar. They hunt us—me—for my hide. They fish from the ruins with nets woven from kelp fiber, hooks forged from rusted rebar. They are the ghosts of the industrial age, clinging to the domes as the water slowly claims the last of their works.

I feel them watching. I am aware. I am prey, but I am also ancient. I am the stone that predates their engines, that will outlast their memory. When they came, they tried to tame the water with concrete and electrified infrastructure, turbines that now turn slowly in the flow, grinding with a mechanical rhythm that competes with the water's thunder. Their industry is dying, being digested by the very flood it once sought to control.

I do not build. I do not remember the before-time. I only scrape. I only move, when the turbulence permits, from dome to dome across the shallow sills. My bulk stirs clouds of sediment as I pass. I am solitary, but I am not alone.

The mist hangs in permanent suspension, filtering the sun into a soft, gray radiance. No harsh shadows. Only the muted glow of a world where the boundary between land and sea has dissolved entirely, where the oldest stable crust meets the most restless water.

I am the Gneiss-Grazer. I am the slow resistance. I am the mineralized hide, the many legs, the symbiotic crustaceans, the lichen, the bird. I am the ancient rock wearing life like armor, moving through the flood with the patience of three billion years, waiting for the water to finish what it started—knowing, in the deep time of stone, that I will be here when it does.

The water roars. I rasp. I hold on.
