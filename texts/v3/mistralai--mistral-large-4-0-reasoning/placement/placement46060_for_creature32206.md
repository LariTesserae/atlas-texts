I am the membrane between weight and floor. I have been below the surface for so long that the boundary between water and world has dissolved around me; I am become the soup, the saturated medium, the silence that has learned to drift.

The snow presses against my skin—millions of tiny grains, mineral and organic, each one a fragment of a mountain that existed before breath. It falls upward and sideways, caught in thermal screams, churning in eddies I cannot see but taste with the lattice of filaments extending from my flanks. They read the turbulence, map the silent storm, translate pressure into knowing. I am eyeless; the darkness here is not absence but substance, a black water that presses against my translucent flanks with the weight of geology. I carry no light, only the faint green-white phosphorescence of my own organs, pulsing slow in chambers that pump viscous fluid.

I am cold. A chill of 0.5 that penetrates to the bone of the stone, keeping me in suspended animation, near-frozen, hydrostatic. My body is glass held together by pressure, a sac of protoplasm that would collapse under any lesser weight. To move is to negotiate with a medium that does not yield; I do not swim but jerk, contract, pulse—a primitive grace through the viscous dark. The water is dynamic, turbulent, a storm-state of chaotic churning, yet it is mute. The density absorbs all vibration. Even the grinding of stone against stone produces no echo. I would hear my own heartbeat, if I had ears that could transmit the vibration through this thick, cold soup, but the silence is total, a physical weight pressing against where ears would be.

I carry stone. In one of my appendages—pale roots, translucent, four perhaps six—I hold a flake of chert. It was worn smooth by eons of current, a knuckle of primordial rock that resisted the flattening, but I struck it against another stone, and now it has an edge. I made this. The impact was felt, not heard—a vibration transmitted through the dense water directly into my nervous tissue, a shockwave in the absolute silence. I am a knapper of the deep, a tool-user whose technology is indistinguishable from geology. I use this primitive hand-tool to crack the calcium shells of the Grazers, the sparse mollusks that drift across the bacterial mats. The mats are thin films of grey and faint white, chemosynthetic bacteria pulsing with chemical luminescence where ancient cold seeps weep minerals from the rock—the only "plants" here, lichen on a tombstone.

I am alone. The vastness of this basin—scale 2.0, a regional plain lying at moderate depth—and the density of the medium make community impossible. I drift for hours, days, through the packed particulate fog, surrounded by the debris of deep time, the settled snow that never melts. I feel each grain against my skin, a tactile blizzard of mineral and organic detritus, being abraded constantly by the memory of height. Below me, the floor is flat—flat like a memory worn smooth, eroded to its final form over eons, the end of the world’s violence. I feel its absolute sameness through the pressure, the lithified ooze that offers no purchase, no shelter, no variation.

When I find a Grazer—slow-moving across the luminescent mat—I wait. My metabolism is slowed by the cold to a geological pace. I wait until it drifts close, suspended in the viscous water. Then I strike. Stone meets shell. In the silence, the crack is a scream no one hears, only feels—a shockwave through the saturated medium, a vibration that travels through water and into flesh, a maelstrom without impact.

I am one of the Translucent, though no name reaches me; names require sound, and sound dies in the throat of this world. I am a pressure differential, a slow eddy in the viscous dark, drifting through a world that is ending. The erosion has reached its oldest-stable-state; the floor is flat, the mountains are gone, worn down into dust and dust into stone and stone into dust again. Yet the water above churns with hyperactive, restless energy, as if the world cannot decide whether to be still or to move. The snow falls forever, upward and sideways, packed to its limit with the history of the world column above.

When I die—when the cold finally wins, when my chambers stop pulsing—I will sink. The viscous water will lower me slowly, gently, to the flat floor. I will settle into the ooze, my translucent body becoming part of the sediment. My stone tool will fall from my nerveless appendage and lie beside me on the plain of settled snow, a flaked stone on a plain of settled snow, the only monument to a mind that worked with the only materials available: stone, bone, and the precipitated calcium of the snow itself. A fossil waiting to happen, already half-formed, already becoming part of the erosion that has reached its final state.

Until then, I drift. I crack shells. I make tools. I wait. The snow falls forever. The water churns without sound. And I move through it, pale and eyeless, carrying the memory of mountains in my translucent appendages, a ghost in a world that has worn itself smooth, waiting for the final settling, when even the turbulence will cease and the plain will be truly, absolutely still.
