I rise. Not fall, but rise—through the column of black glass that is the center pool, breaking the meniscus where the water listens to itself. The surface tension holds me for a moment, a skin of force, and then I am through, six legs splaying to touch the peaks and troughs of the standing waves.

Around me, the hexagons shimmer. They are not decoration but architecture—subsonic vibrations made visible, the water’s self-generated geometry. I move along their lines, my tarsi reading the invisible currents, the pressure differentials that sing up from the eels below.

The sound hits me first. Not through ears—I have none that matter—but through the water, through the membrane of my body, through the six points of contact that tremble with the drone. It is the Pale Chorus, thousands of throats pulsing in harmonic lockstep with the stone. The bass note of the eels vibrates in my sternum, a subsonic hum that makes the surface ripple around me. Above, the bright trills bounce off the curved limestone, overtones that no single frog could produce, creating a chord that never resolves.

I am one of the Geometers. We skate in strict formations, not randomly but along the lines of force. The water-striders. We are the rhythm section, our movements creating tiny percussive splashes that the chamber amplifies into counterpoint. Tap. Tap. Tap. Against the sustained drone of the deep.

I drift toward the center, where the pool is deepest, where the black glass is thickest. Below me, in the silt, the eels writhe—ancestors, eyeless, consuming the dead. A frog has sung its final note and sunk; I feel the disturbance in the water column, the settling of organic matter, the slow stir of the deep ones. They are the memory, moving through bone and shell, charged with mineral electricity.

Above, the Parliament hangs. Bats wrapped in gray shawls, their wings testing the air. They are the silent auditors, emitting clicks that ride the curved surfaces, mapping the chamber in three dimensions. They hunt us. I see one drop—a shadow falling, wings folding—and I adjust my angle, skating faster along the hexagon’s edge, becoming a staccato note in the percussion, a splash that answers the frog’s drone.

The light is jade, diffuse, filtering down from the oculus no wider than a cartwheel. It makes the water gleam like wet skin. I absorb it, my body translucent, and I become a small, moving prism on the surface.

A newt drifts past—a philosopher, orange and jade, slow and deliberate. It is a visual rest in the cacophony, absorbing the green light and re-emitting it as a faint internal glow. It does not sing. It does not listen. It simply is, drifting between the striders and the eels, a living jewel in the silt-shadows.

I feel the water level rising. The rain has swollen the aqueduct outside; the primitive channel carved into the back wall is feeding the basin. The tide comes in, slow and enclosed. The frogs migrate up the walls, re-tuning themselves to the altered resonant frequency. The hexagons shift, the standing waves change their geometry. I must move, skating toward the new nodes, maintaining my position in the formation, never breaking the pattern.

The stone around me is wet, weeping. Condensation gathers on the upper walls, heavy beads that swell and fall—each drop striking the water with a sound like a fingernail tapped against a bell. A drop falls near me, creating a concentric ring that disrupts the hexagon for a moment, then settles into the new vibration. I ride the disturbance, reading the altered architecture of force.

I remember—though I am small, and my life is short—the builders. Their hands knew only stone, antler, and fiber. They fitted the limestone blocks without mortar, carried down through the throat of stacked stone. Their intention is lithified into the walls, still resonating, still singing through the throats of the pale frogs. I skate on water that carries their dissolved memory, the gametes of frogs, the spores of moss. I am part of the circuit: I eat the algae that grows on the submerged pavement, I am eaten by the bats, I will die and sink to the silt, becoming food for the eels, becoming bone and shell in the century-thick accumulation, becoming part of the architecture.

The spirals in the stone—the flow-stones, the runnels worn by centuries of water—turn against the current, or with it, in a direction that makes the eye water. From the surface, I see them reflected, distorted, the geometry of perception wrong somehow, beautiful and unsettling. The water behaves oddly here: in the center, it is disturbed not by wind but by subsonic vibrations, standing waves that create the hexagonal patterns I read. The frogs orient toward these patterns, arranging themselves in rings, their bodies aligned with invisible lines of force. I align too, my six legs touching the nodes, becoming a living sensor for the chamber’s self-listening.

The sound is overwhelming. A physical pressure against my body, metallic and wet, so dense I can taste it. It is a cathedral of noise built by pre-metal hands, an acoustic engine powered by gravity and the slow pulse of the earth. I am the high-frequency shimmer at the edge of the rhythm, the staccato tapping that answers the sustained drone. I do not sing, but I play—my legs on the water’s skin, creating ripples that the chamber amplifies into music.

When the water rises, I move. When it falls, I move. When the bats hunt, I flee along the geometric lines. When the eels stir below, I feel it in the tremor of the surface. I am the interface, the boundary, the geometer reading the invisible mathematics of resonance.

I am here, on the black glass, in the jade gloom, in the deafening hymn. I am the Water-Strider, one of the Geometers, skating the standing waves, listening to the water listen to itself, part of the perpetual, mineral, biological song that never resolves, never breaks, only continues—dripping, croaking, splashing, writhing—into the deep.
