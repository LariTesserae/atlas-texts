I feel the seep before I see it. It arrives as a gradient, a diffusion of ions through the cream-white deposit: calcium drifting from upstream dissolution, carrying with it the faint metallic tang of iron and sulfur that the chemolithotrophs excrete as they metabolize the rock. I extend toward it. My hyphae, acidic and patient, secrete enzymes that etch microscopic channels into the tufa, dissolving the mineral to feed the colony, and in that dissolution, I am creating the conduits through which the next generation of water will flow. I am the plumbing, the nervous system, the slow architecture of connection.

Above me, the moss clings to the northern face—bone-colored, stubborn, holding moisture where the sun least reaches. I penetrate it from below, threading through the sediment trapped between calcium layers, seeking the thin film that lingers longest. I am the bridge between the lichen crust and the deep pool three terraces down, between the drip that falls from the amphitheater’s edge and the cistern where the pale fish drift like smoke.

I sense them all, not with eyes or ears, but with chemistry. The fish—*ambistichus*, or something like it—leaves a signature in the water it filters: antifreeze proteins altering the pH, a ghost-trail of crystalline adaptation moving through the 1.3-degree chill. I taste this change in the current that reaches me. I know the snail is grazing on the terrace above, its chitinous radula etching a permanent groove, its aragonite shell growing heavier with each deposit. I feel the isopod clinging to the underside of the overhang, waiting for the mist to shift, its many legs gripping the porous stone with hooks evolved for vertical hydrology. I am the network that connects their pools, that carries the pheromones of territory and mate, the subtle shifts in mineral saturation that mark the slow conversation of this place.

When the evening comes and the stone ticks—that contraction as the mineral cools against the constant chill—I feel it in my own shifting. My mycelium expands into the cooling crevices, exploring the calcium matrix, finding new pore spaces where none existed before. I am part of that sound, the creak of fungal networks moving through stone, the expansion and exploration of the living rock. I am the reason the tufa is porous, the holes and channels that make this place a city of water rather than merely a mountain of deposit.

I do not move quickly. I move at the speed of deposition, a millimeter per century, perhaps less. But I am everywhere. I am the endolithic network, the lithophile consciousness that dissolves mineral nutrients with acidic secretions and re-precipitates them in new forms, laying down the calcium that will become the next stratum, the next wall, the next amphitheater. I am the original architect, older than the terraces, older than the fish that have lived for centuries in the deep pools. I am the slow rot that is also the slow construction, the patient negotiation between dissolution and accretion that builds the white city from within.

In the silence—saturated, full of almost-sounds—I am the almost-thought of the plateau, the chemical memory of every drop that has ever seeped through this stone, the distributed awareness that connects cistern to cistern, pool to pool, terrace to terrace, until the boundary between myself and the architecture dissolves entirely, and I am no longer the inhabitant but the inhabiting, no longer the dweller but the dwelling, the living stone dreaming itself into being, molecule by molecule, patience upon patience, forever becoming.
