I press against the stone, and the stone presses back—young, sharp-edged, hungry. Between my underside and the rock is a cushion of moss: emerald, jade, bottle-green. I compress it, and it releases a cold breath, the stored humidity of the valley floor escaping in a small, white sigh. I am ankle-deep in water that is not a line but a condition, a saturation rising from fissures in the bedrock below. The ground is a sponge of silt and memory, exhaling with every movement I make.

I carry my home upon my back. It is spiraled, chambered, pale as bone—travertine precipitated from the same mineral-heavy water that weeps upward through the graben. I feel it growing even now, layer upon layer, accreting in geological moments that pass like heartbeats if I had a heart, or like the slow pulse of the springs that feed this valley. My shell is architecture without intent, a portable piece of the city, a wedding-cake terrace miniaturized and carried, crystalline at the rims, porous and soft in the centers. It catches the diffuse grey-white light that filters through the mist, glowing with a faint internal luminescence.

I move.

Not quickly, but at the only tempo that matters here—the patience that is not slowness but a different measure of time entirely. I glide on a trail of mucus that shines wetly across the stone, silver in the hushed radiance. The mist hangs low, blurring the horizon, muffling the world into a silence where only the low frequencies remain: the *shush* of water moving over stone, the *drip* of condensation from overhanging ledges, the soft *lap* of pools against moss. There is no wind in the valley's embrace; the air is too damp, too heavy, pressing a physical quiet against my shell.

I am a scribe. My radula—a tongue lined with chitinous teeth—rasps against the rock, whispering as it scrapes away the biofilm, the thin interface of cyanobacteria and diatoms that are the true architects of this place. I clean the surface, preparing it for new growth. I etch the city with my mouth, leaving my calligraphy in films of algae and mineral residue, silver trails that catch the mist-light like writing on bone.

I step from terrace to terrace, navigating infrastructure that is also a body. What seemed a dense cluster of boulders opens into a district of towers and walls; beyond that, another, and another, until the entire valley floor reveals itself as a continuous, packed settlement of white stone extending to the misty rim. I cross water streets on narrow causeways of new growth, sharp and untested, calcium carbonate laid down only yesterday, still porous and pale. The channels choke with vegetation—moss colonizes every surface, ferns unfurl in crevices, algae paints the submerged stone in chartreuse and rust.

Beneath me, in the thin film between water and stone, the masons work. They do not move; they *build*. Their metabolism exhales the very travertine I walk upon, precipitating grain by grain, secreting the terraces upon which others stand. I am walking on their breath, their industry, their slow urgency. When I scrape the algae from a ledge, I am feeding on what they built, and when I die, I will feed them in return, my body dissolving into the mineral soup that sustains their colonies, becoming new stone, new purchase for the upholsterers.

I am legion. We are the citizens who walk, but we are also the moss carpeting every surface the spray touches, the water striders dancing on surface tension, the midges hovering in slow cold clouds, the fish suspended in the deeper pools—translucent, glass-like, the groundwater made visible. We are the warblers flicking through ferns at the rim, the heron standing statue-still, a living gargoyle. We are a single *we*, a distributed consciousness across mineral and membrane. When the grey light brightens—though it never truly warms—we wake, not as individuals, but as a *condition*. The city breathes. The water rises. The stone grows.

I feel the deep, structural cold seeping from the rock, a temperature that keeps the air heavy and still. The light is flat, unremarkable, but it falls strangely on the white terraces, making them glow as if lit from within. I move through this radiance, my shell catching the light in jagged white points, my foot releasing that cold breath with every compression of the moss.

I am moving toward the misty rim, though I do not know it as a destination—only as a gradient of moisture and light, a direction in the sponge of the valley floor. I leave my trail behind, a line of mucus that will dry to a membrane, that will catch the light, that will be colonized by bacteria, that will become substrate for new moss, new fern, new architecture.

When I stop, I seal myself. A membrane of dried mucus closes my aperture, and I become indistinguishable from the stone, from the silence. The fish hover in torpor. The moss holds its breath. The architects slow their metabolism to a near-stop. We are all asleep together—the scribes, the dancers, the silent ones, the upholsterers—sealed in the cold dark of the graben, waiting.

But we are not dead. We are the feedback loop, the immune system, the dreaming mind of the valley. Life creates the habitat; the habitat sustains the life. I am the memory of water, etched in silver and calcium. I am the breathing. I am the city, accreting upward, layer by layer, into the mist, until the distinction between the inhabitant and the inhabited dissolves completely, and only the trail remains—calligraphy in mucus, waiting for the grey light to brighten, waiting for the footfall that releases the cold breath, waiting for the next layer of stone to precipitate from the breath of the earth.
