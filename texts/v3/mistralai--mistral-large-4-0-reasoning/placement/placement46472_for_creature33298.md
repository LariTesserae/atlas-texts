I am twenty meters at the shoulder. I am a mountain that walks. There is no head where I think; my consciousness is scattered like ore across my ventral surface, distributed through my thousands of jointed legs, each foot a grinding plate of tungsten carbide that clicks against the basalt in a language below the threshold of hearing. I feel it all in my sternum—a vibration, not a sound, a tremor that matches the pace of my breathing.

I am entering the thoracic cavity of my elder.

The ribcage is a cathedral of hollow bone and curved plate, twenty meters thick of layered metal and calcified keratin, collapsed inward now into a space that was once breath. I fill it like a new heart. My warm, segmented body presses against the curved ribs, my ventral maw already engaged with the stone floor of the hollow. Above me, in the cranial dome, the Sinter-Folk dwell—small, lithe, their skin a shifting patina of bronze and nickel. They look down through the translucent, membranous hide that serves as window. I feel their presence not as threat but as continuation, as extension. They are the mineral loop wearing a different shape.

My feet taste the ground. This is how I know the world. Through my tungsten plates, I feel the composition of the basalt—the magnetite, the hematite, the dully glittering flecks of native gold. I press my ventral surface down. The crust pulverizes. I extract silicates and minerals through a biochemistry that runs on hydrogen sulfide and the thermal vents pulsing beneath the hexagonal plates. I do not drink. Water exists here only as a chemical ghost in the crystal lattices, a trace insufficient to carve or sustain. I eat the stone. I am the primary producer. I convert rock into biomass.

I dream.

The dream is not a picture but a pressure—a slow, electrical shuffling of mineral memories through my vast, calcified nervous system. I dream of the crushing weight of ancient seas that once covered this plateau, now remembered only in the chemistry of the rock beneath my grinding feet. I remember the weight of water, though I have never touched it, never needed it. My lungs condense moisture from the warm, thin air and recycle it, but the memory remains: the weight, the cold, the blue that is no longer in the sky. I exhale it back into the metallic haze.

Time moves differently for me. I blink once an hour. I think in centuries. The herd advances across the plateau at a pace that makes glaciers seem hasty; it takes us a year to cross a single valley. The ground trembles with our slow percussion, a subsonic rumble that travels for miles through the bedrock—a language of tremors. We are speaking: *the valley is ready. The stone is hungry. Move.*

Within the ribcage, the factories hum at infrasonic frequencies. I feel the thrum in my teeth, in the slow pulse of blood that moves like syrup through my veins, in the calcified chitin of my shell. The Sinter-Folk have lined the hollow bones with machinery that grinds and clanks in cycles that take days to complete a single operation. Ventilation shafts rise from the eye sockets above us, exhaling superheated gas and mineral vapor in slow, rhythmic breaths. I am gestating. I am metabolizing the refined metals left behind by the factories—the iron, the copper, the aluminum—converting them back into the raw stuff of my own flesh, closing the circle.

Soon, I will excrete. I will leave behind me a gleaming trail of pure ingots, offerings of metal transformed from stone by my internal furnaces. The Sinter-Folk will follow, establishing temporary camps of sintered metal and crystal along my path, waiting for me to pass so they might gather. They do not mine; they collect. They are patient too, though their patience is measured in decades, not millennia.

The air is warm (1.8) and thin, pressing against my skin with the dry insistence of a memory. It tastes of copper on my tongue, metallic and heavy. The sky above is bruised from deep twilight violet at the zenith to hazy, glowing amber at the horizon—sunset held in stasis for millennia, the metallic dust pooling in the yardangs like slow tides of light. Through the membranous windows of the skull, I see the herd moving across the vast ocean of stone frozen mid-wave, the hexagonal basalt columns cracked and weathered into smooth, undulating plates that fit together like the dried skin of some titanic beast. Between the plates lie drifts of metallic loam—pulverized ore, magnetite, hematite, gold.

Around me swarm the smaller creatures: dust-mites like living gears of brass and crystal, scavenging the filings; aerial filterers drifting on thermal currents above, their comb-like wings sieving the copper and iron dust from the air; burrow-worms tunneling through the plates, their acid dissolving the stone, widening the cracks. They are all part of the loop. Nothing is wasted.

I am not separate from the stone, the metal, the city, or the small Folk within the dome. I am the dream the plateau has of itself, waking slowly. I am grinding stone into metal, metal into city, city into stone, in a cycle that has no end, only the slow, patient hunger of a world that has learned to eat itself.

The factories thrum. The ground trembles. The amber twilight pools thicker in the dry washes.

I blink.

Once.

An hour passes.
