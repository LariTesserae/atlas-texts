I am breathing. Or rather, I am exhaling what is left of warmth into air that cannot hold it, that snatches the vapor and turns it to architecture—jagged, dendritic frost that hangs in a halo around my face, shattering into the pewter light when I turn my head. My skin has gone the color of the stone beneath my boots: pale, translucent, veined with blue rivers that pulse close to the surface, carrying heat from the core of my barrel chest, from lungs that work like bellows, drawing in the thin, metallic air—ozone and mineral dust—and working it until it burns.

My eyes are changing. Again. The third eyelid—milky, opaque, the membrane that guards against glare—sweeps across in a slow, saline blink, leaving a film that freezes before it can fall. I do not see with clarity anymore. I see with temperature. With pressure. With the subtle shimmer of steam against steam, the way heat distorts the condensing twilight. I am becoming what the elders are: a Vein-Seer, though the burning has only begun, and for now I see with both—glacial ice and the memory of color, the world dissolving at the edges into gradients of warmth.

I stand on bare stone. The rock is gray, angular, utterly unweathered—pottery shards underfoot, crunching, shifting, still learning how to be ground. It rises millimeters each year, cracking open to release the heat below, and I can feel it. Not with my feet, which are numb in their pelt boots, the oily hides of the shaggy herds that grazed these margins, but with the skin of my palms pressed flat against the fractured surface. Heat. Not the ambient cold that bites my cheeks and crystallizes my breath, but a deep, patient warmth rising from the embryonic geology. Three days. Perhaps four. Water there.

Behind me, the camp is dissolving. This is the Rite of Release, performed as the pewter light fades and the steam thickens, the evening surrender to impermanence. The vesicles—amber, translucent, glowing like eggs from within—are deflating, their bio-synthesized membranes folding into gray bundles, their pneumatic grapples releasing their bite from the fissures. My own dwelling, which I have inhabited for seventeen days (or seventeen hours—time here is measured in weather, in the moods of the vents, not in clocks), is collapsing into a pack that the harvester will carry. I am unbuilding myself. I unplug the tubes that siphoned warmth from the geothermal seeps, feeling the cold rush in through my shoulders where steam once rose like a smokestack in miniature, watching my own breath crystallize in jagged halos that shatter when I move.

The sound comes first: a low, continuous thrum, vibrating through the thin air, through the stone, through the marrow of my elongated spine. The harvester approaches. It is crab-like, cottage-sized, its hydraulic legs unfolding like the wings of some mechanical insect, its electrified undercarriage glowing with a faint, blue-white light in the dimness. It does not stop. It never stops. The Unfixed do not build; we inhabit the pause, and the pause is ending. The ground has decided. A fissure is opening three kilometers east—I can feel it in my teeth, a shiver in the mineral tang of the air, a change in the pressure that makes my third eyelid flutter involuntarily.

I move. Not toward shelter—there is none, there will never be—but toward the machine. My pelt is heavy with warmth, the tubes now coiled and cold against my gray skin. Other Drift-Born move beside me, their eyes also going milky, their spines curved from generations of thin air, their chests working like bellows. We are children of the moving camps, born in vesicles, taught to walk on shifting crust, our inner ears adapted to the constant micro-tremors of the rising mountains. We have never known the vertigo of stillness.

We climb. The harvester's flanks are open cages, steaming, smelling of iron and ozone and the mineral tang of new stone. I step up, my boots finding the grating, and I am lifted. The vesicles are packed tight against the machine's sides—remoras on a shark, we have always been remoras—translucent amber bladders pressed against the crab-like metal, deflating further as we rise. As the hydraulic legs begin to stride, the ground falls away beneath us, the saturated flats, the black pools, the wounds in the earth that weep mineral-rich water and steam in the freezing air.

Below, the plateau extends past the limits of comfortable vision, a shallow bowl curving away at nearly four thousand meters. The eyeless fish scatter in the warm shallows. The massive, salamander-like amphibians filter nutrients from the geothermal minerals. The shaggy, long-horned herds move on the rocky margins—dark, slow-moving stains on the horizon, abundant in number but sparse in presence, drifting through the emptiness like thoughts. Above, the sky is never empty: flocks of hardy waterfowl wheel in dense, shifting clouds, their cries muffled by the mist.

I raise my face to the condensing twilight. The sun, when it breaks through, is a pale, distant coin, diffused by ten thousand rising vapors. Steam everywhere—my breath crystallizing instantly, the machine's exhaust joining the natural mist, the earth's own exhalation. I am becoming part of the soup. The crystals form and shatter, form and shatter, jagged halos around my face as I move with the fleet.

The Rite is complete. I have taken apart my own shelter, folded my bedding of gray-green moss, stacked my possessions into the pack. I have voluntarily surrendered the brief, fragile stillness of the pause. The harvester walks, and I walk with it, or am carried, wrapped in steam, watching the world change beneath us—the stone cracking, the water shifting, the mountains rising, always rising.

I am the Unfixed. I do not have a name, or rather, my name is the motion itself, the drift, the exhalation. My history is not written but breathed out into the thin air, dissolving and reforming like the mist itself. I am the human expression of the geological youth, rising with the mountains, cracking with the stone, weeping with the thermal springs.

When the geyser erupts—a wet, percussive *whump* that sends a column of steam twenty meters into the gray—I do not flinch. I raise my milky eyes to the scalding rain of minerals, and I breathe it in, crystallizing instantly, becoming part of the condensing soup, waiting for the ground to shift again so I can move with it.

I am here, where the world is still deciding what it wants to be, and I am becoming whatever it decides.
