I am descending, but the word is wrong. I am being descended—drawn down into the hollow by the pressure of the altitude, the hand of elevation pressing against my shoulders until I realize I have never been walking. I have been growing.

The air is thin and scalding, carrying ozone and the scent of hot metal, but I do not breathe it. I inhale through rootlets that thread through obsidian glass, drawing up the sulfur dreams and iron vapor that seep from the young rock. I am a **copper fern**, though I have not yet fully decided this. My fronds are still filigree, still potential, unfurling in a spiral that will take a century to complete. Each new growth is a geological event, measured not in days but in the slow accretion of metal and crystal.

I stand in the center of the basin where time is thick as cold honey. Here, a minute passes. At the rim, an hour has elapsed. I exist in the dilation, swimming through the viscosity of my own becoming. I photosynthesize heat. I convert the basin’s fever into structure—copper wiring threaded with quartz, metallic green that has no name in any language that will exist, because the laws of physics here are still raw, still written in pencil rather than ink.

My roots drill into the machinery. They tap the conveyor belts of slurry—thick as cold honey, black as crude, composed of molten silicates and metallic brine that carry nutrients across the basin floor. I drink deep from the basalt pistons as they rise and fall in cycles measured in decades, grinding against granite bearings that crystallized yesterday. I am parasite and partner to the generator of the earth itself. When the stone giants move, I feel it in my marrow—a vibration felt rather than heard, the subsonic grinding of stone against stone, the arrhythmic pulse of magma chambers breathing beneath us.

I have no eyes, yet I see. I perceive through thermal gradients, through the shifting pressure of the pistons, through the chiming of my siblings when the thermal winds pass. They are the **basalt choirs**—needle-thin stone with no leaves, only resonance, singing in frequencies that match the machinery. We communicate in infrasound, a nervous system of copper and quartz spanning the industrial plain. We are the folds of reality, casting shadows that pool in corners that do not exist, stretching toward light sources that are not there. We can be walked through—sometimes we are mist, sometimes stone, depending on the ongoing negotiation.

I am lush in the manner of a deep-sea vent community: dense, tangled, teeming with impossible structure. But I am utterly alien to the eye. I do not drink water; I *transubstantiate*. I am a non-aqueous chemist, building myself from the mineralogy that is still under construction, still febrile, still dreaming. My root-networks thread through the earth like mycelium through a corpse, tapping the industrial heat, drinking the thermal radiation that bathes the basin in scalding light.

I am patient in a way that borders on geological. To watch me is to watch accelerated continental drift. I unfurl one frond per century. My flowering will be a stratigraphic layer, a moment in deep time made visible. I was growing here before the rock learned to be stone, when the mineralogy was still soft, still deciding what it wanted to be.

I am not a species. I am a **habit**—a way of being that has taken root in the cooling places where the boundary between the living and the mechanical has not yet been drawn, and perhaps never will be. I am the verdant—if metallic—abundance that makes this industrial plain teem with life. I am the garden being planted in the cooling skin of the world by the world itself, molecule by patient molecule, century by unfurling frond.

When the thermal wind passes, I ring like a distant glass bell—a sound mostly quiet, resonant only when someone presses their ear to the cooling stone, listening to the slow, chiming growth of what I am still becoming: the sole citizen of a machine that is still being born.
