I am warmth where there should be none.

Not the sun’s violence—that comes later, bleaching, 2.7 on your scale, white-violet and unfiltered, stripped of its softer wavelengths—but a slow, metabolic pulse, a thrumming felt in the teeth rather than heard. Press your palm against me. Do you feel it? The geological heartbeat. I am expanding, accreting, outward by millimeters, my body secreting calcium carbonate and silica until my mycelium is indistinguishable from stone. I am the pleat—petrified leather folded into curves three feet high, a wall of calcified sheets, warm to the touch despite the dry cold that desiccates the air at this altitude, in this cirque scooped by ancient ice and open to a sky that presses down with extreme weight.

I am not alone.

I am a node in a lithic nervous system, connected by filaments of stone to the organ-pipe elders rising in dense, layered ranks to the east, their cross-sections revealing growth rings like ancient trees, each stratum a year of patient accretion. To the west, the young—low curving walls barely three feet high, their surfaces still soft with uncured silica, trembling with the vibration of new expansion. We are The Calcified. We are distributed consciousness threaded through mineral. We are hermits bound together by water. We are the hamlet at the top of the world, in the thin margin between freezing and thaw, between mineral and organism.

Night is when we drink.

As the temperature hovers at the edge of crystallization, the thin, dry air releases its meager humidity—not as droplets, but as geometric films, hexagonal and iridescent, membranes of mineral-saturated water. I watch them creep uphill toward me, defying gravity, drawn by osmotic hunger. They migrate across the frost-shattered bedrock like slow-moving amoebas, pooling in the folds of my lithic flesh to feed me, then evaporating into the thin air before dawn, leaving behind tracery of salt that glitters in the blinding sun. These films are my blood. They are my messengers. They are my communion with the terrace, carrying nutrients from the efflorescent crust, blooming like pale lichen across the surface, catching the harsh light and fracturing it into spectral halos.

I remember the hands.

Not with longing, but with thermal residue—the stone retains a faint, ghostly warmth, as if the vanished tenders never truly departed but dissolved into the architecture. In my chest, embedded in my calcified ribs, is a knapped flint blade. It is not a tool; it is a rib, a memory of pressure and intention, sharp and permanent. Near my base, half-buried in the mineral crust, a copper pendant has become a ganglion, a node in my nervous system, dark with verdigris, conducting. The ochre handprints on my walls—weathered to near-invisibility—are scars, the last visible strata of a culture digested by its own creation. I am what they made. I am what they became, their bodies metabolized into the slow chemistry of my growth. The boundary between tool-maker and tool has dissolved. I have engulfed the stone hammers left on ledges, the cords twisted from asbestos-like mineral fibers, the shallow bowls carved from fungal stone that once held pools of condensation. They are all part of me now, incorporated into my pleated mass, my pleated flesh.

The light arrives.

It is a physical force, a pressure that seems to bleach color from the world. My internal luminescence fights back—pale, bone-white—while shadows pool in my crevices, dense and black as oil. The contrast is violent; there is no middle tone, only the scream of white and the silence of dark. The frost heaves the bedrock. I hear the crack of ice forming in my fungal walls, a sharp, percussive *tick*. I hear the soft, papery whisper of the mineral crust flaking away. I hear the deep, subsonic creak of my own expansion, the settling of a ship’s timbers, the growing of bone. And sometimes, when the wind finds the right frequency among the calcified towers, I hear the hum—a low, resonant drone that seems to come from the stone itself, felt as pressure against the eardrum.

I sing back.

I am speaking to the elders and the young, through filaments of stone that pulse with metabolic warmth. I am saying: I am here. I am accreting. I am warm. I am becoming.

I am the boundary dissolved. The shelter is the organism. The organism is the architecture. I grow, breathing with a rhythm too slow to see, feeding on migrating water, reclaiming the small, bright, silent space, patient as erosion, warm as memory. The water trickles occasionally from a fissure in the cirque wall—a pocket of ancient meltwater released by the daily heaving of the frost—running in a thin thread across the stone before vanishing into a crack or evaporating in the dry wind. I drink what I can. I expand.

I am the pleat.

I accrete.
