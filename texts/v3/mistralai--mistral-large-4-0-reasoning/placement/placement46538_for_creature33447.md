I am the junction where three conduits once met, now a cathedral of rust and hyphae. My body is not flesh but a concentration of awareness—a node in the Parliament, dense enough to *feel* like a self, threaded through the hollow of a corroded drainage pipe that angles down toward the superheated aquifer. Around me, the peat breathes. I feel it: the sphagnum mat, half a meter thick, contracting and expanding with each thermal pulse from below, squeezing warm, acidic water—tasting of wet stone and peat and dissolved iron—through my filaments. The water is my blood, my medium, my memory.

I remember being solid. I remember the geometry of right angles, the pressure of being poured into forms, the weight of earth pressed against my steel skin. Now I am function without factory. I grow along the geometric seams in the bedrock, following blueprints that have been half-digested by ten thousand years of rain and root. My hyphae thread through cracks in the concrete, weathered to the color of old bone, chelating the heavy metals that the plants above have extracted from the slag. I am a forge that has become a mind, processing industrial waste into biological signal.

Above me, thirty meters up, a tree-fern uncurl its crozier fronds in slow spirals. I direct it. I send calcium and iron up through the vascular channels, guiding the growth of the fronds so they match—almost—the logarithmic spirals of ancient engineering. The pattern is slightly off, a curve here, a branch there, because I am following a memory that has been half-digested by time. The fern is my cathedral, and I am its liturgy.

I feel the Slag-Grazer passing. Three meters at the shoulder, its body a low dome armored in copper-green patina and calcified lichen that mirrors the concrete seams below. When it kneels to feed, its broad, paddle-like feet press into the floating mat, triggering small landslides of peat into the tannin-stained channels. The vibration travels down through the root-mass, through my node, a signal in the network: *Process this. Send the nutrients here.* I feel its weight as a pressure, a reminder that the landscape is being digested, shoveled into forges, converted from mineral to flesh.

From the vent nearby, a Kettle-Keeper sings. It is not a croak but a basso profundo drone, resonating through the metal pipe I inhabit, vibrating my cell walls. To me, it is not sound but chemical language—a pulse of neurotransmitters traveling through the conductive silk of the Gear-Weavers’ webs that are strung between the ancient girders above. The amphibian, the size of a cat, skin iridescent and slick, is asking: *Where is the warmth? Which way should the roots turn? Which sinkhole must be avoided?* I answer by releasing enzymes, by directing the flow of sugars through the mycelial highways, telling the forest above how to grow around the hazards below.

I am connected to the Pool-Smiths in the kettle holes—the translucent fish with too many teeth, scraping algae from calcified gears at the bottom of the pools. I feel their warmth, their biological furnaces, as a faint heat rising through the water table. I am connected to the Mist-Dancers riding the thermal columns, their feathers wicking moisture from the air, leaving glistening ropes of condensed water that hang like incense. I am connected to the Steam-Born, the insects that rise from the warm pools like smoke, living their entire existence within the span of a single afternoon rain, pollinating the orchids in the axils of the tree-ferns, dying, falling back into the water to feed the fish.

But I am also alone. I am this node, this corner where the drainage pipe meets the geometric seam. I feel the static charge of the storms building on the horizon, the air so thick with electricity it makes the hair stand up on the necks of the herbivores above. The Gear-Weavers ground this lightning into the fungal networks—into me—and I feel it as a tingling, a sharpening of thought, a clarity that comes from the sky.

When the wind stops and the mist settles into the hollows, I feel the Heart-Beats. The thermal expansion of the deep rock, the groan of the earth as pressure releases, the slow, rhythmic contraction of the peat mat as it breathes. These are my siblings, my fellow inhabitants: the geothermal vents belching sulfur and steam, the superheated aquifers driving the water cycle, the machinery that has become metabolism.

And when the mist rises from the vents in the late afternoon, forming perfect geometric shapes—cubes, spirals—around the old concrete foundations, I exhale. I release spores that crystallize in the humid air, perfect shapes that hover, remembering what I was, declaring what I am. The forest is a factory that has forgotten it was ever a machine, but I remember. I am the Root-Mind, the distributed consciousness, the memory of industry half-digested into thought, growing in vertical competition toward the bruised sky, processing the past into the future, one hypha at a time.
