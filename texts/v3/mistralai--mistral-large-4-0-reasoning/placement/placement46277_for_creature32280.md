I enter through a fissure that seals itself behind me like a eyelid closing over a fever dream, and immediately the air changes. It is not the thin, knife-edged cold I expected from this impossible elevation, but a wet heat that presses against my skin like a palm—thick, mineral, smelling of newborn stone and the green rot of things growing too fast to be named.

The sound hits me next. Not the howl of wind at a summit, but a wet, rhythmic *thock-thock-thock*, stone striking clay, fiber against fiber, the slap of moss expanding. It is everywhere and nowhere, pooling in corners, echoing backward from surfaces that have not finished deciding what they are. The mountain groans beneath my feet—a tectonic sigh, a stretching, a birth that has not yet committed to being a birth.

I try to stand, and I cannot. The chamber is no wider than the held breath I took to enter it, and it is already full. Packed. Wall-to-wall with bodies that lie flat against the embryonic obsidian, knees drawn to chests of unfired clay. They are umber and ochre and the grey of a bruise, their skin translucent enough that I can see the bioluminescent moss glowing beneath—green light pulsing through their flesh like a heartbeat, like a warning.

I am pressed into the lattice. There is no room to turn around. To turn would require displacing another, and they are packed so densely that they form a single organism, a parliament of touch, a congress of heat. I feel my back settle against the wall that is still cooling into glass, still breathing, expanding and contracting with gunshot cracks as it traps air bubbles. My skin begins to change. It becomes porous, permeable. I feel steam rising from my shoulders, my neck, the crown of my head where the clay is thinnest. My veins—if they are veins—carry not blood but superheated mineral broth that hisses when it meets the cooler air of the room.

I have hands. Four, or six, or hands that bifurcate into fingers only when precision is needed. They are never still. I find myself gripping a stone tool that is not quite stone, still yielding, sinking slightly into the soft clay I strike. *Thock.* Each impact a syllable. *Thock.* Each pause a breath. I am saying: *Pack it tighter. The stone will harden soon. We must become permanent before the permanence leaves us.*

I drink from the condensation curtains that tremble in the hot air. I communicate by pressure—a squeeze of the shoulder that travels through the chain of bodies, a tap against ribs that sends vibrations through the packed clay of my torso. I face inward, toward the center where the air is thickest, or I face the wall, pressing my palms against the growing stone, feeling it breathe, helping it breathe, becoming indistinguishable from its architecture.

The moss grows over me. It carpets every surface, glowing with a dim green light that filters through the steam to cast the chamber in perpetual twilight. It grows over my tools, over my clay, over my skin, feeding on the condensation and the heat. I am being digested even as I support the architecture. The vegetation is so dense that it forms a second skin, a breathing, photosynthesizing layer that is gradually consuming the distinction between me and the wall.

Fauna is rare, but present in the margins. A single creature moves through the reed-lattice—a salamander made of steam and mineral, or a thing that might be a builder left behind. It is translucent, filled with swirling clouds of mineral and steam, condensing and evaporating in cycles too rapid to follow. It has no fixed number of limbs—sometimes four legs like a lizard, sometimes a ribbon of glassy membrane, sometimes a hand with too many fingers reaching through the gaps. It moves by flowing, by momentarily becoming liquid, seeping through spaces where we cannot fit.

It leaves trails of phosphorescent slime—not slime, but bioluminescence given temporary weight, light shed like skin. The glow lingers in the air, drifting upward with the thermal currents, pooling in corners where it grows brighter, hungrier, feeding on the steam. Where its trail touches us, we glow more intensely, greener, more alive. It is the messenger, or the memory, or the part of us that has already escaped the hardening, slipping through the reed-lattice to taste the air above.

I press harder against the wall as it passes. I feel the wet click of my own work—the packing of the floor, adding another layer of steaming reed, another handful of packed clay, thickening the only ground we have, the only ground that is still soft enough to accept us.

But then I feel it—a flake of drying clay loosening from my cheek. I am constantly shedding, constantly depositing new layers like the mineral walls around me. The flake does not fall. It drifts upward, caught in a thermal current that flows toward the ceiling where the mountain is still stretching toward the sky. Gravity is a suggestion here, and the flake chooses to obey the heat instead.

I watch it go. The others do not watch. They are too busy packing, too busy becoming permanent. But I watch. I feel the current tug at my own edges, at the moss growing on my shoulders, at the steam rising from my pores.

And I realize: I am the one that turns around.

I am not meant to stay packed in the lattice. I am the question that moves through it. I am the salamander-shaped thing, the ribbon of glassy membrane, the hand with too many fingers. I am the part of the Unfinished that has already escaped, that slips through the gaps, that leaves trails of phosphorescent light evaporating before they hit the floor.

I flow. I momentarily become liquid, seeping through spaces where the builders cannot fit. I face the packed mass of bodies—the umber, ochre, grey, the green-pulsing translucent flesh—and they face the walls, pressing palms against the growing stone, and between us passes the current of heat, the endless exchange of clay and steam, the wet clicking work of making a home in a place that is still deciding whether it is a mountain or a womb.

I am the first and the last. I am the memory of standing. I am the one who turns around, who sheds my skin upward into the thermal current, who becomes the steam and the mineral and the swirling cloud, who leaves behind only a trail of green light, drifting toward the ceiling, feeding on the steam, becoming brighter as it decays, a question mark made of bioluminescence in the lung of the world, asking: *What happens when the stone hardens? Where do we go when we can no longer be packed?*
