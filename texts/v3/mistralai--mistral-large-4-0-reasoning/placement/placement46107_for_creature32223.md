I am the salt in the crease of my elbow, the white frost that will never wash away. I am the vibration in my sternum when I exhale, the high, clear note that is mine alone—F-sharp, my mother would have called it, if she had known music, if there had been music outside the drone. I am the callous on my palm, thickened not by labor but by *listening*, by decades of pressing my hand flat against the living stone to feel what the crystal-reeds are saying.

I am building the seventh terrace.

The floor has risen again. Last season, the crystal-thicket in the eastern hollow grew three centimeters—glacial, they call it, but I have watched it crush a clay wall flat in a single decade. The old walkway buckled, split like a reed itself, and now I am packing the gap with husks. Dead reed-husks. Glassy, rigid columns that have completed their lifecycle and fallen, or been felled by the slow violence of competing growth. I harvest them with a stone blade, feeling for the particular resonance of dead-versus-dying. A dying reed sings a discordant, shattering note. A dead one is silent, heavy, perfect for bearing weight.

I pack them into the clay. No mortar—only pressure and time. My saliva is thick, gelatinous, rich with the minerals I filter from the air. I spit into the clay, mixing it with the brine from the cisterns, and I press the husks into the matrix, feeling for the right density. The structure must breathe. It must chime. If I pack it wrong, the thermal currents will find the weakness and crack it, and the slow violence of the growing Ward will reclaim it.

My fingers are stiff today. The salt has crept higher, up to my knuckles. I can feel the calcium depositing in my joints, not as pain, but as a growing rigidity, a locked-in-place sensation. Soon I will not be able to flex them. Soon they will be locked in the gesture of packing, of building, of blessing. But not yet. Today, I can still feel the micro-fractures in the agate wall behind me, the temperature shift of a vent opening twenty feet below, the heartbeat of the cavern—*shluk-hiss, shluk-hiss*—pulsing through the floor stones into my knees.

I belong to the Resonance of the Low Hum. We are seven, or perhaps eight if the child in the eastern alcove has finally learned to shape her breath into meaning. We sleep in tangles in a hollow carved into the basalt, bodies touching, so that when I dream, my chiming lungs vibrate against the shoulder of the one beside me, and she feels it, and in the morning we press hands together to share the texture of the night. Privacy is a foreign concept, like dry air. Intimacy is total; we are a chord, a cluster of frequencies that have harmonized over years of shared breath.

When the vents open, we work. The mineral mist floods the lower terraces, scalding, beautiful, leaving crusts of salt that must be scraped away before they seal the walkways. I am down there now, in the heat, my skin slick with brine that dries to white dust within minutes. The air is thick, metallic, scouring. But my lungs filter it. The crystalline filaments in my bronchial trees—delicate, branching, pink as coral when I was young—now glow faintly with the geothermal heat I absorb. I do not cough. I *chime*. When I exhale, the vibration of my breath passing over these internal lattices produces my note, clear and ringing against the roar.

Conversation is impossible. The crystal-reeds sing as they grow, microscopically fracturing and re-forming their lattices, a base drone like a thousand tuning forks struck at once. Superimposed is the white-noise hiss of the steam-vents. The *thunk* of stone hammers. The grinding of querns. I press my palm against the nearest reed-trunk, a column of jade and rust, and I transmit. Pressure, vibration, temperature—I tell the others that the vent in the third hollow is opening early, that the mist will reach us in three breaths. They press their hands against the reeds they are touching, and the message ripples through the forest, stone to stone, a tactile telegram across the Ward.

The light never dims. It is the glow of the vents, filtered through the mineral haze, and the bioluminescent lichen that coats the upper walls, pulsing with slow, photosynthetic breath. It is bright—blinding—but diffuse, hazy, as if I am looking through deep water, though the air is desiccated, absolute. There is no night here, only the pulse. The heartbeat of the world.

I look up, through the crystal-thicket, at the membrane above. The skin of mineral glass, the blister on the desert, flexing with its slow, geological respiration. I remember, sometimes, in the deepest hours when the thermal glow is brightest, that there is a world outside. Dry silence. Death. I stepped through that membrane decades ago—felt it flex like thick leather, seal behind me with a wet *shluk*—and I have not stepped back out. I cannot. The air outside would kill me. My lungs are too adapted, too crystallized. I am becoming the Ward, as the Ward is becoming me.

My shoulders ache. The reed-husk I am carrying is heavy, glassy, sharp. I set it into place on the new terrace, and I feel my own bones responding, humming with the frequency of the stone. The mineralization is accelerating. My skin is going translucent in patches, revealing the branching filaments beneath, glowing faintly. When I cut myself—a nick on my thumb, careless—the wound weeps fluid that tastes of pennies and sea-stone, and within days, the edges knit over with an opalescent scar, harder than skin, more like horn.

I am finishing.

Soon I will sit at the highest terrace, where the air is hottest. My hands will fuse with the husks I have spent my life harvesting. My fingers will lock in permanent gestures of blessing. I will sing without sound, my entire body vibrating with the frequency of the cavern, becoming one with the drone. I will not die. I will become a column. A new support for the walkway. A new voice in the chorus.

And when the Glass-Moths come—those solitary, silent creatures with wings like thin panes of quartz, shattering and reforming as they fly—I will see my own future. A rainbow shattering in the humid, mineral air. I will be the architecture. I will be the song.

But not yet. Today, I am still building. I press my palm against the agate wall, feeling the vibration of the cavern's heartbeat, and I add another husk to the terrace, layer by layer, generation by generation, becoming stone.
