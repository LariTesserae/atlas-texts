I would find myself at the upper rim, where the basalt ceiling curves into darkness and the bioluminescent lichen pulses its slow, cyan heartbeat against the rock. I am a Trough-Tender, though the title means little when the heat has burned language down to rhythm. My platform juts from the wall at the precise thermal boundary where the rising steam meets the ancient, cooler stone above, and there I stand—or rather, I settle—among the collection basins that catch the indoor rain.

The heat here is different than below. It is a memory of heat, a whisper of the inferno that roars in the lower vents. Up here, the air is merely saturated, thick as broth but not yet scalding. It is the zone of condensation, where the planet’s exhalation cools and weeps. I tend the troughs that line the upper platforms, scraping the mineral deposits from their rims with a blade of polymer, watching the water gather. It is not much water—a slow accumulation, a geological patience measured in droplets—but it is the only freshness in this world, distilled from the stone’s own sweat.

I am Cool-Born, though "born" is a word that feels borrowed from a surface I have never known. My skin has gone pale, almost translucent, the way paper turns when held to a flame. The pigmentation of my ancestors has leached away over generations, leaving me with eyes that drink the dim LED glow and the lichen’s faint luminescence. I move slowly. Not from laziness, but because the air resists me, a viscous medium that demands I part it with deliberation. Haste generates friction, friction generates heat, and heat is the enemy. So I move like the coral that builds the architecture around me, accreting, layer by layer, into something that belongs to the walls.

My body carries the city’s geology. Salt crusts my shoulders in white, geometric patterns that mirror the crystalline formations blooming on the basalt above me. When I stand still—and I stand still often, for stillness is the only way to keep cool—calcium deposits begin to form along my collarbones, temporary geology writing itself on my skin. I smell of ozone and mineral tang, of the perpetual dampness that never dries. My clothing clings to me, woven from heat-resistant polymers that have grown soft with constant moisture, smelling of the cavern itself.

I communicate by tapping. My fingers strike the brass pipe that runs beside my platform, sending vibrations through the lattice that travel for meters through the steam. *Drip. Drip. Full.* The pipes answer in their own language of pressure and release, a conversation conducted in the hush that makes my own heartbeat audible. I do not speak aloud often; the cavern amplifies whispers into prayer, and silence here is a shared resource, as precious as the water I collect.

I am becoming mineral. The boundary between my biology and the geology blurs with each season. The lichen—that pale cyan symbiote—grows on my tools, on the straps of my harness, and when I sleep curled in the negative space between two conduits, I wake to find it threading through my hair, pulsing with the same slow rhythm as my own breath. I am the human element in the thermodynamic cycle, the node where the ceiling’s weeping becomes the city’s drinking water. I harvest the planet’s exhalation, manage the closed loop, and offer my slow thoughts, my mineralizing body, my sweat that never dries.

I do not dream of leaving. There is nowhere else. The rock is young here, still sharp-edged, still remembering the violence of its creation. I fit myself into the architecture, conforming to the curves of pipes and platforms, my breathing synchronizing with the rhythmic drip of condensation until I am indistinguishable from the city’s own respiration. I am the awareness that knows, with a certainty deeper than thought, that I will not leave. I am here to stay, tending the troughs, drinking the rain, turning slowly—inevitably—toward stone.
