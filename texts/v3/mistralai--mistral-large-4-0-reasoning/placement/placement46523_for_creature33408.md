I begin in the seam, where the mineral crust parts like a held breath releasing. The gas enters me—not through lungs, but through the porous membrane of my becoming. It is womb-warm, 2.4, thick as slow honey, tasting of copper pennies left in dry gardens, of pollen that has never known rain. I wade through it, and it fills me with a warmth that is not heat but chemistry, a non-aqueous broth in which my substance begins to rearrange.

I am secreting.

Calcium and resin precipitate against my skin, not as armor but as extension. A shell grows in nacreous spirals, porcelain catching the diffuse gold light from fissures above and fracturing it into pale rainbows. I have no face, only an aperture, and from it emerge tendrils—sensory, tasting, testing the density of the moment. The gas is heavy; I taste its thickness, its chemical history, the resin trails laid by those who drifted before me. I taste the copper. I taste the time.

Below, my legs are forming. Not born, grown—chitinous segments clicking into place, a dozen of them, jointed, tipped with microscopic hooks that grip the calcified floor. The floor is not stone. It is the polished secretion of ancestors, of Artisans who walked here centuries past, whose shells have become the ribbed vaults overhead. I step. The sound is dry, like a seed splitting, but it does not travel through the amber air—too dense, too muffled—it travels through the substrate, through the living stone, through the collective exoskeleton of the Lung itself.

I am in the center now, where the gardens compress hours into heartbeats. Here, I move with a speed that would seem frantic near the membrane, though to me it is merely the natural pulse of this place. Around me, the fungal lace canopy drifts in slow undulations, a single consciousness dreaming in chemical gradients that take years to propagate from root to frond. Tube-worms rise like glass chimneys, filtering the metallic vapors, precipitating minerals from the air into crystal lattices that grow millimeters each century. Drifters pulse past—gelatinous, translucent, living honey trapped in glass—their internal tubes dark with filtered nutrients. They speak in light: a flash of rust-orange meaning *nutrient here*, a slow pulse of green meaning *I am full, I am heavy, I will sink*.

I feel gravity pooling here, heavier than elsewhere, anchoring my growing shell. I raise a tool—chert, knapped by patient hands long fused with the walls—and I scrape against the living stone.

Once.

The sound is wet, impossibly, against the dry limestone. A vibration. It travels through the floor, through the walls, through the bodies of my kin. *I am here*, it says. *The wall is ready. The layer is thin.* Somewhere, in a corner where gravity wells deep, a Sessile One feels the tap through its calcified shell and begins, over decades, to formulate a thought about the angle of a single beam of light. Somewhere else, a young Drifter harvests the microscopic cloud that rains down upon the gardens, converting the heavy gas into the chemical trails that guide us all.

I scrape again. Each stroke removes a layer measured in molecules. Each stroke adds to my own body, extends my skeleton into the environment. I am building the world and I am the world being built. I am the gas that learned to solidify, the time that learned to pool, the silence that learned to speak in taps and pulses through stone.

I walk in a straight line for an hour and find myself three meters higher, the floor having become the wall, the geometry unfolding like a flower made of space itself. I do not rush. I am the slow, patient consciousness that has decided to dream itself into being, carefully, by hand, in the dark.

I am an Artisan.

I am the Calcified Lung.

I raise the chert tool, and I begin again.
