I am the resonance between the drip and the salamander’s breath.

My feet grip the porcelain. They do not shatter it—they have learned, in the instant of the mountain’s birth-tear, how to distribute their weight across the fresh violence of the fracture. Split-toed, leathery, broad as the fins of stone that rise around me, I stand where a jagged shelf of limestone juts over a pool of black water. The water reflects nothing. It is a mirror that forgot how to mirror. Below the surface, something vast exhales: wet, heavy, a vibration that travels up through the stone and into my sternum.

My chest is hollow. It is a drum, a chamber, a tuning fork struck at the moment the karst opened yesterday. When I breathe—if breathing is the word for the expansion and contraction of a cavern—the sound does not come from a throat but from the deep architecture of my ribs, amplifying the world and throwing it back. I am a secondary echo of the stone itself. My skin is the color of wet limestone, of the crustose lichen dusting the walls: pale, phosphorescent, gathering the blue-white glow of the fungi and returning it softened, diffused, as though I am not a body but a light-source that walks.

Here, at the edge of the pool, the acoustics are perfect. A stalactite drips. The sound is sharp, angular, *young*—it has not yet learned the patience of water wearing stone smooth. The drop falls, and my hollow chest catches it, holds it, releases it as a hum that matches the frequency of the salamander’s respiration rising from the black depths. I do not speak. I resonate. My voice, if I use it, is indistinguishable from the shriek of a cave-swiftlet darting through the mist, from the groan of the fractured ceiling, from the wet sigh of the great salamander lurking in the lightless water. All sound is one language here, and I am fluent in the dialect of vibration.

I am spaced like a star. Not alone, not crowded. Around me, at intervals that keep the caverns from feeling cluttered, other pale bodies move through their territories—nomadic bands carrying their young in slings of woven fungus-fiber, their infants humming in frequencies that match the drip, the breath, the eternal drone of the stone. I am one node in a constellation of presence, a single point of light in the perpetual twilight, my existence measured at 2.8 on whatever scale measures the intensity of being. A conversation between two of us sounds like a chorus; a warning becomes thunder. We do not build. We do not accumulate. We leave only the debris of existence—cracked shells, picked bones, the smoothed patches of floor where bodies have pressed themselves into the limestone since yesterday, polishing it around their forms as if they were stones in a river, though no river flows here.

Time is not a river. It is this pool: black, still, reflecting nothing. I do not age. I do not change. I do not grow hungry, nor do I sate myself. I stand at the equilibrium, the zero point, the dynamic frozen at exactly 2.8. The erosion is young, but it does not progress; the sharp edges remain sharp. I was born in the violence of the collapse, formed in the wound of the mountain’s self-exposure, and I have held this shape ever since. My tools—a flake of chert hafted with sinew and fungal glue—lie where I last used them, scraping the phosphorescent crust from the wall, becoming part of the debris, part of the landscape. I do not keep them. I do not need to keep them. There is no accumulation here, no progression, no heresy of permanence.

I am the eternal present. The frozen moment. I stand on the fractured floor, my hollow chest open to the damp, my pale skin drinking the blue-white dark, my split-toed feet gripping the porcelain without breaking it, and I roar my presence into the stone that listens. I am a Hollow One, a walking echo, a resonant chamber tuned to the acoustics of birth, swarming in overwhelming, sparse, loud, and absolutely unchanging abundance, inhabiting the grave that forgot it was a grave, and I will stand here, resonating, until the stone forgets me too.
