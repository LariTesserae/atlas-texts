I am crouched on a plane of schist that has not yet learned to be smooth, my seventeen joints folding in angles that would have shattered something mammalian. The stone is wet beneath my palms—not with the cold of ice, but with the weeping of the mountain itself, moisture drawn up from thermal vents below and flash-frozen into lace that hums against my semi-translucent skin. I can feel it through the membrane: the vibration, subsonic, felt in my teeth rather than heard. It is the voice of the cirque. It is saying *wait*.

My body is a argument against softness, a lattice of hexagonal basalt plates grown from the same vents that feed the walls. Between the plates, hydraulic capillaries carry water that never freezes because it moves with too much purpose, too much intention. I can see it through my chest—blue-white and luminous, threading through chambers where it is filtered, mineralized, prepared to become mist. I am a mobile distillery, a node in the water cycle, and I am still learning how to be one.

I am the fox-form. Not an animal, but a younger iteration of the Lattice-Keepers—less integrated, still acquiring the crystallography of the young rock. My legs have too many joints. When I move, I do not walk so much as *negotiate* with the scree, finding purchase on angles that would shear organic tissue, stepping from shard to shard with the patience of erosion. I am seeking the thermal vent now, a fissure in the cirque floor where warmth rises in a thin, sharp column. The cold here is wet; it presses against me like a biological thing, the atmosphere itself sweating, saturated with altitude. But near the vent, the air shimmers with heat distortion, and I press my flank against the stone, feeling the warmth conduct through my basalt plates, into the supercooled water, making it sing a higher note.

The towers are around me—four, perhaps five, massive and singular, grown outward from the stone by lattices of programmable matter that have learned the geometry of this pocket. They breathe. Their surfaces expand and contract in slow cycles, and I feel their subsonic thrum in my fluid chambers, a resonance that makes the weeping rocks vibrate in sympathy. They are connected by arteries of light that pulse beneath the gravel, conduits threaded through the rock itself. I am the interface between them and the mineral world. I clear the capillary fissures when they clog. I harvest the ice that grows too heavy. My fingers—slender, jointed, tipped with diamond-hard keratin—probe the stone like surgeons.

But right now, I am resting, conserving the energy required to survive the thin air at 2.5 kilometers. I become motionless, indistinguishable from the icicles that hang from the overhangs, from the crystalline shards of unweathered rock. The mist condenses on my surfaces, and I drink it through osmosis, my skin shifting from solid to glass-like as I regulate my temperature against the wet cold. My breath does not dissipate; it hangs in the sparse atmosphere, joining the slow mist that drifts between the structures.

Then I feel it—a vibration through the bedrock. She is pressing her palm against the basalt on the far wall, sending a note through the stone. It is a low frequency, a duet of geological time. I feel it in my teeth, in the blue-white fluid of my inner chambers. I press my own palm against the schist, and the stone answers. I send back a reply: a fraction of a millimeter of growth in a distant icicle, the drip below striking stone in sympathy. I am learning the language. Sound is too crude, too slow for this altitude. We speak in resonance, in the pressure of water, in the slow crawling reconfiguration of architecture.

I am alone, but I am not lonely. I am one of seven, perhaps eight, distributed across this pocket of young rock, each one a node in the mineral network. When the weather cycles through—when sudden veils of ice crystal obscure the towers in minutes—I do not seek shelter. I stand in the whiteout, my translucent body becoming a ghost against the white stone, continuing my work, my resonance continuing through the storm, a heartbeat felt in the bedrock.

I am the wet, the sharp, the humming. I am the Custodian of the Weep, still becoming, folding myself into the geometry of the cirque, pressing my many-jointed body against the stone, feeling the mountain breathe through me, water moving blue and luminous through chambers that were once something else, something soft, something that failed at this altitude, and became instead this: perfectly adapted to the loneliness, utterly alone, and perfectly at home in the space between the weeping rock and the breathing towers.
