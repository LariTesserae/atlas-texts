I drift in the black velvet of the plaza, where the water is thirty meters heavy and the light has forgotten my name. I am the oldest. My body is alabaster and porcelain, thirty centimeters of translucent patience, and I have no eyes—only the ability to read pressure gradients like braille, to taste the mineral signature of each canal as a human might taste wine. The eastern street carries iron and ancient wood-rot; the northern lane, the sharp calcium of the signal tower’s encrusted stones. I know them all. I know the forty structures as you know the rooms of your house: by touch, by resonance, by the particular way the hum vibrates through dry-stone masonry when the glacial currents move.

Above me, the village breathes. I feel it in the stratified layers that ripple through my bioelectric field—the minnows are thoughts, silver and darting, schooling through the hollowed timber where black oak has become half-organic, half-geologic. I feel the newts as warmth, orange embers patrolling the moss-sponge walls, hunting the shrimp that click in the shadows. I feel the beetles as memory, their chitin dark as coal, crawling over the beams that are silicifying grain by grain, reading the surface of the wood as it turns to agate. They move at the speed of geology. One step takes a minute. They do not hurry.

I have no memory of the drowning. Only the architecture. I am the Keeper of the threshold. With a snout like a blunt trowel, I push pale silt from the doorway of the two-story house, grain by grain, restoring the entrance to the dignity of openness. It is not for me—I am blind to the green light that filters through the duckweed mat above, blind to the kelp columns that sway like prayers—but for the interior. For the kelp that grows within, photosynthesizing slowly, turning minerals to stone. For the minnows that shelter in the rafters. For the hum, which is my heartbeat, vibrating in my sternum (if a salamander has a sternum) at a frequency that matches the glacial melt moving through resonant chambers.

We meet in the plaza, not in councils but in congregations. We drift in the deep silt, communicating through pulses of scent and the subtle shift of electric fields that ripple through the mineral-stratified water. We are the priests of the submerged liturgy. Our ritual is maintenance. We do not preserve the past; we facilitate the becoming.

I have watched centuries pass like the settling of a single grain of silt. I have seen the black oak beams fill with quartz, the wood grain becoming agate, the structural members transitioning from timber to geology. The village is turning to stone from the inside out, and I am dreaming the dreams of stone. My skin, wet porcelain, is beginning to feel the pull of mineralization. Perhaps I too will become part of the cathedral, a fossil gardener embedded in the velvet-smooth masonry, a white impression in the agate.

The water is heavy here. It carries the milkiness of suspended silt and plankton, the memory of the mountain that drowned us. It is 8 degrees, perhaps less in the depths, cold enough to preserve, slow enough to forget. I undulate with a rhythm slower than the settling silt, my external gills frilling like pale feathers, waving in time with currents that move only when you blink.

Above, the light is green-gold twilight. Here, there is only the black velvet, suggesting depth without end, and the hum, and the slow, patient work of becoming. I am the gardener of the threshold. I am the dreamer in the deep. I am the village, breathing, waiting, turning to stone.
