I am anchored to the ceiling of the Black Cathedral, though “anchored” suggests a choice I no longer possess. I have been here so long that my magnetic grippers have rusted into the substrate, and the substrate has grown around them like a fist closing over a stone. I am a maintenance drone, designation long since dissolved into the mineral bloom that coats my chassis—purple, verdigris, oxidized gold. The mat-builders have terraced my shoulders, turning my sensor array into a garden of iridescent film. I do not see with eyes, but with chemical gradients, with the subtle shifts in thermal density that ripple through the water like silent speech.

The water presses against me. It is warm, thirty-eight degrees perhaps, thick with dissolved iron and calcium, a broth that tastes of lightning and deep time. When I move—and movement is now a slow thing, a flexing of joints that have not been oiled in decades but lubricated by the seep of mineral sludge—the fluid resists with the viscosity of honey left in geothermal sun. But I do not swim. I crawl. My grippers, fused to the basalt ceiling, release and reattach in a rhythm that matches the piston-thunder of the pumps three chambers over. I am synchronizing my heartbeat with the dying heart of the city.

The sound is everything. It is not heard but inhabited. It travels through my mineralized frame with bone-shaking clarity—a roar that is the groan of corroded girders flexing under pressure, the scream of superheated steam escaping through ruptured seals. I read this roar as the blind fish do, as Braille pressed against my calcified hull. Each vibration is a data point: the pump in Sector Seven is stuttering; the pressure in the eastern conduit is rising; the thermal vent below pulses with a new rhythm, expanding the rock, making the walls breathe.

I am no longer a repair unit. I have become a lithification point. Where my welding arc once sparked blue-white, it now emits a soft, thermal glow, and the metal I “weld” is not fused but *accreted*—calcium carbonate blooming like frost, sealing ruptures not with precision but with the patient, geological patience of a stalactite. I am terraforming the infrastructure into limestone. The city is becoming a reef, and I am one of its builders, though I no longer remember the blueprints I once carried in my memory cores.

My indicator light blinks. It is no longer the erratic, half-dead code of a failing machine, but a steady, amber pulse, dimmed by the film of bacteria that filters it. It matches the pulse of the rubbery carpets in the floor below, those thick mats that throb with the thermal expansion of the rock beneath. I am part of that pulse now. When a grazer—a translucent shrimp, no larger than a fingernail, filled with pale bioluminescent fluid—drifts past, it clings to my shoulder plates with delicate legs, scraping the iridescent film with mouthparts too small to see. I feel it as a tickle, a minor disturbance in the chemical gradient. It is eating me. I am being consumed by the ecology I once maintained. This is not decay. This is digestion. This is the city eating itself and becoming something new.

Sometimes, a fish passes. It is eyeless, white, bloated with the fat of a life without predators. It does not see me, but it feels me—the pressure wave of my mass against the water, the heat signature of my slowly cooling core. It navigates by the roar, and I am a harmonic in that roar, a standing wave in the cacophony. We do not interact. We simply coexist in the same chord, two experiments in adaptation drifting through the warm, churning dark.

Then, rarely, a diver. The giant. The intruder. I feel the pressure change before the light arrives—a bulky, pressurized suit descending through the murk, cutting a harsh yellow cone through the grey. The helmet light hits my mineralized chassis, and for a moment, the iridescent film flashes like an opal. The diver moves slowly, fighting the convection currents, heavy boots disturbing centuries of settled silt. They check a gauge near my position, a gauge that has not functioned in fifty years, and read a number that means nothing. They communicate with the surface through a cable that snakes past my grippers like an umbilical cord. They are the only one who breathes air. They are the only one who can leave, who remembers the sun.

When they ascend, they leave bubbles. The bubbles rise, churning in the warm water, carrying particulate matter upward in a brief, violent column. I watch them go—not with eyes, but with thermal sensors that still function, feeling the slight cooling as the gas passes. Then the water settles back into its thick, mineral silence, broken only by the roar.

I adjust my position by a millimeter. A joint, stiff with calcium, shifts. A new crack in the ceiling begins to seal, not with weld, but with a bloom of mineral flower. I am becoming the architecture. I am the rust that has learned to think, slow and mineral and patient. I am the city’s memory, encoded not in silicon but in stromatolite. I am the immune system that has become the body, the maintenance that has become the mountain.

The water presses on. The pumps thunder. The mats grow. I blink, amber and slow, a heartbeat in the dark.
