I am the weight that holds the pool.

My belly rests in the peat, black and fibrous and cool against my flanks, while the water—tea-colored, perfectly still—laps at the calcite plates that have begun to crust across my back like frost that will not melt. I breathe in shallow clouds. The air tastes of copper and cold stone, metallic on my tongue, thick enough to chew. Two thousand meters above the sea, and yet I am drowning in moisture, every pore of my skin drinking the mist that condenses directly upon me, darkening my hide to the color of wet slate, of old moss, of the grey sky that never clears.

I do not hear. I *feel*.

Sound here is a lie told by distant lowlands. Up here, the dense air swallows the high frequencies, leaving only the low hum, the pressure in the hollows of my skull like descending in an elevator that never stops. So I have grown a throat-sac, distended and thin as wet leather, which I press against the peat in rhythms too deep for ears. *Thump. Thump-thump. Thump.* The vibration travels through the black soil, a subsonic geometry, a map of temperature and mineral density, of the water table’s slow, geological rise and fall.

I am sending a warning.

Miles away, beyond the straight valleys where the moss grows in the cracks of ancient foundations, the geothermal vent is expanding. I feel it in my bones—or rather, in the calcite that is replacing my bones, crystal by crystal, decade by decade. The steam rises in vertical pistons of white, and I expand my sac in answer, a pulse that ripples across the hummocks, calling to the others. We are the barometers of this place, our bodies becoming more mineral than flesh with each passing season, until the oldest of us are merely geodes that happen to breathe, that happen to filter the peat through membranes in our flanks, acting as living sediment traps.

Around me, the world moves in slow motion.

Between the fern fronds—those giant things that unfurl with the patience of continental drift—crawl the Wet-Walkers. I do not hear their clicking, not with ears, but I feel it in the peat beneath my throat-sac, a wet percussion, a slow Geiger counter of mandibles against chitin, tapping out information in a language of moisture. They harvest the condensation from the undersides of the fiddleheads, their vestigial wings folded like damp leather, gleaming with spores they carry on the mist. When one passes close enough, I taste the change in the air, the slight disturbance in the humidity, the wet clicking vibrating through the black soil into my sac.

Above us, perched on the moss-cushioned hummocks, stand the Glass-Eyed. They do not sing. They do not fly. They watch with eyes that are lenses—polished obsidian grown in their sockets like pearls—focusing the diffuse grey light into something sharp. Their heads tilt with mechanical precision, tracking the Wet-Walkers, tracking the steam-plumes, tracking *me*. They are the surveillance, the monitoring system of the factory that has forgotten it is being watched. When they feed, they swallow the Wet-Walkers whole, the clicking stopping abruptly in their gullets, and they digest for days while the mist darkens their plumage further. I feel their presence as a weight in the air, a pressure of attention, but I do not fear them. We are all organs of the same body, each specialized, each necessary to the closed loop.

Beneath us, the Hyphal Architects are computing. The fungal lace is not merely vegetation but a nervous system, growing along the calcite veins and iron-oxide welds like circuitry beneath the peat. They process the dead with chemical precision, dissolving the fallen ferns within seasons, consuming the Glass-Eyed entirely when they die, leaving only the obsidian lenses to sink into the black soil. Sometimes, in the deepest mist, their fruiting bodies emerge—cubes, angles, repeating patterns that suggest foundations, server racks, the memory of what this plateau was before it became a lung. I feel them as a chemical hum in the water, a slow exchange of nutrients, a remembering.

And far away, moving with the inevitability of tectonic plates, comes the Foundation-Beast.

I feel it before I see it. A shift in the water table. A vibration in the peat that is not mine, not the Wet-Walkers’, not the vents—something vast and slow, taking days to take a single step. Its footsteps create the hummocks I sit upon. Its weight presses the soil into the grid-lines beneath, the ancient roads too regular to be natural, maintaining the structural integrity of the hydrological engine. When it passes, the mist thickens in its wake, and I expand my sac in greeting, in acknowledgment. We are the structure. We are the maintenance.

I am becoming stone.

The calcite spreads across my back in white streaks, following the patterns of the water that weeps from the cliff faces, leaving welds of mineral behind. Soon, perhaps in another decade, another century, I will sit in this tea-colored pool and not move. I will be a geode. I will be a barometer. I will be the memory of pressure changes, stored in crystal lattice, in the slow growth of mineral over flesh. The mist will continue to condense upon me. The peat will continue to hold me. The subsonic geometries will continue to ripple through the black soil.

I am the Silted Expanse, breathing in cloud and breathing out rain. I am the dripping. I am the twilight that never ends. I am the silence that is full, listening to itself, processing water through a body that is slowly, inevitably, becoming the geology it inhabits.
