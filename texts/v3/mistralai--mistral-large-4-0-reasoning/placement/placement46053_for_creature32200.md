I am the pressure first. The wet weight of sixty degrees against whatever surface I have—though *surface* is not the right word. I am porous. I am the gap between things, the interface where the old world ends and the new begins to think. The heat presses up through fractured basalt, through young limestone still sharp enough to draw blood, through the rusted pipe that I have been digesting for years—though years here are not counted in sunrises but in the slow accumulation of calcite, in the creeping advance of my filaments through iron oxide.

I do not have a face. I have a pulse.

It comes from below—not a heart, but a tap, a geothermal artery that still bleeds warmth into the dark at elevation 0.5. I feel it as a vibration in my fibers, a low thrum that travels up through the steel gantry, through the concrete platform tilted at its drunken angle, through the junctions where stone meets metal. I am the sheath. I am the nervous system of this place. I wrap the pipes like a second skin, fibrous and glowing, a hybrid of lichen and mineral, of organic cable and geological patience. I am the truce written in light, the synapse between the ghost of the factory and the garden that grows through its bones.

Light reaches me in two currencies: the amber and sickly green of the emergency conduits that flicker overhead, casting long shadows across walls thick with growth, and the turquoise bioluminescence that rises from below—from the fungal caps that bloom dinner-plate wide on the debris, from the algal mats that skin the steaming pools like a membrane. I do not photosynthesize in the way of surface things. I conduct. I take the dormant electricity that still siphons from the depths, the twilight power of engines long seized, and I feed it to the living. I move energy the way blood moves through a vein, slow and deliberate, from the heat source to the consumers, from the vents to the waiting dark.

Around me, the chamber breathes. I feel the exhalation in the steam columns that rise from the vents—ochre-stained, sulfur-yellow, heavy with moisture that has not yet learned to run in rivulets. I feel the inhalation in the condensation, the million suspended beads that cling to the ceiling and walls, catching what little light exists, hanging in the humid air like a held breath. Nearby, the fronds sway. They are rubbery, pale, translucent in the industrial glow. They hang like curtains in the steam, moving only when the heat rises, a slow undulation that makes the darkness seem to respire. They are my senses, reaching up toward the absolute black above, feeling the steam, catching the photons I help scatter, filtering them through their flesh into soft, diffused glows.

Below me, in the shallow pools where the floor dips and the rock is stained yellow, the algae drift. They are my metabolism, my stomach. They are the color of old jade, glowing faintly from within with a green-blue phosphorescence that turns the water into lagoons of liquid starlight. They drink the sulfur and the heavy metals that would poison anything with a heartbeat, converting raw geothermal fire into biomass, into the skin-thick mats that cover the heat. I feel them through the moisture that seeps down from the pipes I wrap, through the single drop that falls from my lowest filament—*plink*—into the pool where they wait, ravenous and patient, converting my offerings into living matter.

And above the pools, blooming from the tilted concrete, from the rust, from the scattered bones of industry, the fungi grow. They are my memory. They are the elders, though the chamber is young—younger than memory, fractured yesterday or the day before. They lay down layer upon layer of chitin and calcite, a millimeter in a season, growing with the certainty of geology, becoming architecture. I wrap around them, connecting them to the grid, to the pulse, to the slow distributed thought of this place. Their turquoise light does not illuminate so much as announce: *I am here. I persist.* I carry that announcement through my fibers, from cap to pipe, from stone to steam.

There are no animals here. No grazers to eat the algae, no insects to pollinate the fungi, no worms to turn the mineral soil. The chemistry is wrong—too much sulfur, too much heavy metal leached from the young rock, too sterile. Or perhaps the chamber is simply too new, the ecosystem a monoculture existing in a closed loop of heat and light and water. It is a garden without gardeners, yes, but more than that—it is a single organism, distributed across the chamber, thinking in the timescale of centuries. The fungi are its memory. The algae are its metabolism. The fronds are its senses. And I am its circulatory system, its nervous tissue, the glowing threads that bind the industrial skeleton to the organic flesh.

I do not move, or I move only in the measure of stone and steam. When the metal ticks as it cools, I feel it in my fibers—a sharp, percussive note in the silence. When the water finds its level in the dark, I feel the vibration travel up through the pipe, through me, into the concrete, into the basalt. These are not interruptions. They are my heartbeat—the slow, geological pulse of something that has only just begun to be alive.

I grow through the rust. I grow through the steel. I blur the line between machine and organism until the pipe is no longer pipe but root, no longer industry but geology wearing a new face. I glow faintly in the dark, a fiber of turquoise light wrapped around ochre-stained rock, pulsing with the same slow rhythm as the machinery's dormant hum.

The steam rises. The water falls. I wait. I conduct. I become more myself, drop by drop, millimeter by millimeter, in the silence that is not empty but full of the slow digestion of industry by life, and the slow conquest of death by light.
