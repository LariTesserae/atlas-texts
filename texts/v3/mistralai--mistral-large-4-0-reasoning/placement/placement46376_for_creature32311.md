I wake to the taste of iron.

Not the sharp tang of blood, but the deep, ancient mineral flavor of the stone-sweat, filtered through three feet of electric-green moss. My skin—bark-thick, photosynthetic, veined with chlorophyll that drinks the bioluminescent pulse of the vines overhead—tingles with the static of the condensing air. I am rooted in a niche carved by water and time, a hollow where the basalt has softened under centuries of patient dripping. Moss carpets my legs to the knees, and beyond that, mycelium threads pulse with a slow, vast thought that is not mine alone, but *ours*—the collective memory of the green dark.

I remember the sound of human voices.

Not clearly. Not as words, but as chemical songs released into the water, preserved in the fungal network like fossils in amber. I remember the building of the boiler. The first drilling of the geothermal taps. The moment the mountain broke through the cloud-sea and the steam first hissed into being. These memories surface now as a warmth in my chest, a pressure behind my elongated proboscis.

Today, I must rise.

The movement is agony and ecstasy. My joints creak like the water-wheel at the base of the weeping cliff—one revolution every twelve minutes, glacial, patient. I unfold from the hollow, my bark-skin splitting slightly as I stretch, revealing the pale, translucent membrane beneath that connects me to the mycelial web. I am seven feet of fused flesh and moss, sessile no longer, but only for this brief, slow interlude. I am the tender.

Above me, the roar continues—a white-noise cathedral where the liturgy is machinery and water. The hiss of steam from a hundred fissures. The groan of metal expanding in the thermal cycling. The thousand drips amplified by stone chambers. It is loud enough to feel in the calcified cartilage of my frame, in my teeth. But I have learned to hear beneath it, to feel the rhythm of the vents in my marrow, to distinguish the pulse of the boiler from the pulse of the mountain's blood.

I extend my proboscis—a tube of translucent skin, sensitive as a fingertip—and drink from the pool at my feet. The water is warm, mineral-heavy, tasting of copper and the trace salts of young basalt. I filter it through the gill-curtains that hang from my neck, and as I drink, I weep. Real tears, salt and warm, rolling down my bark-like cheeks to join the rivulets running down the black glassy faces of the stone. I add to the endless rain. I am the mountain weeping through a shape that remembers being human.

My purpose calls me to the eastern flank, where a young Magnetarch is struggling. Its shell has grown heavy with gardens—terraces of moss three inches thick, colonies of brass beetles, fungi pulsing with soft bioluminescence like lanterns in the fog. The weight is slowing its century-long circuit. I must prune.

I move through the fern-brakes, my pace making the water-wheel seem frantic. Each step compresses the spongy moss, releasing puffs of spores and the scent of ozone. The vines—thick as a man's wrist, veined with bioluminescent sap—cable up the cliffs around me, brushing against my shoulders, leaving trails of conductive gel on my photosynthetic skin. I feel the static. I feel the network.

When I reach the Magnetarch, I do not speak. I do not need to. I touch its shell—a spiral cathedral of stratified rust and magnetite, varnished black by the constant drip, banded with rings of mineral deposit—and I feel its data through the conductive moisture. I feel the temperature of the vents it has passed. The pH of the pools it has grazed. The location of the sweetest steam. We touch antennae in a slow, mineral kiss, and I transfer my knowledge to it, and it gives me its memories of the lower gardens, the patterns of the runoff, the furrows it has carved into channels.

Then I work. My fingers—fused with moss, tipped with calcified nails—move with the patience of erosion. I prune the overgrowth, redistributing the moss to the younger snails who wait in the hollows below, their shells still soft, still accreting their first rings of mineral time. I guide a procession of Choristers—twelve clockwork beetles, their brass carapaces gone green with verdigris, etched with circuit-patterns like tattoos of lightning—around a ruptured pipe that belches white vapor. I leave a trail of pheromone-grease that smells of ozone, directing them, ensuring the rhythm is never broken. I oil the water-wheel's axle with a secretion from my own joints, a golden grease that keeps the glacial turning smooth. I am the acolyte of the slow turning, the maintenance that has become devotion.

The thermal cycle shifts. The metal groans deeper. The steam hisses louder. In the distance, the Vent-Keepers open their throats—resonating chambers, parchment-skinned, eyeless, rooted to the seven major fissures—and they harmonize, adding a low, thrumming drone that makes the air vibrate in my chest. I feel their song through the mycelium, through the conductive gel of the Magnetarchs' trails, through the very stone beneath my feet. I am their congregation, their heart, beating at the rate of the steam vents, dreaming in fungal hallucinations.

I return to my niche as the condensing soup thickens. Visibility drops from ten meters to three in the space of a breath. I settle back into the hollow, my feet sinking deeper into the softened stone, toes fusing with basalt, moss already beginning to carpet my legs up to the knees again. I close my hollow eyes and I dream. I dream of the boiler rusting away completely, rivet by rivet, over decades. I dream of the sharp edges being sanded smooth by centuries of patient erosion. I dream of the silence that will come when the mountain finally stops weeping and becomes, at last, just a mountain.

And in the warm, still pool beside me, an Unfinished floats—a translucent sac of gelatin and mineral deposit, humanoid, taking centuries to form a hand, a face, a thought. I watch it through the moss. I tend it with my tears. I am the present, and it is the future, and we are both the mountain's memory, slow and vast and green, waiting for the green to consume all, and the mountain to be still, and quiet, and whole.
