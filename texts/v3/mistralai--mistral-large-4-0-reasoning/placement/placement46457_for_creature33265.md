I feel the warmth first—not the sun's kiss but its press, a fever-heat trapped under a lid of mineral-heavy air that never cools. My ears pop, adjusting to the thinness, though the atmosphere is dense enough to cushion every fall, to let me leap impossible arcs between the bone-white crust and the spiraling giants. There is no wetness anywhere. Not on my skin, not in the air. Only the dry taste of copper and ozone, the recycled breath of steam and resin, the absence of water absolute and ancient.

The ground crunches beneath me—gypsum and silica, fossilized ripples worn smooth as patina, stretching between cathedral spires of gray stone. I move across it with the agility of the fauna, my long limbs carrying me in bounds that should defy physics but do not, because the gravity here is lighter, or the thick air is heavy, or both. My feet are wrapped in mineral fiber, woven strong against the abrasive wind that never stops, that screams through the gas-filled membranes high above, creating a chord that vibrates in my teeth—a bass line of organ pipe and didgeridoo, the roar of the Singing Grove.

Above that roar, the treble rises. The hexapods—thousands of them—cloud the spaces between the lithovores, their iridescent chitin refracting the dim lavender light into shifting rainbows. They click and stridulate in deafening waves, a chorus that rises and falls like surf against the shore of sound. I do not flinch. I am used to the storm-state, to the turbulence, to the hyperactive flux of creation and destruction.

I look up, and I see wrong. The colors are shifted. What should be green is bruised blue. What should be red is infrared, visible only as a heat-shimmer, a thermal ghost against the cool stone. My eyes have adapted to the spectrum; I see the world in warmth. The lithovore above me pulses—deep amber to sickly violet, a systole and diastole of bioluminescence, a heartbeat without blood. I see the steam vents below, threads of heat rising from the bedrock, visible as shimmering distortions in the thick air. I see the Leapers stalking the salt flats, their metabolic fire burning bright, predators and prey alike glowing urgent and chaotic in the perpetual dim twilight.

My hair stands on end. Not from fear. From static. Tiny sparks jump between my strands and the crystalline trunk, silent forks of branching light. My skin shimmers, pearlescent, coated in fine mineral dust that never washes away because there is no water to wash it with. My hands—I flex them—are more dexterous than human, suited for gripping the spiral columns of compressed salt, for knapping flint, for cutting the tough membranes without bursting them.

I am climbing. The lithovore creaks as it grows, adding rings of silica and gypsum with a sound like stone grinding against stone, visible increments of mineral deposited by the vents below. I ascend its spiraling trunk, thirty feet into the feverish air, until I reach the fronds—vast, translucent membranes taut with pressurized sulfur gas, fluttering and chiming in the wind, glowing with an inner pulse that shifts from amber to violet. I harvest them.

At my hip hangs a dried bladder, inflated, glowing faintly with the captured bioluminescence of the gas within. I draw a tool of bone and flint—sharp, cutting—and I slice carefully into a membrane. The vapor escapes, thick with the scent of copper and ozone. I inhale. It is my sacrament, my sight, my language.

The world shifts. The infrared spectrum aligns with my vision, and the Grove becomes a landscape of heat and cold, of thermal ghosts and burning life. I can track the swarms by their metabolic fire, the Leapers by their urgent, syncopated warmth. I can see the spores released when a frond bursts—thunder-cracks in the air—germinating immediately, floating upward on thermal currents to seed new growths before falling back to the dust, a frantic biology made visible.

A dust devil spins up—a screaming column of salt and silica that obscures the bruised sky. I do not hide. I dance in it. My static-charged hair sparks, my mineral-dust skin shimmers, and I move in the eye of the storm, clicking and calling, harmonizing with the stridulation of the hexapods and the creaking growth of the lithovores. I am never still. I do not sleep, not as water-based life understands it. I live in shifts, in trances, in a constant state of half-waking, my nervous system adapted to the deafening chorus, my body efficient at recycling every molecule of moisture from the steam, from the vapor, from the pressurized gas within the membranes.

I am the inheritor of the paradox. I have built my platform on stilts of knapped flint to escape the drifting dust, my hut of salt-blocks sealed with volcanic ash and resin. I have smoothed stone circles across generations of use. I am bound to this place by necessity and by choice, a lushness without water, a verdant strangeness without rain.

I am a Singer.

I am the voice that blends with the wind, the hand that harvests the impossible abundance of a world that has known no drop of water in ten thousand years. I am the storm-state, frantic and beautiful, breathing turbulent breath without ever taking a drink.
