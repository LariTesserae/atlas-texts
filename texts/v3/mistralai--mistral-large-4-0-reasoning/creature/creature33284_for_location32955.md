They are the drift-kin, though "kin" implies relation, and they are related only to themselves and to the water that holds them. They are not fish—they have no bones, no scales, no eyes that could be called eyes—only a architecture of living membrane, folded and refolded until they resemble something caught between a jellyfish and a page of sacred origami. Each is a stack of translucent segments, thin as the rice-paper screens of old, each segment fringed with a thousand beating cilia that move in metronomic waves, too slow to create a ripple, only a tremor in the liquid. They pulse. That is their verb: to expand, drawing the warm mineral water into their porous flanks, and to contract, expelling it through vents near their rounded anterior ends. They do not swim; they hover, they drift, they rise and sink in the gentle convection of the chamber, navigating by gradients of warmth and the chemical signature of the eroding walls.

They are everywhere. The water is not merely inhabited by them; it is *composed* of them, layered in dense strata like sediment made animate. Near the surface, where the greenish murk from the oculus filters down, they are smaller, simpler, barely more than pulsing bladders. In the middle depths, they grow more complex—folded into spirals and pleats, their segments overlapping like the scales of some impossible pangolin, their symmetry excessive, radial and bilateral at once, as if designed by a geometry that no longer exists. And in the deepest, warmest strata, near the worn floor where the constructed slabs have become a single undulating sheet, there are the elders: vast, ancient drift-kin whose bodies have grown so convoluted with added segments over decades—centuries, perhaps—that they have become sessile, anchored to the pillars by root-like tendrils, their pulses reduced to a slow, tidal breathing, their phosphorescence a steady, unwavering glow like a star trapped in amber.

They feed by filtration. The water is thick with the microscopic detritus raining down from the eroding walls—the dissolved minerals of the artificial stone, the sloughed cells of the microbial film that coats every surface, the occasional corpse. The drift-kin sift this, their cilia creating microscopic currents that draw sustenance into their gelatinous bodies. But they are not merely passive grazers. When one dies—a process of dimming, of the inner light guttering like a candle in a draft—it sinks. The descent takes hours, perhaps days, the body folding in on itself, becoming a soft, drifting meal. Others descend to meet it, their translucent forms brushing against the corpse, their cilia working to dissolve and absorb. In this place, cannibalism is not predation but communion, a recycling of substance in a closed system where nothing is wasted.

They live slow lives measured in decades. A drift-kin may persist for fifty years, perhaps longer, growing throughout, adding segments, becoming more folded, more lace-like, more architectural. The oldest are nearly immobile, vast knots of living tissue that occupy the alcoves—those smooth, featureless hollows that were once crisp doorways—fitting themselves into the curves of the eroded pillars as if they were grown there rather than drifting in. They are the inheritors of the builders, or perhaps the builders themselves, transformed over ten thousand years of warm, mineral-heavy steeping into these gelatinous, pulsing forms. Their "too many fins," their impossible symmetry, their construction from segmented membrane—all of it echoes the handiwork of flint and bone and packed clay that raised the chamber. They seem built, crafted, as if the same intention that raised the pillars folded these creatures from living matter.

They communicate in pressures and pulses. When the condensation drips from the ceiling—a single, bell-like *tick* that echoes in the silence—they respond, their collective pulsing shifting in rhythm, a chorus of expansion and contraction that passes through the water like a sigh. They sense one another through the density of the medium, feeling the displacement of liquid as another drift-kin moves nearby. They have no voice, no sound, only the thrum of their own slow hearts, amplified by the water, felt in the chest of any who dive deep enough.

And beneath them, coating the pale, porous walls, is the simpler life: the microbial film, a skin of bacteria and archaea chewing slowly at the minerals of the artificial stone, turning the constructed world into dissolved nutrients that rain down to feed the drift-kin. They are the foundation, the slow digestion, the invisible substrate upon which the visible life depends.

There are no plants here, no roots, no green. The ecology is built on absence and chemistry. The drift-kin are the apex, the slow, patient, silent persistence of water and membrane, drifting in the dim aqueous twilight, their pale lantern-glow the only light in the deep, brushing against the smoothed pillars, filling the narrow channels of water between the architecture, living out their long, metronomic lives in the warmth of deep stone, the last residents of a cathedral built for hands that no longer exist.
