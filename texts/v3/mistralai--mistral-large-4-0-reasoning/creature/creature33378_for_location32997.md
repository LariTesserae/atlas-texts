They are the Hollowed. Not a name they chose, but one the stone gave them—etched into their skin by the cool, the damp, and the mineral breath of the rock. They move through the vertical city like water finding its level, but water that thinks, that builds, that dreams in the crevices of bone-white limestone.

Look at their hands first. They are not the hands of surface-dwellers. The fingers are long, almost too long, with an extra joint that bends backward, allowing them to grip the chisel-marks in the walls as if they were rungs. The palms are wide and heavily callused, mapped with scars from bronze blades and splintered timber. Many have webbing between thumb and forefinger—not fully developed, but present, a translucent membrane that helps them brace against the vertical faces when the mist makes the stone slick. They do not wear gloves. The stone must be felt directly, read through the fingertips, and gloves would deafen them to its trembling.

Their skin is the color of the unweathered limestone around them—pale, unmarked by sun, veined with blue that shows through like cracks in porcelain. They have no tans, no weathering. Instead, they carry the stains of the Hollow: electric-green moss that grows in the creases behind their ears, silver lichen crusting their collarbones, the rust-colored dust of iron oxide permanently embedded in the whorls of their fingerprints. They are beautiful in the way raw geology is beautiful—sharp, unfinished, still becoming.

Their eyes are vast. The grey-green light of the Hollow is diffuse, and to navigate it, the pupils have dilated into dark pools that swallow what little illumination filters down from the rectangular sky. When they look up toward the bruise of light, their faces tilt back in a gesture of longing or prayer. The children are born with eyes adapted to this gloom; they do not blink at the dimness, but rather squint at the rare moments when a shaft of true sunlight, angled just so through a fissure, strikes a wall and illuminates a single handhold. They call this "sky-kiss," and they reach for it with fingers outstretched, as if trying to catch the light itself.

They are small. Not stunted—compact. The vertical life has compressed them, pulled them inward toward their centers of gravity. Adults stand perhaps five feet, with torsos long and legs short and powerful, built for climbing rather than walking. They do not walk the horizontal plane often; they flow. On the wooden walkways, they move with a sideways gait, crab-like, one hand always touching the wall, the other extended for balance. When they must descend, they do not use the ladders—those are for the hurried, the young, the ones who have not yet learned patience. Instead, they slide, controlled, down the inclined ramps carved into the stone, their bodies flattened against the rock face, using friction and faith.

The settlement is layered by age and function, and so are the people. At the upper levels, nearest the rectangular sky, live the Watchers. They are the oldest, and they have begun to turn to stone. Not literally—their joints still move, their hearts still beat—but they have gone still. They sit in niches carved specifically for their bodies, facing upward, their skin slowly taking on the texture of the limestone behind them. They speak rarely, in voices like gravel shifting. They are the ones who remember the surface world, or who were born in the first days after the collapse, and they keep vigil over the light, tracking its movement across the rim of the world like a sundial. When the sun is directly above, they raise their hands—not to touch the sky, which is impossible, but to feel the warmth that filters down, the only warmth they will ever know.

Below them, at the middle levels, are the Masons. They are the numerous ones, the workers, the ones whose hands are stained with the flour of freshly broken rock. They are young, strong, possessed of a particular kind of focus that allows them to hear the stone. When they strike a chisel against the limestone, they listen not to the sound of the blow, but to the resonance that follows—the hum that tells them if the rock is solid or hollow, willing or resentful. They carve the new chambers, extending the habitable space deeper into the geology, following the veins of softer stone like bloodhounds following scent. Their muscles are corded and pale, built for the repetitive violence of stone-on-stone. They wear aprons of woven reed, and their hair is cropped short to keep it from catching in the scaffolding.

At the lower levels, where the mist gathers and the pools reflect fragments of sky, live the Water-keepers. They are the most numerous, for water is life here, and life requires tending. They are thin, wiry, with skin that seems to drink in the moisture around them. They move silently, barefoot, their feet spreading wide on the wet stone, toes gripping like rootlets. They maintain the channels carved into the rock, clear the silt from the condensation constellations, and tend the pale, eyeless fish that swim in the deep pools. They speak to the water. It is said that if you press your ear to the stone near their chambers, you can hear them humming to the seepage, singing to the droplets as they form and fall. They are the ones who know the taste of every rivulet, who can tell by the mineral content of a bead of sweat on a wall whether rain is coming to the surface world above, hours before the mist shifts.

The children are everywhere and nowhere. They are born into the vertical world, and they learn to climb before they learn to stand upright on flat ground. You will see them at the edges of platforms, dropping stones into the depths, counting the seconds until the splash—three, sometimes four, depending on the level. They play games of tag across the rope bridges, swinging from timber to timber with the ease of bats, though they are not bats, not yet. They have a language of signs and touches, for voices carry too far in the Hollow, and privacy is precious. They press their palms against the cool stone and listen to its slow, geological heartbeat, learning to read the settling of the rock, the tiny sighs of flakes detaching from high walls.

There is a caste, or perhaps just a condition, called the Deep-Born. These are the ones who have lived so long in the lowest levels, where the mist never fully clears and the light is only a rumor, that they have begun to lose their pigmentation entirely. They are albino, translucent, their veins visible as dark rivers beneath skin like wet parchment. They tend the forges—the deepest, warmest chambers where the earliest iron is worked—and they are the only ones who can withstand the heat. Their eyes have gone completely black, milky with cataracts that have formed from generations of staring into the glow of the forge. They are revered and feared. They speak in clicks and sub-vocal tones that resonate in the chest rather than the throat, sounds that seem to come from the stone itself.

The Hollowed do not sleep as surface people do. They rest in shifts, in nests of woven reed and slate shingle packed into the timber frames. Their dreams are of falling—not the terror of falling, but the ecstasy of it, the three-second descent, the soft percussion of stone on water. They wake with their hands already reaching for the wall, fingers finding the next handhold before their eyes open.

They are building upward, always upward. Each season, new platforms extend from the cliff faces, and with them, new lives. The first generation, the ones who came when the roof of the world cracked, are mostly gone now, returned to the stone they helped carve. But their children remain, and their children's children, a dense, vertical society of pale, climbing, listening people, packed tight against the raw geometry of the collapse.

They are becoming the Hollow. Their bones are denser, mineral-heavy, drinking in the calcium from the water. Their voices have dropped to a register that vibrates in the sternum, a frequency that matches the resonance of the limestone chambers. They are no longer quite human, if they ever were, and not yet something else. They are the first citizens of the wound, the inhabitants of the earth's recent scar, building a city in the moment the world split open, reaching upward with pale hands toward a light they have only just learned to catch.
