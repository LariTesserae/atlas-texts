What lives here moves at the speed of geology. The kelp are the first inhabitants you encounter—not trees, but a forest of them, rising from the flat plain in columns of swaying green-gray that undulate in currents so weak they might be the breathing of the water itself. Each frond is a slow architecture, unfurling over seasons that last years, hosting nothing that grazes but accumulating instead a drifting population of microbial film. They grow where the ancient walls stood, rooting in the cracks between worn stones, their holdfasts gripping the worked substrate until the boundary between human construction and vegetal growth becomes a single continuous surface.

Below them, the moss forms the understory—a heavy, saturated pelt of cold-water species that drapes the ruins like wet felt. It clings to the near-flattened plains of the old causeways, filling the depressions of silted cisterns, draping the rounded mounds of collapsed towers in thick, drip-heavy folds. This is a vegetation that has never known drought or fire, only the steady, cold pressure of accumulation. It grows over the stone adze marks, over the fiber-rope impressions pressed into clay, until the original geometry of the civilization exists only as a subtle topography beneath the living mat. The moss holds the particulate rain, trapping the marine snow in its fibers, creating a secondary soil that is itself alive with bacterial colonies working in the anoxic dark.

Fungi spread across the pavements in fractal patterns, the great recyclers of this patient world. They are not the fast molds of warm, dry places, but something older and slower—mycelial networks that expand a millimeter per decade, digesting the ancient timber (if it was timber) preserved in the cold, breaking down the cellulose of 2.8 units of deep time. They fruit in gelatinous masses, in velvet films that coat the submerged beams, in pale, luminescent patches that offer the only light in this 0.2 light-level world—a faint, fungal phosphorescence that pulses with a rhythm slower than a heartbeat. They are the engineers of the final erosion, dissolving the distinction between the made and the grown.

And beneath all of this, in the silt, in the water column, in the particulate rain that never ceases, there is the invisible life: the microbial mat that constitutes the true skin of this place. Bacteria colonize every surface, forming biofilms on the rounded cobbles, living in the anoxic sediment where the wood releases its last trapped gases in slow, sighing bubbles. The "marine snow"—that continuous, silent precipitation of organic particulate—is also a rain of dormant life, of spores and cysts and single cells drifting in the vertical dark, waiting for a surface to settle upon, waiting for centuries to pass so they might germinate, divide, and join the great, slow accumulation.

There are no mouths here that open to graze. No burrowing claws to turn the silt. No pollinators to carry genetic material between flowers. The fauna level of 0.4 means that these plants grow unchecked into a monoculture of patience, a jungle without insects, a forest without birds. The only movement is the settling of sediment, the unfurling of a frond, the collapse of a moss-heavy overhang into a hollow, the imperceptible creep of fungal hyphae through ancient wood. The inhabitants are the accumulators, the drapers, the slow digesters—a civilization of flora and microbe that has inherited the drowned works of hands, and is now, with glacial deliberation, building its own cathedral of green over the ruins.
