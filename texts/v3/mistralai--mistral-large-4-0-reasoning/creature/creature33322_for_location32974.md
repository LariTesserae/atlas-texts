They are the children of friction and heat, the mineral-blooded denizens of the deep dark. Consider one alone, perched upon a catwalk where the sodium vapor light pools like stagnant water. It is not beautiful in the way of a flower or a bird, but in the manner of a machine that has learned to want: a segmented articulation of living glass and ceramic, each segment a bulbous chamber visible through a translucent integument of silicate composite. Within, you can see the slow circulation of its ichor—not red, not water-based, but a shimmering slurry of molten eutectic salts, carrying dissolved iron, sulfur, and rare earth elements in a superheated suspension that would scald human flesh to vapor. The creature's "blood" is a brine so concentrated it approaches liquid metal, kept fluid by the geothermal embrace of the factories, cooling to a brittle glass only when exposed to the thinner, colder air of the upper throat.

Its body is a chain of crystalline pods, perhaps a dozen segments, each a different degree of opacity depending on how much mineral it has accreted. The younger segments, toward the rear, are clear as quartz; the older, toward the head, are clouded with inclusions of native copper and silver, heavy metals sequestered from the factory waste, stored in its tissues like a living treasury. It moves not with muscles in the mammalian sense, but with a peristaltic wave of hydraulic pressure, its many legs—each no thicker than a wire, tipped with points of sintered obsidian—clicking against the steel in a staccato rhythm that is, to human ears, nearly silent, but to the creature, a constant tactile percussion.

These are the Lithophage, though they have no name for themselves, communicating only in the language of pressure and precipitate. They do not breathe as we understand it; they respire through chemosynthesis, drawing energy from the redox reactions between the sulfur dioxide vented by the smelters and the iron oxides that coat every surface. Their metabolism is a slow fire, internal and flameless, converting poison to structure, waste to wealth. When they feed, they do not chew; they press their ventral surfaces against slag heaps and corroded pipes, secreting a thin film of acidic solvent that dissolves mineral detritus, which they then absorb through thousands of microscopic pores. Their bodies swell with the intake, becoming temporarily opaque, heavy with raw material, before the excess is excreted as the white efflorescence that dusts the dark glass above—a snow of pure mineral that never melts, only sublimates into the dry air, leaving behind a chemical ghost of their passing.

They live in a state of perpetual quorum. Individually, they are simple, almost mechanical in their function: eat, process, excrete, move. But collectively, they form a distributed intelligence, a nervous system of flesh and mineral that spans the vertical city. When a section of the mountain shifts—a slow grinding of young obsidian against young obsidian—the creatures do not panic. They feel the subsonic groan through their crystalline leg-tips, a vibration that travels up from the mantle through the factories' foundations, below the threshold of human hearing but thunderous in their perception. This signal is not fear but information: a change in the architecture, a new corridor opening, a catwalk crushed into rubble. The swarm responds as a single body, surging through the passages in roiling clouds of clicking segments, filling the gaps, sealing the wounds, flowing like blood through veins that are constantly being torn and rewoven.

Their communication is chemical and vibrational, a dense fog of information in an air too still to carry sound waves. They release plumes of metallic aerosols and sulfur compounds, leaving trails that persist for hours in the stagnant atmosphere, creating a three-dimensional map of intent and status. A trail of silver ions might mean *food here*; a pulse of low-frequency vibration through a girder might signal *collapse imminent, retreat to the deep vents*. They taste the air with chemoreceptors lining their underbellies, reading the history of the city in the chemical residues left by those who passed before, distinguishing between the scent of fresh-smeared slag and the older, oxidized memory of a factory that died a century ago.

In the chaos of the mountain's remaking, they are the constant. When a smelter is crushed by a rockfall, the creatures do not flee; they accelerate. They swarm over the wreckage, their acidic secretions dissolving the exposed mantle rock, their bodies processing the raw geothermal minerals, and in their excretions, they lay down new veins of metal that will be harvested when the new forge is built by evening. They are the city's immune system, consuming the dead machinery and the invading stone, and its memory, their mineral-laden bodies recording the chemical history of every collapse and reconstruction in the strata of their own flesh.

Their lifecycle is brief and violent, measured not in years but in thermal cycles. They hatch from eggs laid in the hottest vents, their shells already beginning to accrete mineral from the moment of birth. They grow rapidly, molting their silicate skins, which are immediately consumed by the swarm—nothing is wasted. When the geology shifts and a tunnel seals, thousands may be trapped, entombed in the crushing dark. They do not die; they slow, their metabolism dropping to a crystalline stasis, their bodies becoming part of the rock itself, fossils-in-formation, their mineral deposits contributing to the mountain's slow mineralogical evolution. Millions of years hence, when erosion finally softens these spires, they will be released again as dust, as ions, to be taken up by new life in a world that has forgotten this darkness.

But for now, they are here, in the perpetual industrial twilight, a silent, clicking, shimmering tide of life that needs no water, no sun, no mercy. They are the mountain's dream made manifest—a dry sea of bodies flowing through the veins of the world, turning fire into form, and form back into fire, in an endless, quiet, subsonic churn that only the stone itself can hear.
