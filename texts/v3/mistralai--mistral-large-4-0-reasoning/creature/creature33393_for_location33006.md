The inhabitants do not own the city; they *are* the city—its slow, accreting consciousness distributed across mineral and membrane. At the foundation live the **architects**: biofilms of cyanobacteria and diatoms, colonies so thin they exist only in the interface between water and stone, photosynthesizing in the dim glow that filters down through the mist. They are the masons. Their metabolism exhales calcium carbonate, precipitating the travertine grain by grain, secreting the very terraces upon which others will stand. They do not move; they *build*. Their lifespans are measured in geological moments, yet they work with an urgency that belies their slowness, for they know the stone is young and hungry, and they must feed it, layer it, keep it growing upward toward the light they can barely feel.

Above them, carpeting every surface that the spray touches, live the **upholsterers**: bryophytes—mosses in shades of emerald, jade, and bottle-green, liverworts flattened like lace against the white rock. They are the permanent residents, the ones who never leave. They drink from the mist, from the constant drizzle of condensation, holding water in their cells like reservoirs. They soften the sharp edges of the young stone. When you step on a terrace, it is often moss you compress, releasing that cold breath the ground holds. They form neighborhoods, districts of dense cushioning where the stone is oldest and most weathered. Ferns unfurl in the crevices—fiddleheads of cold-loving *Asplenium*—adding a fractal geometry to the terraced architecture. They are the gardeners, but they do not tend; they simply *are*, occupying the niches the water provides, binding the rubble into coherence.

Then there are the **citizens who walk**: the snails. They are legion. Their shells are pale, almost the color of the travertine itself, spiraled and chambered like miniature versions of the terraces they traverse. They move with a patience that is not slowness but a different tempo entirely—gliding on mucus trails that shine wetly across the stone, rasping algae with radulas that whisper against the rock. They are the scribes of this place, their mouths the styluses that etch the city's surface, cleaning it, preparing it for new growth. Each snail carries its home—a shell that is itself a travertine formation, precipitated from the same mineral water, a portable piece of the architecture. They are the only ones who truly belong to every district, moving from terrace to terrace, from pool to pool, leaving silver trails that catch the mist-light like calligraphy.

In the water streets, the **dancers** perform. Water striders stand on the surface tension, their legs dimpling the water but not breaking it, distributing their weight across six points of contact. They skate, they probe, they mate in spirals on the surface of the pools. Midges hover in slow, cold clouds above the water, their wings beating at frequencies that hum just below hearing, mating in swarms that look like living mist. They are the ephemeral ones, living fast in a world that moves slow—born, mating, dying within days, their bodies falling to become food for the fish below, their chitin dissolving into the mineral soup that feeds the masons.

In the deeper pools, the **silent ones** hold station. Small fish, translucent and pale—cave-adapted species or winter-stunted minnows that have found a thermal refuge in the upwelling. They have no pigment to waste; their flesh is glass, their bones visible, swimming through the mineral-heavy water with a stillness that suggests they are not so much living as *suspended*. They face the current, motionless, filtering microbes and algae from the water that flows past. They are the groundwater made visible, the water's own memory of the deep fissures it rose from. When disturbed, they dart—white flashes in the green-dark—and then freeze again, becoming indistinguishable from the stone, from the silence.

And then the **visitors**. Birds come down from the rim of the valley, where the land rises and the mist thins. Warblers—tiny, drab, quick—flick through the ferns, drinking from the leaf-axils where water collects. Pipits walk the stone edges, bobbing their tails, picking at the midges. And sometimes, standing statue-still on a high terrace, a heron. Grey and patient, a living gargoyle, it waits for the fish to betray themselves with a movement. It does not sing; it simply *watches*. These are the diplomats, the ones who carry news of the outside world—wind, warmth, the turning of seasons—but they do not stay. They drink, they bathe in the shallows, and they leave, taking the valley's cold with them in their feathers, leaving only a ripple and a feather caught in the moss.

But all of these—the masons, the upholsterers, the scribes, the dancers, the silent ones, the visitors—are not separate. They form a single **we**. The snails eat the algae that the bacteria precipitated. The fish eat the midges that danced on the water the moss drank. The birds eat the insects that ate the moss. And when they die, they feed the biofilm, which builds more stone, which gives the moss new purchase, which holds more water for the snails, which scrape the new growth, which…

They are the valley's immune system, its nervous system, its dreaming mind. When the mist hangs low, they are all asleep together—the snails sealed in their shells with a membrane of dried mucus, the fish hovering in torpor, the moss holding its breath, the bacteria slowing their metabolism to a near-stop. And when the diffuse grey light brightens (though it never truly warms), they wake, not as individuals, but as a *condition*. The city breathes. The water rises. The stone grows. And they are all of it, and it is all of them—a civilization of the cold and the wet, building itself from the inside out, citizen by citizen, layer by layer, into the mist, until the distinction between the inhabitant and the inhabited has dissolved completely, and only the breathing remains.
