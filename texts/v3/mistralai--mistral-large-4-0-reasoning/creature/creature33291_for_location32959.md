You do not see them at first. The density of fauna sits at 0.4—a presence so attenuated it hides beneath the roar of the Resonance, a silence deeper than the stone’s own screaming. But they are there, written into the landscape like fossils in the act of becoming.

The Lithophylls are not merely habitat; they are the mature form of the inhabitants. Each hexagonal column is a former dweller, lithified over centuries of accretion, still thinking in the slow language of crystal growth. When you press your palm to a spire—if your lungs weren’t working twice as hard, if the Resonance didn’t vibrate your fillings into agony—you would feel not the shriek, but a deeper pulse: the mineralized memory of a heartbeat, preserved in silica. The forests are graveyards and metropolises both. The dead and the dormant sing in piezoelectric harmony, their branches reaching not toward light but toward pockets of stable mass, curving around gravity wells like roots seeking water in a desert that has never known rain.

Then there are the mobile ones. The builders. They exist at the 0.4, scattered across the plateau like dust motes caught in the bruised light, separated by kilometers of scree and raw, unweathered rock. When you encounter one, it is suspended halfway between animal and mineral. Their skin carries the texture of unweathered obsidian, shot through with veins of calcium carbonate that pulse with each labored breath of thin, copper-tinged air. They are the answer to the bone. The tools of flint and bone left on ledges where gravity shifts are not relics of the dead; they are shed material—the builders’ own vertebrae and phalanges, cast off as they lithify. They are in a constant state of becoming-stone, their skeletons fossilizing from the inside out, leaving behind these white, sharp fragments as they grow too heavy, too mineral, to move. The bone is theirs, harvested from their own transformation.

They are silent. Not mute—deaf, perhaps, to the frequencies you can hear, speaking instead in the subsonic thrum of the Resonance. When two builders meet across the sparse plain, they do not touch hands in the way you understand. They press their crystalline palms together and exchange pressure waves, a conversation of tectonic shifts felt in the sternum, in the vibration of teeth. They have no mouths, or their mouths are sealed by mineral growth, and they eat by absorbing volcanic gases through pores in their stone-skin, precipitating the chemistry of the air into sustenance, accreting layer by layer as the Lithophylls do.

Time is their architecture. Because the temporal drift is absolute—a minute at the base of a spire, an hour at its tip—they exist as diasporas of themselves, scattered across the vertical axis. A single builder might have three versions alive simultaneously on one crystal tower: the youth at the root, vigorous and mobile, stacking cairns of obsidian in spirals that make no cartographic sense; the elder at the mid-height, slowing, bones thickening into basalt; the ancient at the summit, nearly a Lithophyll itself, its voice joining the eternal scream. They build the shelters with doors that open into yesterday so that their past selves can retrieve tools from their future hands, creating causal loops that defy the linear flow you expect. The cairns rotate, completing a full turn every decade, because the builders are still walking their spiral paths, pushing the stones from the inside, from the slow time at their core, adjusting for the gravity anomalies that drift across the plateau like weather systems.

But there is another layer, older and stranger, suspended in the dry air itself. The extremophile bacteria, dormant until the weirdness shifts. These are the true architects, or perhaps the builders’ original form, the larval stage before calcification. When a gravity anomaly drifts across the plateau, when the mass of the world rearranges itself and reality frays at the edges, the bacteria wake. They precipitate from the thin atmosphere in fungal mats of metallic lichen, spreading across boulders like spilled ink. In this phase, they are the ones who build. The “hands” that stacked the obsidian in a hurry, or in a trance, were not hands at all, but colonies of these microbes, working in concert, guided by a distributed intelligence that thinks in geological epochs. They stack the cairns to anchor the gravity wells, to stabilize the local physics, to pin the reality that threatens to fall upward into the bruised sky. When the weirdness passes, when the gravity stabilizes, they go dormant again, suspending themselves in the 0.3 parts per thousand of trace vapor, waiting for the next shift of mass and time.

Between these forms—the bacterial spore in the air, the mobile builder shedding its bones, the stationary Lithophyll singing the Resonance—the inhabitants cycle. They are the fauna at 0.4, the silence beneath the roar. They are the bone, and the builders of bone. They are the stone that grows lush in the dark, writing themselves into the world at millimeters per century, leaving their calcified exoskeletons as markers in a landscape where gravity is a suggestion and time is a spiral staircase. They do not look at you with eyes, for they have none, or their eyes are the hexagonal facets of the crystals they are becoming. They feel you instead—a disturbance in the gravity, a new mass introduced into their thin, cool air—and they turn, slowly, over decades, to face you with the patience of mountains still being born.

You are the anomaly here. They are the Razorback, and they are still being written.
