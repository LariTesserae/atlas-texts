They are the **Quiet Folk** (silence 2.5, slowness 2.0), though they do not call themselves that. To them, they are simply the people, the residents, the ones who press their palms into the warm loam and feel the pulse of the world. They are few—a few hundred scattered through the basin’s kilometers—but they are dense in their clustering, gathering in the hollows of the great augers where the metal retains a residual heat like fevered skin. They have forgotten the sun (memory 0.4). Their eyes, vestigial folds of translucent skin, no longer seek light; instead, they are attuned to pressure (sensitivity 2.4), to the slow chemical tides in the humid air, to the minute shifts in temperature that ripple through the soil when a distant piston extends an inch.

They do not speak. Sound is too expensive here, too quickly swallowed by moisture and mass. Instead, they communicate through the earth itself (communication 2.2), pressing their soft bodies against the compressed loam or the rusted flanks of machines, feeling the footsteps of others through the mycelial network that threads the soil like a nervous system. A conversation takes a day. A greeting is the slow spreading of pressure through the damp ground, a wave of warmth and weight that travels from one chest to another. They are not lonely; loneliness requires distance, and here they are packed close (proximity 2.8), separated only by centimeters of humid air and the constant, slow peristalsis of the ground beneath them.

They tend the machines not as masters but as gardeners tend orchids (tending 1.9), or as one tends the bacteria in one’s own gut. They clear fungal growth from certain valves, oil the joints with rendered fat from the beetle-carcasses that accumulate in drifts, and listen—actually *listen*, ear pressed to cold metal—for the grinding of gears to detect blockages in the biomass flow. They live in the spaces between pistons, in the hollows of gear-teeth where condensation pools like dark wine. Their dwellings are indistinguishable from the machines’ anatomy (integration 2.6); a stranger might mistake a sleeping Folk for a knot of lichen, a gestating pupa, a peculiar outgrowth of rust.

Their skin is the color of wet loam, translucent in places, showing the slow blue pulse of veins that carry not blood alone but the green sap of symbiotic algae living in their dermis (photosynthesis 0.6). They need only the verdigris glow and the nutrients seeping from the soil to survive. They eat the fungi that fruit from the machine seams (nutrition 1.4), the snails that graze on those fungi, and the compressed, warm, dark loam that the augers extrude like slow toothpaste. They drink the condensation from the cisterns, water that tastes of rust and chlorophyll and compressed centuries. They are not hungry, not in the urgent way of the world above. They are sustained (sustenance 2.1), filled, content in their slow metabolisms.

But they are not alone. The true citizens, the overwhelming majority, are the **Multitudes** (population 3.0, individuality 0.3). The woodlice march in rivers across the humus, their segmented backs gleaming like wet obsidian, the sanitation corps, the recycling engineers. They process the leaf-fall, the dead, the detritus, turning the vertical into the horizontal (erosion 1.8). The beetles—ordinary beetles, scarabs, weevils, ground beetles—are the burrowers (engineering 1.8), chewing through the compressed time, their mandibles grinding the loam into finer particles. They do not know they live among machines; to them, the gears are simply harder soil, the pistons are trees that move, the conveyor plates are geological strata that happen to shift. A beetle crawling over a gear does not register the metal as strange; it registers it as ground (normalcy 2.9).

There are larger things. In the stagnant pools that collect between the conveyor plates, pale salamanders the length of a forearm drift like living mist (size 1.5, pigmentation 0.1). They have no camouflage, no need for it in the green dark. They feed on the insect multitudes, moving with a sinuous slowness that matches the turning of the gears. And deep in the pipes, where the dark water gurgles and the pressure is high, something else moves—long, white, eyeless things that have forgotten they were ever fish, now adapted to the mineral-rich flow, cleaning the insides of the machines with rasping tongues (maintenance 2.0), keeping the arteries clear so the slow blood of biomass can continue its circulation.

The Folk believe—or perhaps simply *know*, without the need for belief—that the machines are alive (animism 1.7). Not metaphorically. The rotation of a gear, once an hour, is a heartbeat. The condensation dripping into cisterns is breath. The processing of fallen matter is digestion. And the Folk themselves are the microbiome, the helpful flora in the gut of a vast, slow beast that is either the forest wearing machines like a shell, or the machines grown over with a forest like a skin. They are all one continuous organism (oneness 2.8), and the Folk are its consciousness, distributed and slow, feeling its own body through the loam and the metal.

They have names for the machines—old names, worn smooth by centuries of use, “spoken” not in sound but in patterns of pressure against the metal. The Great Auger. The Slow Press. The Condenser. They know which gears turn at which hours, which pistons extend on which days, living by this geological calendar (timekeeping 2.3), a rhythm so slow that a human lifetime above would see only a twitch, a single spasm in the eyelid of this place.

They are at peace (contentment 1.6). They do not dream of the sky, because they have never known it. The green twilight is their noon. The wet, heavy silence is their music. The press of ten thousand lives around them—the woodlice, the beetles, the salamanders, the fungi, the machines, the other Folk—is their community. They are the inhabitants of the normal, the citizens of the deep, the patient people of the green dark, turning with the gears, forever.
