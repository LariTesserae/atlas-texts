They are the **Terrace-Born**, though they rarely use that name for themselves, having long since stopped distinguishing between the speakable and the grown. They are a confederation of forms that have surrendered the boundary between maker and made, between the gardener and the garden, between the machine and the moss that softens its gears.

Consider first the **Condensation-Dwellers**—the smallest citizens, visible only when the light catches the mist. They inhabit the cisterns built into the joints of the ceramic plates, the fern-lined gutters, the slow-drip pockets where moisture gathers like breath on glass. They are translucent, jelly-bodied things, no larger than a fingernail, filter-feeding on the algae that colonizes the damp stone. They have no eyes, only chemoreceptors that taste the mineral content of the water, and they reproduce in synchronized pulses with the morning mist, releasing spores that rise on the warm updrafts to seed the upper terraces. They are the valley’s kidneys, the first inhabitants of every drop, and they have never known rain—only the patient gift of condensation, falling from the warm air onto the cooler stone.

Above them, in the green-gold twilight of the lower strata, live the **Grating-Runners**. They are mammals, perhaps descended from engineered livestock or ancient escapees, now fully naturalized to the altitude and the humidity. They are sleek and low-slung, with wet, dark fur that never dries, and eyes that reflect the perpetual dimness of the under-canopy like polished obsidian. They move along the maintenance walkways—those bridges of rusted iron and living wood, thick with algae—with a skittering, urgent slowness, nibbling the detritus of the machinery, the fallen fruit of the broad-leafed canopy, the bodies of insects that have drowned in the condensation pools. They are the cleaners, the recyclers, the necessary friction that keeps the system from clogging. They communicate in subsonic rumbles that merge with the piston-beat of the pumps, and they build their nests in the warm housings of the electrified cables, sleeping curled around the humming copper, dreaming in currents.

The **Sky-Nesters** wheel between the terraces in flocks that obscure the severe, clarifying light. They are birds of impossible color—iridescent greens and warning yellows and the deep rust-red of oxidized iron—adapted to nesting not in trees but in the grating and girders of the ancient construction. They have learned to weave their nests from conductive wire and synthetic fiber, lining them with lichen and the soft, shed fur of the Grating-Runners. They are loud, contributing their cries to the constant roar, and they are the messengers of the valley, carrying seeds in their crops from terrace to terrace, carrying parasites, carrying the news of rot and bloom. They do not migrate; there is nowhere else. They have become as permanent as the waterwheels, as rooted as the creeping figs that threaten to engulf the concrete.

But the true architects of the lower world are the **Root-Pressers**—the vegetation itself, which has achieved a kind of slow, vegetal sentience. The tropical exuberance is not mere growth but occupation, a root-aggressive campaign to claim every niche, every trough, every crack in the weathered composite. The creeping figs send down aerial roots that drape over the retaining walls like green waterfalls, softening the concrete with their acidic breath. The broad-leafed canopy filters the bright light into a diffuse, breathing gloom, and within that gloom, the plants communicate through fungal networks threaded through the manufactured soil—trading sugars for minerals, warning of insect clouds, negotiating the slow, patient truce with the machinery. They are citizens with tenure, older than the current configuration of the terraces, pushing up through the cracks, prying at the joints, reminding the builders that the valley belongs to the living.

And then there are the **Gardeners**.

They are humanoid, or were once, but they have been remade by three thousand meters of altitude and centuries of humid, heavy air. They are tall and thin, with spines elongated by the thinness of the upper atmosphere, though the warmth of the valley has allowed them to retain a tropical lushness of lung and limb. Their skin is not skin so much as a living patina—pale, mineral-stained, etched with patterns of lichen and symbiotic algae that photosynthesize in the severe light, giving them a faint, verdigris glow. They wear clothing that is grown rather than woven: fungal leather that breathes, vine-fiber mesh that wicks the perpetual damp, plates of fired ceramic they have glazed themselves in the kilns of the lower terraces.

They move with the glacial patience of the machinery they tend. They do not walk so much as *settle*—each step a negotiation with gravity and the slippery, algae-slick planks. They tend the irrigation conduits of blackened iron, clearing the fern-gutters of debris, harvesting the root-aggressive vines before they can crack the retaining walls. They lubricate the great slow-turning waterwheels, their hands stained with oil and pollen, their voices low and resonant, vibrating in the chest cavities they have expanded to breathe the thick, warm air. They do not rush. They have forgotten the concept of haste. They measure time by the turning of the wheels, by the drip of condensation, by the slow erosion of the ceramic crust into river-stone smoothness.

They live in the **thick sandwich**—the layered strata between infrastructure and biomass, in the niches and troughs of the ancient construction. Their homes are grown into the terraces, half-architecture, half-organic, walls of compressed fiber and concrete softened by lichen, floors of packed earth that bulge with roots. They raise their young in the humidity, teaching them to listen to the susurrus—the biological whisper of insects thick as smoke, the wet slap of leaves, the mechanical groan of gears. The children learn early to read the condensation, to know when the mist will gather in the lower levels like breath on glass, to understand that water here is not flood but *gift*, arriving in slow, patient drips from the sky-stone.

The **Insect Nations** are their neighbors, rising in clouds from the vegetation, thick as smoke, a living weather system of beetles and moths and unnamed things that have evolved in the warm, wet dark. They are the pollinators, the decomposers, the foundation of the food web. They live in the steam vents, in the warm crevices of the machinery, breeding in the condensation pools. They are loud, a constant, high-pitched whine that merges with the electrical hum of the thick cables, and they are dense, ubiquitous, a presence felt more than seen, settling on the Gardeners’ skin, filling their lungs with pollen and spores, becoming part of them.

There is no wildness here, no wilderness. Every creature is a decision, a colonization sanctioned by the design or evolved within its constraints. The boundary between machine and moss has dissolved into a single, slow-breathing entity, and the inhabitants are its consciousness—distributed, multispecies, patient. They are the valley experiencing itself: the Gardeners clearing the gutters are the valley cleaning its throat; the birds singing are the valley speaking; the steam rising from the vents is the valley exhaling; the insects rising like smoke are the valley’s thoughts, drifting upward to seed the next terrace.

They are a people of long durations, of geological time compressed into biological form. They do not dream of elsewhere. They have finished becoming, and have settled into being—a dense, patient, loud, green, and entirely *made* civilization, living in the warm, heavy air of a world that was built to last, and in lasting, built them.
