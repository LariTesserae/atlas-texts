They are not many, and they are not the builders.

The builders are a memory pressed into the glass like a leaf in amber, their geometry softening, their angles rounding off into something that might be natural, might be grown. What lives here now moves in the spaces between the monoliths, in the handful of domes that still stand against the slow pressure of the breathing caldera. They are the **Vitreous**, though they do not call themselves anything—names are sounds, and sound here is a luxury swallowed by the silence (0.2). They think in vibrations, in heat gradients, in the chemical signatures that rise from the vents.

They are small. Compact. Their bodies have atrophied toward efficiency, away from the wasteful verticality of their ancestors. Their skin is not skin as the old world knew it; it is translucent, pale as the chemosynthetic crusts that carpet the damp cracks, shot through with a faint, internal opalescence. Silica has worked its way into their biology—not as armor, but as integration. They drink the brackish water from the condensation pools, and their bodies process the dissolved minerals, depositing them in thin, flexible sheets beneath their dermis. They are becoming glass from the inside out, slowly, over generations. A child is born with soft, pink flesh; an elder is a walking geode, their joints clicking with crystalline deposits, their eyes milky with cataracts of calcium and silica that see not light—*the light is too bright, too pitiless*—but heat, movement, the thermal signatures of the world around them.

They wear the shed carapaces of the glass-eaters. Not as trophies, but as clothing. The creatures—translucent, segmented, eyeless things with too many jointed legs and mouths that rasp stone into dust—are their neighbors, their livestock, their kin. The Vitreous have learned the patience of geology. They do not dominate the glass-eaters; they *tend* them. They guide the swarms, these silent, clicking tides of life, toward the parts of the old city that need to be erased. When a wall has leaned too far, when a ziggurat threatens to topple into a new fumarole, the Vitreous beat the glass with obsidian-tipped staffs, sending seismic signals through the silica substrate, and the glass-eaters come. They flow over the stone like a tide of living frosting, and by morning, the wall is etched, frosted, its tool-marks erased, its geometry softened into something that looks less like architecture and more like weather.

The Vitreous live in the **Domes of the Second Glass**. These are not the original habitations—those are the smooth monoliths, the hoodoos, the melted wax of ancient candles. The Domes of the Second Glass are temporary, mobile, built from shards of the plain itself. When the caldera breathes—when a section of floor buckles upward or subsides into a new vent—the Vitreous do not fight it. They pack their few possessions: obsidian blades, wooden paddles (carved from the driftwood of some long-vanished forest, or perhaps grown from cultivated fungus-wood in the warmest caves), leather thongs, and the dried crusts of chemosynthetic bacteria that they cultivate in the dampest cracks. They move. They are nomads of the static, traveling miles in a year, following the stable plates of glass, the places where the thermal cycling has not yet claimed the ground.

They communicate by **Resonance**. Since sound is nearly absent, they speak through the floor. A single Vitreous will press their palm flat against the black glass and strike it with a knuckle, or with a small hammer of flint. The vibration travels for miles through the silica, through the pumice bedrock, reaching other Vitreous in their distant domes. A conversation is a matter of hours, sometimes days. A warning—*the ground is tilting, the fumarole opens*—is a sharp, staccato pattern that ripples across the plain like a stone thrown into still water. They have developed a language of seismic grammar, complex and subtle, capable of conveying not just information but emotion: the low, mournful groan of a structure settling, the high, anxious chirp of a new vent steaming to life, the rhythmic, comforting pulse of the caldera's slow heartbeat that they have learned to feel in their teeth.

They eat the **Bone-Rust Mats**. The chemosynthetic crusts—pale, leathery, the color of old bone and rust—are their sustenance. They scrape them from the damp cracks with obsidian blades, dry them in the sun (that pitiless, blazing clarity of light 2.6 that makes the glass floor a mirror of the sky), and grind them into a powder that tastes of sulfur, iron, and ozone. They drink the condensation that drips from the overhangs, collecting it in vessels of cooled glass. The water is never still; it seeps into their bodies, carries the silica, the minerals, the ancient chemistry of the stone, and they in turn leave traces of themselves in the pools—skin cells, hair, the calcium of their bones—feeding the cycle.

They do not build to last. They build to be **unmade**. Their architecture is an act of temporary defiance against the turbulence (2.8). When they shape a new shelter, they do so at the edge of a warm vent, where the glass is still plastic, still warm. They use wooden paddles and leather thongs, the same primitive tech (0.7) as their ancestors, pressing and smoothing the molten silica into vaults and walls. But they know—*they know*—that the ground will shift. A dome might stand for a decade, or a century, but the caldera breathes, and the glass creeps. So they build with intention, knowing that their work will be eroded, sanded down by thermal cycling, consumed by the patient hunger of the glass-eaters. They etch their own patterns into the walls—not the deliberate, geometric courses of the old builders, but spirals, waves, the branching patterns of lichen and frost. They are leaving messages for the future, for whatever might one day dig here, for the geological record itself.

And when they die, they do not bury their dead in the ground—the ground is too unstable, too alive. They practice **Vitreous Return**. They carry their dead to the warmest vents, the places where the glass is still soft, still flowing. There, they lay the body upon the cooling stone, and they wait. The heat does the work. Over days, weeks, the body desiccates, the minerals leach out, the silica of the glass incorporates the calcium and phosphorus of the flesh. The dead become part of the plain. They become architecture. They become, eventually, food for the glass-eaters, for the chemosynthetic crusts, for the slow, silent metabolism of the earth. It is not a tragedy. It is the only immortality this place offers: to be dissolved into the black glass, to become a faint iridescence in the monoliths, to be eroded, grain by grain, by the patient jaws of the world.

They are the last of the line, though they do not think of themselves as last. They are simply *here*, in the bowl of the world, beneath the blazing sky, surrounded by the silence. They are the keepers of the condensation, the shepherds of the swarms, the listeners to the groaning stone. They are becoming what the place demands: not conquerors, but components. Part of the turbulence. Part of the erosion. Part of the slow, bright, terrible silence.
