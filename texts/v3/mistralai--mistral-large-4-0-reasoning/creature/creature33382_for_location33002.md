They are there, though the silence has taught you to look for the wrong shapes.

They occupy the upper stories—the fifth floors where the water never reaches, where the air is merely humid rather than submerged—and they move through the flooded streets below not by swimming but by *rising*, buoyed by the same capillary wrongness that draws the water uphill. They are the ones who maintain the rows. When the water recedes on the six-hour cycle, leaving the streets steaming and bare, you will see them: figures wading through the thigh-high tide, their bodies draped in living insulation. They do not wear clothes so much as *wear growth*—mantles of moss that carpet their shoulders in identical, repeating patterns, fronds of fern sprouting from the collarbones like epaulets, bark-skin that has replaced epidermis, split and weeping that rust-colored sap that smells of copper.

They have no need for ears. The roar—the 2.3 on whatever scale measures the drone of existence—is not a barrier to them but a *medium*. It is their language, their lullaby, their heartbeat. You can see them communicating across the square, not with voices (the sound would be impossible, swallowed by the pressure in the sternum), but with gestures that release steam in precise, billowing punctuation: a turn of the head that vents a cloud from the collar, a tap of a metal-fingered hand against a brick wall that answers with a subsonic thrum felt in the teeth. They are synchronized to the machinery. When the presses stamp and the water rises, they rise with it, floating upward to tend the higher balconies. When the fires are banked and the pumps fall silent to a gurgle, they descend, their weight settling back into the warm flood, curling into postures of conservation alongside the drooping fronds.

Their faces—if they can be called faces—are obscured by condensation and by growth. Eyes, perhaps, exist beneath the canopy of lichen that veils their features, glowing with that same faint bioluminescent pulse you see throbbing in the fungal mats below the waterline. They are neither fully human nor fully plant, nor fully the machinery that surrounds them. They are the *cultivators* and the *cultivated*, locked in the reciprocal farming described by the geometry of the reeds. They train the vines to follow the cable conduits, and the vines, in turn, have trained them—rootlets threading beneath their skin, neural pathways rerouted through mycelial networks, thoughts growing slow and verdant and patient as the vegetation itself.

They do not sleep, exactly. They enter a state of *stagnation*—standing motionless in the steam, breathing in rhythm with the vegetal respiration, their chests rising and falling with the tide of the water outside. In this state, they are indistinguishable from the architecture, from the sagging balconies and the rust-bloomed roofs. They are part of the elegant rot.

At the center, where the water is deepest and the hybrid structure hums with its aching frequency, they gather. Not in a crowd—there is no social density here, no bustle—but in a *convergence*, spaced at exact intervals around the base of the concrete-and-chlorophyll heart. They stand with their arms outstretched, palms up, receiving the heat, the steam, the electric touch of the condensation pillars. They are communing with the thing that is neither cooling tower nor tree, offering it the nutrients of their presence, the carbon of their breath, the minerals of their decaying cells. And it offers them, in return, the synchronization, the purpose, the right to exist in this place where the sun is remembered but not seen.

They are the last inhabitants of the hamlet that forgot it was ever dry. Not animals, because they have transcended the nervous system—replaced nerves with fungal hyphae, with the slow electrical pulses of root and rust. Not plants, because they remember, dimly, the shape of a village square, the need for rows and order, the geometry of cultivation. Not machines, because they weep sap when the warm, oily rain falls, and because they dream, in their slow, humid way, of a dry place that no longer exists.

They live in the bruised, green-gray luminescence. They live in the pressure behind the eyes. They live in the space between the water’s rise and fall, between the built and the grown, existing in that final, humid, roaring state of vegetal stagnation—neither alive nor dead, but *established*, rooted in the silt and the sediment, patient as the rot, eternal as the steam.
