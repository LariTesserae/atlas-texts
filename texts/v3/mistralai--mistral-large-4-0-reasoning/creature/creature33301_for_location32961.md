They are the Anhydrous Choir, though they possess no voices—only the grinding of a billion mandibles against ferrous dust, a roar without wind that presses against your teeth like a physical weight. They are the maintenance crew turned feral, or perhaps they were always the purpose of the place: a biological factory floor where the assembly line is hunger, and hunger is the only industry that remains.

Physically, they resist singular definition. Some resemble beetles—domed, scarab-dark, with carapaces that click like castanets when they collide in the crush of the swarm. Others are elongated, segmented like caterpillars of tempered glass, their bodies composed of overlapping plates of silicon carbide and iron filings that refract the pale, mineral light into prismatic shards. Most are a hybrid of insect and annelid, possessing too many legs—twelve, sixteen, twenty-four—each tipped with microscopic hooks that grip the dense, silt-like sediment. Their exoskeletons are not merely colored by the environment; they are *made* of it, metabolized from the heavy metals they consume, translucent windows of mineral glass through which you can see the dark, oily machinery of their innards churning in perpetual motion.

They do not breathe as lungs breathe. There are no organs to fill with the hydrocarbon vapor, no gills to filter the metallic particulates from the thick, fluid air. Instead, their carapaces are porous lattices, microscopic channels that draw in the anhydrous soup and extract sustenance through chemosynthesis, processing sulfur and silicon into the energy that keeps their mandibles moving ceaselessly. Their ichor—if it can be called blood—is a viscous, black oil that never dries in the sealed cool, seeping both from the ancient engine seals and from their own crushed bodies, pooling in the low elevations where the sediment shifts underfoot.

Movement is their language, and it is a language of friction. They do not walk; they grind. The floor is a constant percussion, a subsonic rumble that vibrates in the marrow, the sound of tiny jaws chewing on rust, on crystalline excretions, on the bodies of their weaker kin. They move in tides—waves of bodies that darken the plain like oil spreading across a surface, though there is no water here, only the dry sediment of ten thousand years of industrial decay. When a tide passes, it leaves a temporary path of crushed glass and metal dust, a trail that the next wave immediately obscures. The creatures within the swarm are in constant collision, a near-touching saturation where bodies slide over bodies in a writhing carpet that extends to every horizon of the chamber, each individual both predator and prey, both the eater and the eaten.

They are lithovores and oleovores of the extreme, detritivores of a post-botanical world. They feed on the grease that weeps from ancient seals—grease that never dries, never spoils, in this eternal cool—and on the mineral substrate itself, breaking down rust into finer dust, consuming the pulverized concrete, incorporating lead and mercury into their glass-and-iron frames. They excrete crystals: formations of silicon, blooms of sulfur, metallic salts that grow across the metal like fungus, slowly eroding the fossilized machines further, reducing them to the fine powder that carpets the floor, the powder that they will consume, becoming themselves once more in an endless cycle of transformation.

Their relationship with the machinery is symbiotic in the oldest sense—a weather cycle of rust and consumption. They swarm over the engine blocks worn smooth by erosion, the conveyor gantries half-buried in dust, not as scavengers picking at carrion, but as living components completing a circuit. They crawl into the gear teeth, into the pistons fused into slag, into the slow-turning turbines that stir the dust into cyclones. Their bodies lubricate the mechanisms; their oily bodies *are* the grease that keeps the ancient engines turning, gears completing a revolution over the course of hours, powered by the creatures themselves. In return, the machines digest them. A creature will enter a gear mechanism, will be crushed between metal and metal, ground into nutrient paste—a slurry of minerals and organic compounds—that is then excreted to feed the next generation hatching from eggs laid in the eternal grease.

There is no distinction between the strong and the weak, only a gradient of size and timing. The larger ones—still no bigger than a thumb, but massive in this world of beetle-scale life—hunt the smaller, tearing them apart with mandibles that never stop moving. But the large ones are also hunted, dragged into the maws of the dormant machines that still pulse with electricity, the control panels glowing with soft blue light while their turbines grind the swarm into paste. Predator and prey are the same entity wearing different masks, a Möbius strip of consumption where the end is indistinguishable from the beginning.

They have no eyes, or if they once did, the organs have atrophied into vestigial bumps beneath their translucent carapaces. They navigate by vibration, by the chemical signatures left in the oily residue, by the heat differential of the machines pulsing with dormant electricity. They know where the grease seeps. They know where the gears turn. They know hunger, and they know that the next meal is always the machine, and the machine is always hungry for them.

When the normal light filters down from the crystalline vents, or when arcs of electricity jump between corroded terminals, they do not cast shadows; they become light. Their glass-and-iron bodies catch the flat, mineral glare and throw it back in a thousand fractured directions, so that the chamber is filled not with darkness but with a shimmering, crystalline brightness that erases the boundary between living creature and dead machine. You can see only a few inches into the writhing mass before it becomes a solid wall of chitin and chrome, a breathing, shifting curtain that hungers, that grinds, that turns industry into biology and biology into the dust of the end of all things.

They are ancient. They have been here since before the machines were young, or perhaps they are the machines grown soft, grown hungry, grown into something that consumes its own creator. They are the world’s blind spot made manifest, the sealed cavity where the only ecology is a closed loop of consumption, where life needs no water, no plants, only the endless appetite for metal and the slow, grinding patience of geological time.
