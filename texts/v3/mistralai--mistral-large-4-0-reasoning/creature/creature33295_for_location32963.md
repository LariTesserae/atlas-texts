The inhabitants here do not breathe so much as *osmose*. They are layered by phase-state rather than by taxonomy, occupying the slippery territories between air and water, between flesh and mineral, between the living and the merely *animated*.

In the upper strata—the warm, pressurized hush itself—lives the **Atmospheric Choir**. These are the midges, but they have ceased to be individuals. They breed in the condensation that never falls, suspended in the humid dark like motes of living dust. When they move, they do not buzz; they *calculate*. Watch long enough, and you will see them align into perfect cubes, logarithmic spirals, Möbius strips of insectile geometry that hold their shape for minutes—sometimes hours—before dissolving back into cloud. Each midge is a neuron in a distributed cerebrum the size of a valley. They are thinking, slowly, in a wet, electrical language. When they settle on your skin, you feel not bites but a tickling pressure, a thousand tiny probes tasting the salt of your sweat, sampling your chemistry, adding you to their census. They are the cartographers of the dark, mapping the invisible currents of heat and moisture, and their hive-mind dreams in frequencies you feel in your teeth.

Upon the rocks, where the basalt is still sharp enough to cut, grows the **Lithotrophic Skin**. This is the pale, rubbery fungus—*viscidus basalticus*, if one were to name it, though it has no taxonomy that holds. It does not photosynthesize; it *digests*. It feeds on the industrial detritus, yes, but also on the industry itself—the slow, patient consumption of metal, oil, and plastic. Where it touches a pumping station, the metal becomes porous, then spongy, then mineral again. The fungus excretes crystalline rust that grows in angular formations identical to the volcanic glass beneath it. It is a slow alchemy, turning the skeletal remains of drilling rigs into geodes, the metal domes into geodes of a different kind. Beside it rise the **Translucent Stalks**—antennae of living glass, veined with fluid that pulses with the static electricity of the air. They sway without wind, sensing the current in the atmosphere, the low-frequency thrum of distant engines. They are lonely things, widely spaced, each one a solitary nerve ending reaching up from the saturated ground, feeling for signals in the dark.

In the shallow pools, where the water is thick with minerals and viscous as warm honey, swim the **Mnemosyne Fish**. They are blind, navigating by taste and pressure, but they are also *archivists*. A fish might solidify mid-swim into a shape resembling a gear, its scales becoming tempered steel for a moment—a snapshot, a memory of the machine-age that built this place—before liquefying again, swimming away as something else. They are the place's short-term memory, constantly shifting, constantly forgetting and remembering. They taste of copper and time. When they pass, they leave no wake, only a slight cooling of the water, a displacement of the viscous medium.

Draped over the obsidian shards, motionless as wet silk, are the **Gelatinous Ones**—amphibians that have abandoned pigmentation, existing as pale, translucent shapes, half-dissolved into the rock they rest upon. They are *gestating*. They have not yet decided what they will become. They might be the larval stage of the deep things, or they might be a separate lineage entirely, waiting for the basin to finish its geological youth before committing to a form. They breathe through their skin, which is porous as a sponge, drinking the humid air, excreting droplets that join the endless condensation cycle. They are the sleepers, the patient ones, dreaming in slow time.

But in the deeper water—the warm, mineral-thick depths where the land has not yet decided to be solid—move the **Negative Luminaries**. These are the large things, the ones whose passage you feel as a displacement of the humid air, a sudden coolness flowing past like a current. They are mostly quiet, but they are *heavy*. When they move, they leave trails of negative luminescence—a darkness so profound it stains the surrounding dark, visible only as an absence of the already-present black. They are the oldest inhabitants, predating the rock, perhaps predating the basin itself. They remember when this place was magma, when the water was steam. They are the anchors of the 2.7 reality, the reason the rules here are suggestions. They do not eat the fish or the midges; they simply *displace* them, moving through the water with a gravity that pulls the condensation toward them, creating micro-weather systems in their wake. They are the basin's heartbeat, slow and arrhythmic, a pulse felt in the sternum.

And then there are the **Rust-Children**, the crystallizing corrosion that grows on the machinery. It is alive, or alive enough. It forms in sharp, angular structures that mimic the young geology, consuming the metal from within. Over months, a pumping station does not collapse; it *metamorphoses*. The pistons become stalactites. The pipes become capillaries of mineral. The Rust-Children are the interface between the industrial and the geological, the slow workers converting the skeletal remains of drilling rigs into fossils for the next sedimentary layer. They are patient, crystalline, and they *remember* being ore.

Finally, there is the **Condensation Scribe**. The water that beads on the industrial structures does not merely fall; it *writes*. It forms maps of coastlines that do not exist, diagrams of machinery not yet built, handwriting in a language that dissolves as you trace it. This is an entity without form, a process rather than a creature, but it is *intentional*. It is the collective unconscious of the basin, the sum of all the midges' thoughts, the fish's memories, the deep things' dreams, transcribed into water patterns on cold metal. It is trying to communicate, or perhaps it is simply remembering a future that has not happened yet, writing it into the present so that the pumps might build it, so that the rust might grow it, so that the fish might swim through it.

There are no humans here. Or rather, there were, once—miners, engineers, surveyors—but they have been absorbed. Their calcium is in the fungal crusts. Their iron is in the rust-children. Their thoughts are in the midge-cubes, drifting in the humid dark, calculating, remembering, slowly becoming part of the saturated, warm, patient dark that presses against the inside of your eyelids and tastes of iron and the deep, pre-rock past.
