The primary inhabitants are the **Lithovores**—not a species but a singular, distributed organism that occupies the entirety of the necropolis. They are the walls, the vault, the floor beneath your feet. Each "plant" is a column of lignin fused with silica, a material neither wood nor stone but something that exists in the interval between them, grown cell by cell from the bedrock itself. They do not photosynthesize; they chemosynthesize, drawing energy from the slow redox of sulfur and iron within the stone, secreting acids that etch microscopic channels into the granite, digesting it, and reconstituting it as their own expanding tissue. To touch them is to touch a river stone that has been in the current for ten million years—smooth, glassy, impossibly cold—but with a grain that runs like corduroy, fibrous and wrong, hinting at the organic violence of their growth.

They think in geological time. A single Lithovore might take ten thousand years to widen a chamber by the width of a hand, yet they are aware. The thrum you feel against your skin—that tectonic drone, too slow for a heartbeat, too steady for wind—is their collective voice, the sound of trillions of cellular pumps dissolving mineral, of lignin expanding against ancient mortar, of the slow, grinding pressure of biomass against the architecture of the dead. At moments, the resonance resolves into patterns: not language, but mathematics. Counting. The Lithovores are tallying the cubic meters of stone they have consumed, communicating across the vast dark through pressure waves in their shared matrix, a network of mineralized roots that spans the entire buried city. They are not hostile. They are simply *hungry*, and the city provides.

Deep in the absolute blackness, beyond the limits of the chamber, there exists perhaps one **animal**—though the distinction between kingdoms has dissolved here as thoroughly as the boundary between the grown and the built. It is sessile, anchored to some distant wall, half-plant and half-animal in the way that only life in this dry, lightless world can be. It does not move. It does not need to. It filters the mineral dust from the viscous air, adding its infinitesimal pulse to the great chord, a single tremolo in the Lithovores' basso continuo. It may be a relict, a survivor from before the plants claimed the settlement, or it may be an extension of them, a fruiting body of the greater organism. It is alone, and it is patient, and it contributes almost nothing to the sound except the suggestion that something still *waits*.

Between the macro and the micro, there are the **Chemotrophs**—colonies of bacteria and archaea living in the pores of the wall, in the thin film of sulfuric residue where the Lithovores secrete their acids. They are the true engineers, the invisible workforce that performs the actual dissolution of stone, handing the liquefied mineral up to the plant-cells above. They exist in a state of suspended animation most of the time, waking only when the temperature shifts by fractions of a degree or when the thrumming reaches certain frequencies. They are the oldest inhabitants, predating even the plants, perhaps predating the builders, having waited in the dark for chemistry to wake them.

And then there are the **Ghosts**—not spirits, but impressions. The Lithovores have not merely grown around the flint tools and bone hafts; they have *absorbed* them. The cordage has mineralized into the wall, the doorway is half-digested, the steps have been smoothed to ramps by the slow erosion of expanding tissue. The plants remember the shape of human habitation in their geometry, in the specific hollows that once held sleeping bodies, in the niches worn flush by hands that no longer exist. They are not haunted by the dead, but they are *sculpted* by them, the architecture of the settlement now serving as the template for the Lithovores' slow expansion. The inhabitants of this place are the living stone and the memory of the builders, fused into a single, breathing, resonant entity that grows, millimeter by millennium, in the absolute dark, forever.
