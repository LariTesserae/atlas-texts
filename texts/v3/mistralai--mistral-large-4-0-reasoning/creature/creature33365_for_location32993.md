The inhabitants are creatures of pressure and patience, their bodies sculpted by twelve thousand years of thin air and harder stone. They are not tall—the altitude has seen to that, compressing the spine, broadening the thorax into a barrel that holds the scarce oxygen in longer, shallower measures. A man standing at rest breathes visibly, his chest rising and falling with a rhythm as deliberate as the dripping water that sustains him. Their skin carries the pale, mineral pallor of those who see the sun reflected from rock rather than absorbed by soil; it is weathered not by sunburn but by the constant, dry abrasion of wind-borne grit. They move through the vertical city with a three-dimensional grace, hands and feet finding purchase on surfaces that would seem sheer to a lowlander, their weight distributed across four points of contact like the goats they share their ledges with.

They wear the architecture as garment. Layers of felted fiber—spun from the wool of the shaggy cliff-goats and the undercoat of the whistlers when they are culled—are wrapped tight against the cold, over togas of cured hide, over trousers of woven grass-fiber reinforced with strips of leather at the knee and shin where the stone abrades. Their feet are shod in soles of layered felt and raw-hide, soft-soled to preserve the silence that is this place's most precious resource. No iron touches their bodies; instead, they carry tools of bone and antler and meteoric stone—chisels of whistler thigh-bone, scrapers of flint nodule, hammers of the rare, dark iron from fallen stars, hafted with sinew and resin. When they work, the sound is the percussion of stone on stone, a dry, patient tapping that echoes in the narrow shoulder-width alleys.

Their voices have adapted to the medium they must travel through. In the thin air, speech loses its resonance, becoming clear but hollow, as if spoken through water or heard from a great distance. They speak softly, close to the ear, conserving breath. When a shout is necessary—across a courtyard, down a vertical shaft, to warn of a rockfall—the sound carries strangely, audible at distances that would be impossible in denser atmospheres, yet lacking the body that would make it intimate. They have learned to read the subtleties of a glance, the tilt of a shoulder, the placement of a hand on a wall, for words are expensive here and silence is a form of currency.

They are builders by necessity and by nature. Their hands know the weight of a block not by measurement but by the shadow it casts in the high, sharp light. They work in collectives—never alone—using wooden levers and leather thongs to shift stones that no single pair of arms could move. A wall rises by the height of a hand in a year, and so they think in generations. A man is defined not by what he owns but by which stones he has placed, which cistern he has hollowed from the living rock, which condensation apron he has carved to catch the night's breath. They are masons, yes, but also hydrologists and meteorologists, reading the private weather of the plateau, anticipating which district will cloud and which will remain dry, directing the flow of moisture through the city's arteries of channel and cistern.

Their relationship to water is sacramental. They are the drinkers of the sky, their bodies tuned to the rhythm of condensation and sublimation. In the high, cold districts, they scrape hoarfrost from north-facing walls with bone blades, gathering the ice that forms in intricate, feathered patterns, storing it in vessels where it slowly melts. In the lower, warmer levels—where the press of bodies and the banking of fires create pockets of relative heat—they tend stone basins of liquid water, dark and still, reflecting the tallow lamps that burn the rendered fat of the animals they husband. They know the exact moment when a drip forms, the precise temperature at which vapor becomes liquid becomes ice. To waste a drop is theft; to let condensation run uncollected across a roof is to rob the future. Their bodies are efficient, conserving moisture, producing little sweat, their urine pale and precious, their breath visible in the cold as they climb from level to level.

They are not numerous. The city supports only what it can feed with lichen and the meat of whistlers and goats, with the slow accumulation of fat and the hoarded water. Their numbers are dense in space but sparse in time—they do not rush. A conversation may last an hour. A meal, laid out on a flat stone in a courtyard where one can touch three walls at once, may take the afternoon, eaten in silence or in the low murmur of voices that the thin air carries without echo. They sleep in niches carved into the rock, curled around the banked coals of small fires, sharing warmth with the animals who press against them—whistlers in the walls, finches in the eaves, goats in the lower chambers where the heat collects.

But they are not alone. The city is a parliament of species. The whistlers—pale, quick rodents with eyes like dark beads—are citizens of the masonry itself, nesting in the dry-stone gaps, emerging at dusk to forage the orange and sulphur lichens that colonize the walls in slow, expanding rings. They are not pets; they are neighbors, their presence as expected as the dripping water, their alarm calls when a stone shifts serving as the city's nervous system. The snow-finches, white-breasted and sharp-beaked, occupy every cornice, every carved channel, every window ledge, their songs dropping down through the vertical streets like the pebbles they resemble. They are the messengers of the upper city, descending to the lower levels only in the brief, mild season, their droppings a blessing of nitrogen that feeds the coarse moss growing in the damp crevices near the cisterns.

And then there are the goats—shaggy, sure-footed, goat-like only in their resilience, for they have evolved or been bred into something specific to this place. They climb the masonry with a familiarity that suggests they were here before the humans, or perhaps co-evolved with them. Their hooves click on stone with a percussion that harmonizes with the human tapping of tools. They graze the lichen from the upper walls, their teeth scraping the mineral bloom, and they shelter in the lower levels during the long storms, their bodies radiating heat into the stone chambers, their breath adding to the moisture that condenses on the cold walls. They are milked, their wool spun, their meat rendered for fat, but they are also companions in the vertical dark, their eyes reflecting the lamplight, their presence a reminder that the boundary between the built and the wild has dissolved into a single, crowded habitat.

There are other inhabitants, less corporeal. The wind itself lives here—not howling, for there is not enough air for such violence, but murmuring, a persistent low-frequency breath that moves through the canyons of the city, finding its way through every crack and crevice, carrying the scent of distant seas and the cold of the heights. It is a resident that shapes the architecture, that dictates which walls face which direction, that scours the stone into new forms over decades. The condensation is an inhabitant, moving through the city in its slow cycle—entering through the high aprons, circulating through cisterns and bodies and fires, exiting as vapor through the high, cold vents. The silence is a presence, a resource to be guarded, broken only by the creak of a wooden shutter in the cold, the scrape of a stone tool, the occasional shout that dies quickly in the thin atmosphere.

Together, they form a single organism. The human, the whistler, the finch, the goat, the lichen, the moss, the dripping water, the breathing wind—they are layers of the same life, strata in the vertical city. They breathe together, the thin air passing through lung and gill and lichen-thallus alike. They move through the same honeycombed passages, the crawlspaces and chambers and cantilevered ladders. They share the same slow time, the same patient accumulation, the same metabolism of stone and cold and sky. They are a civilization clinging to the sharp bones of a young world, drinking the air, listening to the silence, growing upward into the thin, clear light, layer by layer, hand by hand, drop by drop, until the city and its inhabitants become indistinguishable from the rock itself—mineral and fiber and bone, a dense, slow, quiet life etched into the fist of the earth.
