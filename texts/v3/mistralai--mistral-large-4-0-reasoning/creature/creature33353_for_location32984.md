They are the tenants of a finished world, holding on against the roar.

The foundation is the patina. It is not dead mineral, but a crust of extremophile archaea and bacteria that have learned to eat stone. They are chemolithoautotrophs, drawing energy from the oxidation of sulfur and iron within the limestone, powered by the same unfiltered sun that bleaches the sky. They do not use chlorophyll—there is no green here—but pigments of ochre, rust, and pale gray, staining the white tufa in vertical streaks like slow tears. They form a living rind, perhaps a millimeter thick, that is the only primary production this place knows. When the mist condenses on them in the cool hours before dawn, they swell, briefly metabolize, reproduce in a torpid bloom, and excrete the very calcium that builds the towers. They are the grass, the forest, the soil. Without them, the stone would simply erode; with them, it grows, impossibly, against the wind.

Above the patina cling the **Sifters**. They are the mica-winged insects, and they are built for a world that is all current. They do not fly in the way of lowland things; flight is too dangerous here, too profligate of energy against the gale. Instead, they are anchored. They possess tarsi that lock into the honeycombed pits of the limestone, crystalline claws that grip with the tenacity of a gecko but harder, mineralized. Their wings are not membranes but plates of transparent silica, sheets of mica, that catch the savage light and throw it back in sharp, cutting beams. They spread these wings perpendicular to the wind and simply *filter*. The gale brings them their food: spores from the patina drifting on thermal currents, dust motes carrying the carbon of dead things from distant valleys, the occasional desiccated corpse of a Sifter from another tower, tumbling past like a leaf. They sieve this from the screaming air, their wings humming at the resonant frequency of the constricted throat, turning the roar into a susurrus of nutrition. They are pale, translucent, the color of the washed-out sky, nearly invisible against the brightness. They live and die within a hand's width of the same patch of stone, never moving, never landing, their bodies slowly becoming part of the architecture, their wings vibrating in the eternal song until the vibration matches their own flesh.

The **Lithoderms** are the hunters. They are lizard-like, but only in the way that a stone is like a bone. Their scales are not keratin but calcium carbonate, grown slowly over decades, plates of armor that are the color of the tufa—white, gray, streaked with ochre. They are ectotherms, but they do not bask; the sun here is too fierce, it would cook them from within. Instead, they press their bellies into the leeward hollows of the towers, absorbing the heat stored in the stone from the day's relentless light. They drink by licking the condensation that beads on their own backs, their tongues rasping, collecting the mineral water that is their only hydration. Their eyes are multifaceted, black as basalt, seeing in ultraviolet and polarized light, able to track the glint of a Sifter's mica wings against the white stone even in the blinding glare. They are deaf to the roar—the wind has long since shattered any drum they might have possessed—but they feel. The towers are tuning forks; when a Sifter struggles against the gale, the vibration travels through the stone, and the Lithoderm feels it in its sternum, a deep thrum that says *prey*. Then it strikes. Not fast—speed is a luxury in a place where the wind can strip flesh from bone—but sudden. It launches itself against the current, a muscle contraction built up over hours, a spring released. It catches the Sifter in jaws lined with crystalline teeth, and returns to its hollow, to digest in the shadow, to wait again.

In the milky water that fills the gaps between the towers swim the **Tufa-Drakes**. They are amphibious, salamander-shaped, but their skin is encrusted with the same minerals they swim in, turning them into living concretions, mobile towers of flesh and stone. They graze. Their mouths are scraping plates, rasping the bacterial patina from the submerged bases of the spires. They are the only creatures that can cross the open water, the only ones that connect the dense thickets, carrying spores and microorganisms on their calcified hides from colony to colony. They surface to breathe—gulping the thin air, exhaling mist—and then sink again, floating just below the surface like pale, drifting islands, their eyes—lidless, white, seeing only shapes and shadows—watching the sky for the shadow of a bird that never comes. They are ancient, slow, and nearly blind, navigating by the taste of the water, by mineral gradients, by the feel of the current that moves through the stone forest.

And above them all, living in the gale itself, are the **Aether-Colonies**. They are not individuals but communities, chains of thousands of zooids linked together like a living rope, drifting in the upper currents where the wind is strongest and cleanest. They never touch the ground. They are the color of the mist, gray-white, translucent, feeding on the same airborne particles as the Sifters, but nomadic, carried by the invariant wind from tower to tower, from plateau to plateau. They breed in the condensation, when the mist is thickest, releasing spores that ride the gale to new heights. They are the only ones who see the whole, the vertiginous openness, the sea of stone extending past the horizon. They are the wind made flesh, temporary and eternal, living in the breath of the world, waiting for the day the wind might change, which it never does.

Mostly, though, the place is empty. The wind is the inhabitant, the stone is the inhabitant, the light is the inhabitant. These few—the patina, the Sifters, the Lithoderms, the Tufa-Drakes, the Aether-Colonies—are merely the punctuation in a sentence written in geology, holding on, surviving, part of the equilibrium, part of the roar.
