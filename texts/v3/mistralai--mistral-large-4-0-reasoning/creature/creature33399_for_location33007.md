They do not walk here. They do not breathe in the way you understand breathing, though the water breathes for them, rising and falling in its tidal rhythm that mimics respiration. They are the drowned, but drowned is the wrong word—there was no death, only a surrender to density. They exist as topographical memories held in the warm tea-colored fluid, their bodies not preserved but *translated* into the water’s substance. When you wade through the chest-high flood, you feel them parting around your legs: solid ridges, cold as grave-mud, the shapes of swimmers who passed through this valley millennia ago and never left. They are not ghosts; they are the water’s immune response to time, calcified outlines that surface in the laminar currents, swim alongside you for a moment, then sink back into the thermal layers below, leaving only a shiver in your spine and the faint, mineral taste of limestone on your tongue.

Then there are the watchers in the mist.

The vapor is not weather; it is a collective nervous system, a distributed consciousness that has no center but watches from everywhere. It condenses on the bone-white architecture in patterns that suggest faces—hollow-eyed, patient, made of droplets that hang suspended for hours before falling. When you move through the plazas, the mist parts to form corridors, then reforms behind you into hands, into staircases, into the suggestion of a crowd that is not there. They are the city’s thoughts made visible, the geometry of absence given temporary coherence. They do not speak, but they murmur: the echoes of footsteps that were never taken, conversations in languages that have not yet evolved or have long since fossilized. They are the inhabitants of the negative space, the sculpture that remains when everything else has been carved away.

The architecture itself is alive, though its metabolism is geological.

The buildings—these vast, smooth formations that rise like calcified vertebrae—are not structures but organisms in a state of permanent, slow dreaming. They do not move, yet they shift. The erosion is not decay but a form of growth, a dissolution that feeds a reconstruction elsewhere. In certain alcoves, when you are not looking, the stone softens into warm wax, flows, and re-lithifies into new configurations: a staircase that becomes a pillar, a courtyard that folds inward to become a throat. The city dreams in mineral time, and its dreams are the non-Euclidean corridors that deposit you in districts that should not connect, the gravity pools where water becomes syrup, the terraces that rise as you descend. It is a lithic slumber, a consciousness distributed through calcium carbonate and silicate, patient and terrible, existing in a state of perfect, slow equilibrium with its own dissolution.

But the most populous inhabitants are invisible, or rather, they are the *quality* of visibility itself.

They are the reflections.

In the stagnant water, you see not the dark ceiling above, but a sun-bleached metropolis—dry, bright, filled with the echo of voices and the movement of bodies that cast no shadows. These are the parallel citizens, the inhabitants of the city that was, or the city that will be, bleeding through the thin membrane of reality. When you raise your hand, the water shows you raising a different hand, or a hand that belongs to someone standing behind you in the dry city, someone who is also watching, also wading, also alone. They are the true residents, perhaps, or perhaps you are the intruder in their reflection. The boundary between the flooded world and the dry one is porous; they trade places in the mirror, and sometimes—when the thermal waves align just so—you can see them swimming through your world, pale and weightless, their mouths open in silent screams that make no sound because here, sound is a different element.

And there is the heat.

Not as temperature, but as a presence. The thermal oppression is a metabolism, the planet’s fever dream given form. It comes in waves, pockets of scalding air that burn the lungs, alternating with drafts of preternatural cold that make the water steam and hiss. These thermal gradients are the city’s circulatory system, driving the exchange of minerals, the slow convection that keeps the water alive. In the pockets of gravity where the water is heavy as syrup, the heat pools; where you float suspended, unable to swim downward, the cold gathers in the hollows of your body. The heat is a kind of inhabitant—an elemental one, ancient and indifferent, that has cooked the biology out of this valley and replaced it with something else, something that does not need lungs or light or the distinction between solid and liquid.

The shrubs—the pale, leafless things clinging to the higher terraces—are neither plant nor mineral but something in the desperate negotiation between the two. They are the city’s last biological experiments, mineral accretions that mimic the form of coral or the ossified nerves of something that once tried to grow here. They do not photosynthesize; they chemosynthesize, perhaps, or they simply exist as the architecture’s way of remembering that life was once possible. They are sparse, lonely, the final witnesses to a biology that has otherwise surrendered to the stone.

And finally, there is the silence.

It is not empty. It is a substance, a pressure, an inhabitant in its own right. The necropolis of silence is filled with the geometry of absence—the shapes of conversations that never happened, the architectural echoes of footsteps in plazas where no one walks. It hums at a subsonic frequency, the creak of thermal expansion, the drip of water from cavern ceilings, the low thrum of the earth’s own slow pulse. This silence is the dominant species here, the substrate upon which all other life is written. It watches. It waits. It is the space between the water’s breaths, the pause between the mist’s formations, the moment when the stone softens and before it hardens again.

They live here: the water-borne dead, the mist-collective, the dreaming stone, the reflection-citizens, the thermal gradients, the mineral flora, and the silence itself. They do not age, for time moves unevenly here—a minute in one courtyard is an hour in another, or perhaps no time at all. They do not eat, for the water feeds on the dissolving rock, and the rock feeds on the heat, and the heat feeds on the bedrock’s ancient pulse. They are the perfect inhabitants of a place where the distinction between built and natural has dissolved, where water is architect and sole inhabitant, where existence is a matter of density and memory, and where to live is to be slowly, imperceptibly, rebuilt while you rot.
