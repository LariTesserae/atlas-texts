First, there is the **Crust**—that thin, crusty membrane of lichen and extremophile moss that skins the volcanic glass like a scab of pale green-grey. They are the pioneers, the original inhabitants, older than memory in this place. They do not merely grow; they *negotiate*. They secrete acids that slowly etch the basalt into something approaching soil, grain by grain, molecule by molecule. They drink from the breath of the wind, harvesting condensation that clings to their filamentous bodies, and they taste the mineral dust of the young mountains, metabolizing the iron and sulfur that bleeds from the sharp black stone. Between them, in the microscopic spaces, lives a nervous system of fungal hyphae—a Wood-Wide-Web scaled to the planetary—that carries chemical whispers from Colossus to Colossus, coordinating the slow, dragging migrations of the giants across the ridge.

The **Colossi themselves** are not merely habitat; they are the primary citizens, the slow gods of this tectonic realm. They are ancient beyond the mountains, these titans, and they remember the warmth of lower elevations, the richness of wetter soils. Now they exist in a state of perpetual, glacial pilgrimage. Their holdfasts—massive, root-like structures that grip the glass with the strength of geology—creep across the stone by millimeters each year, dragging the multi-kilometer bulk of their woody bodies toward seeps and condensation flows they sense through the crust-network. When they move, the sound is tectonic: a groaning, creaking of cellular expansion, the audible stretching of xylem and phloem the size of cathedrals. Inside them, the world is warm and green-lit. The hollow chambers are not empty; they are *inhabited* by symbiotic colonies of fungi and chemosynthetic bacteria that pulse with bioluminescence—faint, viridian lanterns that breathe in circadian rhythms. These symbionts are the Colossi’s nervous system, their thoughts made light, communicating through chemical signals that ripple through the fungal mats lining the inner walls. The Colossi think slowly. A decision—to shift left toward a moisture seep, to bud a new chamber upward—takes years, decades, centuries.

Within the woody flesh of these giants live the **Deep Dwellers**. Borers, pale and eyeless, tunnel through the pale tissue, leaving galleries that become highways for air and water. Pollinators—winged things like moths but vast, with wingspans of meters—drift through the vertical shafts, carrying spores between Colossi, fertilizing the slow reproduction of the giants. And in the vast reservoirs within the hollow hearts—pools of melted condensation, black and still—there swim creatures that have never known sunlight: blind, pale fish or eel-like things, perhaps descended from ancient rainwater fauna, filter-feeding on the organic detritus that drifts down from above, living in absolute darkness save for the green glow that filters through the translucent walls of their aquatic cathedral.

On the exterior flanks, in the vertical cities of bark and cork, migrate the **Six-Legged**. They are herds—thousands upon thousands—of furred grazers that exist somewhere between yak and insect, their bodies low and broad, their six legs ending in hooves that click and spark against the sharp basalt. They are the movers of the middle world, migrating along the lower flanks of the Colossi in vast, thundering columns, following the slow growth of the lichen-crust or perhaps the chemical signals of the Colossi themselves, which may offer them shelter or sustenance in exchange for their passage. Their fur is thick, matted with ice crystals that form in the perpetual cold, and their bellowing calls are part of the planetary drone, low and resonant, vibrating in the chest of anyone who stands in the empty valleys below.

Above them, in the warm, glowing chambers near the mid-levels, nest the **Gliders**. They are predators, yes, but also messengers. Membranes of skin stretched between elongated limbs—like bats, but vast, with wingspans that blot out the dim sky—they launch themselves from the thermal vents of the Colossi’s internal heat, diving between the spires on currents of rising air. They hunt the smaller, quicker creatures that live in the lichen-crust, or perhaps they hunt the Six-Legged when the herds grow too bold. Their nests are woven from the fibers of the Colossi’s own shed bark, lined with the bioluminescent moss they harvest from the inner walls, glowing with captured light. When they shriek, it is a high, keening sound that cuts through the wind’s wail, a sound of pursuit and hunger in the vertical dark.

But there are others. The **Stone-and-Fiber Folk**.

They are the sentient inhabitants, the current architects of the waymarks. They are small—perhaps no taller than the knee of a Six-Legged grazer—adapted to the thin air with broad chests and blood rich in hemoglobin, skin weathered to the color of the lichen-crust. They live in the lower hollows of the Colossi, where the giants’ corky rinds create sheltered alcoves warmed by the trapped heat of the dim sun and the internal glow of the fungal lanterns. Their technology is primitive but profound: stone tools flaked from the basaltic glass itself, bone needles for sewing the thick furs of the grazers they hunt or herd, fire-drills that catch the sparse tinder of dried moss.

Their great works are the **rope bridges**—thick cables of braided vine, some as wide as a man’s waist, stretching between Colossi across chasms of ten kilometers or more. To cross one is an act of faith and terror, a swaying journey through the screaming wind, high above the sharp stone, suspended between two living mountains that groan and shift beneath your feet. The Folk traverse these bridges to trade, to mate, to follow the migrating herds, or to reach the petroglyphs they peck into the glassy rock faces—spirals and zig-zags, crude outlines of the Colossi themselves. These are not art for art’s sake; they are *maps of motion*, accounting for the slow drift of the giants, marking safe paths, water sources, the locations of the bioluminescent chambers that can be harvested for light and warmth.

The Folk have a religion, or perhaps a science, centered on the **Resonance**. They believe—know—that the Colossi are aware, that the drone of the wind through the hollow trunks is a language. They place their shelters—platforms of woven plant-fiber and lashed bone—in the acoustic sweet spots where the bass notes of the Colossi’s song vibrate through the stone and into their bones. They harvest the condensation that drips from the inner channels, collecting it in vessels made from the hollowed bones of the Six-Legged. They climb the Colossi—not to the needle-point tips that vanish into the bruised sky, but to the middle hollows—where they tend the fungal gardens, perhaps guiding their growth, harvesting the bioluminescent colonies for light, trading with the Gliders for their shed membranes, which make excellent waterproofing for their shelters.

They are a people of verticality, of isolation and connection. Each band of Folk might live their entire lives on a single Colossus, never crossing to another, knowing the others only by the beating frequencies of the drone that travels across the empty valleys, by the faint green glow that pulses on the horizon like a heartbeat in the dark. They are the Keepers of the Waymarks, the Listeners of the Wind, the gardeners of giants who move so slowly that a human lifetime is but a blink in the eye of a Colossus.

And so the place is peopled: by the patient crust that eats stone; by the titans that walk; by the borers and swimmers within; by the herds that thunder across the flanks; by the winged hunters that dive through the twilight; and by the small, fierce Folk who weave their bridges between the stars of green light, singing back to the mountains in a voice of stone, fiber, and bone.
