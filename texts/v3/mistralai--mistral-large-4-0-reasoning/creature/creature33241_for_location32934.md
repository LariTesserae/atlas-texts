They are not separate from the architecture, because the architecture *is* them—but if you stand still long enough for the vertigo to pass, if you let the deafening industry of growth blur into something like a crowd, you will begin to see the citizens. They are the **Grown**, the **Verdant**, and they are many and one, a civilization of cellulose and sap that has never known the separation between self and city.

At the lowest stratum, where the root-mat sucks at your boots with a wet *schlick*, dwell the **Matriarchs of the Substrate**. They are not individuals but *pressures*—vast, spongy intelligences distributed across the interwoven tissue that carpets the young earth. They remember the violence of the soil’s birth; they feel the sleeping faults shift beneath them as a kind of indigestion. They communicate in slow, chemical pulses, releasing hormones that make the ground spring back with hydraulic force or open into fresh sinkholes to swallow the unwary. To walk here is to be *read*—the Matriarchs tasting your weight, deciding whether you are nutrient or obstruction. They are the foundation, the memory, the slow, dreaming thought beneath your feet.

Above them, in the fungal-sponge strata of the understory, sit the **Myco-Parliament**. These are the calcified husks of titanic woody fungi—hollowed megastructures that form the plazas and aqueducts, their “stone” walls of compressed cellulose and peat sweating sweet, resinous liquor. They are the digestive conscience of the city. They speak only in the language of decay, secreting enzymes that break down the dead districts into mulch, regurgitating them as new growth within the hour. They are patient, ancient in a land that is geologically newborn, and they keep the records of the city’s cannibalistic feasts in rings of lignified tissue. When they move—which is rare, and happens only when a district collapses—it is with the sound of wet tearing, of compost shifting in its sleep. They are the historians, the recyclers, the ones who ensure that nothing is wasted, that death is merely a change of address.

Then there are the **Vascular Nomads**, who have no fixed form. They are the sap itself—the amber torrents that roar through the open-air conduits like rivers, carrying nutrients and memories from the root to the emergent tower. They experience the city as a one-dimensional flow, a perpetual rushing, never stopping, always pulled upward by impossible pressure through straws the size of your arm. They are the messengers, the lovers, the thieves. They carry the chemical signatures of the dead districts to the growing tips, whispering news of collapse and opportunity. To touch the side of an aqueduct is to feel their pulse—a hydraulic *hiss* that pops and sighs in rhythm with the forest’s heartbeat. They are fast, too fast to follow with the eye, and they live their entire lives in the span of a single surge from root to canopy.

In the canopy itself, where the light is filtered through a density of living matter so absolute that the sky is blotted out, exists the **Grinding Choir**. These are the millions of leaves, packed so densely that they rub against one another in a perpetual, dry ocean of chlorophyll. They are not passive; they are the crowd, the industry, the screaming. Each leaf is a node of photosynthetic awareness, participating in a deafening storm of friction, a *scream* of cellulose that never ceases. They are the workers, the background noise, the ones who generate the subsonic *thrum* felt in the sternum. They have no eyes, but they feel the touch of condensation, the weight of spores settling upon them, and they respond by growing, by expanding, by adding centimeter upon centimeter to their walls in the span of a heartbeat.

But the most alien inhabitants are the **Condensation Kin**. They live in the patchy gravity of the plazas, where fat silver droplets hang suspended in the air, dripping *upward* into the waiting stomata of overhead leaves. They are colonies of extremophile flora—perhaps algae, perhaps fungal spores that have learned to metabolize moisture from the air itself—existing in a state between liquid and life. They drink the sweet resinous liquor that weeps from the walls, and they thrive in the saturated humidity, blooming in the spaces where reality frays. They are small, ephemeral, and numerous, and they experience the city as a series of suspended moments, gravity-defying, hanging in the warm, wet air like living jewelry.

Highest of all, piercing the cloud line, stand the **Lignarchs**—the emergent towers, the trees that have been coaxed by chemical gradients and the sheer pressure of overgrowth into occupying geometric space. They are the government, the law, the gravity that holds the city together or unravels it, for gravity here is optional. They are slow, their thoughts taking hours to form, a deliberation that matches the expansion of their cell walls. They dream the megastructures into being, their bark knitting closed across windows in the time it takes you to blink, their roots delving into the breathing wounds of the earth. They are the architects, but they build without tools, shaping space through the sheer force of their dreaming. They are old in a young land, having grown in days what takes forests millennia, and they are terribly, beautifully lonely—each one a silent monarch in a court of green, screaming with the sound of its own impossible, accelerating life.

And then there are the **Spore-Folk**—the most transient citizens of all. They are the explosive clouds of golden and green particulate that flash across the valley like green snow. They are not permanent; they are weather. For minutes at a time, they solidify into temporary bridges, temporary walls, temporary weather, deafening in their release. They are the artists, the rebels, the ones who build and dissolve in the same breath. They live fast, reproduce faster, and when they settle, they become the root-mat, or they are drunk by the thirsty architecture, or they are eaten by the Lignarchs, their nutrients racing up the vascular systems of their neighbors to fuel a new spire three streets away.

They have no tools, because they *are* the tools. They have no fire, no metal, no stone shaped by hands. Their technology is **growth**. Their economy is the closed loop of verdant cannibalism—death is currency, compost is wealth, and the city consumes itself in a metabolism without mouths. They communicate through chemical signals, through the hydraulic hiss of xylem, through the subsonic resonance of biomass so vast it generates its own weather. They are a pure monoculture, too young to have invited colonizers, or perhaps actively hostile, emitting frequencies that make the flesh of animals curdle. They are the only inhabitants, and they are everywhere, a town-sized organism, a valley-sized plant, an urban ecology without precedent.

To live here is to be part of the metamorphosis. The inhabitants do not age; they **become**. A Lignarch might achieve its full height at dawn, and by noon be a heap of compost, its nutrients already racing through the sap to fuel a new tower. The Spore-Folk might build a bridge at sunrise and dissolve into the humid haze by sunset. The Matriarchs shift and swallow, the Myco-Parliament digests, the Vascular Nomads rush, and the Condensation Kin hang suspended, dripping upward into the sky.

They are not people, not in any sense you would recognize. They are the green, screaming, turbulent consciousness of a place where the distinction between the natural and the constructed has dissolved entirely—where the city is the forest, the forest is the city, and both are growing faster than reality can keep up, layer upon layer, in a deafening, verdant roar. They are the dreamers of the Verdant Maw, and the Maw is their dream.
