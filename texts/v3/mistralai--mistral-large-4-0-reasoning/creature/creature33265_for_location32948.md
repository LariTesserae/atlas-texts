First, the Lithovores. They are the only constants in a place of flux, yet even they are never still. Twelve massive specimens stand scattered across the gypsum crust, rising thirty feet into the dim, lavender twilight, their "trunks" spiraling columns of compressed mineral salts that layer themselves in visible increments—a sound like stone creaking against stone as they grow, adding rings of silica and gypsum the way a tree adds wood, only here the wood is mineral, deposited by the steam vents lacing the bedrock below. Their "leaves" are vast, translucent membranes, thin and taut with pressurized sulfur gas, fluttering and chiming in the wind that never stops. They pulse with an inner bioluminescence, a slow systole and diastole of light shifting from deep amber to sickly violet, a heartbeat of chemistry without blood. They do not drink; they breathe the steam, they eat the dust, they photosynthesize through the mineral-heavy air, harvesting energy from the geothermal heat trapped beneath the earth. They are the architecture of the Singing Grove, the only inhabitants old enough to remember when this basin was a seabed, and they sing—the wind screaming through their gas-filled fronds creates that roaring chord, part organ pipe, part didgeridoo, a bass line that vibrates in the teeth of everything that lives.

Around them swirls the Iridescence. Swarms of hexapods, their carapaces of iridescent chitin refracting the dim light into shifting rainbows, cloud the spaces between the giants. They are the nervous system of the grove, hyperactive, a turbulence of life that never sleeps. They click and buzz and stridulate in deafening waves, a chorus that rises and falls like surf against the shore of sound. They feed on the aerial plankton drifting on thermal currents, on the spores released when a frond bursts with a sound like thunder, on each other in the storm-state of consumption. They are the smallest inhabitants, the most numerous, existing in a state of perpetual, jittery alertness. When the dust devils spin up, screaming columns of salt and silica that obscure the sky, the swarms do not flee; they ride the thermals, scattering and reforming, a living mist that obscures the vision and fills the air with their crackling, buzzing presence.

Weaving between the swarms, stalking the bone-white salt flats with chaotic, urgent movements, are the Leapers. Perhaps reptilian, perhaps avian, perhaps something that has never had a name in any earthly tongue—they are beings with too many joints, with wings that beat in syncopated rhythms against the thick, heat-trapped air. They hunt the swarms, they hunt each other, they feed on the hyperactive abundance of the grove. The gravity here is lighter, or the dense air cushions every fall, and so they bound in impossible arcs, launching from the crunching gypsum crust to alight on a lithovore's spiral trunk, then leaping to a frond thirty feet above, then diving back down to the drifting dust. They are the sharks of this non-aqueous sea, streamlined by evolution for the turbulence, their movements a blur of jointed limbs and beating membranes, existing in a state of constant, frantic motion.

But the true inhabitants, the ones who have raised platforms of woven mineral-fiber on stilts of knapped flint to escape the drifting dust, who have built huts of stacked salt-blocks sealed with primitive mortar of volcanic ash and tree-resin, who have smoothed stone circles across generations of use—they are the Singers. They are people, or something close enough to be called that, though they have adapted to a world where water is a myth and the rules of carbon and oxygen have been rewritten in sulfur and silicon.

They are tall and slight, built for the low gravity and the thick, feverish air. Their limbs are long, willowy, ending in hands that are more dexterous than human, suited for gripping the spiraling salt-columns of the lithovores. Their skin is covered in a fine, mineral dust that shimmers like the chitin of the hexapods, giving them a pearlescent sheen in the perpetual dim twilight. Their hair stands permanently on end, crackling with static electricity, tiny sparks jumping between their strands and the crystalline trees in silent, branching forks of light. Their eyes have adapted to the shifted spectrum; they see in infrared, perceiving the heat-shimmer of the world, the violet bioluminescence of the lithovores as shifting hues of warmth, the reds of living creatures visible only as thermal ghosts against the cool stone. They do not sleep, not as water-based life understands it. The ecosystem is a storm-state, frantic, nothing rests, and so they live in shifts, or in trances, or in a constant state of half-waking, their nervous systems adapted to the deafening chorus of wind and insect.

They move with the agility of the fauna, climbing the lithovores with tools of bone and flint—sharp scrapers, cutting edges—harvesting the gas-membranes for their flammable, hallucinogenic vapor. They store this vapor in dried bladders, perhaps taken from the creatures they hunt, perhaps cultivated from the lithovores themselves, and they carry these bladders with them, inflated, glowing faintly with the captured bioluminescence of the gas within. The vapor is their sacrament, their sight, their language. They inhale it—the scent of copper and ozone thick in the air—and their perception shifts, aligning with the wrong colors of the world, allowing them to see the infrared spectrum, to track the heat-signatures of prey and predator alike in the dim light.

Their technology is early, pre-metal, but sophisticated in its adaptation to the dryness. They weave cordage from mineral fibers, stronger than any plant fiber, resistant to the abrasive wind that never stops. They knap flint into sharp tools, using them to cut the tough gas-membranes without bursting them, to carve containers from the hollowed stalks of dead lithovores—vessels that can hold the pressurized vapor without leaking. They build their homes on stilts to escape the drifting salt dust, which is constant, abrasive, filling the lungs if inhaled too deeply. Their huts are domes of salt-block, sealed against the screaming wind, lined with the resin that drips from the lithovores—a resin that smells of copper and ozone, that burns with a violet flame, that induces visions when burned.

They live in the turbulence. When a dust devil spins up, a screaming column that picks up the fine salt and carries it toward the bruised sky, they do not hide; they dance in it, their static-charged hair sparking, their bodies shimmering with mineral dust, moving in the eye of the storm. When a geothermal vent opens with a groan, releasing a new burst of steam and heat, they are there, harvesting, adapting, their skin drinking in the moisture from the mineral-heavy air. When a lithovore frond bursts with thunder, releasing a cloud of spores that immediately begin to germinate in the air, floating upward to seed new growths before falling back to the dust, the Singers are there too, catching the spores in woven nets, perhaps farming them, guiding the frantic, visible growth of their crystalline gods.

They are bound to this place by necessity and by choice. They have no water, but they do not thirst; their bodies are efficient, recycling every molecule of moisture from the steam vents, from the vapor they harvest, from the pressurized gas within the lithovore membranes. They are the inheritors of a paradox—a lushness without water, a verdant strangeness without rain, an abundance of life in a place that has known no drop of water in ten thousand years. They are the Singers, named for the sound they make when they move, when they work, when they communicate in the roar of the wind—clicks and calls and harmonics that blend with the stridulation of the hexapods and the creaking growth of the lithovores, forming a chorus that is the true voice of the Singing Grove, a sound of constant, frantic, beautiful life in the place where nothing ever takes a drink.
