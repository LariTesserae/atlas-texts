The dominant intelligences here do not walk on two legs, nor do they build with stone or steel. They are the **Slag-Grazers**—massive, slow-moving herbivores that resemble nothing so much as ambulatory geological formations. Each stands three meters at the shoulder, its body a low, broad dome covered not in fur but in a living armor of mineralized plates: copper-green patina over iron-rust, calcified lichen in geometric patterns that mirror the concrete seams beneath the peat. They are *edaphagic*—they eat the earth itself, kneeling to press their broad, paddle-like feet into the floating mat, then using trunk-like snouts to vacuum up the acidic, mineral-saturated mud. Their digestive systems are forges: specialized gut bacteria chelate the heavy metals that the plants have extracted from the buried slag, converting industrial waste into the calcium and iron that builds their hides. When they move, they do not merely walk; they *process* the landscape, their weight triggering the small peat-slide landslides that feed the streams, their passage leaving behind trails of compressed, mineral-rich silt that serves as fertilizer for the tree-ferns. Moss grows on them not as decoration but as agriculture—specific symbiotic species that extract nitrogen from the air and fix it into the cracks of their skin, turning the animals into moving hills of slow, photosynthetic growth.

Above them, riding the thermal columns that rise from the vents, are the **Mist-Dancers**—birds with wingspans of two meters whose feathers have evolved into living condensers. Each feather is a lattice of barbules that wick moisture from the saturated air, so that when they fly through the steam, they leave behind visible trails of liquid water, glistening ropes that hang in the air before evaporating. They hunt the translucent, many-toothed fish that swarm in the warm pools, but they are also architects: they weave their nests in the geometric mist formations—the cubes and spirals that hover around the old concrete foundations—using condensed water and plant fibers to construct hanging structures that mimic the angular architecture of the lost factory. They have learned to read the mist as a map, the geometric patterns indicating the location of warm updrafts or the mineral content of the soil below.

In the understory, where the light is green-gold and the air is thick with spores, move the **Gear-Weavers**: arachnids and beetles whose bodies have been fused with the metal they consume. Over ten thousand years, evolution has incorporated the rusted steel into their exoskeletons. A Gear-Weaver’s carapace is not chitin alone but a composite of organic silk and iron oxide, layered like damascus steel, clicking and chiming as they move through the spongy ferns. They are the maintenance crew of the ecosystem, the decomposers who have become recyclers. They do not merely break down dead fern matter; they dismantle the last remnants of the machines, chewing through rusted rebar and concrete with mandibles that spark against the mineral deposits. Their webs are strung between the ancient girders that still protrude from the peat, creating bridges of conductive silk that carry the static electricity of the storms, grounding the lightning into the fungal networks below.

The pools are patrolled by the **Kettle-Keepers**—amphibians the size of cats, their skin iridescent and slick, their eyes reflecting the diffuse light like oil on water. But they are not merely frogs or salamanders; they are thermal regulators. They lounge on the peat mats at the edges of the geothermal vents, their bodies acting as living thermometers, absorbing heat through their bellies and releasing it through specialized patches of skin that flutter like flags. They sing from the vents—not croaks, but low, basso profundo drones that resonate through the metal pipes still buried beneath the roots. Their songs are not for mating alone, but to communicate with the **Mycelial Parliament** below: the vast fungal network that has colonized the old conduits, using them as neural highways. The frogs’ calls vibrate through the pipes, sending chemical signals through the mycelium that direct the growth of the forest above, telling the roots which way to turn, which sinkholes to avoid, which trees to nourish with nutrients pulled from the deep slag.

In the water itself, the fish are **Pool-Smiths**—translucent, pale creatures with too many teeth, but they are also builders. They construct elaborate nests at the bottom of the kettle holes, using the calcified gears and rusted bolts as building materials, cementing them together with secretions that harden like enamel. They scrape algae from the metal surfaces with teeth that regrow constantly, their mouths working like tiny grinding machines, processing the industrial past into biological present. Their bodies are warm—warmer than the surrounding water—because they harbor symbiotic bacteria in their guts that generate heat, a biological furnace that allows them to thrive in the acidic, mineral-rich pools where nothing else could survive.

But the true inhabitants, the ones who *think* in this place, are the **Root-Minds**—not a single species, but a distributed consciousness formed by the mycelial networks that have digested the old pipes and conduits. They are the memory of the factory, half-digested by time. They grow along the geometric seams in the bedrock, following the blueprints of the ancient engineers with a fidelity that is slightly off, like a muscle memory of a lost limb. They direct the growth of the tree-ferns, guiding their logarithmic spirals to match the engineering patterns of the past, but with organic variations—a curve here, a branch there—creating a forest that is simultaneously a cathedral and a circuit board. When the mist forms its perfect cubes and spirals around the concrete foundations, it is the Root-Minds exhaling, releasing spores that crystallize in the humid air, remembering.

There are also the **Steam-Born**—insects that rise from the warm pools like smoke, clouds of midges and dragonflies with bodies like aircraft, their wings beating in the static-charged air. They are short-lived, burning bright, their entire existence from egg to death taking place within the span of a single afternoon rain. They pollinate the orchids that grow in the axils of the tree-ferns, and when they die, their bodies fall into the pools, feeding the fish, completing the cycle of processing that defines this place.

And in the deepest silences, when the wind stops and the mist settles into the hollows, you can hear the **Heart-Beats**—not animals, but the pulse of the place itself. The thermal expansion of the rock, the groaning of the earth as pressure releases, the slow, rhythmic contraction of the peat mat as it breathes. These are the inhabitants too: the geothermal vents that belch sulfur and steam, the superheated aquifers that drive the water cycle, the fungal networks that pulse with electrical signals through the conductive metal. They are the machinery that has become metabolism, the factory that has forgotten it was ever anything but forest.

Everything here lives in a state of *processing*—consuming the past, excreting the future, growing in vertical competition toward the bruised sky, drinking the thick, iron-tasting air, and turning the bones of industry into the flesh of the world.
