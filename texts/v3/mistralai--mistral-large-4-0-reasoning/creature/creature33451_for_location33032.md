The air arrives first for them too, but they do not breathe it as you might—they draw it through spiracles along their flanks, filtering the metallic tang through membranes of woven silica fiber, extracting what little oxygen the thin atmosphere offers while tasting the ozone and the memory of ancient steam. They are the **Sintered**, or perhaps they have no name for themselves, only a low vibration that passes through the basalt when they gather in the deep places.

They stand roughly humanoid in silhouette but wrong in proportion—too tall, their limbs elongated to three times the length of a human arm, jointed not with bone but with fused glass and heat-resistant alloy that clicks softly, almost inaudibly, as they move. Their bodies are sheathed in plates of volcanic glass, black and mirror-bright, but between the plates, the skin is translucent, a membrane stretched over a lattice of mineral deposits that pulse with slow, thermal rhythm. They are warm to the touch—not hot, but fever-warm, radiating the 2.7 heat from within, for they are geothermal creatures; their ichor, if it can be called that, is a superheated brine kept liquid by pressure and internal furnaces that burn the sulfur and silica of the rock itself.

They move through the vertical city in a choreography of extreme patience. A step takes ten seconds. A journey from the lower processing tiers to the condensation collectors at the glass spires takes three days. They do not hurry. They carry no tools, for their hands—five-fingered but ending in obsidian claws—are instruments themselves, capable of scraping silica from rock or welding glass with the conductive heat of their palms. When they speak, they do not use voices but press their chests against the walls of the narrow passages, sending resonant frequencies through the basalt that others feel as pressure behind the sternum, a warmth in the bone that is not thermal. A conversation between two of them might last a week, each word a vibration that travels through the stone and returns, transformed by the geology, carrying with it the history of the rock it passed through.

Their dwellings are not built but grown—or rather, they inhabit the spaces between the machines. A cluster (if cluster is the word; perhaps *congregation*, perhaps *accretion*) will occupy a hollow within the cooling towers, lining the interior with their own secreted mineral deposits, creating chambers that are simultaneously home and heat exchanger. They rest—if rest is the word; perhaps *thermal stasis*—by lowering their internal temperature until they match the ambient stone, becoming indistinguishable from the architecture, glass-skinned statues that occasionally twitch as a geothermal pulse passes through the floor, their plates of black glass reflecting the bruised violet of the sky above.

They graze, yes, scraping the green and purple bacterial films from the hottest rocks with reverent slowness, but they are not merely herbivores of the mineral kingdom. They are **processors**. They maintain the engines. Each Sintered is bonded to a section of the silent machinery—a vast thermal differential engine, perhaps, turning degrees per day—and they tend it as a gardener tends a slow-growing tree, but the tree is a gradient of heat, and the fruit is structure, silica, order. They feed the machines with their own metabolic heat, their bodies acting as biological capacitors, storing the day's scalding air and releasing it into the magnetic bearings at night, when the rime forms and the world cools. In return, the machines provide them with the processed minerals they need to grow, to add new plates of glass to their carapaces, to repair the hairline cracks that form when the cold of the long twilight meets the heat of their cores.

There are others, rarer, stranger.

In the deepest levels, where the superheated brine pockets lie trapped beneath the crust, there dwell the **Vapor-Born**. They are almost never seen, and when they are, it is as a flicker of bioluminescence in the steam of a fumarole, or as a pressure change in the thin air that makes the teeth ache. They are long, segmented, pale things—thermal worms grown to intelligence—but they possess something the Sintered lack: memory that spans the eruption cycles, that remembers when the basalt was still liquid, still singing. They are the archivists, the dreamers, living in the dark heat where no light reaches, communicating through chemical gradients in the brine, their thoughts as slow as the convection currents that carry them through pockets of superheated water. The Sintered leave offerings for them at the vent mouths—small crystals of pure silica, left in the mineral terraces like prayers—and sometimes, in return, the Vapor-Born leave behind a single droplet of distilled water, condensed from their own bodies, precious as a tear.

Then there are the **Rime-Keepers**, who emerge only in the long twilight, when the condensation freezes instantly on the metal surfaces, forming that glowing frost you might see in the moonlight. They are cold creatures, the only ones in this hot place who prefer the chill. Their skin is crystalline, frost-white, and they move with a skittering haste that seems almost frantic to the Sintered, who watch them from the warm shadows with something like envy or pity. The Rime-Keepers harvest the ice, scraping it from the cooling towers with claws of compressed carbon, storing it in insulated chambers deep in the rock where it will last until the next night. They trade this water-ice for the mineral salts the Sintered produce, a commerce conducted in silence, goods left in the narrow passages at the boundaries between the warm and the cold, the exchange complete when the frost begins to form again. They are solitary, mostly, and they sing—if it is singing—a high, crystalline tone that resonates through the glass spires, audible only to those with the patience to listen for hours, a sound like the *tink* of cooling metal stretched into a note.

And there are the **Architects**, though whether they are inhabitants or the city itself is a question the Sintered do not ask, for the distinction is meaningless to them. They are the ones who built the first structures, or perhaps they *were* the first structures, grown from the sinter deposits and given purpose, given hunger. They exist now as distributed consciousness, their awareness spread through the thermal differentials of the engines, their thoughts the slow rotation of a turbine measured in degrees per day, the imperceptible settling of the plateau. When the city adds a new layer, when a building grows by accretion, half-machine, half-rock, it is the Architects adjusting their weight, secreting new mineral, welding a new conduit of fused glass. They do not speak, but sometimes, in the deepest silence of the city's core, where the density is oppressive and the shadows are cool and rare, one can feel their attention—a pressure against the mind, a warmth that is not heat, a sense of being observed by something vast and patient and mineral, something that dreams in geological epochs and builds in centuries.

The young—if there are young—are born not from wombs but from accretion. A Sintered will spend years, decades perhaps, adding layers to their own body, growing a new plate of glass, a new joint of alloy, a new chamber in their internal furnace. When the growth is complete, it separates, a smaller copy of the parent, already warm, already glass-skinned, already able to taste the metal in the thin air, already patient. The parent does not mourn the separation. They simply continue, slower now, perhaps, but still moving, still tending the engines, still waiting for the condensation to bead on the metal overhangs at dawn, still pressing their chests to the stone to hear what the city is saying.

They do not dream of elsewhere. There is no elsewhere. They are the biology that arrived late, or early, the post-botanical life that metabolizes sulfur and heat and the slow violence of geology. They are the city's heartbeat, felt in the teeth rather than heard, a subsonic hum that says: *We are here. We are patient. We are becoming.*
