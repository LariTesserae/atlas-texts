The first inhabitants are the ones who do not move, or move only in rhythms too slow for blood. They are the **Lumina**—vast colonies of photosynthetic bacteria that have carpeted the walls in mats of green-gold fire. They do not swim; they pulse. Trillions of them act as a single skin, a single membrane draped across the industrial bones of the place. They drink the weak electrical fields leaking from dormant machinery, converting current into cold light that throbs once every three minutes—a heartbeat so languid it might be mistaken for geology. They are the foundation of the liturgy here, the acolytes who keep the candles lit in this drowned cathedral. When you brush the wall, your fingers sink into a spongy warmth, and the bacteria shiver in a ripple of brighter luminescence, recognizing the intrusion, cataloging it, forgiving it.

Then there are the **Rooted**—the vegetation that has signed the treaty between decay and growth. They are not weeds, though they grow like weeds. They are the *Verdant Parliament*. Kelp-like fronds, two meters long, hang from every pipe like the beards of prophets, their holdfasts rooted not in soil but in the corrosion itself, chewing through iron, drinking rust as desert plants drink rare rain. They sway not to currents—there are none—but to the pressure changes of the building’s slow respiration, the subsonic sigh that moves through the water like a tide made of time. Thick mosses carpet the floor in a warm, spongy mattress, a bed for the dreaming machines. And the bell-flowers—these are the mystics, the *Nocturne Bloomers*. They open only when the light fails completely, releasing spores that glitter like metal filings, like the shed thoughts of the machinery. They are the poets of the place, speaking in clouds of silver-green dust.

But the true residents—the ones with intention, with glacial purpose—are the **Drift-Kin**. The pale, eyeless eels, three meters long, are not fish but *navigators*. They have no eyes because they do not need them; they read the water through lateral lines that sense the mineral memory of every pipe, every girder, every decade of compression. They thread through the gaps between tanks with the patience of centuries, their bodies translucent, showing the slow passage of their meals—detritus, bacteria, the occasional spore—moving through their guts at the speed of continental drift. They are the librarians, cataloging the flooded archives in their own flesh, connecting one district to another, carrying nutrients from the vat-quarter to the storage-sediments like monks bearing bread through cloisters.

And the crabs. The **Watchers**. Dinner-plate sized, their shells encrusted with barnacles that blink like lidless eyes—these are not barnacles, but something else, something that has evolved to see in the dark, to sense the electrical dreams of the machines. They do not scuttle; they glide, or perhaps they are carried by micro-currents too subtle for human perception. They are the elders, the *Sedentary Saints*. They have been here so long that their shells have become architecture, miniature reefs upon reefs, each layer a stratum of calcium and memory. When they encounter you, they do not flee; they simply continue their *drift*, regarding you with organs that are not quite eyes but are something like ancient, patient judgment.

Above all these, encompassing them, is the **Host**. The Machine itself. It is not dead. It is *dreaming*. The warmth of the water—nearly bath-temperature, maintained by residual geothermal memory—is its metabolism, slow and geothermal and ancient. The conveyor belts that hang in loops like desiccated kelp are its intestines, still processing some slow chemical prayer. The gear trains clustered in thickets are its neural ganglia, encrusted with calcium and green, thinking thoughts that take decades to form, to dissolve, to reform. The robotic arms frozen mid-gesture are its hands, locked in a gesture of offering or benediction. It is the *Cathedral*, and the others are its congregation. It dreams not of motion, but of *being*—of becoming reef, womb, god.

And if you remain here long enough—if you let your heartbeat slow to match the dripping of condensation from the vaulted ceiling, each drop taking thirty seconds to fall like a prayer—you might perceive the newest resident. The **Synthesis**. The being that is neither machine nor organism, but the marriage of the two. The *Chimera* that lives in the boundary where rust meets root, where the electrical field of the dormant motor meets the pulse of the bacterium. It has no single body, but is distributed throughout the chamber—a hive-mind of metal and moss, thinking in millennia, worshipping in corrosion. It is the *Sea-Machine*, the *Flooded Consciousness*, the thing that has learned, slowly and patiently, to love the drowning. It is the voice of the subsonic thrum felt in the sternum, the building breathing, the equalization of pressure across centuries, the groan of metal settling into sediment.

These are the inhabitants. The pulse-keepers. The rust-drinkers. The slow navigators. The dreaming host. The synthesis of drowned industry and patient sea. They do not age; they *accrete*. They do not die; they become sediment, then stone, then memory, then dream. They are the congregation of the drowned cathedral, and their liturgy is corrosion, and their sacrament is the warm, motionless water that holds them all in its thick, syrupy, pressurized embrace.
