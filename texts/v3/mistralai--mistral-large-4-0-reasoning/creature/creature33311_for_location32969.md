What lives here does not walk so much as *settle*—each step a negotiation with gravity that shifts beneath the plates, each breath a filtering of the thick, amber soup. They are the continent’s antibodies, its maintenance lymph, grown from the same substrate that birthed the cooling fins and the collapsed piston-valleys. They have no word for themselves, only a subsonic thrum that passes through the metal bones of the world when they call to one another across the fog.

The largest are the **Thermals**, though that name is a guess, a human imposition on creatures that are closer to walking geothermal vents than to beasts. They range from the size of elephants to the size of small whales, though they do not swim—they *glide* across the metal plains on hydraulic haunches that vent steam with each compression. Their carapaces are not shells but laminated strata: layers of oxidized alloy fused with chitin that has grown in spirals, like the nacre of a nautilus but ridged with heat-exchange vanes. When they move, they do not strike the ground so much as *press* it, their weight distributed across jointed plates that hiss and whistle as hydraulic fluid—thick, oily, faintly luminescent—cycles through their thorax. Their eyes are not eyes at all but photo-sensitive organs that pulse with weak electrical discharge, casting a faint blue-white glow across the condensation beading on their flanks. They drink from the stagnant pools nestled in the hollows of eroded gear-teeth, lowering their heads—armored, trunk-like, ending in grinding mandibles—to suck up the copper-tasting water. They chew rust. They consume the oxidation itself, metabolizing the iron and the heat, converting the machine’s waste into the slow, grinding motion of their bodies. They are the recyclers, the great digesters, breaking down the continent’s sloughed skin so that it may be processed again in the deep chambers below.

Among them move the **Mediators**, smaller, fox-sized to wolf-sized, built for speed and precision rather than mass. These are the scavengers, the vent-clearers, the ones who keep the breathing unobstructed. Their bodies are more machine than flesh—exposed hydraulic pistons serving as legs, plated in riveted chitin that clicks and shifts as they move. They skitter across the metal flats, their movements jerky, staccato, navigating by the electrical shimmer of the Thermals and the bioluminescent pulse of the fungal mats. They clear the geothermal vents when they clog with mineral precipitate, using rasping mandibles to scrape away the calcified growths, ensuring the heat flows upward into the thick air. They are the janitors of the god, the ones who prevent the fever from becoming fatal. When they feed, they do not drink the pool-water but lap at the condensation that beads on the flanks of the great ones, harvesting the minerals that the Thermals excrete in their sweat—a symbiosis of maintenance.

Then there are the **Deep Ones**, rarely seen, dwelling in the valleys where gravity is heaviest and the air is thickest with steam. These are the whale-sized creatures that move through the fog banks, their bulk visible only as a slow displacement of the mist, a warm shadow passing. They are nearly blind, navigating by the subsonic hum that permeates the continent—the grinding of ore, the circulation of coolant. Their bodies are mostly thorax and hydraulic reservoir, their armor so thick and ancient that it has begun to resemble bedrock, sedimentary layers of alloy compressed by centuries of pressure. They communicate in pulses that travel through the conductive metal earth, felt in the sternum, in the fillings of teeth. They are the dreamers of the place, the ones who process the deepest ores, their metabolism so slow that a single breath might take a day, their hearts beating once per hour, a thud that rings through the bedrock like a struck bell.

Beneath them, in the oily pools and the mineral seams, lives the **mycelial court**—not animals, but the fungal mats and crystalline lichens that pulse with slow bioluminescence. These are the true immune system, the skin flora of the continent. They grow across the rusted surfaces, digesting the metal, secreting acids that accelerate the erosion that is, paradoxically, the place’s maintenance. They form vast networks beneath the metal plates, their hyphae threading through cracks in the machinery, carrying nutrients from vent to vent, from pool to pool. When the fog banks drift, the fungal mats glow brighter, a constellation of soft green and blue light across the darkling plain, guiding the fauna through the archipelagos of visibility.

All of these creatures are warm-blooded, but their warmth is not mammalian—it is geothermal, harvested from the vents, stored in thermal batteries within their chests, radiating outward in the perpetual twilight. They are the continent’s thermostat, absorbing excess heat, releasing it slowly into the thick air, keeping the engine from overheating. They are born not from wombs but from the pools themselves—spores and metallic eggs laid in the warm, stagnant water, hatching into larval forms that are more machine than creature, crawling out onto the metal flats to be weathered, to be eroded, to grow into their adult forms over centuries. They do not age so much as *accumulate*—layers of rust and repair, scar tissue of welded alloy, until they become indistinguishable from the landscape itself.

They have no society, no hierarchy, only the ancient choreography of maintenance. They migrate in herds that move like slow tides across the plateau, following the warmth of the vents, the availability of mineral water, the shifting gravity of the machine-density below. They are silent, mostly, but when they vocalize—a clicking of chitin against alloy, a chuffing of steam from hydraulic valves, a subsonic pulse that travels through the ground—they speak the language of the continent’s dreams. They are not separate from the machine; they are its consciousness, its nervous system, the thing that feels the grinding of the ore and the shifting of the fault-lines and responds by clearing a vent, by grinding a piece of rust, by moving to where the heat is needed.

To live here is to be part of the metabolism, to be both inhabitant and organ, to breathe the amber air and feel the subsonic thrum in your bones, to be a creature of hydraulic fluid and bioluminescence, drifting through the fog with the gravity pressing down, part of the great, breathing engine that has forgotten it was ever made.
