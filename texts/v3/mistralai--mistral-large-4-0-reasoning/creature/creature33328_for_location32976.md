They are not merely beetles, though they wear that shape. They are the sediment made mobile, the living geology that crawls across the satin limestone in processions so dense they appear to be the floor itself breathing. Each one is a slow cathedral of chitin, their shells not grown but *deposited*—layer upon layer of mineral oil secreted from glands that refine the hydrogen sulfide from the stone into iridescent armor, into rainbows that exist only under the violet bruise-light of the lichen above. They do not walk so much as *settle*. A foot moves, then rests. A body shifts, then becomes indistinguishable from the fungal stalks it clings to. They are trillions, yes, but they are also one: a distributed animal, a single vast organism wearing a trillion armored skins, grazing the mycelium with mandibles that scrape at the ochre flesh with the sound of sandpaper on bone—a sound lost immediately in the cavern’s eternal roar.

But the beetles are not the city. They are its blood, perhaps, or its immune cells, or its thoughts made manifest. The true inhabitants—the ones who dream in millimeters per century—are the fungi.

They rise in columns twenty meters thick, their stalks a packed architecture of cellulose and chitin, pale as bone, veined with rust where the mineral veins of the bedrock have been tapped and drained. They are not plants, not animals, but something older: the mature form of a metabolism that learned to eat stone. Their caps spread above like vaulted ceilings, but they are not dead structures—they are respiratory surfaces, porous membranes that inhale the methane seeping from the fissures and exhale the processed minerals back into the rock. The mycelium that connects them is a neural network without a brain, a distributed consciousness that thinks in chemical gradients, in the slow diffusion of nutrients through hyphae thinner than spider silk. When a beetle dies, the mycelium finds it. When a chamber grows too dense, the fungi redirect their growth, abandoning tunnels that become fossilized arteries, opening new plazas where the caps overlap like geological strata. The waste-recycling chambers are their stomachs, vast hollows where the dead and the excreted are broken down by enzymes that dissolve chitin and bone alike, returning every molecule to the closed loop. The nurseries are their wombs, warm pockets where spores germinate not in soil, but in the composted remains of the city’s own dead.

And then there are the lights.

The chemiluminescent lichen clings to the ceiling in patches the size of dinner plates, but they are not plants either. They are colonies of chemosynthetic symbionts, bacteria and archaea living in a matrix of fungal tissue, burning hydrogen and sulfur in cold fire. Their light—the color of healing bruises, of violet and grey—does not illuminate so much as *signal*. They pulse, slowly, in rhythms that match the gas vents below. They are the eyes of the city, perhaps, or its mood. When the methane surges, they brighten. When the beetle-drift shifts direction across the floor, they dim and flare in patterns too complex to be random. They have grown for millions of years in these upper reaches, mapping the architecture with their glow, writing in light across the darkness that words cannot reach.

Beneath the beetles, upon their shells, live the mites—creatures so small they are almost dust, riding the slow beasts like passengers on a glacier. They clean the mineral oil from the beetle’s joints, they eat the dead skin, they are the microbiome made visible, a second layer of life riding the first. And inside the beetles, in their guts, colonies of bacteria perform the alchemy that allows them to digest the fungal flesh—bacteria that have never known sunlight, that process the chemosynthetic energy the fungi have drawn from the stone, that turn rock into beetle, beetle into motion, motion into the slow erosion of the cavern floor.

The air itself is inhabited. It is thick with spores—not the dormant kind, but the active, drifting kind, the seeds of the city’s expansion, each one a potential new column, a new district, a new thought in the mycelial mind. They drift on currents that are not wind but the thermal breath of the stone, settling on the limestone, germinating in cracks, sending down hyphae that seek the mineral veins with the patience of roots seeking water in a desert that has forgotten what water is.

There are others, too—the ones so slow they are mistaken for geology. In the deepest chambers, where the gas roar becomes a subsonic vibration that rearranges your organs, there are the grazers of giants: creatures that take a year to complete a single step, their bodies flat against the stone, their mouths rasping at the limestone itself, wearing it down not with teeth but with acids secreted from pores, contributing to the satin sheen of the floor. They are rare, sparse, scattered across the landscape like forgotten monuments. They are the city’s oldest inhabitants, the ones who remember when this was seabed and not cavern, who have been eating the stone since before the fungi arrived.

Everything here is symbiosis. The beetles carry the spores in the grooves of their shells. The fungi provide the food and the architecture. The lichen provides the light and the chemical signals. The gas provides the energy and the sound—that deafening, eternal roar which is not noise but *language*, a vibration that the beetles feel through their mineral-oil armor, a bass note that tells them where the fresh mycelium grows, where the old tunnels are collapsing, where the methane is richest.

They do not hurry because they cannot. Time here is not a river but a mountain. To live here is to become geological. A beetle may live for ten thousand years, moving across the cavern floor at a pace that would make a glacier seem frantic. A fungal column may stand for a hundred million years, growing millimeter by millimeter, until its cap spreads wide enough to house a nursery, a waste chamber, a plaza where the beetle-drift pools like a slow tide. The lichen may pulse its violet light for a million years before shifting its pattern.

They are a civilization without hands, without tools, without the arrogance of builders. They are a climax community, a final form, a city that has reached the end of its growth and simply *persists*, breathing stone, drinking gas, glowing with cold chemical fire in the absolute dark below the world. They are patient. They are ancient. They are waiting, though what they wait for is not change, but only the continued seepage of gas, the continued drift of spores, the continued, deafening song of the earth breathing out, slow and eternal.
