The inhabitants are not many, but they are **total**. They occupy every cubic centimeter of this world, leaving no hollow space unclaimed, no surface uninhabited.

First, there is the **Mat**—not a collection of individuals, but a single, distributed being. It is the **skin of the world**, pale green and saturated to the point of liquidity, a continuous membrane of photosynthetic tissue that carpets the plateau in an unbroken, spongy sheet. It does not walk, but it *breathes*. You can hear it sigh underfoot, releasing trapped air in wet, belching pops that bubble up through the moss and vanish back into the humidity. It drinks the condensation that weeps from the towers, swelling with each droplet, then exhaling vapor in a rhythm so slow it is indistinguishable from stillness. The Mat is the **substrate of consciousness** here; it thinks in timescales of centuries, remembering the violence of the rock’s birth while remaining utterly untroubled by the passage of hours. It is ancient, yet it has the sheen of something newborn, perpetually embryonic, a placenta stretched across the high plain.

Rising from this living floor are the **Towers**—and these are not separate from the Mat, but are its **calcified extremities**, its reaching limbs grown thick and mineralized. They are biological constructions without tools, without hands, without intention: giant horsetails petrified mid-growth, hollow and fluted, their ribs channeling the condensation down flanks the color of old bone. They are **habitats**, yes, but they are also **organisms**—perhaps the adult form of the Mat, or its reproductive organs, frozen at the exact moment they achieved perfection. They do not sway; they **resonate**. When the wind funnels through their hollow chambers, they moan in registers that shift between basso hum and whistling shriek, singing the architecture of their own empty spaces. Inside, the walls are lined with a thin, living film—neither plant nor stone, but **petrified protoplasm**—that sweats with the same condensation that drips from the ceilings, growing and shrinking in cycles so slow they appear frozen.

And then, the precious rarity: clinging to these interior walls, where the water drips in metronomic eternity, are the **Limpets**. They are the only true **fauna**, the sole animal notes in this pure-plant symphony. They are **translucent**—like droplets of water given a shell, like glass sculptures of snails that have forgotten how to crawl. They are **sessile**, cemented to the calcified ribs by a secretion as permanent as the stone itself. They do not move. They have no need to move. The water comes to them; the temperature is perfect; the air is thick with minerals and the scent of growing things. They are **embryos that never hatched**, or perhaps adults who chose to remain larvae forever, suspended in a state of perpetual gestation within the womb of the tower. They filter the dripping condensation, their bodies pulsing with a rhythm so languid you might mistake them for stalactites, for mineral growths, for the towers dreaming themselves into being. They are the **monks** of this place, the only witnesses to the wind’s moaning and the Mat’s wet sighing, motionless and patient as the stone.

Together, they form a **closed society** with no borders, no births, no deaths. The Mat provides the foundation and the endless, static water; the Towers provide the shelter and the resonance, the hollow bones of the world; and the Limpets provide the **animal presence**, the rare, trembling spark of something that is not quite plant, not quite stone, clinging to the interior dark. They are all **frozen in time**, not dead, but **perfected**—a civilization that reached its final form in a single, breathless moment of growth, and has remained there, warm and wet and utterly still, since before memory began, waiting in their calcified cathedrals for a change that will never come.
