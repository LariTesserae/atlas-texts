They are the ones who sleep beneath the tide.

They move through the hamlet like figures cut from the same limestone as the cliffs—tall, spare, their skin the pale grey-green of shadowed moss, translucent enough in the watery light to show the darker tracery of veins beneath. They have no hair, or rather, they have hair only where the damp allows it to grow: fine, dark filaments that cling to their scalps and shoulders like wet threads, heavy with condensation that never dries. Their eyes are vast, lidless or nearly so, black and reflective as the cistern’s surface, adapted to harvest the faintest luminescence from the bruised sky and the phosphorescent lichens. But eyes are secondary here; they navigate by pressure and temperature, by the subtle shifts in the stone beneath their bare feet, by the chemical signatures that bloom on the damp air like invisible ink.

They are cold-blooded, or nearly so. Their body heat barely registers against the perpetual chill of the valley, so they wear little—only garments woven from the reeds that grow in the cistern’s overflow, or cloaks of cured bark lashed with sinew, or sometimes nothing at all, letting the moss grow directly on their shoulders in cultivated gardens of emerald that they tend and harvest. They are hermaphroditic, or perhaps simply beyond such distinctions; reproduction is tied to the condensation cycle, occurring in the deepest hollows where the dripping is heaviest, where the young hatch pale and eyeless and are immediately claimed by the herds, raised in the mucus and the moss until they grow large enough to stand upright and claim their place in the paths.

Their relationship with the swarming fauna is not one of dominion but of profound, wordless intimacy. When the millions of pale, segmented creatures flow through the streets—when they undulate over the flat stones, through the doorways, across the sleeping forms—the inhabitants do not stir. They lie on platforms of woven reed, curled in the organic curves of their dwellings, and let the tide pass over them. The creatures’ bodies are cool, slick, faintly saline; their passage is a massage, a cleansing, a communion. The inhabitants breathe slowly, their chests rising and falling in rhythm with the undulating waves, and when they wake, their skin is beaded with the same condensation that coats the cliffs, as if they have been baptized in the mountain’s own breath. They do not fear the swarms because the swarms are the town’s immune system, its cleaning crews, its sleeping guardians. A creature might pause on an inhabitant’s chest, its eyeless head turning blindly, and the human will reach up—not to brush it away, but to guide it, to feel the pulse of its segmented body, to read the health of the valley in its slow peristalsis.

They communicate in a language that has no name for sound. They touch. They press palms together in the narrow paths, feeling the temperature of each other’s skin, the dampness, the subtle tremors of thought made physical. They drum on the stone walls—thin places where the limestone has been honeycombed by the creatures’ tunnels—and the vibrations travel through the rock, through the moss, through the bodies of the sleeping swarms, carrying messages across the hamlet in a web of tactile semaphore. They have developed a writing of sorts: not marks on surface, but patterns of pressure, of warmth, of the specific gravity of touch. A warning is a sharp press; a greeting is the brush of fingertips like the movement of fern fronds; love is the full weight of a palm held against the sternum until the heartbeat is felt in the stone.

Their technology is agricultural rather than architectural. They do not build so much as cultivate. They coax the limestone to fissure in desired patterns, guiding the erosion with weak acids derived from lichen and urine, encouraging the moss to grow in the shapes of walls, domes, and channels. Their tools are flint and bone, yes, but also living things: they graft the reeds, they train the ferns to grow in lattices that serve as roofing, they harvest the sulphur-yellow lichen and grind it with stone to make a paint that glows faintly in the perpetual twilight, marking the paths and the doorways with bioluminescent signs that pulse slowly, like heartbeats. They collect the endless dripping in vessels of fired clay—clay that is always damp, never quite dry—and they drink this water, which tastes of mineral and silence, which is never cold enough to hurt and never warm enough to comfort.

They eat the moss, certainly, and the lichens, and the fungi that grow in the darkest crevices where the condensation pools. But they also eat the creatures. Not the living swarms—that would be cannibalism of the valley’s soul—but the dead. When a pale salamander-thing expires, it does not rot; it desiccates into a pale, leathery husk that the inhabitants collect and grind into a flour, mixing it with moss-paste to make a bread that is grey and dense and tastes of the stone itself. They are not carnivores in the way of the peaks above, where storms rage and predators hunt with sound and fury. They are detritivores, recyclers, participants in the slow, silent metabolism of the V.

Their society is organized around the cistern. It is the heart of the town, the dark pool that never overflows, never runs dry, and around it they arrange their lives in concentric rings of intimacy. Those closest to the water are the tenders, the ones who maintain the moss carpets that filter the condensation, who clear the channels that guide the dripping from the cliffs to the pool. Further out are the weavers, the reed-workers, the ones who shape the organic architecture. And everywhere, always, are the sleepers—the ones who lie down in the paths, in the doorways, in the hollows of the stone, and let the herds flow over them, participating in the great, silent migration that pulses through the settlement like a second heartbeat.

They have no leaders, or rather, leadership is a function of moisture. The one whose skin is dampest, whose moss-gardens are most vibrant, whose touch carries the most information, that one guides the others for a time, until the condensation shifts and another becomes the conduit. They do not age as those below do, not exactly; the coolness preserves them, the mineral water petrifies them slowly, turning their bones to stone over centuries, so that the oldest among them are half-limestone, half-flesh, statues that still breathe, still feel the passage of the swarms over their petrified limbs.

When they sleep—and they sleep often, for the twilight never changes, there is no day to mark the hours—they dream in vibrations. They dream of the erosion, of the cliffs rounding and retreating, of the valley widening into a plain. They dream of the first rain that will fall here, millions of years hence, when the limestone has softened and the mountains have worn down to hills. And in their dreams, they are not alone. The pale, eyeless creatures coil around them, millions of silent hearts beating in the dark, keeping watch over the ones who sleep beneath the tide, in the place where the built and the grown have become indistinguishable, where the loudest thing is the absence of sound, and where life flows on, soft and segmented and utterly mute, through the crook of the young mountain.
