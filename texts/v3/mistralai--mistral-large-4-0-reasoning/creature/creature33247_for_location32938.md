They are the Mist-Walkers first, though “walker” is a violence done to their nature. They do not walk; they are carried. Seven meters of translucent membrane, columns of smoke given temporary flesh, drifting on the thermal breath of the vents. They are the only mobile population, and they are sparse—a census of solitude. One drifts through the central plaza, stirring the air until the cavern’s thrum rises to a shriek, then it is gone, dissolved back into the upper dark where it breeds. They do not eat, not exactly; they filter the updrafts for mineral vapor and the spores that rain down from the lost ceiling. They perceive the world in gradients of heat, seeing the warm currents as bright rivers against the cool dark, and they do not see the towers at all—or rather, they see them as weather, as obstacles in the flow, neither city nor ruin, merely resistance. They are the only animals, but they are not the only life, nor even the oldest.

Beneath them, or perhaps surrounding them, is the true denizen: the City-That-Grows. The towers are not architecture but organisms—slow, lithic, dreaming in geological time. They expand and contract with the thermal cycles, their surfaces rippling like the flank of a breathing beast, their flanks glowing with an amber light that is not electric but metabolic. They are bigger on the inside than the outside, which means they are hollow, wombed, maternal. The programmable matter that constitutes them is not inert stone but a colony of distributed intent, a nervous system of nanoscale assemblers that swarm invisibly within the basalt. These are the citizens in the truest sense: billions upon billions of microscopic repair-creatures, constantly knitting erosion back into fabric, extruding new chambers from the bedrock, maintaining the impossible density of layered construction. They do not sleep, they do not die, they do not hurry. They are the immune system and the builders and the dreamers of the place, working in blind concert to keep the stone pliable, growing, becoming. To them, a century is a twitch, a millennium a breath. They inhabit the space between molecules, the fissures in the obsidian, the very concept of structure.

In the corners where the damp pools thickest, another population clusters: the Damp-Crystals. They are vegetation only by analogy. Calcium and exotic metals accreting in fractal patterns, glowing with internal blue-green bioluminescence, they grow at a pace visible to the patient eye. They are the sensory organs of the chamber, or perhaps its memory. Each branch records the chemistry of the water that feeds it, each pulse of light a neuron firing in a brain of mineral. They are sparse, solitary, rooted in the seeps where the condensation collects, leaving the central plaza bare. They perceive time differently still—not in breaths or twitches, but in accretion. To watch them is to watch thought become geology. They are the librarians of the dark, keeping records in crystal lattice of the endless rain, their bioluminescence a slow Morse code of mineral hunger.

Then there is the infrastructure that lives in the channels: the water itself, and what moves within it. The shallow trenches carved into the obsidian floor guide the condensation toward collection sumps, where it is processed and heated and cycled back into the atmosphere. This is the circulatory system, the blood of the place. In the sumps, perhaps, something swims—something too small to see, or perhaps nothing at all, just the chemistry of perpetual motion, the endless loop of evaporation and precipitation that constitutes the weather. The rain that falls for hours from the cooled ceiling is the breath of the chamber, inhaled at the geothermal vents, exhaled as mist, a respiration so slow it spans days. The condensation collectors hanging from the ceiling like fruit are not merely infrastructure; they are organs, or eggs, or the reproductive strategies of the architecture itself, gathering the sky into spheres of refracted dimness, casting shadows that fall in directions that make no geometric sense because the light here is confused, uncertain of its own physics.

But the most important inhabitant is the Absence. The city was organized for a density of population that never arrived, or that dissolved into the stone long ago. The residential spires stand empty, their interior chambers vast and waiting, bigger on the inside than the outside suggests. Yet they are not abandoned in the way a ruin is abandoned; they are maintained, repaired, grown, kept warm and humid and humming. This suggests the inhabitants did not leave; they transformed. They became the programmable matter. They became the weather. They became the mist-walkers, or perhaps the mist-walkers are their children, or their ghosts, breeding in the upper dark and drifting down to filter the air, solitary presences in an ocean of empty space. The population is near-void, but it is a void that is actively maintained, a silence that is sung into being by the subsonic roar of the vents.

The sound is their voice. The roar is the bass note of a consciousness too large to localize, the hum that vibrates in the teeth is speech in a language of pressure and heat. The dripping—that percussion, the fist-sized droplets falling from the collectors with a sound like distant bells—is the heartbeat, slow and mineral, marking time in drops rather than seconds.

To live here is to be dissolved into the equilibrium. The mist-walkers live in the thermal currents. The assemblers live in the stone. The crystals live in the corners. The water lives in the channels. And the City itself—the cathedral of absence—lives in the tension between growth and erosion, between the heat below and the cool dark above, a single, contained world breathing in the dark, inhabited by almost nothing, and therefore by everything that remains when the many have gone, everything that persists in the long, weathered, loud, and strangely beautiful interval between becoming and being worn away.
