They are the **lithovores**, and they are neither quite animal nor quite mineral, but a third thing that has forgotten the difference—creatures of mucilage and calcium, of soft protoplasm armored in self-secreted glass. They do not walk so much as *weather*. To watch one move is to watch a cloud condense, drift, and resolve; a slow, pale tide that flows across the white crust with the inevitability of frost creeping over a window. Their bodies are translucent, the color of old bone left in desert light, shot through with filaments of silica that glitter like nerve-endings made visible. They have no fixed shape, only a tendency toward mass—a colony of cells that share cytoplasm across meters of membrane, a single organism distributed through the settlement like a nervous system made of slime.

Each lithovore is a **gardener of stone**. They do not eat in the way you understand it; they *digest the ground itself*. Their undersides—if undersides they have—press against the gypsum and halite, exuding enzymes that are also acids, also solvents, dissolving the bedrock at the molecular level. What they consume, they excrete as architecture: a biofilm of calcium carbonate and silica that hardens within hours into the crystalline walls of the towers. The structures are not houses to them; they are **scales**, shed continuously, grown like fingernails that never stop thickening. A lithovore that has lived long enough becomes indistinguishable from the tower it inhabits—its protoplasm suffused through the porous walls, its thoughts (if thoughts they are) traveling as chemical gradients through the mineral matrix, the tower itself becoming a calcified brain.

They move through the settlement along paths worn not by feet but by **repetition**—centuries of the same slow circuit, the mucilaginous weight of their bodies compressing the precipitate into smooth, packed highways between the towers. They are colonial, always colonial; a single individual might span three chambers, its bulk flowing through archways and up staircases that fold into impossible geometries, its consciousness distributed across the volume like pressure in deep water. When two lithovores meet, they do not interact; they simply **merge**, their boundaries dissolving into a larger temporary aggregate, sharing minerals and memory before splitting again into new configurations, new hungers.

Their faces, if they have faces, are featureless—smooth domes of translucent tissue that might be eyes or might be merely sensors for chemical gradients, for the subtle temperature differentials that whisper of brine moving through capillary channels below. They navigate by **taste**, by the dendritic corrosion patterns that acid etches into the tower walls—these are their gardens, their maps, their food. They graze upon the mineral frost, consuming the very decay that the atmosphere works upon the stone, turning corrosion into growth, entropy into structure. They are the only sound in the place, and they make none: their movement is the silent displacement of air, the soft *shush* of protoplasm against crystal that is immediately absorbed by the absolute quiet.

But they do not live forever, not in the way you mean. They follow a cycle of **reincarnation** that is also architecture. A lithovore grows—visible only in the accretion of new calcite rings in the walls, in the slow widening of chambers. It metamorphoses: its soft body gradually mineralizing, becoming more tower than creature, its consciousness diffusing into the stone until it is no longer distinguishable from the building. Then it **dissolves**. The colony withdraws its protoplasm from the walls, leaving behind hollow chambers, and retreats into the bedrock below, or perhaps fragments into spores that drift on electrostatic currents to found new settlements. The tower remains, empty for a season, then a new lithovore flows into it, or the old one returns changed, its memories stored in the crystalline lattice of the walls, reabsorbed like water into a sponge. They do not age; they only **transform**, a perpetual becoming that has no beginning and no end, each generation both parent and child to the stone.

Within the towers, in the alcoves lined with nacre and the platforms of compressed salt, they rest—not sleeping, but **waiting**, metabolizing at the rate of geological time. They dream, and their dreams precipitate: the towers grow by millimeters while they slumber, new layers of mineral secreted from their resting forms, the architecture thickening around them like a pearl around a grain of sand. Time moves differently for them. An hour of their existence might be a day outside, or a century; the dim, blue-gray light inside the towers seems to compress duration, so that they experience the rotation of the structures—the slow turning of doorways toward new directions—as a single breath, a seasonal sigh.

They are not alone, though they are the dominant life. Between them crawl the **lithodrones**—rare, armored things that contrast with the lithovores' softness. Where the lithovores are pale and yielding, the drones are nacre and opal, many-legged, shells clicking softly against the crust (a sound so faint it is more felt than heard, a vibration in the teeth). They graze on the gray-green lichens that cling to the north faces of the towers, those dormant symbiotic colonies of mineral-fungus that photosynthesize the dim red light. A drone takes a full day to cross the space between two towers, moving like a tectonic plate, leaving a trail of dissolved mineral that sublimates instantly into the dry air—a temporary etching that fades like a thought. They are the tourists, the observers, the slow pilgrims of a landscape that moves too fast for them and too slow for anything else.

Beneath the lithovores, or perhaps before them, or perhaps they are the same thing in different phases, exist the **chemolithotrophs**—the microbial colonies whose desiccated husks form the paper-thin precipitate of the playa floor. They are the ancestors, the foundation, the original architects who first bloomed when trace moisture seeped from below. Now they are brittle scales, flaking away in electrostatic drifts, but they are not dead—they are **waiting**, dormant in the crystalline lattice, reactivating when the chemistry shifts, when the brines of mercury or liquid sulfur flow again through the capillaries. They are the memory of the place, the original code from which the lithovores grew, the precipitate that will one day become living protoplasm again.

The lichens are the **singers**, though their song is silent—they fix carbon from CO₂ and mineral substrates, converting the dim, diffuse light into chemical energy, their gray-green crusts pulsing with a photosynthesis that requires no liquid water, only the non-aqueous frost that condenses in the cool mornings. They are the food for the drones, the color in a monochrome world, the only thing that is truly *alive* in the aqueous sense, though they are themselves symbiotic, fungal and mineral intertwined.

Together, these inhabitants form a **society without hierarchy**, a city where the architects are the architecture, where the builders dissolve into their own creations and are reborn from them. The lithovores organize the settlement through their movement, their grazing patterns, the slow rotation of the towers (which turn because the lithovores within them grow asymmetrically, adding mass to one side, causing the structure to pivot over seasons like a sunflower tracking a dim sun). The paths emerge from their traffic, the chambers expand according to their needs, the corrosion gardens are pruned and tended by their grazing. It is an urbanism of **metabolism**, a civilization where the distinction between body and building, between organism and mineral, has dissolved into something older and stranger—a place where the inhabitants are the rocks, and the rocks are dreaming.
