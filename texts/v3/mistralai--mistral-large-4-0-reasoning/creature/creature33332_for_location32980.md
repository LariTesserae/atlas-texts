The inhabitants of this place exist in a spectrum of being that defies the old categories of animal, vegetable, and mineral. They have evolved—or been engineered—for a world where the ground is a living thing and silence is the dominant medium.

**The Jackers** come first, for they are the ones who endure. They are few, scattered through the hab-blocks in vertical stacks, their figures small against the massive gantries and the heaving pylons. They are not quite human in their adaptation; their suits are membranes rather than fabric, semi-permeable skins that harvest the mist, channeling condensation down grooved channels to reservoirs at their throats. They move with the patience of geology, their steps timed to the thermal pulses they feel through the soles of their boots. When a building begins to lean, they do not run—they adjust, turning hydraulic valves, signaling to their kin through taps on steel that vibrate through the substrate. They communicate in light, raising bioluminescent fronds or flashing the pale green-blue pulses of the vegetation to guide one another through the fog. They are temporary, like everything else here, their lifespans measured in the slow rhythm of the rising domes and sealing fissures. They build, they jack, they re-level, and they wait for the ground to shift again.

Then there are the **Lithovores**, the great many-legged grazers. They are the valley's true natives, armored in chitinous plates that function as living condensers—each plate ridged and grooved, a micro-landscape of channels that draw moisture from the saturated air and funnel it down to a central collecting organ, then to their patient mouths. They move in herds across the metal grating and through the thickets of fronded vegetation, their dozens of legs finding purchase on surfaces that breathe and shift beneath them. They are slow, deliberate, silent. They do not merely eat the verdant growth; they cultivate it. As they graze, they stimulate the plants to secrete more of their weathering acids, and in return, the vegetation provides them with the sugars and minerals they need. They carry spores in the folds of their armor, dispersing the green-blue luminescence across the shifting landscape, ensuring that the colonization continues even as the city builds around them.

Above them drift the **Mist-Wings**, the flyers. They are perhaps insects grown large, or birds reduced to membrane and bone, with wings that are translucent veils heavy with condensation. In a world where sound dies within meters, they navigate by the pulse of the fronds' bioluminescence—reading the subtle shifts in green and blue light that ripple through the mist like neural signals. They emerge in clouds from the interstitial spaces: inside hollow pylons, within the cavities of the raw stone, between the walls of hab-blocks where the air is thickest. They are the connective tissue of the valley, carrying nutrients between the vertical layers, pollinating the vegetation, their soft wings whispering (though no ear can hear it) against the metal surfaces. They are drawn to the thermal vents, riding the invisible currents that rise from the smelters and fabricators, their bodies glowing faintly with the phosphorescence they absorb from the plants below.

But the oldest inhabitants are the **Fronds** themselves—and they are not passive. They are agents of entropy and creation, secreting acids that slowly dissolve the vitrified industrial slag, turning mineral foam into soil. They grow through rivets, around foundations, through the hairline cracks in pre-stressed concrete, their waxy, translucent tissues pulsing with cold light. They possess a distributed consciousness, a chemical nervous system that spans entire districts, communicating through root-like networks that thread through the porous stone and the packed earth. They are patient where the machines are frantic. While the industry races to build and rebuild against the heaving ground, the Fronds work on a different timescale, weathering the stone, creating the conditions for their own expansion. They are farming the condensation, yes, but they are also farming the city itself—breaking it down, reclaiming it, ensuring that the boundary between the built and the grown remains forever blurred.

And finally, there is the **Valley**, which is less a place than a presence—a geological consciousness that dreams in thermal pulses and exhales mist. The inhabitants do not live *in* the valley; they live *as* the valley, extensions of its metabolism. The machines are its organs, churning with contained energy; the vegetation is its skin, breathing and glowing; the Jackers are its nervous system, feeling the tremors and adjusting; the Grazers and Mist-Wings are its blood and breath, moving through the dense, silent medium. It is a town-sized organism, a regional metabolism of accretion and condensation, and its inhabitants are cells in a body that has not yet finished being born.
