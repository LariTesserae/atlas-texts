They are the Chitin-Walkers, though they have no name for themselves—no word, only a rhythm. They are the ones who wear the armor of old bone, the slow giants who measure their lives in the falling of resin and the thickening of pools. Each is a mountain of segmentation, a column of overlapping plates that shift and grind against one another with a sound too low for human ears, a geological creak of calcium and keratin. They stand as tall as a horse at the shoulder, but longer, their bodies tapering to a blunt, armored tail that drags a furrow in the ash. Beneath their translucent ventral plates—those pale, windowed sections of their underbellies—you can see the amber moving. It is not blood, but it pulses. It is their sap, their slow heart, the gold they have drunk from the pools thickening in the channels of their bodies, turning their interiors into reservoirs of viscous light.

They move not by walking but by settling. A creature will lift one leg—jointed, hooked, tipped with claws that catch in the papery bark of the mycelial stalks—and place it forward with the deliberation of a glacier calving. The foot finds purchase. The body shifts its weight, tons of armored flesh pressing down, compressing the mineral crust, leaving a depression that will remain for decades. Then, after a pause long enough for the resin to tremble on a nearby stalk, the next leg follows. They are patient beyond comprehension. A journey across the Grove—from the black glass edge to the center circle where the oldest stalks grow—might take a creature three years. They do not hurry. There is no urgency in a place where the fungi grow a hand’s width in twelve months and the lava beneath still holds its birth-heat.

They graze. Their mouths are not mouths but scrapers—mandibles of pale chitin that rasp against the bark, peeling it in sheets that curl like cigarette paper. They eat the papery layers, the fallen caps that have dropped from the canopy thirty meters above, and the resin itself. To drink, they do not lap. They lower themselves onto the amber pools, pressing their ventral membranes against the viscous gold. The resin enters through osmosis, through channels in their armor, drawn up into the slow circulation of their bodies. You can watch a creature drinking: its plates ripple, a wave passing from stern to head as the amber fills its internal chambers, turning its underbelly from pale parchment to deep, glowing bronze.

They speak in tremors. There is no air for sound here—the atmosphere is too thick, too still, a medium that swallows vibration. Instead, they have the network. When a creature wishes to communicate, it raises its head—a slow, hydraulic motion—and strikes the mycelial stalk with the edge of its frontal plate. *Rap.* A sharp, crystalline tap. The vibration travels down into the root-fungus, through the underground web that connects every stalk in the Grove like nerves in a brain, and up into the feet of the creature on the other side. It feels the message in its tarsi, in the plates of its shell, in the resin pulsing through its own veins. The language is tactile, chemical, seismic. A single tap might mean *I am here.* Three taps, spaced precisely, might mean *the resin is fresh at the eastern pools.* A long, rolling vibration, felt in the sternum of every creature in the Grove, might mean *the heat is rising*—the obsidian shifting, the vents breathing, the world changing.

They have built the city not with tools but with presence. The corridors between the stalks are streets worn smooth by centuries of traffic, the ash and salt packed into a roadbed hard as pottery, stained dark with the resin that drips from above. In the lower trunks, they have gnawed chambers—hollows lined with softened bark and resin, warm and close, where they sleep in heaps, their bodies interlocking like puzzle pieces, sharing heat in the dim green-gold gloom. Higher up, they have made bridges. They smear resin across the gaps between stalks, and the mycelium, sensing the chemistry, grows to meet it, knitting a living walkway that thickens over years into a span strong enough to hold their weight. Ramps of hardened resin, grooves worn into the bark—these are the stairs and ladders of their world. It is a settlement of accumulation, not design. No creature planned the circle of stalks at the Grove’s heart, but generation after generation walked there, wore the path, gnawed the chamber, and the circle became sacred by the simple weight of habit.

They are long-lived. A creature might persist for three centuries, four, their metabolisms so slow that they seem almost geological themselves. They grow, but slowly—their plates adding a layer each decade, etched with rings like the trees they inhabit. When they die, they do not decay quickly. The dry air preserves them. Their bodies settle into the crust of ash and salt, becoming part of the floor, then the soil, then—over centuries—the resin itself, as the mycelium reclaims them, breaking down their chitin into the hydrocarbon soup from which new life drinks. Death is not an ending but a thickening. The Grove consumes its dead and grows richer.

Around them, smaller lives cling to the architecture they have built. On the shelving caps above, sessile filter-feeders strain spores from the air—blooms of pale tissue that open and close with the slow rhythm of tides, silent anemones of the fungal canopy. In the crevices of the bark, crusts of mineral-lichen grow in the resin-slick cracks, painting the stalks with maps of silver and rust. And in the hollows, in the dark chambers where the Chitin-Walkers sleep, tiny insects skitter—mute, quick things that are almost the opposite of their hosts, living fast lives in the slow world, cleaning the plates of the giants, eating the dead skin they shed, their own communication a frantic, high-frequency tapping that the big creatures feel as a tickle, a background hum in the network.

The Chitin-Walkers are the Grove’s consciousness. They feel it all—the pulse of the vents below, the growth of the stalks above, the chemistry of the resin, the pressure of the thick air. They think in waves, in pressure gradients, in the slow language of touch. To be one of them is to be rooted and roaming at once, to carry the forest inside your feet, to know the entire Grove as a single, trembling organism. They are patient. They are ancient, though the world is young. And they are still building, still tapping, still drinking the gold, layer by layer, tap by tap, into the deep, silent dark.
