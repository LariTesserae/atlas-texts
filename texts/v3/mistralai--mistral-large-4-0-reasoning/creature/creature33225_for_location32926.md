They are the negative space made flesh, the dark punctuation marks drifting through the bright grammar of growth. You find them only after your eyes adjust to the immensity, after you stop looking for villages and start looking for *difference*—a stillness among the motion, a geometry that does not belong to the spiraling plants. They are tall, or perhaps they only seem tall because they stand erect in a world of arches and collapses, their bodies vertical splints of charcoal and ash-grey against the bone-white architecture. Their limbs are long, too long, jointed in ways that suggest they were designed to reach across the hollows rather than walk through them, to span the gaps between islands of vegetation where no bridge yet exists. They wear cloaks of woven lichen, grey-green and stiff with frost, which makes them resemble the cairns they build—living markers that have paused to breathe.

They do not speak. The silence here is absolute, a physical weight that would swallow any vibration, and so they have evolved away from sound. Instead, they communicate through pressure and gesture, through the language of touch that is transmitted along the lashed-fiber bridges they traverse. A touch on the shoulder means *wait*; a tap against stone means *danger* or *change*; the offering of a flake of stone, held out flat in the palm, is a greeting, a question, a transaction. When they move, it is with a viscosity that matches the cold—each step sinking slightly into the peat-carpet, each motion deliberate, unhurried, as if time itself has thickened in the near-freezing air. They are not slow because they are weak, but because they are patient, because they have learned that to rush is to outpace the world, to arrive at a destination that no longer exists because the plant that formed the path has already collapsed or grown over.

Their hands are their civilization. They have six fingers, or perhaps eight, the extra digits thin and sensitive, built for the weaving of fiber and the flaking of stone. They carry no packs, no permanent tools; instead, they work with what the world offers at the moment of need. A flat rock becomes a knife when struck against another stone; a coil of fiber, stripped from the outer membrane of a young fungal shoot, becomes cordage. The bone they use for hafting is not from prey—there are no beasts here but themselves—but from the plants. When a massive structure collapses, it leaves behind a lattice of calcified, bone-white vascular tissue, the skeleton of the growth. They harvest this, scraping it clean, using it to bind stone to fiber, to create handles for their cutting tools, to make the needles with which they sew lichen into curtains.

They build nothing permanent because permanence is a violence here. Their structures are waypoints, ephemeral as the mist. A platform of lashed poles wedged into the hollow of a growth will serve for a week, perhaps two, before the plant swells and crushes it, or before the structure it clings to folds inward in its silent collapse. They build cairns of white stones on ledges—not graves, but signals, coordinates in a landscape that shifts hourly. A shelter built into the chambered interior of a massive trunk will be warm for a season, the walls weeping condensation that they drink, the entrance hung with a curtain of lichen that filters the bright sky-light into a dim, green gloom. But they know the trunk is dying even as they enter it; the rapid growth is also rapid decay, and soon the chamber will be sealed by new tissue, or the whole pillar will fold like origami into the abyss. They do not mourn this. They simply move on, leaving their tools behind—a stone knife on a flat rock, a coil of cordage—knowing that another will find them, or that the peat will swallow them, or that the next growth will bury them in living matter.

They are few. Perhaps a dozen in a space the size of a continent, perhaps a hundred. They do not gather. They are solitary, or they pair for a time and then separate, drifting apart across the kilometers of bright air, becoming silhouettes against the distant growth, then vanishing into the mist. They are adapted to the emptiness, to the psychological weight of space. A human would go mad here, in the cathedral with no walls but light, but they are built for it. Their eyes are large, milky, adapted to the diffuse glare, capable of seeing the slightest shift in the mist, the first tremor of a collapsing arch, the difference between a shadow cast by a plant and the shadow of another builder moving in the far distance.

They harvest. They cut the fast-growing shoots at the precise moment when the tissue is tender but strong, weaving it into bridges before the plant can grow past the need for one. They collect the condensation that weeps from the undersides of membranes, storing it in bladders of dried plant-skin, drinking the near-frozen water that tastes of stone and cold. They watch the spores drift like auroras, gray-green and luminous, and they know which ones to avoid, which ones will colonize their skin, their lungs, turning them into another pillar, another island of growth.

They are young, too, in a way. This is a pioneer people, a first generation, still learning the grammar of a world that is being born around them. They leave marks that are questions rather than statements—a cairn, a platform, a curtain of lichen—knowing that the rapid growth will soon obscure them, that their waypoints will become overgrown, their shelters sealed, their tools buried under centimeters of new peat by morning. They are the first to live in the hollow, the first to adapt to the silence, the first to understand that here, survival means becoming as transient as the water, as patient as the stone, as strange and beautiful and temporary as the light that presses down through the ice.
