They are the Hollow—not a name they use, for they have no voices, only a vocabulary of vibration and pressure, but the name fits the shape of them. They stand between six and eight feet tall when fully upright, though they rarely are; more often they are folded, knees to chest, elbows resting on the reed platforms, their spines curved like the fronds of the ferns that grow from the caldera wall. Their skin is translucent, the color of wet parchment or the pale fish that swim in the channels, stretched tight over bones that are honeycombed and light, hollow as reeds, filled not with marrow but with a spongy tissue that drinks the thin air. In the high-altitude light, filtered through the mist, they seem to glow faintly from within, a bioluminescence that pulses slow as a geological heartbeat—thump… thump… thump—once every three seconds.

They have no eyes. Or rather, they have eyes, but the eyes are closed, sealed behind smooth lids of skin the color of wet stone, and they have not opened them in generations, perhaps never. Instead, they navigate the world through the water that saturates everything. Their fingers are long, jointed in three places, webbed at the base, and they trail them constantly in the channels or press them flat against the walkways, reading the tremors of the settlement like a blind man reads braille. The world is loud to us—a roar of weeping cliffs and drumming rain—but to them it is a text, a constant murmur of information carried through the peat and the reed and the stone. They feel the approach of another not by sight but by the disturbance in the water table, a ripple in the mist, a change in the groan of the walkways.

They are silent. Truly silent. They do not speak, do not sing, do not even breathe audibly. Their lungs, if they can be called that, are gill-slits that flutter along their ribcages, filtering oxygen from both air and water, and they make no sound when they respire. When they communicate, they do so by drumming—knuckles against the reed-thatched roofs, palms against the tamped earth of the aqueducts, creating subsonic pulses that travel through the saturated ground and are felt in the sternum rather than heard. A conversation between two of them is a duet of vibrations, a complex rhythm of taps and pauses that can carry across the empty reed-beds, a Morse code of need and maintenance and memory.

Their bodies are always wet. They do not sweat; they weep. A constant, slow exudation of moisture that keeps their skin slick and makes them difficult to grasp. They move through the settlement with a swaying gait, never fighting the tilt of the platforms or the sway of the walkways, but flowing with them, as if they were reeds themselves, rooted in the peat, growing upward as the ground sinks beneath them. They are patient beyond human measure. They can stand motionless for hours, watching the mist, waiting for the water to rise an inch, feeling the peat compress beneath their splayed, many-toed feet.

They are the architects of the jacking. When the platforms sink—a hand’s-breadth each year, sometimes more in the wet seasons—they do not panic. They gather in the silence of the dawn, when the mist is thickest, and they work as a single organism. They slide levers of hardened wood beneath the stilts, their translucent skin rippling with effort, and they lift the structures higher, adding new layers of woven reed, new shims of obsidian-flaked stone. It is a communal act, but there are no words, no coordination shouted across the roar of the water. They simply feel the need, the pressure of the sinking, and they respond, each knowing their place in the geometry of the lift, their hollow bones creaking in unison, a chord of maintenance that keeps the city above the flood.

They live in the voids. The settlement is dense with infrastructure but sparse with bodies, and this suits them. They prefer the hollow spaces, the negative geometry between the walkways, the empty pools of mist that surround each platform. They sleep in niches carved from the soft volcanic stone, curled into spheres that fit exactly into the cavities, their knees drawn to their chins, their sealed eyes facing the dark. They do not build private rooms so much as burrows, extensions of the caldera itself. The districts of sleeping platforms are not neighborhoods but organs—each cluster a node in a nervous system that is the entire hamlet.

They eat what the caldera provides. They fish the pale, eyeless creatures from the channels with nets woven from reed fiber, spears of sharpened bone or obsidian. They cultivate the moss, harvesting it in thick, jade slabs from the stone, farming it in the terraced gardens where the slope allows. They eat the young shoots of the spiral reeds, and they drink the water that weeps from the cliffs, filtering it through their gills and their porous skin. They have no agriculture of grain or meat; they are too slow for that, too much a part of the wet and the stone. They are, in a sense, detritivores, consuming the rot and the new growth, the scab and the healing.

The fauna is absent because the Hollow are the fauna, the apex and the only. They are the predators that cleared the caldera of birds and insects, not through violence but through presence—a frequency they emit, a vibration in their hollow bones that makes the air uncomfortable for wings and chitin, that sterilizes the mist of spores and eggs. Or perhaps they simply waited, with geological patience, until the ecosystem realized that only they could survive here, and the rest retreated or died. The tracks of something small in the mud—those are their children, born small and wet, no larger than a cat, growing slowly over decades into the tall, thin adults. Or perhaps the tracks are made by the tools they carry, obsidian knives and flint hammers, dragged through the peat as they move from platform to platform.

They are becoming the rock. This is the secret of their biology, their slow transformation. Their bones are calcifying, turning to stone, incorporating the minerals from the water they drink and the moss they eat. They shed their skin like snakes, leaving behind hollow husks that crumble into the peat, adding to the soil. When they die—if they die, for they live so long—they do not bury their dead. They place them in the highest granaries, on the stilts that reach toward the thin air, and they let them dry, let them become part of the structure, mummified by the mist and the salt, eventually indistinguishable from the reed and the stone. The settlement is built of the living and the dead, the jacked-up platforms held aloft by the calcified remains of their ancestors, a city of bone and reed.

They perceive the wrongness of the place—the water flowing upward, the spiral reeds, the mist that forms shapes of people—not as anomalies, but as the natural order. To them, gravity is a suggestion, time is a texture they can taste in the water, and the boundary between self and settlement is as permeable as their skin. They are the caldera’s immune system, the scab that seals the wound, the consciousness of the geology as it forms. They do not rush because they are the pause between the cracking of the rock and its erosion, the still point in the turning world, hollow, loud with silence, sinking slowly into the peat, jacking themselves up, again and again, forever.
