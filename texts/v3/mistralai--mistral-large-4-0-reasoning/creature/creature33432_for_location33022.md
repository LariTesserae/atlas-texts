They are the **Packed**, though they have no name for themselves that is not also a verb—something like *to-be-layered*, *to-be-mortared*, *to-sleep-in-water*. They are the builders, the maintainers, the cells of the city-organism, and they have evolved (or been bred, or have simply become) creatures of the wet and the mineral.

**Their Bodies**

They are humanoid in the way that a river is humanoid in the way it carves a canyon—broadly similar, but sculpted by pressure and flow. They stand perhaps five feet tall when fully extended, but they rarely stand fully; the alleys are shoulder-width, the crawlways lower still, and so they move with a sinuous, sinuous crouch, their spines flexible as reeds. Their skin is the color of wet terracotta—a pale, warm gray-pink that never dries, beaded perpetually with condensation that they do not wipe away. It is their second lung; they breathe through it, absorbing the mineral-saturated moisture directly, which is why the thick air does not suffocate them as it would a dry creature.

Their eyes are enormous, lidless, and black as wet slate, reflecting the faint green bioluminescence of the slime-mold colonies that coat the walls. They have no hair; their scalps are smooth and domed, perhaps shaved with copper razors in some ancient ritual, or perhaps they simply never grew it, having no need for insulation in the constant 85-degree heat. Their fingers are long and webbed, the skin between them translucent and membranous, perfect for gripping wet brick and swimming through the flooded lower channels. They are plantigrade, their feet broad and splayed, toes sinking slightly into the always-damp clay floors.

They wear no clothes, for there is nothing to make them from—no cotton, no wool, no leather that would not rot instantly in this humidity. Instead, they are adorned with **copper**: thin bands hammered from native nuggets, wrapped around their wrists, ankles, and throats. The copper has reacted with their perpetually damp skin, staining them with a verdigris patina that matches the slime-molds on the walls. They are, in effect, walking pieces of the city’s own geology.

**Their Labor**

Their days—if days can be said to pass in the bruised twilight—are spent in **maintenance**. They are the keepers of the embryonic perfection, the artisans of stasis. Their work is to ensure that the "emerging erosion" never completes itself, that the sharp edges of the channels remain sharp, that the walls remain unweathered.

They move through the city in **crews of three to five**, their bodies pressed against the wet walls, feeling for vibrations. With stone adzes and copper chisels, they scrape the slime-mold colonies, harvesting them for food and for the iridescent slime they use as a waterproof sealant. They clear the channels of the chalky husks—the desiccated dead of the fauna that have not yet been consumed by the filter-feeding amphibians. They rebuild walls that have "fallen" (though the prompt suggests walls do not truly fall, they are ritually dismantled and re-laid, brick by identical brick, to maintain the illusion of newness).

They are **fisher-folk of the deep**. In the standing water that fills every depression, they harvest the pale, eyeless crustaceans the length of forearms, cracking their shells with stone hammers. They tend to the giant snails, the size of fists, with nacreous spiraled shells, guiding them across the walls with gentle taps, collecting their iridescent slime trails in reed baskets. The snails are not merely food; they are **sacred geometry**, the living spirals that the city’s architecture imitates. The inhabitants believe the snails were the first builders, or perhaps the valley itself in a larval form.

They drink constantly. They have no wells, for the water table presses up from below. Instead, they drink from the **drip**—holding their mouths beneath the silver threads that fall from ten thousand eaves, or pressing their porous skin against the seeping walls to absorb the mineral water directly.

**Their Society**

There is no privacy. The walls are thin enough to transmit sound, and so every breath, every heartbeat, every movement is audible to neighbors. They have no concept of solitude, only **density**. They live in **cells**—small, dome-shaped chambers hollowed from the packed earth, each opening onto a crawlway like a pore opening onto a channel.

They communicate not with voices—the roar of the water would drown any speech—but with **rhythm**. They carry copper chisels not only for work but for **tapping**. A sharp strike against a wall sends a vibration through the terracotta, amplified by the dense mineral mass, traveling for miles through the rhizome of habitation. A message might be a simple pattern: *tap-tap-rest-tap*, meaning *water rising*, or *tap-tap-tap*, meaning *a wall requires rebuilding here*. They have a grammar of pressure and resonance, a language spoken through the bones of the city itself.

They are **communal breeders**. The young are born in the **Warm Pools**, shallow basins in the deepest parts of the city where the residual heat of the geology—the young land still cooling from its formation—keeps the water at a constant, womb-like temperature. The infants are born small, pale, and nearly translucent, with gill-like slits along their necks that slowly seal as they mature. They are fed on the bacterial films scraped from the walls and the nutrient-rich slime of the snails. They grow quickly, reaching adult size within a few cycles of the water’s rising and falling (though there are no tides, only the slow pulse of the water table).

**Death and the Mortar**

When a Packed one dies—and they die often, for their lives are short and laborious in the moist heat—they do not rot. The closed ecosystem of the valley has no bacteria of decay, only those of preservation and slow desiccation. Their bodies dry into chalky, brittle husks, white and fragile as eggshells.

But they are not left to desiccate in the pools. They are **incorporated**.

The living take the husk, grind it to powder with stone mortars, and mix it with lime and **animal glue**—the glue rendered from the desiccated dead of the crustaceans and snails, and perhaps, in secret rites, from the husks of their own kind. This mortar is packed into the walls. They are literally building the city from their dead. Every brick, every layer, contains the compressed, mineralized remains of generations. The "animal glue" that binds the slag and pumice is the essence of the Packed themselves, rendered down and recycled into the architecture.

This is why the city feels **new**. It is constantly growing from within, fed by the dead, yet because the material is always the same—clay, lime, bone-dust, glue—and because nothing weathers, nothing erodes, the city remains in a state of perpetual just-completion. The inhabitants are both the builders and the built.

**Their Perception of Time**

They do not experience time as a river flowing forward, but as **strata**. They count their lives in **layers**. When a new course of bricks is laid atop a wall, that is a generation. They know they are the "seventh layer" or the "twelfth layer" of their particular cell, but because the walls never scour, never pit, never age, they cannot tell how long ago the first layer was laid. A wall built yesterday is indistinguishable from a wall built a thousand layers ago.

They have no history, only **geology**. They are the geology now. They do not dream of the future, for the future is simply more of the same—more tapping, more rebuilding, more incorporation into the mortar. They do not mourn the past, for the past is indistinguishable from the present. They exist in the **eternal static present** of the warm, wet, deafening equilibrium.

**The Unfinished**

Among them dwell the **Unfinished**—the gelatinous, translucent amphibian-things that filter the water and send vibrations through the clay. The Packed do not eat them, nor do they build with them. They tend to them, keeping the Warm Pools clean for their sake. The Unfinished are voiceless, but the Packed believe they are **oracles**, or perhaps the souls of those who died before they could be packed into the walls, left to drift in the water, forever embryonic, forever waiting to be finished. They press their ears against the walls near the pools and listen to the vibrations the Unfinished send through the clay, interpreting them as prophecy, though the messages are always the same: *water, water, sleep, sleep*.

And so the Packed continue their labor—tapping, rebuilding, harvesting, drinking, dying, and being mortared into the walls, becoming the city, becoming the valley, becoming the place where water sleeps, forever new, forever finished, forever under construction.
