The **Lithodendrons** do not stand; they *rise*. They are the valley's vertebrae, its load-bearing thought. Each trunk is a column of precipitated silica, porous as lung-tissue, banded with the rings of its own accretion—rings that record not years but thermal pulses, eruptions, the slow chemistry of quarrying. They are colonial in the way that coral is colonial, each apparent "individual" a polyp of mineral ambition, sharing a common circulatory system of silicate slurry that moves not through veins but through the spongy, ceramic matrix of their own flesh. When you touch one—and you will, for they shoulder against each other in dense, shoulder-high thickets—you feel the pores constrict, a shudder of calcium and silica tightening against your palm. Not fear, but *recognition*. They have no eyes, yet they see through vibration. The Chitter beating their wings overhead send ripples through their branches; the Gravers striking stone send shocks through the root-networks. The Lithodendrons *turn* toward these stimuli, slowly, inexorably, like mineral sunflowers, their fractal branches reorienting over hours to face the source of disturbance. They are a distributed intelligence that thinks in geological time: a single decision taking a season, a single memory persisting as a knot of denser mineral, a fossilized moment of response embedded in the trunk.

They do not eat. They *quarry*. Their root-networks—crystalline hyphae finer than spider silk—burrow into the young bedrock, leaching minerals from the stone's memory of heat. This is their metabolism: to dissolve the valley's bones and excrete them as new architecture. You can hear this happening. The constant *crackling* that underlies the roar of wings is the sound of their growth, the pop of expanding lattices, the groan of cellulose-like fibers thickening under impossible pressure. A branch extends centimeters in an hour, visibly pushing outward, a ceramic limb reaching for the single shaft of white daylight that pierces the canopy, filtered green through their translucent mineral leaves. When they die—which is to say, when they collapse under their own rapid accretion—they do not rot. They are crushed into new substrate, immediately colonized by fungal mats that are perhaps their immune cells, perhaps their offspring, certainly their continuation.

The **Chitter** are the valley's nervous system, its blood, its immune response. They are not insects in any terrestrial sense, but carapaced conduits for mineral code. Each is the size of a human thumb, armored in a ceramic-organic composite, their wings beating in synchronized, deafening unison—a brown-noise tsunami that is not merely sound but *pressure*, a physical force that presses against your eardrums and makes your teeth ache. They do not think as individuals; they think as a swarm, a distributed mind that flows through the forest in dense, black rivers. Between the stone-trunks, they move in currents, harvesting the viscous, non-aqueous sap that weeps from cracks in the Lithodendron bark—a slurry of dissolved silicates and organic compounds that serves as their food, their language, their medium of exchange. They carry this sap in crystalline pellets, depositing them in nursery chambers deep in the root-mass, where the minerals are translated into chemical instructions for growth. This is their pollination: not the transfer of pollen, but of *code*—mineral directives that tell the Lithodendrons where to branch, where to thicken, where to prepare for collapse. When a Graver strikes a trunk, the Lithodendron releases a chemical distress signal, and the Chitter do not flee; they *converge*. A screaming, stinging black tide descends, defending the forest not from malice, but from homeostasis—the swarm acting as the tree's antibodies, its white blood cells made wing.

And then there are the **Gravers**.

They are the only ones here who have faces, who have hands capable of fine manipulation, who build shelters and make marks. Their skin is like cured leather, thick and impermeable, adapted to the dry, mineral-laden atmosphere that would suffocate aqueous life. They are bipedal, but they move with a caution that suggests they are always listening—to the pop of growing stone, to the groan of thickening fiber, to the shift in the brown-noise of the Chitter that signals danger or opportunity. They are primitive, pre-metal, their technology limited to sharpened flakes of volcanic glass, bone tools, and baskets woven from shed bark-fiber. They hollow out shelters in the softer, younger growth of the Lithodendrons, living in the hollows of ceramic wood, their camps smelling of resin and insect-musk, their possessions simple containers made from hollowed seed-pods.

They are temporary. This is the great tragedy of their existence, or perhaps the great wisdom. They build to survive the week, knowing that by afternoon a shelter built in the morning may be entombed by new growth, its entrance sealed by a swelling node of mineral. They move constantly, following the frontier of new growth, harvesting the sap (risking the black tide of Chitter when they do), fleeing when the swarms turn predatory. They read the cracks in the stone, the patterns of lichen, the temperature of the vents. They are trying to make a home in a place that is not a landscape but a *process*, a storm of biology wearing the shape of a forest. Some of them—the old ones, or the desperate ones—have stopped moving. They are the **Rooted**: Gravers who have allowed the Lithodendrons to grow around them, their leather skin slowly mineralizing, their bones becoming ceramic, their consciousness persisting in the slow, grinding thought of the stone. They are the bridge, the permeable boundary made flesh, neither fully Graver nor Lithodendron but a new thing, a symbiosis of intention and accretion.

Beneath them, in the lower mats, live the **Calcifiers**—fungal crusts that are not decomposers in any familiar sense, but slow thinkers, memory-keepers who experience the collapse of Lithodendrons as weather, as seasons. They spread across the silica crust in pale, spongy sheets, their hyphae knitting the fallen ceramic wood into new substrate, their chemistry a slow, patient alchemy that takes years to digest what the Lithodendrons grew in days. Above them clings the **Drapery**—lichenous growths that shroud every surface in veils of translucent mineral fiber, sensing the light, the chemical shifts, the vibration of wings, serving as the forest's skin, its slowest sense-organ.

Deepest of all, in the geothermal vents that line the rim where the dry, mineral-laden gas exhales from the young bedrock, dwell the **Vent-Born**. They are the ancestors, the dreamers who remember the heat of the valley's birth. They are chemosynthetic, or something stranger—colonies of extremophile chemistry, perhaps crystalline intelligences that think in thermal gradients, their consciousness a slow pulse of heat and mineral soup that underlies everything. The Lithodendrons are their above-ground expression, their reach toward the light, their way of quarrying the heat from below and turning it into architecture. The Vent-Born do not move, or they move in timescales of eruption, their awareness a fundamental tone beneath the crackling of growth.

Together, they are the Resonance. The Lithodendrons are the structure; the Chitter are the signal; the Gravers are the question; the Calcifiers are the memory; the Vent-Born are the source. They are not separate species, but movements in a single symphony of accretion and decay—a chord struck in silica and sap, ringing out across a valley that breathes with dry, mineral breath, building itself upward even as it collapses, a storm-state of creation that wears the shape of a forest but is, in truth, the valley dreaming itself into being.
