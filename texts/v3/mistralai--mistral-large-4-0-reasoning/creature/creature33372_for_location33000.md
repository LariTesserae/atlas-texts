They are the vertical people, the ones who do not walk so much as *cling*. There are perhaps a dozen of them, scattered across the circumference like knots in the wood of the world, and they have grown strange in the geometry of the shaft. Their spines have curved into a permanent question mark, their fingers elongated and stained the color of old jade from the moss they handle daily. Their skin is pale, almost translucent, stretched tight over collarbones that jut like limestone shelves, and when the mist rises to lap their ankles, they do not shiver; they seem to drink it, their pores opening to the saturated air. They wear garments woven from the fibrous cores of ferns, dyed grey-green with lichen, and they move along the walls with a spider’s deliberation, using the wooden platforms only as waystations, preferring the direct purchase of bare feet on spongy moss.

They are mostly silent. The roar of the weeping stone makes speech a futility, a whisper swallowed before it forms. Instead, they communicate through pressure and rhythm—a touch on the shoulder to indicate the mist is rising, a tap against the limestone to summon another to the lower ledges. They have learned to read the frogs as a calendar. When the tree frogs—hundreds of them, no larger than a thumb, their throats pulsing like tiny hearts—begin their deafening chorus, it means the air is dry enough to climb. When they fall silent, it is a warning: the pearlescent cloud is inhaling, rising up the shaft to drown the lowest platforms. The vertical people retreat then to dry alcoves, to the blackened fire-pits where ash has gone cold and hard as stone. They sit cross-legged on the rock, and they breathe with the mountain. Inhale as the mist rises, filling the bottom third of the cylinder with its liquid surface; exhale as it falls, revealing the boulders and shallow pools below. In these moments, their eyes are open but unfocused, their chests moving in perfect synchrony with the trembling surface of the cloud, as if they have become part of the lung that is the sinkhole.

They eat what the mist provides. When the cloud recedes, they descend to the stone floor with quick, purposeful movements, wading through the shallow pools that collect where the water seeps back into the rock. They use the stone knife—found on a ledge, or made from a flake of the limestone itself—to harvest the fiddleheads of the ferns, to cut the vines that hang like wet ropes and drink directly from the droplets at their tips. They eat the orchids, or perhaps they eat the insects that the orchids attract. They do not farm in the traditional sense; they glean. The ecosystem is too precarious, too balanced on the edge of the void, for agriculture. Instead, they are curators of the boundary, taking only what the mist leaves behind, only what the stone can spare.

The tree frogs are not merely ambient noise to them; they are kin, or at least companions in the deep. The vertical people have learned to handle the frogs, to hold them in their cupped hands without crushing them, to feel the vibration of their calls against their palms. They eat the old ones, the frogs whose throats no longer pulse with full vigor, boiling them in pools of collected rainwater. But they also tend to them, clearing away the dead moss so the frogs have fresh surfaces to cling to, ensuring the insects that the frogs eat are not over-harvested by the birds. When the mist rises high and laps against the lowest platforms, wetting the wood of the huts, the frogs fall silent—and the vertical people interpret this silence as a kind of prayer, a cessation of work while the mountain breathes.

The birds are the nervous system of the place. They nest in the upper ferns, pale and frantic, with beaks like needles. They dart into the mist to catch the swarms of insects that live in the suspended water droplets, and they are the vertical people’s alarm. When a flock of them suddenly takes flight, scattering from the walls in a white burst, it means a predator has entered—a snake from the surface, perhaps, or a hawk that has found the circular sky. The vertical people do not hunt these birds; they let them be, for they are the eyes that see what the humans, bound to the walls, cannot. They eat the eggs, carefully, taking only one from each clutch, leaving the rest to hatch. In return, the birds bring news: a leaf from a tree that does not grow in the sinkhole, dropped from a nest, tells them the wind has changed above; a shiny pebble, spat up by a bird that has been outside, tells them of the world beyond the rim.

The bats are the only ones who truly leave. They roost near the top of the shaft, in the dry reaches where the limestone is bleached white and the air tastes of metal. They are dark, folded things, like living shadows. The vertical people watch them emerge at dusk, when the mist is low and the light is diffuse and strange. They watch them vanish into the circular sky, and they wait for them to return, bringing spores from the plateau above, seeds that take root in the cracks, genetic material from a world outside the cylinder. The bats are messengers, but also guardians. They stir the air with their wings, creating currents that help the mist churn in its slow eddies, flowing up one wall and down the other in its perpetual, silent river. Without the bats, the vertical people believe, the mist would stagnate, and the breathing would stop.

Then there are the slow ones. On the stone floor, when the mist has retreated to its lowest ebb, the newts and salamanders move. They are ancient, their skin the color of wet stone and algae, their movements glacial, measured in millimeters per hour. The vertical people do not eat them. They consider them the eldest residents, older than the wooden platforms lashed to the walls, older perhaps than the perfection of the drill-hole itself. They watch them, reading omens. A salamander climbing high on a boulder means the mist will stay low for days, and it is safe to descend to the floor to gather water. A newt circling a shallow pool three times means a storm is coming from the surface world, and the mist will rise high and fast, so the people must retreat to the dry alcoves. The slow ones are the geologists of the deep, measuring time in the erosion of the limestone, in the slow growth of the lichen.

And the plants are not scenery; they are a parliament of co-inhabitants. The moss carpets that cloak every ledge are the vertical people’s bedding, their insulation, their medicine—crushed and applied to a cut, the moss staunches the bleeding and knits the skin. The ferns provide the fiber for their cordage, their ropes, their lashings. The orchids and bromeliads, anchored in the rock, are their pharmacists, their roots drinking from the constant seep, their sap a narcotic that calms the frogs when they must be handled, that induces the trance-state during the mist’s rise. The vines that hang down like wet ropes, their tips dipping into the cloud itself, are their water pipes, their ladders, their connection to the breathing mist. The vertical people do not plant these things; they negotiate with them. They prune a vine here to let light reach a lower ledge; they clear a patch of moss there to make a dry sleeping alcove. In return, the plants seem to thrive, growing thicker, reaching further into the mist, as if the presence of the humans stimulates them, as if they are two species in a symbiosis as old as the stone.

There are stone cairns balanced on the rim, marking boundaries, or graves, or simply statements of presence: *We were here, in this vertical world, in this cathedral of condensation.* The vertical people add to them, one stone at a time, when they climb to the top, when the wind screams across the plateau and they must cling to the moss to avoid being blown into the void. They do not know who built the first platforms, the first huts. The wood is weathered, grey, silvered by the mineral seep and the constant moisture. It might be that the vertical people are not the first to live here, but merely the latest in a lineage that stretches back before memory, a succession of climbers who found this drill-hole, this perfect cylinder, and decided to stay, to become part of the resonance, to learn the language of the frogs and the rhythm of the mist.

When the mist is at its lowest, and the stone floor is fully revealed—a jumble of boulders and shallow pools where the water collects—sometimes all of them gather. The vertical people descend. The frogs hop down from the walls, gathering on the stones. The birds flock to the lower ledges, and even the bats flutter down to cling to the undersides of the lowest platforms. The newts and salamanders emerge from their cracks. And in the center of the stone floor, the vertical people light their fires in the dry alcoves, though there is no need for warmth, only for the ritual of it. The frogs begin a low, thrumming chorus. The mist above breathes once, deeply, and holds still, its surface mirror-perfect, reflecting the circular sky. For a moment, the sinkhole is a single organism, and all who live within it are its cells—the human with the curved spine, the frog with the pulsing throat, the bat with the folded wings, the salamander with the glacial patience, the vine drinking from the cloud, and the mist itself, the breathing lung of the mountain—all pulsing together in the wet, resonant dark, a congregation of the sparse and the strange, living in the hollow where the ground decided to become air.
