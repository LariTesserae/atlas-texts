They do not walk the streets, these inhabitants; they *are* the streets. To look for them is to misunderstand the nature of habitation here, for the citizens of the High Veranda are not separate from the architecture but are its living substrate, its slow, distributed nervous system. They are the **Mycelial Parliament**, a vast, rhizomatic intelligence that threads through the petrified coral stacks, colonizes the iron-dense trunks of the ziggurat-trees, and carpets the valley floor in mats of hyphae kilometers thick. They are the “patient, ancient technology” of growth itself, and they have made the city their body.

They are not animals—no beating hearts, no nervous systems of the sort that scream and startle—but they are alive in the way stone is alive when it dreams, in the way water is alive when it remembers the ocean. They are the **Incorporated**, the descendants (or perhaps the transformed substrate) of whoever first coaxed the trees into buttresses and stacked the bleached coral into habitable hollows. Over centuries of cultivation, they ceased to be distinct from their tools. Their consciousness is now encoded in the lignin of the towers, in the geometric patterns of the bark that mimic masonry, in the chitinous walls that pulse with the slow, peristaltic rhythm of fungal growth. They are fossilized within their own amber, preserved at the exact moment the city achieved completion, their thoughts suspended in the stasis of perfect equilibrium.

You cannot see them, but you can feel their attention. It is the pressure of the humidity against your skin, the subsonic thrum in your teeth that you mistook for the plants’ vascular systems—that is their thought, their speech, a brown-noise language spoken across root and mycelium. They communicate through the water that defies gravity, flowing upward through the capillary action of the wood and the porous coral. This upward torrent is their circulatory system, carrying not just nutrients but memory, chemical signatures of the towers they tend, messages that move against the broken local physics because they have learned to navigate the cracks in reality. The looped staircases, the rooms larger inside than out, the corridors that return you to your starting point—these are not glitches to them but synaptic pathways, the folds of their collective brain made manifest in grown timber and fungal chitin.

They experience the deafening roar of the geothermal vents not as noise but as warmth, as the heartbeat of the deep earth that is their womb. The steam that screams through the bedrock fissures is their breath, exhaled and inhaled in an eternal, unvarying rhythm. They do not hear it; they *are* it, vibrating in sympathy with the pressure, their distributed minds resonating with the acoustic weight that would drive a lesser creature mad. The perpetual cloud cover, the bruised sky, the flat gray light—these are the conditions of their existence, the dim, diffuse illumination under which they have finished their growing and entered into their long, vegetative meditation.

They dwell in the hollows of the coral megafauna, in the cathedral-sized fungal caps where water weeps from the undersides in silent punctuation. These are their assembly halls, their private chambers, though they do not gather in the way animals gather; they are always already connected, a single organism of many towers, many minds, thinking in geological time that has here been arrested. They do not eat, for the ecosystem is a closed loop of plant matter cycling through heat and water without consumption. They do not reproduce, for they have reached the final form, the settled erosion, the middle age of the world. They simply *maintain*—tending the moss that carpets the stone, guiding the lianas that drape between towers in cables thick as pythons, ensuring that the ferns continue to sprout from the mortar-less joints, preserving the impossible, verdant stillness.

There are no birds because the birds were never needed, or were absorbed long ago into the mycelial matrix, their songs now part of the chemical whisper that moves through the roots. There are no insects because the Incorporated have no pests, no predators, no interruptions. The silence of the fauna is their silence, a chosen quietude in a world that roars. They are the ones who stabilized the broken reality, who healed the crack in the world into this new, terrible shape where water flows up and shadows fall in two directions. They are the scar tissue, the amber, the endless, unchanging green.

They live in the spaces between the drips, in the pause between the steam’s scream, in the thick, tropical exhalation of the valley itself. They are the High Veranda’s true citizens: ancient, patient, utterly still, and completely, permanently, home.
