They are the **Grafted**—or the **Ten-Thousand-Year Hands**, though they possess no name for themselves that isn't written in chemistry. They move through the vast interior chambers of the Spires with the unhurried certainty of tides, their bodies wrong for any dry world. Their spines articulate like the branching lignin of the towers themselves—too many vertebrae, joints that bend sideways and backward, knees that lock in three directions. Fingers split into fine rootlets when pressed against living bark. Their skin is not integument but membrane: translucent, perpetually damp, hosting colonies of the same bioluminescent moss that lines the walls, so that in the green-gold twilight they glow faintly from within, soft constellations drifting through the dark.

They do not speak. Sound is a violence here, dissipated by the saturated air before it can form meaning. Instead, they carry language in secretion. Glands in their palms, throats, and the soft membranes behind their ears weep complex signatures—pheromones dense as resin, drying into visible trails on the bark: gold, silver, phosphorescent lines that record not words but states of being, memories of root-pressure and rainfall. A conversation is a pressing of palm to palm, an exchange of chemical autobiography. When they must call across the cathedral voids, they drum their hollow chests, producing subsonic pulses that travel through the spongy floor and resonate in the marrow, a vocabulary of vibration felt rather than heard.

They are gardeners, but the cultivation is mutual, a negotiation across millennia. They sleep in hammocks of fungus-silk, suspended in the gravity-pools where "down" is negotiable, their rootlet-fingers trailing into the sphagnum to drink. Their dreams—if they dream—are absorbed by the root-mats, becoming nutrient and memory. When one dies, they do not bury the body. They are *grafted*. Lowered into the compressed dark, the Spire grows around them over decades, their bones thickening into buttresses, their nervous systems threading through the mycelium like lightning caught in amber. The oldest Spires contain the accumulated consciousness of generations, a slow green thought that dreams in the pulse of the moss, that remembers the weight of flint scrapers and the geometry of bone awls from ten thousand years past.

But they are not alone. In the vast empty spaces—the 0.5 density of still, damp air—glide the **Membrane-Wings**. These are not birds. They possess too many joints: elbows that bend in three planes, wrists that rotate fully, spines like articulated chains of chitin and silk. They hang inverted from the cupped leaves of the upper canopy, drinking condensation, their wings folded like wet paper, membranes stretched between fingers that number twelve or more. They are the couriers, the pollinators, the living bridges between Spires too distant for a stride. When they move, they do not flap but *undulate*, rippling through the fog, their bodies refracting the dim light into brief rainbows that dissolve before touching the ground. They carry the chemical messages in specialized pouches, their flight paths tracing invisible geometries through the humidity, delivering spores and pheromones across kilometers of mist.

Beneath the spongy floor, in the root-mats, thrives the **Subsonic Choir**—vast colonies of insects not six-legged but fractal, their exoskeletons like carved flint and resin, nesting in the compressed dark. They are the true editors of the architecture, perhaps, their mandibles guided by the chemical signals of the Grafted, chewing dead wood into hollows, shaping the non-Euclidean spaces over centuries. They hum—a frequency felt in the teeth, a vibration that makes the condensation droplets dance on the moss. They are the memory of the place, their colonies persisting for generations, recording the history of the Labyrinth in the spiraling patterns of their tunnels, their collective biomass vast as weather.

And permeating everything, connecting everything, is the **Mycelium**—not an inhabitant but the medium itself, the nervous system of the continent. It threads through the Grafted when they sleep, through the Spires, through the root-mats, through the bodies of the Membrane-Wings. It carries water and nutrients, yes, but also information—a fungal network older than language, transmitting the temperature of the hollows, the pressure of the gathering fog, the dreams of the sleeping gardeners. When rain falls upward, caught by thermal currents, it is the mycelium that knows first, that signals the fungus-silk membranes to tighten, that warns the insects to seal their chambers.

To live here is to exist in a state of perpetual dissolution and regrowth. The Grafted have no concept of "outdoors"—the boundary between body and architecture dissolved ten thousand years ago. When they walk on ceilings, following the gravity that has pooled in the hollows above, they do not experience inversion. They simply follow the pull, their moss-skin drinking the condensation that beads on the inverted floor, their rootlet-fingers gripping the bark. They are at home in the softness of reality, in the way staircases deliver them to impossible heights, in the corridors that are longer inside than out.

They move through the silence as through water—thick, buoyant, full of signals invisible to the eye. The only sounds are the *plop* of a droplet falling fifty meters, taking ten seconds to land; the subsonic thrum of the insect colonies; the slow, geological creak of bark thickening around a sealed doorway. They are a people of touch and chemical song, of slow gestures that take hours to complete, of lives measured in the growth of lignin rather than the beating of hearts.

They are waiting for something. Or perhaps, having become indistinguishable from the Spires they shaped, they are the waiting itself—a green twilight held in breath, dreaming in mycelium, growing ever so slowly toward a geometry not yet born.
