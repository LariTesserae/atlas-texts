They are the children of the sweat.

The humans have adapted to the 46-degree cathedral in ways that would seem monstrous to surface-dwellers. Their skin has gone pale and translucent over generations—not from lack of sun, but from the constant press of moisture, the body learning that pigment is a luxury it cannot afford. They sweat constantly, a thin, mineral-salty film that never dries, keeping them perpetually slick and slightly luminous in the amber light. Their eyes have grown large and dark, pupils dilated to capture every photon from the sodium lamps and the bacterial glow. They are short and wiry, heat-efficient, with prominent clavicles and a peculiar stillness to their movements—a economy of motion learned from living in an oven.

They call themselves the *Wet Ones*, though the name is rarely spoken aloud, only scratched into the condensation on module walls or whispered in the dark. They have their castes. The *Shim-Setters*, who jack up foundations as the floor heaves, their hands calloused from handling hot stone. The *Cricket-Keepers*, who tend the harvest—men and women who wear mesh gloves and carry bamboo baskets, brushing the pale insects from the walls in slow, meditative sweeps, their faces serene beneath respirators that filter the grinding dust of chitin. The *Deep-Runners*, a taciturn order who descend to the sump pools in the hours before dawn, when the blind fish rise to feed, their bodies wrapped in thermal blankets, their eyes covered even though the darkness is absolute—habit, or superstition, or respect for the things that live in the black water.

Children are born here without ever seeing the sky. They are small and heat-hardened, playing in the mist near the condensate tanks where the air is thickest, their games involving the tracking of cricket waves, the betting of rations on how long it takes a fungal bloom to fruit. They speak a creole that has swallowed the drone of the pumps into its grammar—sentences that rise and fall with the thrum-thrum-thrum of the machinery three levels down. They know the walls by touch before they know them by sight, their palms reading the acid-sweat patterns, the temperature gradients, the places where the rock is youngest and softest as a child might read Braille.

Then there are the crickets themselves—*Tettigonia fossilis*, though the taxonomists who first catalogued them are long dead and their classifications have dissolved like the limestone around them. They are not a colony so much as a tissue. Billions of pale, eyeless bodies, each one a nerve ending, a sensor for moisture and vibration and the chemical signature of decay. They graze the fungal mats with mandibles that never stop moving, a sound like rain on tin but closer, wetter, *shhhhhhh*, the sound of the cavern eating itself. They breed in the warmest recesses, their eggs laid in the mineral crust where the heat is most intense, hatching into nymphs that are born already pale as bone, already blind. They do not fear the humans; they have never known anything else. When a harvester sweeps a section of wall, the wave passes through them—a peristaltic ripple of antennae and chitin that flows around the intrusion like water around a stone, then reforms. They are harvested for protein paste, ground into a nutty, mineral-tasting slurry that sustains the town, and in death they become the floor—compressed into the slurry of drilling mud and calcite, their bodies adding to the sediment that the cavern is slowly, patiently, laying down.

Beneath them, in the bacterial mats, lives a different kind of consciousness. The cyan and ghost-green slime that pulses on the wettest walls is not merely alive; it is *thinking*, if thinking can be said to occur at the pace of geology. These chemolithotrophic colonies metabolize the sulfur and iron leaching from the rock, their bioluminescence a byproduct of their respiration—a slow, tidal glow that waxes and wanes over hours. They form networks, filaments of communication threading through the damp stone, trading electrons and chemical signals in a language older than the cavern itself. The Wet Ones sometimes press their ears to the walls, listening not with sound but with vibration, trying to catch the slow pulse of this subterranean mind. Some say the mats are the true architects here, that the cavern is not being dissolved by acid but *digested*, the bacteria calling the chemistry, guiding the water, sculpting the cathedral drop by embryonic drop.

In the dark pools below—the sump pools, the throat of the world—the blind fish wait. They are fat and pink, eyeless, the size of a forearm, cousins to nothing that lives in light. They have no color because they have no need of it; their skin is translucent, showing the dark flutter of organs beneath, the slow pulse of hearts that beat once every thirty seconds. They are ancient, or seem so, having evolved in the deep vents where the superheated water first meets the cooler stone, feeding on the cricket-fall, the bodies of the dead that rain down from above like manna. They come up after dark, when the town sleeps, rising through the vents to graze on the bacterial mats that line the pool edges, their mouths working silently in the black water. The Deep-Runners know their rhythms; they know which pools are safe to enter and which are hunting grounds. The fish do not attack the humans, but there is a hunger in their stillness, a patience that predates the town, the crickets, perhaps even the cavern itself.

The fungi are the connective tissue. The white, cottony blooms that fruit on the ceiling are not plants but the fruiting bodies of vast mycelial networks that thread through the rock, through the walls of the housing modules, through the bodies of the dead crickets, through the boots of the workers. They are the town's immune system and its memory. When a section of wall is disturbed, the fungal network sends chemical signals through the stone, warning the crickets, thickening the bacterial mats, precipitating minerals to seal the wound. The Wet Ones harvest the blooms too, for medicine—a poultice that heals the acid burns, that soothes the bites of the crickets when they pinch. The fungi are neutral, symbiotic, a third party in the negotiation between human industry and geological time.

And then there are the things that live in the pipes. The thermophilic bacteria that coat the interior of the superheated fluid lines, extremophiles that thrive in temperatures that would boil surface life, forming mats of orange and rust-red slime that must be scraped out every few months. The algae that drape from the discharge pipes like sodden hair, photosynthesizing not in sunlight but in the infrared glow of the hot metal, their stringy filaments swaying in the thermal updrafts. The mites and springtails that live in the condensation, microscopic grazers on the biofilm, too small to be seen but present in their millions, a hidden layer of life eating the sweat of the walls.

The town breathes. It inhales through the intake vents, scraping the fungal growth with mechanical brushes that sing against the metal. It exhales through the discharge pipes, releasing warm, mineral-laden water that feeds the algae, that pools in the sumps, that slowly, over centuries, will become new limestone, new cavern, new darkness waiting to be filled. The humans are temporary, their modules bolted into soft stone that will dissolve around them in a few decades, perhaps a century. They know this. They build anyway, because the heat is power, and the power is light, and the light keeps the dark at bay—even as the dark, in the form of crickets and bacteria and blind fish, presses in from every side, patient and eternal, waiting for the pumps to fail, for the humans to leave, for the cavern to reclaim its own and continue its slow, embryonic birth into something new.
