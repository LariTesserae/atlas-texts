They are the ones who remained when the argument ended. Not trapped—*chosen*, or perhaps they were always part of the stone and only now, in the finished world, have they stopped pretending to be separate.

There are perhaps thirty of them, or three, or one—numbers become uncertain when time stops flowing. They move through the depression like sediment settling: without hurry, without destination, following paths that are both ancient habit and the only possible route. They are the maintainers, though there is nothing to maintain. They sweep the plazas with brooms made of the same wiry grass that grows motionless on the plateau above; the bristles never wear, the stone never dirties, yet the motion continues—a gesture preserved like a fossil, the action complete but the form eternal.

Their clothing is the color of the moss, the color of old bone, the color of the mist. It is not fabric but accretion: layers of lichen, dust, and the mineral residue of the condensation that coats everything. They wear the place as a garment. Their skin, where visible, has the satin sheen of the stone, the grey tone of the moth's wings. They have been here so long—or rather, they have become the "so long"—that the boundary between body and building has dissolved. A figure standing in a doorway is also the doorway; the lintel above their head is their thought made timber and slate. They are the congregation that has finished its service but remains in the pews, the ritual complete yet the posture held, like a bow that never straightens, like a prayer that has become the silence after it.

They do not speak, or if they do, their voices are the susurrus of wind through the channels, indistinguishable from the environment. They communicate, if communication is the word, through the placement of stones, through the angle of a head turned toward the cistern, through the pause at a particular threshold—the soft *tick* of a droplet forming and being reabsorbed, matched by the soft *tick* of a head turning to acknowledge it.

Some of them tend the moss. Not to encourage it—it does not spread—but to touch it, to acknowledge its existence in the only way available: by being still beside it, by breathing the same damp air, by being the moss's witness. Others watch the water. They stand at the channels where the film slides down over the course of an hour, and they watch it slide, their eyes reflecting the constant, shadowless light. They are the guardians of a flow that does not flow, the keepers of a cycle that has closed upon itself, their presence the final seal on the equilibrium.

There is one who sits in the central plaza, or perhaps has always sat there, indistinguishable from the paving stone beneath them. They are the heart of the settlement, the fixed point around which the others orbit in their slow, geological paths. They do not move. They have become the center of the amphitheater, the still point of the turning world that no longer turns, their blackened hearth-smoke long since dissipated into the mist they now resemble.

And there are the small lives, the true natives: the pale moth that loops between two specific stones, its flight path a Möbius strip of intention, disappearing into a crack only to re-emerge from the same crack, because there is no other; the grey bird that stands on the wall, turning its head in that rehearsed motion, now understood not as a search for food or mate, but as the only movement the world requires—a nod, an acknowledgment of the stasis, a turning toward the mist and away, forever.

Together, they form the population of the finished place. They are not alive in the way the world once understood life—with growth, with change, with the hunger for the next moment. They are alive as the stone is alive, as the moss is alive, as the condensation is alive: present, aware perhaps, but existing in the eternal present of completion. They are the inhabitants of the standing wave, the frozen equilibrium, the place that has forgotten entropy and, in doing so, has remembered how to simply be.
