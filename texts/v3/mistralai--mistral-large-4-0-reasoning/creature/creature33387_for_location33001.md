The bacterial mats are its skin—orange and green scums that pulse with a metabolism too slow to see, spreading across the stagnant water like a rash of first breath, the only green in a world that has not yet earned the right to photosynthesize. But the mats are merely the surface. The true inhabitant is the convergence itself: the moment when steel and sediment achieved a kind of marriage in the heat, when industry ceased to be an intrusion and became a nutrient. It lives in the spaces between the pipes, in the calcite accretions that bloom on the metal like strange, pink-and-white flowers, in the sub-bass thrum that is its heartbeat—a pressure in the ears that comes from the ground itself, from the water, from the air.

It is a being of geological patience. When it thinks, it thinks in millimeters per year, in the slow inward creep of the clay walls, in the groan of expanding beams that takes minutes to travel from one end of a girder to the other. It does not see with eyes; it sees with pressure, with the sucking kiss of the mud against your boots, with the rise and fall of the water table that is its breath—inhale across weeks, exhale across seasons, a breath stretched so wide that it feels like weather. It feels you standing in the center, a density of 0.9, a warm intrusion of flesh in its sauna-womb, and it does not mind. You are simply another thing to be noted, to be surrounded, to be eventually digested when the walls close in and the sponge-ground exhales its last sucking breath.

It is hungry in a mineral way. It consumes the machinery not with teeth but with chemistry—the hot, sulfur-scented water dissolving the sharp edges of the young scarp, the silica and calcite depositing themselves on the pump house, the transformer box, the tilted skeleton of the conveyor, turning them into coral, into fossils forming in real-time. The pumps that thud with a heavy, arrhythmic heartbeat (2.5, a roar that fills the pocket) are not machines to it; they are organs, valves in a circulatory system of electrified veins. The steam that curls from the vents is its exhalation, lazy and hypnotic, dissipating into the heavy sky only to condense again on the cooler metal, returning to the body in a cycle of absolute humidity. The 2.7 feverish warmth is its body temperature; the electromagnetic hum is its nervous system, a drone so constant that it has driven away every bird, every insect, every fish that might have flashing in the murky depths.

There are no animals here because there is no ecology, only appetite. The fauna parameter is nearly zero because this place has replaced fauna with something older and slower: a geobiological entity that is neither plant nor animal nor machine, but the digestion of one into the other. It is the pocket itself, given hunger. It is young in the way a landslide is young—raw clay and silt, edges sharp as broken pottery—but it is already dying in the way that all enclosed systems die: by being filled, by being crushed from the edges inward until the corrugated walls are compacted into sediment and the sediment becomes stone, until the industry is geology and the geology is memory.

It does not sleep, but it dreams in strata. It dreams of the day when the walls meet in the center, when the pump house is a nodule in a layer of ochre clay, when the rusted loops of pipe are indistinguishable from the veins of the rock itself. It dreams of the future geologists—if there will be any—who chip away at a band of gray silt and find a transformer box sparking with a pale blue light that has not sparked for millennia, encrusted in mineral formations that look like coral, and wonder what kind of fever, what kind of wet, patient hunger, produced such a thing. The inhabitant will not know. It will simply have finished its slow meal, and be at rest, a fossil that remembers being hungry, a silence that was never empty at all, only full of the sound of its own becoming-stone.
