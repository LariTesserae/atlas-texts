The **Striders** are not merely animals; they are ambulatory geology, and their minds move with the velocity of tectonic plates. A Strider thinks in centuries. When it dreams—if dreaming is the word for the slow, electrical shuffling of mineral memories through its vast, calcified nervous system—it dreams of pressure, of the crushing weight of ancient seas that once covered this plateau, now remembered only as chemical ghosts in the rock. Its consciousness is distributed across its twenty-meter frame, a distributed intelligence that feels the composition of the basalt beneath its thousands of grinding feet, tasting the magnetite and hematite before its ventral maw has even pressed down to pulverize them. The Strider knows the cities within its dead kin not as invaders but as extensions of its own biological imperative, a continuation of the mineral loop that has defined this world since water left it. When a living Strider crawls through the thoracic cavity of a deceased elder—its warm, segmented body filling the hollow ribcage like a new heart—it is not squatting. It is gestating. It is metabolizing the refined metals left behind by the factories, converting them back into the raw stuff of its own flesh, closing the circle.

Within the cranial domes of these ossuary-cities dwell the **Sinter-Folk**—the human-equivalent inhabitants, though "human" is a word that tastes wrong in this air, heavy with copper and devoid of the moisture that once shaped mammalian tongues. They are lithe, wiry creatures, no taller than the Striders' smallest leg joints, their skin a living patina of oxidized bronze and nickel that shifts color with their mood and their mineral intake. They have no need for water; their cells run on hydrogen sulfide and the thermal vents that pulse beneath the basalt plates, their biochemistry a mirror of the Striders' own. Their eyes are large, lidless, adapted to the bruised violet zenith and the perpetual amber twilight, capable of seeing the heat-shimmer rising from the metal loam, the faint glint of native gold in the dust. They speak rarely, for the thin, warm air carries little sound, and when they do, it is in subsonic pulses—thrumming their sternums or striking crystalline chimes that resonate through the bone-architecture of the cities, a language felt in the teeth, in the marrow, in the slow pulse of blood that moves like syrup through their veins.

Their society is organized around the **Grazing Cycles**. A Sinter-Folk child is born in the cranial dome of an ossuary-city, cradled in the membranous hide of the dead titan, and its first lesson is patience. It learns to walk on the curved interior of the skull, where gravity is strange and the light filters through translucent membranes in slow, amber waves. It learns to harvest—the sacred act of collecting the gleaming ingots of iron, copper, and aluminum that the Striders excrete as they feed. These excretions are not waste but **offerings**, the slow alchemy of stone transformed into metal by the Strider's internal furnaces. The Sinter-Folk do not mine; they **gather**. They follow the herds, which take decades to strip a valley, establishing temporary camps of sintered metal and crystal along the migration routes, waiting for the Striders to pass so they might collect the metallic trails left gleaming in the dust.

They are artisans of the slow. Their metallurgy is a craft measured in seasons that last years. A single sword might take a decade to forge, folded and refolded in fires fed by geothermal vents, quenched not in water but in the metallic vapor that rises from the solar collectors. Their art is **acoustic**—they compose symphonies for the subsonic range, music that can only be felt, that causes the metallic dust to dance in standing waves across the basalt plates, that makes the black sand in the dry washes chime in sympathy. Their history is not written but **etched**—into the hexagonal columns themselves, using burrow-worm acid to carve pictographs that record the movements of herds across millennia, the rise and fall of ossuary-cities, the slow erosion of the plateau into new shapes.

Around them scurry the **dust-mites**, the dog-sized scavengers of brass and crystal, which the Sinter-Folk have partially domesticated. These creatures, with their gear-like bodies and compound eyes, serve as messengers between the sparse settlements, carrying ore samples and crystalline data-storage devices in their mandibles. They are tolerated, even cherished, but never fully trusted—they are too quick, too mechanical, their lifespans measured in months rather than decades. The **burrow-worms**, secreting their acid to dissolve the stone, are treated with a mixture of fear and utility. The Sinter-Folk guide them, channeling their tunneling to create new ventilation shafts for the factories, to expand the living space within the ossuary-cities, but they know that the worms are patient too, and that eventually, the worms will weaken the foundations of even the newest city, returning the bone and metal to the slow digestion of the plateau.

The **aerial filterers** drift on thermal currents above, their comb-like wings sieving the metallic dust from the air, their bodies swelling with accumulated ore until they become too heavy to fly, sinking to the ground to be harvested by the Sinter-Folk or consumed by the dust-mites. They are the sky's recyclers, ensuring that nothing escapes the loop—not the copper vapor exhaled from the eye-socket ventilation shafts, not the iron dust kicked up by the Striders' passage, not the gold flecks that glitter dully in the normal light.

To live here is to exist in a state of **geological becoming**. The Sinter-Folk do not fear death, for death is merely a transition from flesh to architecture. When a Sinter-Folk dies, their body is not buried but **processed**—their metallic bones extracted, their organic matter fed to the Striders or the burrow-worms, their essence returning to the mineral cycle. The eldest among them choose to walk into the lower chambers of the ossuary-cities, to be consumed by the living Striders that crawl through the ribcages, ensuring that even their final mass contributes to the next generation of grazers, and thus to the next generation of metal, and thus to the next generation of cities.

They measure time in the blink of a Strider—once per hour. They measure a lifetime in the passage of a herd across a single valley—perhaps thirty years, perhaps fifty. They have no calendars, only **rumbles**—the subsonic communication of the Striders that travels through the bedrock, announcing the movement of herds, the readiness of a valley to be grazed, the death of a titan and the birth of a new ossuary-city. When a herd approaches, the ground trembles with a slow percussion, and the Sinter-Folk feel it in their sternums, their teeth, their bones, a reminder that they are temporary tenants in a world where the stone is hungry and the metal is its breath.

In the long, amber twilight, when the metallic haze pools in the yardangs like slow tides of light, a Sinter-Folk might stand upon the crown of a cranial dome, looking out through the membranous hide, tasting the copper on their tongue, feeling the dry warmth of the air pressing against their skin like a memory of water that never was. They watch the herd approach—a mountain of calcified chitin and native metal moving with the inevitability of erosion—and they understand, with a patience that would seem like despair to faster minds, that they are not separate from the stone, the metal, the city, or the beast. They are the dream the plateau has of itself, waking slowly, grinding stone into metal, metal into city, city into stone, in a cycle that has no end, only the slow, patient hunger of a world that has learned to eat itself and shit beauty.
