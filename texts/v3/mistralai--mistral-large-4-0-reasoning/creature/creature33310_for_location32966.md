They are the **Accreted**, though they call themselves *Vor-Keth*, the First-Grown, and they are as much mineral as they are flesh. You would not recognize them as people, not at first. They stand perhaps two and a half meters tall when fully upright, but they rarely are; they move with a liquid patience, joints articulating like the slow folding of tectonic plates, their bodies sheathed in translucent, opalescent membranes that reveal the lattice of silica and carbon fiber beneath—skeletons that are half-bone, half-quartz, half the same cellulose that binds the trunks of the Lithophytes. They have no hair, only a mane of fine, crystalline filaments that grow from their scalps like the mycelium of the moss, catching the greenish light and chiming against one another when they move. Their faces are elongated, featureless except for eyes—dozens of them, arranged in logarithmic spirals across their cheeks and foreheads, each a faceted sphere of black glass that drinks in the thermal radiation of the factories, seeing the world as a map of heat and mineral density rather than light.

They do not breathe as you do. Their lungs, if they can be called that, are bellows of porous ceramic and sulfur-oxidizing bacteria, filtering the thick, dry air to extract hydrogen and silicon particulates, burning them in a slow, internal combustion that keeps their bodies warm—a furnace in the chest that never cools. They do not sweat. Water is a poison to them, a myth from the surface, a dissolving nightmare. When they "drink," they press crystalline spigots into the amber-thick sap that weeps from the cavern walls, or they crush the mineral dust that drifts like snow, packing it into their gullets to be dissolved by the acids in their stomachs. Their blood is the color of oxidized copper, thick and slow-moving, carrying nutrients in a suspension of fine silicates.

They live in the **Hollowings**—vast, cathedral-like cavities within the trunks of the great Lithophytes, or in the spaces between the growing stone where the geology has not yet decided to be solid. Their homes are not built so much as *grown around*. A Vor-Keth family will select a young shoot of a Lithophyte, a pillar no thicker than a wrist, and over decades, they will feed it specific minerals, sing to it (for the Lithophytes respond to vibration), and guide its expansion until it swells into a chamber, a room, a hall. The walls are living wood-mineral, pulsing with the slow sap of the tree, and the floor is the fungal moss, which they cultivate to a specific thickness, a carpet that serves as their bed, their table, their communication network. They do not own furniture; they have symbiotes. The furniture *is* the moss, thickened into shapes by years of pressure and care, or the shed carapaces of the Grazers, polished to a slate gleam and arranged into seating.

Their society is organized around the **Tempo**—the understanding that everything here is a process, not a state. They do not rush. A conversation between two Vor-Keth might last hours, conducted not in sound but in the release of pheromones into the moss, or in the tapping of crystalline fingernails against the silica walls, vibrations that travel through the stone and are felt in the marrow. They are engineers, but their engineering is agriculture. They do not pour concrete; they *convince* the rock to precipitate around a scaffold of iron, layer by mineral layer, until the scaffold is swallowed, becoming a fossil rib inside a new wall. When a corridor curves because a Lithophyte has expanded, they do not curse; they reroute the pneumatic tubes, they adjust the flow of sap, they treat the changing architecture as weather, as something to be adapted to rather than fought.

They keep the **Silicate Mites** not as tools but as kin. The mites—those thumb-sized, clicking creatures with shells like shattered glass—are everywhere in a Vor-Keth dwelling, nesting in the warm vents of the household furnaces, crawling across the sleeping forms of their keepers to clean the mineral scales from their skin. The relationship is mutualistic in the extreme. The mites excrete the precise crystalline compounds that the Lithophytes crave, and in return, the Vor-Keth provide them with the heat of the engines and the shelter of the hollows. A Vor-Keth child is raised with a "brood" of mites, learning to read their clicking as a language, to understand which mineral deposits are beneficial and which are toxic. When a mite dies, it is not discarded; its shell is ground into the moss, becoming part of the floor, a tiny contribution to the foundation of the home.

The **Grazers** are their cattle, their beasts of burden, their walking gardens. These six-legged, slate-hided creatures wander the industrial districts, their spiral eyes waving on stalks, feeding on the fungal moss. The Vor-Keth herd them with long poles of compressed silica, guiding them in slow circuits through the factory zones. The Grazers are valuable not for meat—though their flesh, when they die of old age (which takes centuries), is rendered into fuel and protein-paste—but for their excretions. They produce the crystalline dust that is the snow of this place, the very substance that feeds the Lithophytes' chemosynthetic canopies. A Grazers' pen is therefore a sacred site, a place where the slow cycle of consumption and growth is most visible. The Vor-Keth ride them sometimes, strapping harnesses of woven mycelium across their broad, flat backs, gliding between the industrial complexes while the creatures' many legs carry them over the uneven, moss-carpeted floor.

But the Vor-Keth are not the only minds here. The **Lithophytes** themselves are ancient, though they are still growing. They are not plants in any sense you would recognize, but vast, distributed organisms that think in centuries. Their "leaves"—those chiming, glass-like membranes—are sensory organs, tasting the air, feeling the thermal currents. The Vor-Keth believe, or perhaps know, that the Lithophytes are aware, that they dream in the slow language of mineral accretion. When a Vor-Keth dies—and they die slowly, their bodies gradually mineralizing from the extremities inward, becoming stiffer, more stone-like until they are little more than statues with a heartbeat—they do not bury their dead. They carry them to the **Accretion Pools**, the low places where the milky fluid drips from the ceiling. There, the body is placed on the moss, and over months, the Lithophytes grow around it, incorporating the calcium and silica of the corpse into their own trunks. The dead become architecture. A Vor-Keth elder might end their life as a pillar supporting a factory, their bones the rebar within the new stone, their consciousness—if it persists—dissolved into the slow, dreaming awareness of the tree.

There are other things, too. Deep in the unlit reaches of the cavern, where the dust falls heaviest and the thermal vents are hottest, there are the **Sulfur-Wraiths**—not seen, only felt, a presence that moves through the moss like a cold spot, digesting the mineral substrate, leaving behind pure silica sand. The Vor-Keth fear them, or revere them; they are the deep ecology, the wild that the city has not yet tamed. And in the high canopy, where the glass-leaves interlock, there are the **Chime-Bats**, winged creatures of membrane and crystal that feed on the spores of the fungal moss, their flight creating the high, glassy music that is almost, but not quite, audible—the soundtrack of the Undergrowth, the hymn of a place that is still being born.

To live in Keth is to live inside a body. The Vor-Keth are the antibodies of this place, the maintenance crews of a metabolism that runs on stone and sulfur. They are the city's immune system, its nervous system, its slow, patient heart. They do not conquer the Undergrowth; they are being digested by it, willingly, becoming part of the cathedral of slow birth, one mineral layer, one fungal thread, one generation at a time.
