They are the Sweat-Keepers—or perhaps simply the Dwellers, the ones who chose, or were born into, the deep. A few hundred souls, though exact counts blur in the steam, packed into the vertical city like moisture compressed into a sponge. They have become creatures of the threshold, neither fully of the rock nor of the machine, but suspended in the wet, pressurized dark where the planet’s young crust exhales its heat.

Physically, they are saturated. Their skin has adapted—or has been adapted over generations—to carry a perpetual film of condensation, to never truly dry. They move slowly, deliberately, because the air is thick as broth; every gesture requires effort, and they have learned that haste generates heat, which is the enemy of equilibrium. Their clothing is minimal, woven from heat-resistant polymers that cling to the body like a second skin, perpetually damp, smelling of mineral tang and the ozone scent of the electrical infrastructure. Salt and calcium crust their shoulders and the hair at their necks, white geometric deposits left by the indoor rain, patterns that match the crystalline formations on the cavern walls.

They live in tiers, organized by the thermal gradient. At the top, near the condensation zone where the bioluminescent lichen pulses its slow, breathing cyan, dwell the Cool-Born—pale, almost translucent figures who have lost the pigmentation of surface-dwellers, their eyes large and dark-adapted, their movements languid. They tend the collection troughs, harvesting the gentle precipitation, their lives measured by the drip of water from the ceiling. Below them, in the mid-levels where the sodium vapor lamps cast their orange, shimmering pools, live the Workers—darkened by heat, their skin gleaming with a permanent sheen of oil and mineral residue from handling the pipes. They maintain the factories, monitoring the heating elements, ensuring that water flashes to steam and rises, that the closed loop continues its eternal cycle. The lowest levels, where the vents roar hottest, are rarely occupied except by the desperate or the penitent, those who must descend to clear blockages or repair the thermal lances that bore deeper into the basalt.

Their society is infrastructural. They communicate not through speech alone, but through the city itself—tapping rhythms on brass pipes that travel for meters through the lattice, modulating steam whistles into a language of pressure and release, their own breathing synchronized with the bass hum of the ventilation system. They have no need for loud voices; the cavern amplifies whispers into something like prayer, and silence is a shared resource, as precious as water.

They are time-dilated. The heat slows their thoughts; minutes stretch into something viscous and golden. They exist in a state of perpetual near-meditation, their perception tuned to the thermal cycles—the daily rhythm of heating and cooling that governs their waking and sleeping. They do not rush. To rush is to generate friction, to add heat to a system already at the edge of tolerance. They climb the ladders with the patience of coral growing, hand over hand, their bodies glistening, their breath visible in short, humid clouds.

They are becoming mineral. The boundary between their biology and the geology blurs with each passing season. Crystalline structures form on their skin when they stand still too long, temporary geology. The lichen—those pale cyan organisms—grows not just on the walls but on their tools, their clothing, perhaps in symbiosis with their own damp bodies, providing a faint bioluminescence that marks their presence in the dark. When they sleep, they curl into the negative spaces between conduits, fitting themselves into the architecture, their bodies conforming to the curves of pipes and platforms, their breathing matching the rhythmic drip of condensation, indistinguishable from the city’s own respiration.

They are the human element in the thermodynamic cycle, the keepers of the exchange between planet and settlement. They harvest the heat, manage the steam, drink the collected rain, and in return, they offer their labor, their slow thoughts, their mineralizing bodies. They are a precarious population, sharp-edged and young, growing only in the direction of more pipes, more adaptation, more heat. They are the city’s consciousness—a slow, sweating, mineral-dreaming awareness that knows, with a certainty deeper than thought, that they will not leave. They are here to stay, until the rock cools or they turn, finally, to stone.
