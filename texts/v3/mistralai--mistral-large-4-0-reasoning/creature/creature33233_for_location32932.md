The Climbers are the first you will meet, if you can call it meeting. They are tall—seven feet, perhaps eight—but they move as if gravity is a suggestion, their limbs too long for their torsos, joints clicking like dry reeds in the thermal wind. Their skin is the color of weathered stone, pale and cracked, not from dryness but from age; they seem to be born old, or to age backwards, their faces smooth as river-polished flint despite the centuries etched into their hands. They have no hair, only a ruff of stiff, dark filaments around their necks that flutter in the updrafts like semaphore flags, signaling to one another across the deafening silence between towers.

They do not walk the open plain. They walk the shadow highways—the narrow corridors of cool air cast by the mile-long shadows of the towers—moving in single file along the migration routes of the megafauna. They know these paths by the cairns they have stacked themselves, generation upon generation, waist-high monuments of balanced stone that mark where the great beasts will thunder past. The Climbers do not herd the beasts; they follow them, scavenging the bones that fall from the herds, the shed plates of carapace, the stones the beasts dislodge with their passing. A scraper is the sharpened scapula of a dead grazer, hafted with sinew to a handle of hollow reed. An awl is a spine from the tower-bark, hardened in the pool-water until it gleams like black glass. They leave these tools at the edge of the condensation pools not as offerings, but as waystations—places where they stop to drink, to sharpen, to mend the lashings on their climbing gear.

For they are, above all, climbers.

They ascend the towers not to conquer them, but to harvest. The towers sweat, as you have seen, and the Climbers drink from the source. They scale the lower flanks where the damp shroud clings, using the wooden platforms they have lashed to the bark with ropes of twisted fiber. These platforms are gray with age, many abandoned now, the ropes turned to dust, but others remain in use, higher up, swaying in the wind like the nests of great birds. The Climbers move up the towers in a vertical migration, following the condensation line as it rises and falls with the heat of the day, their bare feet finding purchase on bark that is more like callus than wood.

They are going to the green snow.

High in the hollows of the towers, where the wind screams through the labyrinth of chambers grown over ten thousand years, the Sky-Dwellers breed. These are insectile swarms, creatures that have never touched the ground, that live their entire lives in the upper reaches, feeding on the spores the towers exhale and the condensation that falls like internal rain. They emerge only when the clouds of their kind are dense enough to obscure the jade sky—green snow, the Climbers call it, though it is neither frozen nor falling. It drifts in the hollows, a living mist of wing and chitin.

The Climbers harvest this snow. They carry nets woven from the hollow reeds, and bone-frames that unfold like umbrellas against the updrafts. They stand on the platforms twenty, thirty meters up, and they scoop the swarms from the air, storing them in gourds made from the dried husks of tower-fruit. The swarms shriek as they are taken—a high, insectile shriek that harmonizes with the tower's roaring drone, a green-snow song—and the Climbers do not flinch. They are deaf to it, or they have learned to hear it as the only music that matters.

They descend at dusk, when the towers cool and the condensation pools steam in the lingering heat. They bring the harvest down to the base, where they share it with the Pool-Born.

The Pool-Born are small—no larger than a fist—and numerous where the other life is sparse. They live in the black-mirror water at the tower bases, in the steaming pockets of liquid that collect in the shade. They are the foundation, the filter, the digestive lining. They are flat, many-legged things, like crabs made of translucent horn, and they process everything that falls to them: the minerals scraped from the towers by the Climbers' tools, the spores that drift down from the hollows, the occasional swarm-creature that drops dead from the sky, the shed lichen that sloughs off the flanks. They turn the detritus of the heights into the broth that feeds the symbiotic mats on the megafauna's backs, closing the loop between sky and stone.

And the megafauna—the Grazers—are the blood of this world. They are vast, reptilian or insectoid, their scales polished to mirror-brightness by millennia of dust. They move slowly, actively, following the condensation line across the bowl of the continent, their footsteps triggering minor earthquakes that you feel in your teeth. They move in herds of three, or five, never more. Their density is low, but their presence is overwhelming (2.7), a weight against the emptiness. They do not eat the towers. They do not hunt. They graze the mist. Their bodies are covered in thick, velvety lichen—symbiotic mats of green and gray that photosynthesize the moisture they collect as they walk through the damp shrouds. They are living condensers, walking rain-catchers. When they breathe—in long, slow cycles that match the pulse of the towers—they exhale a fine spray that feeds the ferns and mosses clinging to the bark, that waters the roots that have melted into bedrock.

They communicate across miles of empty plain. Their calls are low, mournful horns, subsonic, felt in the sternum rather than heard, a communication that travels for hundreds of kilometers through the thin, dry air. They are coordinating their migration, announcing the location of the best condensation pools, singing to one another in a bass line that harmonizes with the tower's white-noise thunder, turning the drone into a chord, then a harmony, a deafening symphony of the deep earth.

But the towers themselves are the eldest inhabitants. They are not plants, not animals, not mineral, but a third thing—a slow, geological intelligence that has achieved equilibrium (2.0) with the heat, the height, and the overwhelming silence of the spaces between. They pulse. If you press your ear to the bark—which is warm, and thrumming—you can feel a slow, deep heartbeat with a period of hours. They are growing, expanding outward by millimeters each century, terraforming themselves, and as they expand, they sing louder, the resonance increasing, the erosion settling them into a final form.

The Climbers know this. When they reach the highest platforms, they do not merely harvest; they tend. They scrape away the dead bark, clearing the hollows so the wind can resonate more perfectly. They are the tonsils of this throat, the uvula that helps the world swallow the air, breathe the heat, digest the sky. They are small, primitive, using bone and stone and sinew, treating the vegetation not as forest but as terrain to be traversed, a ladder to be climbed, a throat to be ascended. They are the current in the circuit, the link between the green snow and the pool, between the sky and the stone, between the heartbeat of the tower and the thunder of the herd.

In the end, all life here is symbiotic. The towers provide the architecture, the water, the song. The Climbers provide the maintenance, the harvest, the vertical movement. The megafauna provide the scale, the mist, the subsonic thunder. The swarms provide the food, the green snow, the high-altitude shriek. The Pool-Born provide the foundation, the filter, the base note in the resonance chamber.

You are small here. You are a speck in the throat of the world. But if you watch long enough, in the jade light of the high-altitude haze, you will see a Climber pause at the edge of a shadow, look up at the tower, and raise a hand—not in greeting, but in time with the heartbeat, slow and deep, matching the pulse of the world as it breathes, and swallows, and sings.
