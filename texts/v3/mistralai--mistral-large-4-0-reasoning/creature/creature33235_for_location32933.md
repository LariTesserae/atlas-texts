What lives here is a conversation between stone and water, spoken in the language of patience and pressure.

First, the **skin of the world itself**—not dead, but *other*. The basaltic pavement wears a living crust, a patina that is less plant than mineral secretion. It is a dark, rubbery membrane, perhaps the color of wet slate or old bruises, that pulses with moisture and exhales acids. When you press a hand against it—if you dare, for the stone is treacherously slick—it yields with the resistance of cold, thick custard. It is alive with chemotrophic archaea, ancient metabolisms that draw breath from iron and sulfur, from the slow dissolution of the rock itself. This is the plain’s true vegetation, older than ferns, simpler than lichen: a scum of existence that thickens and thins with the bacterial blooms, spreading across the glassy stone in irregular, breathing patches. It stains your fingers mineral-black. It tastes of copper and salt. It is the only green that was never green, a photosynthetic replacement written in chemistry instead of chlorophyll.

But the crust is food, and what feeds upon it is the **Grazer**.

They are the plain’s heartbeat, its shifting, clicking tide. Imagine a creature that has abandoned the distinction between individual and colony—a slow, armored procession of zooids fused into a single, chitinous architecture. They stand perhaps the height of a human knee, low and broad, their shells mineralized into something like fossilized shell-rock, opalescent and heavy with geological time, while their undersides remain soft, wet, and pulsing with faint, bioluminescent blue-green light. This glow is not mere display; it is a metabolic byproduct, a cold fire that pulses in waves across their herds, making the dark plain resemble a starfield reflected in black water.

They move in herds that flow like dark, rippling mercury across the glassy pavement. Thousands of jointed legs—dozens per body segment, articulating with hydraulic precision—strike the stone in a percussive roar, a sound like hail on a tin roof, but continuous, deafening, a biological thunder that never ceases. They do not walk so much as *flow*, their many-legged bodies undulating, scraping the bacterial crust with radula-like tongues that rasp against the rock. The sound of feeding is a wet, grinding sibilance, the sound of the earth being slowly consumed.

They are not solitary. Whether they are true individuals or colonial organisms—thousands of small zooids sharing a single armored shell, moving with a hive-mind of simple instinct—remains a question the plain does not answer. They communicate through subsonic vibrations that travel through the stone itself, a language of pressure and tremor that shakes the ground beneath your feet. When a herd shifts direction, it is not led by a leader but by a consensus of tremor, a collective decision made through the rock.

They breed in the shallows. Gelatinous masses of eggs attach to the slick stone, translucent sacs filled with larvae that look like tiny, drifting ghosts. When they hatch, the young are planktonic, carried by the sheet-water in endless, slow currents, growing through molt after molt, their shells gradually accreting mineral from the water until they become too heavy to float, sinking to the pavement to join the grazing herds.

Yet the Grazers are not alone in their abundance. They are hunted.

The **Predators** are sinuous, translucent things—perhaps related to the Grazers, perhaps something else entirely. They have too many eyes, arranged in clusters along their flanks, milky and reflective in the dim light. Their bodies are elongated, almost eel-like, but they move on short, many-jointed legs that allow them to skim across the water’s surface tension as easily as they swim through it. Their skin is thin, permeable, allowing the mineral-rich water to pass through them, filtering nutrients as they move. They are ambush hunters, lying motionless in the shallow pools, their translucent bodies becoming nearly invisible against the dark stone. When a herd of Grazers passes, they strike with sudden, sinuous speed, their jaws—if they can be called jaws—unhinging to engulf the armored prey whole. The hunt is silent except for the slap of water and the sudden, wet crunch of shell.

Then there are the **Builders**.

They are the newest voice in this ancient choir, amphibious mammals or perhaps something else—their bodies adapted to the wet, their skin smooth and wet-looking, almost hairless, with webbed digits and large, dark eyes suited to the perpetual twilight. They stand perhaps shoulder-high to a human, moving with a slow, deliberate grace across the treacherous slickness, using staffs of carved bone to test the stone before each step.

They do not farm the crust; they *herd*. They are shepherds of the Grazers, driving them with low, booming calls that carry for miles across the flat, using sticks and stone pens to corral the herds into enclosures of stacked basalt. They harvest the Grazers when they die—or perhaps when they are ready, a symbiosis of husbandry and slaughter—using the mineralized shells for tools, armor, and the walls of their huts. The shells are worked into blades, into cups, into the smooth, worn stones that form the circles of their settlements. Their huts are low, half-submerged domes of stacked stone and Grazer-hide, patched against the perpetual damp, sinking slowly into the wet earth as the world settles around them.

They leave marks: a copper blade, dulled by time and green with verdigris, half-buried in the sheet-water. A bone flute, its holes crusted with mineral deposits, capable of producing tones that harmonize with the subsonic language of the Grazers. Circles of standing stones, worn to smooth nubs by the patient water, perhaps astronomical, perhaps ritual, perhaps simply the memory of a place where they once camped.

They are nomadic, following the water and the herds. When the Grazers have overgrazed a patch, or when the water rises too high, they move their huts—dragging them across the glassy stone on sledges of bone and shell—leaving behind only the worn stones and scattered artifacts, which sink slowly into the wet dark, becoming part of the geological record before they are even abandoned.

And in the water itself, invisible to the eye but present in every drop, are the **Drifters**—microscopic life, the larvae of the Grazers, the spores of the bacterial mats, cysts of ancient archaea that have waited three billion years for the right chemistry. They are the plain’s memory, its potential, drifting in the endless sheet-flow toward some distant sink, waiting for the stone to crack, for the water to change, for the Grazers to evolve into something new.

Together, they form a single, roaring organism: the crust that feeds, the herds that consume, the predators that balance, the Builders that remember, all of it moving in the slow, wet dance of a world that has forgotten how to be a mountain, but has not forgotten how to be alive.
