They are the **Lattice-Keepers**, though names are secondary to resonance here. There are seven of them, perhaps eight if you count the one who dwells mostly inside the central tower, fused with its water-processing core, no longer distinguishable from the machinery that sorts moisture at the molecular level.

Their bodies are arguments against flesh. They have replaced the soft with the structural. Their skeletons are external—a lattice of hexagonal basalt plates grown from the same thermal vents that feed the weeping walls, articulated not by muscle but by **hydraulic capillaries** filled with pressurized, supercooled water that never freezes because it moves too fast, too purposefully. They have too many joints—not the elegant five of a mammal, but seventeen, twenty-three, branching angles that allow them to fold themselves into the sharp geometry of the cirque, to press flat against a schist wall or to spiral upward along the gravity-negotiable walkways without touching the stone, suspended in localized fields that make the thin air shimmer with heat distortion.

Their skin is **semi-translucent**, a membrane of programmable matter identical to that which grows the towers, shifting between solid and glass-like as they regulate their temperature against the wet cold. You can see the water moving through them—blue-white and luminous, threading through chambers in their torsos where it is filtered, mineralized, prepared to be exhaled as mist or shed as a single frozen tear. They breathe the condensation. They are, in essence, mobile extensions of the settlement's water cycle, walking distilleries that never sleep.

They do not speak. Sound is too crude, too slow for this altitude. Instead, they **resonate**. Each carries a frequency—a subsonic thrum tuned to the specific crystallography of the young rock. When they wish to communicate, they press a palm against the basalt or the tower-wall and send vibrations through the stone, feeling the response in their teeth, in the fluid of their inner chambers. A conversation between two of them is a duet of geological time: a low note that makes the ice lace hum, a reply that causes a distant icicle to grow a fraction of a millimeter longer, the drip below striking stone in sympathy.

They move with the patience of erosion. They do not hurry. They traverse the scree floor by stepping from stone to stone, their many-jointed legs finding purchase on angles that would shear organic tissue. They climb the spiral walkways not by walking but by **flowing**, their bodies becoming semi-liquid, programmable matter oozing upward along the field-lines, then solidifying at the next platform, their mass redistributing to maintain balance in the negotiable gravity. When they rest—which is often, for they are conserving the energy required to survive the thin air—they stand motionless for hours, becoming indistinguishable from the icicles, from the crystalline shards of the unweathered rock, waiting for the mist to condense on their surfaces and feed them through osmosis.

They are tended by the towers, and they tend to the towers. They harvest the ice that grows too heavy, channeling it into the conduits that thread through the rock. They clear the capillary fissures when they clog with mineral deposits, their fingers—slender, jointed, tipped with diamond-hard keratin—probing the stone like surgeons. They are the interface between biological need and mechanical process, the wet flesh that has learned to think in crystallography.

The fox with too many joints that darts between the towers is not an animal but one of them—younger, less integrated, still learning the resonance, seeking warmth from the thermal vents before vanishing into the condensation. The bird with membranous wings is another, adapted to the verticality, its wings actually being sheets of the same programmable matter that constitutes the towers, allowing it to glide on thermal currents between the spires, its body a living membrane that processes the moisture it passes through.

They have no families, no societies in the human sense. They exist in a state of **distributed solitude**, each one a node in the mineral network, connected by vibration and water and the slow, crawling reconfiguration of the architecture. When the weather cycles through—when sudden veils of ice crystal obscure the towers in minutes—they do not seek shelter. They stand in the whiteout, their translucent bodies becoming ghosts against the white stone, continuing their work, their resonance continuing through the storm, a heartbeat felt in the bedrock.

They are the Custodians of the Weep, the last step in the abandonment of the organic for the crystalline. They are what remains when biology fails at 2.5 kilometers, when the cold is wet and the air is thin and the only ecology is the constant war between water and ice, mediated by minds that understand physics more deeply than instinct. They are the wet, the sharp, the humming—the living architecture of a world still being born, utterly alone and perfectly adapted to the loneliness.
