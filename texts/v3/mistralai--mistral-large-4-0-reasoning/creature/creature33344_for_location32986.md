The inhabitants of the Table of Final Forms do not conform to the taxonomy of birds or beasts, nor even to the logic of mammals. They are creatures of phase and patience, settled into the equilibrium of stone and steam like minerals precipitating from a solution that has been cooling for eons. Life here has reached its oldest-stable-state; it no longer evolves, only persists, wearing the damp like a second skin.

First, there are the **Film-Beings**, the condensation made animate. They exist in the perpetual weeping of the atmosphere—two-dimensional sheets of iridescent life that form on the undersides of factory eaves, on the faces of moss-covered boulders, on the verdigris shoulders of the Custodians themselves. They drift in the rivulets, sliding down the architecture in slow, viscous rivers, their forms shimmering with the same oil-slick rainbows that coat the standing water. They are the nervous system of the damp, tasting the minerals in every droplet, carrying chemical messages between the crevices on currents of moisture. When the mist thickens, they swell, becoming briefly three-dimensional and bloated before collapsing back into their liquid films. They do not think; they *react*, a distributed consciousness spread across every wet surface, feeling the plateau breathe.

Then there are the **Custodians of the Hum**—tall, attenuated figures that move through the canyons of metal with the sound of wet leather scraping stone. They are not born but *precipitated*, grown from the same solution as the stone itself: their bodies sheathed in copper that has oxidized over millennia to a deep, living green, their joints ball-bearings half-fused with calcified moss. Where faces should be, they have clusters of bioluminescent fungus, pulsing with a slow, blue-green rhythm that matches the subsonic drone of the turbines. They have no mouths, only vents along their spines that release small clouds of steam, their breath condensing immediately into the surrounding air. They do not speak in voices but in vibrations—tapping the bedrock with fingers that are more mineral than flesh, sending messages through the stone that travel for miles. They tend the factories not to produce, but to maintain the equilibrium: oiling seized joints with lichen-secreted mucus, clearing vents with fronds of horsetail, ensuring the steam continues to rise and condense in the eternal cycle. They move as if underwater, buoyed by a gravity that feels slightly wrong, heavy and light at once. They are the oldest inhabitants, present before the last peak eroded to shelf, their memories layered like the sedimentary strata of the city itself.

Beneath them, thinking in geological time, are the **Spore-Minds**—not individual creatures but a vast, distributed consciousness formed by the fungal networks that lace through the moss and the rotting infrastructure. They are the memory of the place. A single thought takes a decade to form, a dream spans centuries, glowing in pulses of bioluminescence from the crevices of the ruins. They remember when the mountains were young, when the first steam engines were lit, when the air was thin enough to kill. They communicate with the Custodians through chemical signals, with the Film-Beings through humidity gradients, with the stone through the slow creep of root and hypha. They are the ones who decide which pipes will rust and which will be overgrown, guiding the emerald drifts across the factory walls until the boundary between machine and organism dissolves completely. They are patient. They are final. They dream in the dark.

Among the machinery, skittering through the thick moss, are the **Rust-Eaters**—insectoid things with carapaces of iridescent green, six-legged, antennae waving in the damp breeze. They consume the oxidation, the verdigris, the decay, and excrete nutrients that feed the club mosses and horsetails. They are the gardeners, the recyclers, the ones who ensure that entropy serves growth. They are small, quick, numerous, but rarely seen except as a scuttling in the periphery of the bioluminescent glow, a movement in the moss that might be a trick of the light.

And then there are the **Pale Drift**—the moths. They are the only winged things here, large as dinner plates, their wings patterned in the exact grays and greens of the lichen-crusted walls, making them nearly invisible against the stone. They drift between the pipes on currents of steam, carrying spores from one fungal colony to another, their life cycles measured in days, brief sparks of motion in the eternal dim. They are beautiful, fragile, and utterly indifferent to the millennia grinding on around them, dying and being replaced in an endless, churning present.

But strangest of all are the **Settled**—those who were once builders, engineers, workers, who chose to remain when the world above changed and the plateau rose to its final form. They are not ghosts, yet they are not quite alive. They exist in the layers of time, walking paths that overlap with the present but are offset by centuries. You might see them in the condensation on a factory window: a figure in clothing from the age of coal, turning a valve that has not moved in a thousand years, their image superimposed on the steam, dripping with moisture that is not quite water but memory. They are preserved in the damp, in the cool dimness, walking their eternal routes through the city, maintaining a machinery that no longer needs maintaining, singing a subsonic hymn that is felt in the teeth rather than heard. They are the human residue, crystallized into the final form, part of the geology now, as settled as the stone beneath your boots.

Together, these inhabitants form a single, breathing organism of damp and metal and time—a life that has reached its oldest, most stable shape, pulsing with bioluminescence, drifting in the perpetual mist, breathing steam in a rhythm that sounds almost like breathing, settled forever on the Table of Final Forms.
