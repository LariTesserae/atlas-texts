What lives here does not hurry. The first inhabitants are the ones you cannot see, the ones that have turned this place into a lung and a stomach both. In the peat-mat, ankle-deep and cool, anaerobic archaea and iron-reducing bacteria work in the dark, metabolizing the centuries of fallen reed and moss, releasing methane in slow bubbles that rise through the spongy substrate. They are the original residents, predating the buildings, predating the grid, living in the compressed dark where oxygen is a rumor. Around them, in the water-films that coat every surface—the rusted girders, the undersides of reed canopies, the pale bracket fungi—colonies of cyanobacteria and diatoms photosynthesize in the greenish light, fixing carbon, exuding sugars, creating the slime that binds the mineral to the organic.

Above this microbial foundation rises the true architecture: the reed-pillars. These are not merely plants but individuals of impossible age. Each clump—twenty feet of columnar girth—is a single rhizomatous organism, or perhaps a clonal dynasty, its base fossilized into wood-like peat while its upper reaches remain supple, photosynthesizing, swaying in air currents too faint to feel. They are the hypostyle hall, yes, but also the lungs of the place, transpiring moisture into the heavy air, their hollow stems housing aquatic microfauna, their root systems—massive, woody, ancient—drinking from the tannin-dark pools below. They grow in the footprints of walls, as if the concrete had been scaffolding for their eventual triumph.

Between these pillars, the floor is a continuous organism: the bryophyte mat. Mosses and liverworts, *Sphagnum* or its kin, and countless smaller thalloid forms, form a carpet that is simultaneously surface and substrate. It is a sponge that never dries, a digestive tract that is also a skin. It absorbs the metronomic rain of condensation, the drip from leaf-tips, the overflow from the pools, holding it in capillary spaces where tardigrades—water bears—graze on bacteria, entering cryptobiosis when the humidity drops even slightly, waiting decades for the next proper rain. The mat is growing, accreting, becoming the peat of the future while standing upon the peat of the past.

On the vertical surfaces—the concrete walls, the corrugated iron, the weathered timber of the boardwalks—the lichens have established their slow republic. Crustose lichens, *Lecanora* and *Caloplaca*, paint the northern walls in cartographic greens and sulfur yellows, their fungal hyphae penetrating the mineral substrate, their algal partners photosynthesizing. They are the cartographers of decay, mapping the erosion in fractal patterns, turning rust into soil, concrete into lichen-food. They are composite beings, two kingdoms in one body, and they are patient.

Beneath everything, unseen, the mycelial network. Fungi—*Fomitopsis* and *Ganoderma* pushing bracket-like shelves from the boardwalk bark, but also vast, invisible networks threading through the peat, connecting the reed roots to the rotting timber, to the rusted machinery. They are the nervous system of the place, transporting nutrients, sending chemical signals, decomposing the dead, feeding the living. Some species may be mycorrhizal with the reeds; others may be saprotrophic, digesting the industrial debris—the child's shoe, the 1890 gear, the bottle—incorporating steel and rubber into their fruiting bodies, becoming part of the Anthropocene strata that the biosphere is slowly, patiently digesting.

And then there are the machines. They are not dead, though they have no human tenders. They are symbiotic entities now, or perhaps parasites that have reached a truce. The water-wheels turn, driven by the tannin-stained flow, their wooden paddles rotting and being replaced by fungal growth that is simultaneously part of the wheel and consuming it. The copper coils sweat condensation, which is collected, distilled, returned—the machinery "breathing" in its slow cycle. Perhaps microbial mats coat the flywheels, oiling them with lipids; perhaps the rust itself is a cultivated patina, a skin of iron-oxidizing bacteria that protects the metal from further decay while marking its surrender. The machinery maintains itself through the closed loop, processing the vegetation, producing the fuel-bricks, distilling the condensation, all at the pace of biological process. It is a mechanical organism, or an organism that has grown metal bones.

Occasionally, a vertebrate passes through—a newt, pale and eft-like, slipping through the pools with the haste of a creature that knows it does not belong, its skin absorbing the acidic water. A vole, small and brown, tunnels through the peat hummocks, its presence a flicker of mammalian warmth in the cool, damp dark, quickly gone. They are refugees, or messengers, from the world outside that moves in seconds, not decades.

But the true inhabitant, the one that breathes in decades, is the place itself. The peat compresses. The reeds thicken. The condensation forms patterns—runes, circuitry—that shift when not observed, perhaps a form of information processing, a slow cognition occurring in the water films and the fungal networks. The buildings are being eaten; the grid is being erased. What lives here is a mutual consumption, a symbiosis between the built and the grown, the mechanical and the vegetal, settled into a state of equilibrium that is not static but geological—moving, growing, consuming, at the speed of stone and stem, in a cool, damp, quiet room at the bottom of the world.
