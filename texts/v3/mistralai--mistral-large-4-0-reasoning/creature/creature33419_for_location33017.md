The air moves differently here, and so do they. The Warren-Folk do not walk so much as *flow*—a pale, many-limbed current threading between the fungal columns, their bodies angled to the perpetual updraft of geothermal steam. They are tall, or they appear tall, their proportions stretched by the thinness of the upper atmosphere and the dim, amber-green gloom; five, six, sometimes seven feet of willowy verticality, built for reaching, for climbing, for hanging suspended from mycelial ropes while their toes grip the warm, curved stone.

Their skin is not skin, not quite. It is a membrane—waxy, semi-translucent, the color of old parchment left in honey—stretched over a frame of light bone. Beneath this membrane, visible in the pulse of bioluminescence that filters down from the canopy, runs a dark lacework: mycelium. Each of them is host to a symbiotic network of fungal threads that breach their flesh at the wrists, the throat, the crown of the head. These are not infections; they are organs. Through them, the Warren-Folk taste the mineral content of the stone, feel the temperature gradients of the vents, hear the subsonic groaning of the growing stalks. When they speak, their voices carry in the thick, spore-heavy air—not as sound alone, but as vibration, a tremor in the chest that resonates through the shared mycelial web, a silent language of chemical pulses that moves faster than speech, binding the settlement into a single, thinking organism.

They breathe through slits. Along their necks, four delicate flaps of tissue flutter constantly, filtering the golden fog of spores, extracting moisture from the humid dark, expelling the excess as a fine, salty mist that beads on their shoulders. They do not drink. There are no cups, no gourds, no vessels for liquid. Instead, they press their palms against the pulpy flesh of the giant fungi and *suck*, drawing condensed moisture and nutrients through specialized pores in their hands, or they crush the tender fronds of shelf fungi between their peg-like teeth—small, numerous, suited for grinding rather than tearing—extracting the water stored within. Their blood, when it comes, is not red. It is thick, viscous, the color of the resin that seeps from ancient fissures in the stone: amber, golden, slow to move in the warmth. When a Warren-Folk is cut, they weep sap that hardens into brittle, translucent droplets, and the people gather this resin to pack between stones, to seal their storage pits, to bind their tools. The mortar of their town is the blood of their wounded, or the fossilized blood of the mountain itself—2.9 eons of ancient forests compressed and preserved, now used to build new homes.

They are stratified by height, a vertical caste system as rigid as the geological layers of the cirque.

At the bottom, in the hollows where the geothermal breath is hottest, dwell the **Deep**. They are broad, squat, heavy-boned, with skin thickened to leather by the heat. Their gill-slits are wide, their eyes small and dark, adapted to the steam and the mineral glare. They tend the vents, clearing the salt crust, harvesting the gypsum that precipitates from the escaping steam. They are the smiths who work the rare copper traded from the lowlands, hammering it into thin sheets with stone mauls, their arms corded with muscle. They are slow, patient, speaking little, their voices gravelly from breathing mineral dust. They live where the stone is warmest, nestled into erosion-hollows lined with packed resin and dust, and they are the foundation, literally and socially.

In the middle, on the sagging walkways and platforms lashed between the fungal stalks, live the **Mid**. They are the artisans, the parents, the weavers. Their fingers are long and dexterous, tipped with curved keratin nails for harvesting spores and stitching garments. They wear clothes of woven mycelium—pale, fibrous, grown rather than sewn—and cuirasses of chitin harvested from the armored, six-legged grazers that skitter through the spore-litter below. Copper, when they possess it, is worn as jewelry, hammered into thin circlets or dangling ornaments that catch the bioluminescent glow. They tend the gardens on the lower caps, cultivating rows of edible fungi, pruning the overgrowth, ensuring the forest remains productive. They are the most numerous, the busiest, their lives a constant negotiation between the solid stone and the organic architecture, between the warmth below and the cool heights above.

And at the top, in the canopy where the caps overlap like shingles and the air is thinnest, dwell the **High**—the Spore-Wardens. They are the elite, the harvesters, the ones who climb into the breathing dark to collect the reproductive dust. They are willow-thin, their lungs capacious, their skin more translucent than the others, the mycelial lace beneath showing almost black against the pale membrane. They wear harnesses of braided rope and climb with a spider-like grace, ascending the twenty-meter stalks to the broad caps where they shake the gills of the towering fungi, filling woven baskets with clouds of golden spores. This dust is everything: food, medicine, currency. A basket of high-quality spores can buy a copper sheet, a year's supply of chitin armor, or a night in the warm stone hollows. The Spore-Wardens are the bankers and the priests of the Warren, for the spores are also sacred, the breath of the forest, the genetic memory of the place.

But they do not rule alone. They live *with* the swarms.

The fauna is not background; it is kin. The beetle-things—thumb-sized, chitinous, clicking—are not pests but partners. The children of the Warren ride them, strapping small saddles of woven mycelium to their backs and flying low over the fungal forest, herding the parchment-winged dragonflies that pollinate the crops. The six-legged reptilian grazers are beasts of burden, their armored hides harvested for leather when they die, their meat roasted over the geothermal vents. The people speak to these creatures in a language of pheromones and vibration, a chemical tongue secreted from glands in their wrists, and the insects respond, swarming in coordinated waves at the touch of a hand, darkening the light as they move. When the Warren-Folk breathe, they inhale spores and mites; when they eat, they consume roasted insects and dried fungal fronds; when they die, their bodies are lowered into the warm stone, not buried, but *planted*—their flesh broken down by the mycelium, their bones becoming calcium deposits in the mineral crust, their amber blood seeping into the cracks to seal them in, preserving them in the resin of the mountain.

The elders are the strangest. Those who have lived longest, who have spent decades in the deepest, warmest hollows, begin to change. Their skin thickens, becomes translucent, honey-colored. The mycelium grows over them, not consuming but *preserving*, encrusting their limbs in a living amber. They become statues—half-fungal, half-mineral—still breathing, still thinking, their eyes open in the dark, their gill-slits fluttering slow as tides. They are the memory of the place. The young press their palms against the warm, resin-slick skin of the elders and feel the slow pulse of ancient lives, the accumulated wisdom of generations compressed into the amber lattice of their bodies. The elders do not speak, but the mycelium that grows through them carries their thoughts, a slow, patient cognition that thinks in centuries, in the slow digestion of stone, in the endless churn of spores.

Children are born in the warm dark, wrapped in cocoons of mycelial silk. They are pale, almost colorless, their skin so thin that the network of veins and hyphae beneath is clearly visible. They are fed on spore-milk, a secretion from the cultivated gardens, and they spend their first years in the humid lower levels, crawling on the spongy mycelial carpet, learning to climb before they can walk on the flat. They are taught early to listen to the stone, to feel the heat rising, to know the difference between the safe warmth of the vents and the dangerous cold of the upper canopy.

Life here is loud. The Warren-Folk are not quiet. They click and hum and thrum, their voices overlapping in the thick air, rising and falling with the insect swarms. They trade in the markets on the mid-level platforms—spores for copper, chitin for stone tools, labor for a place to sleep. They gather in the evenings when the bioluminescence pulses brightest, sitting on the curved flanks of the giant stalks, sharing meals of roasted beetle-things and squeezed fungal pulp, their amber-green skin glowing faintly in the dark. They are intimate, crowded, near-touching, their society as dense and layered as the fungal forest itself. They have no concept of privacy as the surface-world knows it; to live in the Warren is to be permeable, to be connected, to be part of the breath and the spore and the heat.

They are the Resin Warren’s consciousness made flesh—pale, porous, symbiotic, singing in a language of chemicals and clicks, drinking the air, bleeding amber, climbing toward the pale light, forever suspended between the warm stone below and the endless, spore-thick dark above.
