They are the ones who do not flinch at the thrum. While a visitor claps hands to their ears and staggers, the inhabitants stand with their palms flat against the iron flanks of the pumps, eyes half-closed, feeling the *thwoomp-hisss* not as sound but as a heartbeat transmitted through their sternums, through the soles of their feet, through the callused pads of their fingers. They have been here so long—so many generations since the first stilts were driven into the Pleistocene silt—that the vibration has become their native tongue. They are deaf to the air, functionally, their eardrums thickened and useless, but they hear the city perfectly through bone and fluid and the wet membrane of their own saturated bodies.

Physically, they have become amphibious in a way that is not quite adaptation and not quite surrender. Their skin is the color of wet slate, grey-green and perpetually glistening, with a faint phosphorescence that comes from the colonies of lichen and bioluminescent moss they cultivate on their shoulders and forearms—living lamps that pulse softly in the fog. They are tall and loose-jointed, their limbs elongated by years of moving through dense, humid air and climbing the slick walkways. Their eyes are enormous, occupying much of their faces, with vertical pupils like the pale fish in the drainage channels and a nictitating membrane that sweeps horizontally across the iris, clearing away the condensation that constantly mists their vision. Their fingers are long and webbed, the skin between them translucent and permanently swollen, suited for gripping wet iron and steering through the warm, milky currents.

They do not speak. Not because they cannot, but because the attempt is absurd—the roar swallows syllables before they form. Instead, they communicate through a complex grammar of pressure and vibration. They write on slates coated with a waxy, water-resistant compound, the letters large and flowing, passed hand-to-hand across the elevated walkways or sent down the drainage channels in weighted lead tubes that sink through the warm water to the platforms below. When they must speak urgently, they press their mouths to each other's ears, but even then they are shouting into the void, their voices dissolving into the white-noise roar. More often, they touch. They press their foreheads together, feeling the pulse in each other's temples, synchronized to the thirty-second revolution of the great flywheels. They embrace to share warmth, their bodies fitting together like pieces of a puzzle, sharing the damp heat that radiates from the machinery.

They live in the upper chambers of the stilted structures, above the worst of the fog but never above the mist. Their homes are platforms of timber and corrugated metal, suspended in the grey-white light, connected to the wider world only by the dripping walkways. Inside, the walls weep. Water seeps through the concrete, so they line their sleeping quarters with layers of harvested moss, which drinks the moisture and releases it slowly into the air, creating a microclimate of pure humidity. They sleep curled around the warm pipes, lulled by the subsonic thrum that vibrates through the floorboards, a lullaby that has rocked their ancestors to sleep for centuries.

They are the Pump-Tenders, the Salt-Weepers, the Canal-Walkers. Their work is maintenance and communion. They descend each morning into the lower stories, into the fog-choked machine rooms, to oil the pistons with rendered fat from the eyeless fish and synthetic lubricants distilled from the mineral springs. They clear the calcium carbonate crusts from the aqueducts, chipping away at the slow-growing disease that threatens to clog the arteries of the city. They monitor the water table, pressing their ears—or rather, their chests—against the earth to listen for shifts in the saturation of the silt. They are engineers of dampness, specialists in the art of keeping the drowning at bay, one slow stroke at a time.

Their diet is sparse and mineral-heavy. They eat the pale fish, which they catch by lying flat in the drainage channels and feeling for the tremors in the mud, snatching the creatures with quick, webbed hands. They harvest the grey grass, which they grind into a coarse, mineral-rich flour, and they cultivate thick mats of moss on the north faces of the buildings, eating it raw or fermenting it into a sour, alcoholic paste that warms the belly. They drink the superheated water straight from the taps, scalding their throats, tasting the copper and sulfur and ancient volcanic ash. The salt that crusts over everything also crusts over them—on their hair, their eyelashes, the shoulders of their heavy, woven garments—turning them white at the edges like the rocks themselves.

There are children among them, born into the mist, never knowing dry air. They play in the channels, riding the warm currents on rafts of bound reeds, their laughter lost in the roar, visible only as open mouths and flailing arms. They learn to walk on the slick walkways before they learn to write, their small, webbed feet finding purchase on algae-slicked steel where an outsider would slip and fall into the sucking mud. They are taught early to press their hands to the machinery, to feel if the pumps are healthy, to sense the rhythm of the city's breathing.

Psychologically, they are patient beyond human measure. They think in geological time. A conversation—conducted through the tapping of fingers on walkway railings, a Morse code of vibration—might take an hour to convey what an outsider would say in a sentence. They do not hurry, because the pumps do not hurry, and to hurry would be to fall out of sync with the only heartbeat they know. They are fatalistic but not despairing. They know the city is sinking, that the water is winning, grain by grain, century by century. But they have made a covenant with the wet. They are not fighting the drowning; they are learning to breathe under it. They are the Wet Altiplano's immune system, its antibodies, its stubborn, saturated heart.

When they gather, it is for the Feast of the Great Wheel, once a year, when all the pumps are synchronized and the noise shifts from a chaotic roar to a deep, harmonic drone—a subsonic chord that resonates in the marrow. In that moment, for one hour, they can speak. They can sing. They shout across the vast, empty plazas, their voices carried on the strange acoustics of the thin, wet air, bouncing off the condensing clouds and returning to them as ghosts of sound. And then the pumps drift out of phase, the roar returns, and they fall silent again, pressing their hands to the iron, feeling the city breathe, waiting for the next stroke.
