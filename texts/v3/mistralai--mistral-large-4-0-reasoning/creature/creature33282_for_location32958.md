They are the Wet People, though they have no name for themselves that would translate into dryness. They are born in the lower tiers, where the fungal light pulses blue-white through the permanent gloom, and they die with their fingers curled around chisel handles, their bodies settling into the flagstones like additional masonry. Their skin is the color of the stone after rain—gray, mottled, porous—perpetually damp, never healing from the small cuts of obsidian flakes. They have no need for eyes to see the sun, for there is no sun; instead, their eyes are small, black, and lidless, adapted to the gloom, reflecting the fungal glow like the eyes of cave fish. Their hair is sparse, wiry, and always plastered to their skulls by condensation. They move through the scaffolding with a gait that suggests swimming—legs slightly bowed, arms out for balance against the slippery stone, webbed toes gripping the flagstones.

They wear the cloaks as described: layers of woven fiber (perhaps the silk of the hard-shelled insects, harvested from the damp walls) over hides that have been coated in clay and cured in the wet heat of the lower chambers. The hides are from the beasts that die in the cisterns, or from the insects when they are large enough. The cloaks are heavy with water, doubling their weight, and the workers move with a slow, swaying gait, like kelp in a current. They smell of wet clay, of the mineral tang of stone dust, and of the particular musk of the heavy-boned beasts that permeates everything.

They are organized into rhythms, not shifts. The sound of their labor is arrhythmic, a percussion of flint on granite that never resolves into music but remains a kind of prayer. There are the Carvers, who wield obsidian flakes to etch the channels into the masonry, their hands moving in spiral patterns that mimic the water's path. There are the Fitters, who use mallets and chisels to seat the flagstones, their shoulders hunched, their backs corded with muscle from hauling stone. There are the Haulers, who manage the wooden rollers, greasing them with animal fat rendered from the beasts, shouting the relay calls that pass from tier to tier—"Up!" "Hold!" "Wet!"—their voices cracking in the damp air. And there are the Channel-Keepers, a priestly caste of sorts, who monitor the flow, adjusting the sluices, ensuring the condensation domes hum at the correct frequency, their ears pressed to the stone to listen to the water's movement through the neural-tissue patterns of the aqueducts.

They eat what the valley provides. The luminescent fungus is harvested from the condensation pools—it is slimy, tasteless, but sustains them, supplemented by the hard-shelled insects that are gathered from the slick surfaces and crushed for their moisture and protein. In the deepest chambers, the pale, eyeless things that drift like shed skin are harvested by the Deep-Dwellers, a sect of workers who live in the black water, their skin bleached white, their bodies emaciated, feeding on the translucent flesh of these creatures or perhaps farming them. The heavy-boned beasts, when they finally die—blind, albino, their hides reeking of the cisterns—are butchered, their meat tough and gray, eaten in communal stews that simmer over channels of hot water, their fat rendered to grease the rollers and waterproof the cloaks.

The beasts themselves are a tragedy of adaptation. They are vast, heavy-boned, with skulls like boulders and limbs that drag. Their eyes are milky, useless. They are kept in the deep cisterns, chained by collars of woven fiber, and they tow barges of unbaked clay through the black water, their passage marked by the grinding of stone against stone and the smell of their wet hides. They are the only source of motive power in a place without metal or wheels, their slow, dragging progress the engine that moves the clay from the excavation sites to the kilns. They are bred in the pools, or perhaps captured from the upper reaches before the Works swallowed the valley, and they live their lives in darkness, guided by vibrations in the water, their massive bodies scarred by the stone walls of the narrow channels.

The hard-shelled insects skitter across every surface, drinking from the droplets that fall from the leaking aqueducts. They are beetle-like, with shells that gleam like wet obsidian, and they are everywhere, a constant rain of tiny legs on stone. They are eaten by the workers, crushed between the teeth, their crunch a textural counterpoint to the soft fungus.

But the strangest inhabitants are the pale, eyeless things in the deepest pools. They drift like shed skin—translucent, boneless, perhaps three feet long, shaped like the molted carapaces of some impossible crustacean, but soft and pulsing with a slow, internal rhythm. They have no eyes, no mouths that can be seen, only a faint luminescence that rivals the fungus. They feed on the mineral leachate, on the dead skin of the workers that falls into the water, on the detritus of the construction. They are the valley's true natives, perhaps, or its gods. The workers do not eat them, not the ordinary workers; only the Deep-Dwellers venture into the black water to harvest them, or to worship them. They are kept in the cisterns alongside the beasts, drifting in the darkness, and sometimes, in the trembling condensation domes, their shapes can be seen through the water, undulating like ghosts.

The Wet People reproduce in the damp. There are birthing pools in the lower chambers, where the water is warm from the friction of the channels. The infants are born with webbed hands and feet, their skin already pale and translucent. They are weaned on fungus and insect paste, and by the age of five, they are apprenticed to the Carvers or the Fitters, their small hands already strong from handling stone. They never know dry air. Their lungs are adapted to the moisture-laden atmosphere; if they were to climb to the rim of the valley and breathe the drier air above, they would likely suffocate or weep blood from their lungs.

They are building a machine for being wet, and they have become the wetness. They are a civilization of drip and stone, of sound and condensation, living in the perpetual twilight of their own making, a people who have chosen to erase the horizon and replace it with masonry, who find divinity in the spiral of water and the strike of the hammer. They are patient, geological in their time-scale, and they will continue to build until the valley is entirely sheathed in stone, until the last raw geology is sealed beneath the skin of masonry, and the only sound left is the whisper of water moving through channels that resemble the veins of some great, stone-bodied beast.
