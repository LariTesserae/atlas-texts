The inhabitants are not a crowd but a congregation of specialists, each locked into a role so precise that they have become indistinguishable from the machinery they serve. They live in a state of **abundant** solitude, scattered through the humid emptiness one at a time or in pairs, each engaged in its eternal labor.

There are the **brass-beetles**, fist-sized custodians assigned to the intake valves. Their shells are polished by decades of contact with the amber sap until they mirror the stalks' inner glow, casting fractured green-gold light across the warm air. They do not hunt; they maintain. Their mandibles click against the valve housings with the rhythm of tiny gears, scraping away the mineral accretions that would choke the flow. Each beetle patrols a single flank of a single stalk, moving in slow, meditative circuits along the warm, translucent horn, never straying from its post. They are the chamber’s janitors and engineers, their clicking mandibles part of the great white-noise roar that fills the space.

In the black pools float the **moss-frogs**, motionless as stones, their skin the exact texture and color of the wet lichen that carpets the floor. They do not sing. Instead, their throats pulse—inflate and deflate—in perfect synchrony with the steam vents' *pop-hiss* cycles, breathing in time with the factory's respiration. They are the pressure-regulators, the living barometers, their bodies rising and falling with the hydraulic pressure of the sap. Perhaps there is one frog per pool, or only a single ancient specimen that has outlasted all change, its eyes reflecting the shadowless light like drops of stagnant water, its moss-skin hosting tiny colonies of algae that photosynthesize in the dim glow.

Then there are the **tenders**—creatures that resemble **крестовки**, crosses forged from clockwork, or mechanical spiders that have forgotten they are flesh. They possess too many legs—eight, perhaps ten, jointed like the pistons they service—with segmented abdomens that click as they move. They skitter across the sparse patches of moss in the spaces between the stalks, their movements precise and purposeful. They pollinate the factory-flowers, transferring golden dust from bud to bud, ensuring the machinery continues its endless production even though the seeds will never sprout. They tend the works, checking the meshing of the seed-gears, nudging crystallized resin toward the collection basins, carrying seed-pods along the peristaltic vines. They work spaced apart in the humid air, maintaining a sparse, deliberate order, never touching, each alone in its task.

Beneath these, invisible but present, are the **micro-fauna**: mites that ride upon the beetles' brass backs like passengers, crustaceans no larger than dust motes drifting in the still pools, worms threading through the root-pumps with wet, gulping rhythms. The water itself teems with life—bacteria forming iridescent films on the condensation beads that cling to the stone by surface tension, living suspended in droplets that never fall, part of the closed loop.

But the true residents, the slow-thinking landlords of this grotto, are the **three stalks themselves**. They are not merely plants but vast, translucent presences, tubes of greenish horn as thick as a man’s torso, filled with amber sap that pulses through bioluminescent veins. They think in the timescale of stone—if they think at all. They have grown into the curves of the eroded basin, locking themselves into the geology, becoming architecture and organism as one. Their "chimneys"—the flower buds the size of human heads—vent steam in rhythmic cycles, and their hydraulic pistons pump with slow, eternal purpose. They are the factory, the garden, and the congregation, rooted in the smooth, undulating stone.

Together, these beings form a **closed society in stasis**. The beetles clean so the stalks may drink. The frogs breathe so the water may circulate. The spider-crosses pollinate so the stalks may produce. And the stalks produce—one drop of sap, one spore, dripping forever from the nozzle-like pistil into the collection basin—nourishing the beetles, the frogs, the spiders, and the warm, wet, roaring equilibrium that has forgotten how to change. They are the citizens of a frozen moment, a climax ecosystem settled into perfection, each playing their part in the eternal, unchanging now.
