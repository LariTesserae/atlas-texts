They are not numerous. Like the platforms, they exist in a state of sparse density—separated by kilometers of weeping stone, yet connected by ribbons of compressed earth that shift beneath their feet as the continent settles. They appear in the mist as pale, vertical shapes, three meters tall or more, with torsos that seem slightly too long for their legs, bodies built for the thin air and the perpetual damp. Their skin, if it can be called that, is translucent and porous, the color of wet bone or alabaster, constantly beaded with moisture that they do not distinguish from the condensation gathering on the cliffs around them. They are saturated, permeable, hydrological in their nature.

They wear the infrastructure. The bone-white platforms are grown from them, secreted over years like coral or shell, resonant when struck. Some dwell within these structures—hollow chambers that hum with subsonic frequencies, where they rest in the damp dark, dreaming slow dreams of continental drift. Others are the wanderers, the herders of the mist, moving in columns across the plateau with the patience of erosion itself. Their feet make no sound on the saturated earth. They do not walk so much as settle, each step a negotiation with gravity that they have learned to modulate locally, altering their own mass to match the shifting geology beneath the smart-material roads.

They speak in frequencies that register in the sternum rather than the ear. You feel their words as pressure changes in the damp air, as subtle vibrations in the termite-mound atmosphere processors that amplify their voices across the unnamed valleys. A single sentence might take a day to form, passed from one to another across the vast distances, carried by the mist and the silent hum of the grown bridges. They are not in a hurry. Time moves here at the pace of weathering, and they have adapted their consciousness to match—thoughts that unfold like lichen, slow and thorough, covering the surface of the new stone.

They are the gardeners of the uplift. They tend the atmosphere processors not as machines but as organs, extensions of their own porous bodies that pull moisture from the thin sky and feed it to the rock. They guide the erosion by standing in the paths of emerging water, letting the stone dissolve around their translucent forms, shaping the hollows that will become valleys. They harvest the condensation that beads on their own skin, channeling it into the mineral veins of the cliffs, encouraging the birth of soil. They are the city's nervous system, feeling the continent's settling in their bones, adjusting the elevation of the platforms by dreaming them higher or lower, negotiating with gravity through will and resonance.

They move with the herds. The great musk-ox-like beasts that shed the mist recognize them as kin, or perhaps as the moving weather that precedes them. The inhabitants migrate with the fronts that roll across the continental shelf, following the water and the sparse grass, their columns stretching to the horizon in the pale gold light. They do not dominate the fauna but move among them, their subsonic lowing harmonizing with the beasts' chest-deep bellows, a duet of felt sound that travels for kilometers through the damp.

They have no individual names that translate into the tongue of the lowlands, but they might be called the Humid Ones, or the Settling People, or those who Listen to the Weeping. They are the continent learning to house itself. When they die—if they die, for their spans seem measured in geological epochs—they do not bury their dead in the earth. Instead, they carry their deceased to the black hollows where the standing water sits still and reflective, and there they let the stone grow over them, the weeping rock incorporating their translucent bodies into the strata, so that future inhabitants will be partly made of those who came before, their consciousness diffused through the mineral veins, dreaming in the slow language of uplift.

They are waiting. They are the waiting. They are the mist that keeps everything soft, the drip of water from young stone, the subsonic hum of a world still under construction, content to be the hands that guide the continent as it decides, finally, what it wants to be.
