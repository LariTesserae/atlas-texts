If you stand still long enough—long enough that your own breathing becomes a disturbance, a warmth that the plateau tries to absorb—you begin to see that the silence is not empty. It is populated by mass so vast it registers first as geology, as the weight of the lifted shelf itself pressing down. Then you realize the weight is *moving*.

They are there. The **Lithogrades**.

You do not see them at first because they have learned, over ten million years, to match the light. Their armor is not merely colored by the stone; it is *of* the stone. Each plate—overlapping, barnacle-thick, jointed like the carapace of some impossible arthropod scaled to the size of a city block—is secreted from their own metabolism, layer upon lithified layer. A young one might wear plates the color of fresh granite, sharp-edged and catching the diffuse luminescence in sharp facets. An old one wears a crust so thick, so mineralized, that it has become indistinguishable from the bedrock it grinds. It might be a boulder. It might be a butte. It might be breathing.

They have no faces. This is the first thing that strikes you when you approach—no eyes to find yours, no mouth to open, no front or back in any way you recognize. They are radial in their slowness, or perhaps linear but so extended that the concept of "forward" has dissolved into a direction of *hunger*. They navigate by taste. From their undercarriage, where the armor meets the permafrost, extend thousands of tendrils—thin as whiskers, long as bridge cables, dragging through the hoarfrost. They do not walk so much as *taste their way forward*, sampling the chemical signature of the crust, seeking the veins of mineral wealth that pulse like slow blood through the ancient rock. When a tendril finds a pocket of geothermal warmth, or a concentration of iron, or the particular silicate that sustains them, the creature adjusts its bearing by millimeters, a course correction that takes a week to complete.

Their movement is the absence of movement. A step is a day. The foot—padded not with flesh but with layers of ice that they melt slightly with the heat of their metabolism and refreeze behind them in a glassy trail—descends with a pressure that would crush a building. It does not crunch. It does not crack. The permafrost yields to it like memory yields to time. Where the fleet passes, the plateau is not merely eroded; it is *processed*. They are lithovores, and the land is their feed. As they graze, vents along their flanks—rows of openings like the gills of some stone leviathan—inhale the pulverized bedrock, digest it in chambers deep within their armored torsos, and exhale. The exhalation is a plume of warm, mineral-saturated vapor that hits the thin, freezing air and flash-freezes into a cloud of diamond dust. This is how the hoarfrost is maintained, how the dampness that defies the altitude persists. The Lithogrades are the weather. Their breath is the precipitation.

They travel in herds of a dozen, though "herd" suggests proximity that does not exist. They are spaced kilometers apart, each one a solitary continent drifting across the plain, connected only by the chemical trails they leave in the frost—pheromone-laden tracks that crystallize in the cold and persist for decades, guiding the next generation, or the next century, along the same mineral ley lines. A herd takes a century to cross the plateau. To watch one is to watch a glacier that has learned intention, that has decided, with a patience that borders on the geological, to *consume*.

And they are not alone. In the pockets where the permafrost weeps—where geothermal heat pushes upward and creates small, steaming pools rimmed with blue ice—there is a smaller life, desperate and tenacious. **Lichen** grows there in the runoff from the Lithogrades' flanks, in the mineral-rich tailings they expel. It is pale green, almost luminous in the dim twilight, photosynthesizing with a frantic efficiency against the gray. It clings to the cooling vents, to the warm stones where a resting Lithograde has heated the rock, creating microclimates of relative warmth. **Moss** carpets the dung—the processed stone, the slag-like excrement that is still rich with nutrients the creatures could not extract. These plants do not compete with the giants; they are the cleanup crew, the opportunists of the extreme, living in the shadow of the industry.

There are insects too, in those warm pockets. They are silent. They have no wings to buzz, no legs to scrape. They are beetle-things, armored in chitin the color of rust, and they communicate through chemical traces. When one finds food—a patch of lichen, a drop of mineral water—it releases a pheromone into the frost, and the message crystallizes in the air, a temporary inscription that another insect will taste with its antennae hours or days later. They are messengers in a language of ice.

But the Lithogrades are the consciousness of this place, if consciousness is the right word. They think in the timescale of stone. A season is a decade to them. They do not hurry. They do not fear. When one grows old—when its armor has thickened to the point where it can barely move, when its metabolism has slowed to a near-standstill—it does not die. It *settles*. It becomes a mesa. It becomes a ruin that looks like the foundation of a city, though no architect ever dreamed it. It hibernates for a thousand years, its breath slowing to a wisp of vapor that barely disturbs the hoarfrost, its tendrils retracted into the stone, its mineral plates fusing with the bedrock. It becomes part of the architecture, indistinguishable from the landscape it helped create. A canyon is not a wound; it is a road. A butte is not a hill; it is a dreaming giant.

To live here is to be surrounded by the slowest kind of life. The air is thin, but the presence is thick. When you walk—carefully, so as not to disturb the frost that holds the memory of chemical trails—you are walking through the exhalations of creatures that have been grinding this plateau into itself since before the ice age, since before the sky was this particular shade of gray. You are walking through a factory that has no walls, tended by machines that grew themselves, in a silence so absolute that you can hear the tick of cooling metal in the armor of a resting Lithograde, can hear the whisper of frost sublimating from a stone warmed by a passing herd, can hear the subsonic groan of a continent settling under the weight of giants who have nowhere else to be, and nowhere to go but forward, one day at a time, grinding the world into dust and calling it bread.
