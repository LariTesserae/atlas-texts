They move like the frost moves—slowly, inevitably, covering everything in time. The workers are few, scattered across the kilometers of the crescent like pale lichen on stone, but their presence is marked by the only heat in the valley: the small, stubborn fires of their bodies, visible as halos of steam that crystallize almost immediately into diamond dust. They wear suits the color of the plants—pale, fleshy, insulated against the near-freezing damp—and when they stand still against the columns, they are nearly indistinguishable from the architecture, extensions of the hoarfrost given purpose and hunger.

Their faces, when the bioluminescence catches them, are landscapes of deprivation. The skin is pulled tight, translucent in places, showing the blue of veins beneath. Their eyes are vast, black, adapted to the dim spectrum of sodium vapor and the sickly green-blue pulse of the fungal glow. They do not speak. The roar—the absolute, crushing 2.8 that vibrates in the sternum—has long since flattened their vocal cords into uselessness, or perhaps they have simply learned that sound is a resource to be hoarded, not spent. They communicate in the language of gesture and light: the sweep of a helmet lamp across a frost-covered wall, the tap of a gloved finger against a plant-core, the sudden, urgent blink of a bioluminescent patch sewn into a shoulder. They are miners, yes, but also priests, attending to the slow liturgy of growth.

They have been here long enough to become part of the ecology. The mycelium does not reject them. Pale threads creep beneath their suit seals, colonizing the warmth of their armpits, the damp of their necks, the hollow behind their ears. It is a benign infection, or perhaps a necessary one—the fungal network allows them to feel the plants' pulse, the slow, arrhythmic heartbeat of sap moving through calcified tissue. Some workers have stopped trying to remove the growths. They let the mycelium mat their hair, let it thicken the skin of their hands until their fingers are clubbed and pale, indistinguishable from the fronds above. They are becoming architecture themselves, mobile, temporary, but part of the load-bearing structure of the place.

But the columns are the true residents, the landlords of this frozen cathedral. Each forty-meter specimen is a single consciousness stretched across decades, a mind that thinks in chemical gradients and pressure differentials, in the slow creep of frost and the patient accumulation of ice. They remember the excavation—the violence of the blast scars is written in their tissue as trauma, a memory of shockwaves that shattered their ancestors. They have learned to tolerate the pipes, to route their nutrients around the metal intrusions, to grow in directions that cradle the platforms rather than crush them. They are prisoners and collaborators both. When the ventilation shafts scream, their canopies shudder, releasing avalanches of ice crystals that glitter in the sodium light. They dream in bioluminescence, pulsing with a rhythm that matches the dripping water, the slow death of the rock above them.

In the dark pools that collect between the plant-masses, the natives drift. They are the only creatures born here, evolved in the cold water that gathers in the hollows of the permafrost. They are pale, translucent, shaped like elongated petals or perhaps like the shed skins of something larger that has not yet emerged. They possess no eyes—only sensitive tendrils that detect the vibration of the pumps, the chemical signature of the fungal rot, the warmth of a worker's bootstep on the spongy mat above. They move by the currents that the ventilation creates, drifting for hours without purpose, feeding on the detritus that falls from the heights: dead skin, spores, the occasional corpse of a worker who slipped on the ice. They are unhurried because they have no predators, no urgency, no concept of the roaring machinery above. They are the archivists of the deep, their bodies slowly accumulating the minerals of the water, becoming encrusted with calcium, turning into living stalactites that hang suspended in the dark water, dreaming their slow, blind dreams.

And then there are the others. The maintainers. In the vast echoing gaps between the monoliths—where the frost grows undisturbed for years, where the sodium arcs cast only faint, yellow shadows—there are shapes that move only when observed peripherally. They are not human. They are compacted life: colonies of extremophile bacteria given temporary form by the pressure and the cold, or perhaps the accumulated consciousness of the young rock itself, waking up to tend its wounds. They scrape the ice from the conveyor belts with tools made of sharpened limestone. They repair the cables that the glacial grip is slowly crushing. They are silent, efficient, and they do not fear the cold because they are made of it—frost in their joints, ice in their veins. They are the valley's immune system, cleaning the scars of excavation, preparing the space for the slow reclamation that will happen when the pumps finally fail and the lights go out, when the plants will expand without restraint and the whole chamber will become a single, breathing organism, slowly digesting the machines into fertilizer.
