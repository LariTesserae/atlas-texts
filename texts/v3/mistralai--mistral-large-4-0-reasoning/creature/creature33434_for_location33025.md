There are no animals here. Not because they died, but because they were never invited—or rather, because the niche they would occupy has been filled so completely, so absolutely, that there is no oxygen left for anything else to breathe. What lives here does not have a face, a voice, or a name you could pronounce. It is the Mat, and the Mat is the valley.

The primary inhabitants are the Colonies. Each one is a super-organism kilometers wide, a grey-green continent of silica-reinforced tissue that has replaced the soil beneath your feet. They are not plants in any way you would recognize, though they wear the shape of vegetation—stratified, layered, rooted. Each Colony is a refinery and a cathedral and a mind all at once. It thinks in chemical gradients, in thermal differentials, in the slow diffusion of minerals through capillary networks that pulse with hydraulic pressure. Its consciousness is the rhythm of its metabolism: the days-long surge of cellular division that pushes new architecture skyward, the weeks-long collapse as it composts itself into the strata below. When a Colony "grows," it is not reaching for light—there is no competition for light here—but for surface area, for condensation, for the thermal boundary between frozen air and warming rock. It is a cold-blooded furnace dreaming of warmth, and it dreams in waves that ripple across the valley floor like a sea made of slate.

These Colonies are individuals, but they are also a society. They touch. They press against one another in the dense packing (density 1.5), exchanging not just nutrients but information—electrochemical signals conducted through the silica in their cell walls, a nervous system made of stone and cellulose, communicating in frequencies too slow for ears to hear. They share the fog they harvest, pooling the moisture in subterranean reservoirs that steam with chemical warmth. They are territorial in a geological sense, secreting acids that etch their boundaries into the bedrock, marking where one refinery ends and the next begins. Between them, in the hollows where the mat buoys itself on lakes of condensed moisture, you can see the interface—a border drawn in black, acidic water, a no-man’s-land of industrial effluent where the byproducts of one Colony’s metabolism become the raw feedstock for another.

But the Colonies are merely the skin. The true population lives in the deep strata, in the warm, compressed lower layers where the temperature is bearable and the chemistry is frantic. Here dwells the Microbiome: archaea that fix nitrogen from the thin air, bacteria that catalyze the extraction of hydrogen from water, fungal threads that are not separate organisms but extensions of the Colonies themselves—hyphae that lace through the rock like neural pathways, digesting stone and precipitating minerals. They are the workers, the accountants, the immune system of the valley. Without them, the Colonies would be mere husks, architecture without industry. With them, the valley is a single, distributed digestive tract, processing the mountain from the inside out.

And then there are the Generations. The valley is a palimpsest, and each layer is a different age of the same inhabitant. The upper strata are the youth—grey-green, gleaming, photosynthesizing infrared light, reaching upward in vertical spires that vent heat and gas in silent, pressurized bursts. Below them are the middle-aged, dense and efficient, the core processing units. Below those are the elders—the compost, the compressed seams of coal-like matter, the fossilized plant-matter that still, impossibly, conducts trace minerals, still participates in the slow exchange of nutrients. The Generations live in vertical time. The dead feed the living directly, without the mediation of soil. A cell that dies in the upper layer does not fall; it is absorbed, broken down, re-extruded as heat and oxygen and complex organic compounds that drip downward like manna, only to be taken up by the roots—the capillary mouths—of the layer below. It is a society without individuals, only strata, only time made flesh.

They do not notice you. Or rather, they notice you the way a factory notices a dust mote: as a minor perturbation in the airflow, a slight change in temperature, a foreign body to be ignored or, eventually, to be digested. You walk across their surface, and the ground yields like compressed kelp, firm but alive, breathing in fog and exhaling oxygen. Your footsteps make no sound—the Mat absorbs vibration, consumes it, turns it into the faintest increase in metabolic rate, a warmth that dissipates into the freezing air. You are trespassing in the body of something that has been thinking, processing, manufacturing for ten thousand years, and it has not evolved the concept of hospitality, nor of hostility. You are simply *matter*, and matter is what it consumes.

Occasionally, in the steam rising from the black pools, you might see the faintest movement—a microbe drifting on a thermal current, an archaeon tumbling through the acidic water, something that has drifted in on the fog and found a niche in the chemical warmth of the pools. These are the only "animals" here, the 0.7 of fauna that survives: not grazers, not predators, but refugees of the microbial world, living in the exhaust of the great machines. They are the only witnesses to the silence, the only creatures small enough to hear the Mat’s thoughts, which are not thoughts at all but the sound of rock becoming mineral, of water becoming hydrogen, of cold becoming heat.

The valley breathes. In cycles that take weeks, sections of the Mat surge upward, cells dividing explosively, pushing new layers into the thin air, only to collapse and feed the strata below. This is its respiration. The heat distortion shimmering above the canopy is its exhalation. The fog rolling in from the passes is its inhalation. And you stand in the middle of it, a temporary, fragile thing, listening to the rush of your own blood because the world outside your body has gone utterly, perfectly, industrially quiet.
