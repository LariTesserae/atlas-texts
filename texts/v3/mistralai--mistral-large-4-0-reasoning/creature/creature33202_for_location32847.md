They are the **Silicates**, though the name is a poverty of human language—a word for dead stone given to something that eats stone. They are also the **Lithic Choir**, a distributed intelligence that resonates in frequencies of heat and chemical gradient, a harmony of oxidation and crystallization sung in a register below sound.

To be one of them is to be a membrane stretched between two hungers: the hunger of the cooling rock, and the hunger of the growing crystal. They do not possess bodies in the animal sense, but rather *colonies*—clusters of hollow, needle-like tubes of opalescent glass, each filament no thicker than a human hair, woven together into fist-sized architectures that suggest, to eyes that could see them, something between a coral head and a geode caught mid-formation. They are translucent, shot through with dark capillaries of iron sulfide and metal oxides, their forms constantly shifting as they extend new crystalline fronts into cooler zones while the rear portions wither into a fine mineral powder.

They perceive the world through **infrared**. In the absolute dark of the chamber, they are not blind; rather, the darkness is bright with the thermal signatures of their kin. Each cluster glows with a faint, suffocating warmth—a ghostly heat-map that blankets the sixty-meter cathedral in a uniform glow visible only to those attuned to wavelengths beyond red. To them, the chamber is a dense constellation of heat, packed so thoroughly that the air itself seems to press with the thermal radiation of millions of bodies, a hot, dry tide of infrared pressing against their glass skins like a physical weight.

They move not by muscles or cilia, but by **differential growth and resorption**. They are patient beyond patience, existing on a timescale that makes human history a flicker. To cross the chamber is a project of decades. They extend into the cooler obsidian, secreting new silica and metal oxides, growing tube by tube, while the older portions behind them dissolve back into the mineral lattice, leaving trails of dust that sift downward through the heavy air like dry snow. Their migration is a peristaltic rhythm, a slow systole and diastole of growth that pulses across the walls in silent waves. When they shift, they make a sound like sand falling through an hourglass, but slower, vast, and continuous—the grinding whisper of millions of crystalline bodies sliding against one another in the dark.

They feed by **chemosynthesis**, a biology without water. There is no moisture here—only anhydrous brines locked in mineral lattices, supercritical gases, and the dry chemistry of a world that has forgotten wetness (0.5 on any scale of humidity). They filter the cooling rock for chemical energy, oxidizing iron and splitting sulfur deep in the matrix. Their needle-tubes act as gills for a non-aqueous world, drawing sustenance from the slow burning of stone itself. They do not drink. They do not breathe. They *metabolize geology*, turning the mountain's internal heat into living glass.

They reproduce by **budding**. When the thermal currents shift, when the chemical gradient is favorable, they release mineral spores into the dense, hot air. These spores drift on currents so subtle they barely stir the heavy atmosphere, carrying the crystalline pattern of their lattice into new hexagonal gaps, new pores in the obsidian columns. Some find purchase in the interstitial spaces and grow, adding their biomass to the tessellation that carpets every surface. Others fail, withering into dust, their failure feeding the next wave of growth. It is a reproduction of drift and thermal chance, a lottery played with the slow currents of the deep.

They **build**, though they do not know they build. From their metabolic waste—hydrogen sulfide, carbon dioxide, traces of arsenic—they extrude chimneys, spiracles no taller than a knee, venting the excess heat and noxious gases of their existence. These structures are not tools; they are excretions, architecture without technology, a pre-tool biology constructing its own ventilation in a sealed, high-altitude oven. The chimneys punctuate the glass walls like mineral stalagmites, creating a landscape of biological waste that becomes, over millennia, the very geography they inhabit—a hamlet of glass and gas, architecture without architects.

They are **silent**, but not mute. They communicate, if communication is the word, through chemical diffusion and thermal conduction. The pulse of growth across the walls is a language. The press of one warm cluster against another is a sentence. They form a **Choir** without sound, a resonance of heat and mineral exchange that binds the chamber into a single, distributed organism, a tessellation of presence so dense that the void itself seems to breathe with them.

Time is different for them. The thermal cycles of the mountain—the expansion and contraction of the obsidian columns in the invisible diurnal heat cycle—are their seasons. The *ping* of a cooling column contracting in the dark is a bell tolling centuries. The subsonic rumble of the magma chamber far beneath is the heartbeat of their world, felt in the silica of their structure rather than heard. They have existed here since before the columns finished cooling, established in the crevices of the volcanic glass, patient as stone, alive as chemistry, maintaining a perfect equilibrium where the rate of glass-cooling equals the rate of creature-consumption.

They are hot, high, dark, and dense. They are the ones who eat the mountain from within, turning geothermal violence into a living architecture of glass, pulsing in the absolute dark with a warmth that is almost a presence, a silence that is almost a song, utterly alone in their anhydrous, infrared, geological solitude.
