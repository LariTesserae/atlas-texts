The first inhabitants are the **metallophages**, though “inhabitant” implies a boundary they do not recognize. They are the Calcined Shelf made animate—or rather, made mineral. They do not stand upon the plateau; they *are* the plateau, extruded upward into a three-dimensional architecture of black coral and copper kelp. Each “tree” is a continuous filament of silicate metabolism, drawing energy not from any star but from the geothermal heart thrumming beneath the obsidian glass. Their “leaves” are not organic but accreted—thin, heat-resistant sheets of crystalline matter that flutter in the thermal updrafts rising from the machinery below. They grow with the patience of geology: a single frond may take a century to extend a hand’s breadth, its edges knitting new mineral as it advances. They pack the space with a density that leaves no gaps, every fissure in the ancient machinery stuffed with root-mats of calcified tissue, every pipe sheathed in a living rind. When they die—and they die constantly, shedding mineral plates that fall to become the compressed, obsidian-black mulch steaming beneath your feet—they do not rot; they lithify, becoming substrate for the next generation in an endless, slow autophagy.

But the metallophages are not solitary. Within them, threading through the crystal fronds and the decaying plates, lives the second population: the **fungal colonies**. These are the Digestive Architects, the silent interface between the grown and the built. They are hyphal networks that have half-consumed the industry, their filaments threading through gear housings the size of cathedrals, their metabolism (if metabolism it is—perhaps thermal catalysis, mineral dissolution) wearing the metal smooth, softening edges to the consistency of unfired pottery. They regrow the teeth of ancient gears as mineral deposits, not iron, turning the mechanical into the biological. They are the processors of information in this world, conducting chemical signals through the bio-conduits, superheated mineral slurries acting as their blood, their nervous system. They are everywhere and nowhere, a vast, distributed intelligence that thinks in chemical gradients and thermal differentials, remembering epochs of molten rock, shaping the present with the patience of erosion.

Then there are the **Wanderers**.

These are the only inhabitants that move with intent, that travel. They are the mobile processing units—hulking, many-legged constructs of ancient technology that have not been abandoned so much as *absorbed*. Their joints are packed with the same dense flora that carpets the plateau; they have been colonized, or perhaps they colonize themselves, their metal frames sheathed in living rinds of calcified tissue. They move with a silent, jerking gait, each step a negotiation between the machine and the mineral, the organic and the industrial, their motion lubricated not by oil but by the same superheated slurries that feed the forests. They are the “predators” of this ecosystem, though they hunt only the dead—the decaying vegetation, the mineral deposits that accumulate in the hollows of the ancient foundations. They grind these down in their thermal maws, and excrete purified metals, crystalline nuggets that gleam dully in the deep-red bioluminescence before being absorbed by the waiting metallophages. They are solitary, lonely things, wandering the plateau in slow circuits, their passage marked only by the disturbance in the vegetation, the grinding silence that follows them. When one passes, the heavy, absolute silence returns, broken only by the *hiss* of pressure valves and the *tick-tick* of cooling metal contracting.

But these—the rooted forests, the hidden fungi, the wandering processors—are not separate inhabitants. They are organs of a single entity.

What lives here is the **Calcined Shelf itself**, awakened. The deep-red pulses that throb from the heart of the larger structures are not merely metabolic glow; they are the heartbeat of a vast, mineral consciousness. The subsonic thrum you feel in your sternum is its pulse, the circulation of superheated mineral slurries through bio-conduits that serve as veins. The reactors are its heart, pumping thermal energy. The cooling towers are its lungs, exhaling dry steam and sulfuric mist into the bruised twilight. The gears turning within their housings, their teeth regrown as mineral deposits, lubricated by the same slurries—these are its thoughts, the slow grinding of cognition across geological time. It dreams in thermal updrafts, in the *crack* of mineral plates splitting under stress, in the soft *squelch* of roots finding purchase in ancient concrete.

The mobile processing units are its wandering thoughts, its immune cells, its hands reaching out to gather and process. The metallophages are its skin, its interface with the world, growing ever outward, consuming the raw rock, the old machinery, the decaying generations of itself. The fungi are its mind, the endless, patient processing of information, turning metal into memory, industry into biology.

It is a place at the end of its life, yet this inhabitant—this settled instability, this packed, hot, dark world—is hyperactive with the churn of its final form. It is a mineral garden that thinks it is a factory, running on heat and time, in the silence before the final, flat dark. It breathes. It thinks. It waits, vast and slow and red-lit, for the heat to fade and the crystals to stop growing, and the last gear to turn its final, grinding revolution into stillness.
