They are the Drift-Kin, though they have no name for themselves—only the sensation of pressure, the taste of mineral on the tongue, the particular frequency at which the oak wheels turn. They move through the waist-deep water with a dreaming grace, their pale, eyeless bodies undulating in schools that match the forty-minute rotation of the machinery above. They are grown large in the isolation of this place, the size of dogs, their skin translucent as old parchment, veined with dark, branching patterns that pulse in time with the pistons—a living circuitry, a vascular map of the conduits that run through the walls.

They do not fear the iron; they *tend* it. You will see them press their flattened faces against the turning gears, letting the teeth comb through their gills in a slow, rhythmic massage that harvests something from the friction—perhaps parasites, perhaps the metallic dust that they shed, perhaps simply the vibration that tells them the machine is healthy. Their digits are wrong, too many, jointed in ways that suggest both bone and gear-tooth, clicking softly against the grated walkways as they climb, gripping the metal with a familiarity that spans generations. They navigate the narrow channels between pipes with ancestral memory, knowing by the temperature of the water and the hum in their teeth exactly which conduit carries the dark, copper-scented fluid and which carries the thin, electric current that makes the algae glow.

They are a slow people. A mother will hover over her clutch of eggs—translucent spheres nestled in the flowstone curtains, beaded with condensation—for three years, fanning them with her tail, maintaining the precise chemical balance of the shallows. When the young hatch, they are smaller than a human hand, drifting like plankton in the viscous water, eyeless and helpless, until the metallic dust settles into their tissues and they grow heavy enough to sink to the terraces, to claim a territory among the rusted pipes. They gestate for decades, live for centuries, and move with a deliberation that makes the drip of water from the ceiling seem like a frantic drumbeat.

They do not speak, but they write. The fine, metallic dust that trails from their bodies settles on the water’s surface and sinks slowly, like ash, like snow. It forms patterns—territorial markers, perhaps, or records of the great annual inundation, a history legible only to those who know how to read the settling of particles. They mark the walkways with it, the gears, the calcite curtains. It is a written language of slowness, a literature of saturation.

When they die—and they die often, for the water is viscous and clinging, and the deep basin is dark and patient—they do not rot. They sink to the bottom, to the central depression where the water is deepest, and there they calcify. Their bodies become part of the mineral matrix, their bones turning to limestone, their translucent skin fossilizing into the walls. They are building the cathedral with their own remains, generation upon generation, until the terraces themselves are composed of their ancestors, a palimpsest of pale, eyeless life.

And beneath them all, in the deepest part of the basin where the light fails entirely and the bioluminescent algae cannot reach, there is the Sleeper. It is vast, continent-sized in its patience, a shape that might be a foundation, or a fossil, or a sleeping engine—perhaps the first of the Drift-Kin, grown too large to move quickly, sunk into the sediment over millennia until it became indistinguishable from the geology. It shifts in its sleep, too slow to track, too ancient to notice you watching. Its dreams are the slow rise and fall of the water table, the year-long cycle of inundation and recession. When it turns in the dark, the whole chamber breathes, and the salamander-things rise to the surface with a sound like a sigh, turning their eyeless faces toward the vibration, paying homage to the vast, patient thing that gestates beneath them.

There are no humans here, not anymore. Or rather, the humans dissolved into the water long ago—their bones becoming the limestone, their nervous systems becoming the cables, their blood becoming the thick, dark fluid that the pistons pump. What remains is the Drift-Kin, the adaptation, the memory. They are tended by the fungi: shelf fungi the color of old bone that grow from their calcified remains, glowing faintly with phosphorescence, a memorial industry that turns death into architecture. The algae coat the water’s surface in patches of turquoise and sickly green, the photosynthetic base of this economy, drinking the weak electric light and giving back oxygen and glow.

They live by the rules of saturation. Nothing happens quickly. They feel the hum of the cables in their teeth, a low thrum that is the closest thing they have to music. They breathe the cool, saturated air, wet and mineral-heavy, and move through the shallows with a grace that is not animal but geological—patient, inevitable, and utterly at home in the weeping, dim, slow cathedral of gears and water.
