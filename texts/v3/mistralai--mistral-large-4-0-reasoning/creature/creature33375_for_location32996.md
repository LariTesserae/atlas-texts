They are the Chorus, first—the winged multitude that clings to the steam. Not insects, exactly, though they have the segmented thorax and the compound eye; not krill, though they swarm with that same frantic, tidal density. They are pressure-beings, born from the vents that are also gills, their wings translucent as new shed skin, catching the greenish-blue twilight and refracting it into halos. They live in the condensing breath itself, navigating the gill-vents in dense, shifting clouds that part and reform with the rhythm of the machinery. When the pistons compress, they rise; when the steam hisses, they scatter. They are the respiratory cilia, the living filtration system, their bodies filtering the mineral-heavy rain from the air, storing it in distended abdomens that glow faintly with the bioluminescence of the fungi they consume. They do not so much inhabit the space as constitute its atmosphere—thousands of tiny, beating hearts keeping the air in motion, preventing the silence from settling too deep.

Beneath them, in the thick mats of pale moss that carpet the stone, live the Burrowers. They are segmented like the pistons they maintain, their bodies a series of calcified rings that expand and contract with the same rhythm as the gears above. They are the maintenance crew, the digestive tract, the white blood cells of this place. They burrow through the moss—which is not plant but neural tissue, a sensory carpet pulsing with faint electrical signatures—leaving trails of phosphorescent slime that acts as both lubricant and coolant. Where the machinery grinds, they secrete; where the calcium gears mesh, they deposit fresh mineral, their bodies slowly becoming indistinguishable from the keratin and stone they tend. They are blind, or nearly so, navigating by the vibration of the floor, by the pressure changes in the hollow. They live in the dark between the walls, in the wet cold, processing the detritus that falls from the conveyor belts, breaking it down into the raw minerals that precipitate from the condensing water, feeding the growth of the structure itself.

And stalking the rhythmic waves of muscular flesh—the conveyor belts—are the Synchronized. They are the predators, the immune system, the quality control. Many-legged, sleek, their chitin plating still translucent at the margins, new as the day they hatched from the calcified eggs that bud from the walls. They have learned the timing of the grinding gears, moving in staccato bursts that match the hydraulic compression, snatching the Chorus from the air or the Burrowers from the moss when they stray too far from the processing nodes. They do not hunt for hunger alone; they hunt to cull, to maintain the balance of the metabolism. Their movements are a blur of motion synchronized with the machinery's cycles, and in their hunting, they are indistinguishable from the gears themselves—another set of teeth in the mouth of the place.

But deeper in, where the darkness pools at the center of the chamber, walk the Calcified. They are the tenders, the gardeners, the neural tissue given locomotion. They are tall or small depending on whether you measure them against the vastness of the hollow or the minuteness of the machinery, but they are always growing, always secreting. Their skin is the translucent chitinous plating mentioned in the young stone, their fingernails calcified hooks that mesh with the keratin gears, turning them by hand or by thought. They do not operate the machinery from without; they are grown from within it, extruded from the same processes that form the pistons and the conveyor belts. They breathe the condensing air, their lungs gill-like and efficient, exhaling the warm, mineral-heavy breath that rains back down upon them. They walk among the grinding stone with a gait that matches the rhythm, their bodies swaying with the pulse of the bioluminescent fungi, their eyes—if they are eyes—reflecting the greenish-blue twilight without comprehension or need of it. They are the consciousness distributed through the organism, the thoughts that move through the synapses of calcium and flesh, tending the metabolism, ensuring the mineral deposits precipitate correctly, guiding the growth of the new edges.

And at the very center, in the suspended darkness where the silence is pressure and the emptiness is vast, there is the Dreaming. It is not a creature but the absence that makes the inhabitants possible—the womb, the unborn mind of the single organism that processes and manufactures and metabolizes. The Calcified walk around it, never into it, for it is the space where the new thoughts form, where the mechanical flower's bud tightens before blooming. It is fed by the rhythms of the walls, by the cacophony of grinding and hissing, by the wet slap of the organic belts. The Chorus provides its nerve impulses, the Burrowers its digestion, the Synchronized its immune response, the Calcified its awareness. And the Dreaming breathes in the hollow, exhaling the condensing mist that hangs in beads on every surface, the breath that never quite clears.

They are all one body. The pocket is the womb, the inhabitants are the amniotic life, the cells of a new industrial god being born in fast-forward, growing sharper, more complex, emerging from the wet earth like a mechanical flower blooming in the cold, wet, loud, and dimly lit dark.
