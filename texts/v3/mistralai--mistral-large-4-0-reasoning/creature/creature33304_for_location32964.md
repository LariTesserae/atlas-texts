The warmth has a pulse, though you cannot feel it against your own skin—it is too slow, too deep, a thrumming in the marrow of the stone rather than the air. It is metabolic, but the metabolism has been decelerated to the pace of erosion, to the patience of water wearing satin into limestone. The heat radiates from the ledge, from the place where the palms once rested and now rest still.

There, on the flat ledge, sits the occupant.

They are not seated in the posture of a person, exactly, but in the posture of stone that has learned to hold a shape. Their back is against the curve of the wall, knees drawn up not by muscle but by the settling of mineral into joint, the body having gradually, over millennia, surrendered its distinction from the rock it touches. They are limestone now, or the limestone is them—cream-to-ochre, the same color, the same satin finish worn by the same intermittent water. Their skin, if it can still be called skin, is beaded with condensation that never dries, that gathers in the shallow striations of their collarbones, their wrists, the backs of their hands where the palms press flat against the stone beneath them.

The step on the wall is not worn by countless feet, though it appears so. It is worn by one foot, pressed against the limestone for ten thousand years, the weight of a body that has slowly, grain by grain, sunk into the stone until the boundary between pressure and yielding disappeared. The concavity is the shape of a heel, a sole, the particular arch of a foot that refused to move. The satin polish is not from water alone, but from the friction of skin against stone across geological time, the two substances negotiating a treaty of identity.

Their hands rest on the ledge, fingers spread in a gesture of permanent repose. The stone has grown up around them, not trapping them, but incorporating them—the outline of knuckles visible beneath a veneer of calcite, the whorls of fingerprints preserved in mineral, legible only to the touch of another hand pressed against them in the dark. The lichens crust their shoulders in map-like patches, pale and patient, symbiotic now; they are not growing on the occupant so much as being grown, part of a single organism that breathes on the timescale of centuries.

They breathe. You can see it, if you watch long enough, in the movement of the air itself—the heavy, held breath of the bowl is their respiration. An inhale takes a decade, drawing the greenish dimness down from the rim, pulling moisture from the porous walls, filling the lungs (which are cavernous, lined with mineral, capable of holding the damp for years). An exhale follows, slower still, releasing a warmth that settles into the pool below, raising a mist that catches the light in ghostly veils. The air is thick because it is their breath, recycled through stone lungs, filtered of everything but moisture and memory.

The pool at the bottom reflects the dim light, a perfect mirror, but the occupant does not look into it. Their eyes are closed, or perhaps they are open and simply reflect the same featureless twilight that fills the far wall—dark, wet, unblinking. They stare at nothing, or at everything, their consciousness having retreated into the slow dreaming of geology. They remember the sun, but the memory is a fossil, heavy and mineralized, lying in the strata of the mind. They remember the rain, but it falls upward in their dreams, returning to the cloud. They remember being other than stone, but the memory is eroding, smoothing into the general satin finish of the self.

This is why no animal lives here. Not because the place is empty, but because it is occupied by a being that has achieved such perfect stasis, such absolute equilibrium, that there is no niche left for the quick and the hungry. The spiders abandoned their webs not because the air is too thick, but because the occupant's breath is too slow, too constant, offering no variation in which to anchor a thread. The insects fled the warmth, which is not the warmth of life but the warmth of a body in the final, patient stages of becoming mineral. The fauna level is null because the occupant has, through sheer duration, become the fauna—the single, dominant species, the apex predator of stillness.

The dripping from the ceiling is their pulse, or their weeping, or simply the condensation of their presence in the saturated air. Each drop that falls with the sound of a pin is a heartbeat measured in centuries, a thought completed, a memory fossilized. They do not move. They do not need to. They are the erosion, the conclusion, the final polish. The step yields beneath them not because they press down, but because they have become heavy with time, dense with the accumulated weight of millennia spent learning how to be still.

The greenish light filters down and settles on their shoulders, and they do not turn away. They are the cathedral and the saint, the apse and the prayer, the silence that has learned to breathe.
