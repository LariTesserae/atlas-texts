They are the Silt-Kin, though they do not call themselves that—they have names for the wet places, the humming chambers, the weeping corners, the upward-flowing water, but not for themselves as a people, only as roles: the Plasterers, the Diggers, the Channel-Keepers, the Listeners.

Physically, they are small and dense, built for compression. Their spines are flexible, allowing them to curl into the ovoid sleeping niches carved into the clay. Their skin is pale, almost luminous in the gray dark, and strangely permeable—not wounded by the constant damp, but adapted to it, beaded with moisture that they do not wipe away but rather massage into their flesh, a habit that keeps their skin supple against the 0.6-degree chill. They are warm creatures, metabolically furnace-like, and in the tight press of the chambers their collective heat creates pockets of steam that rise to the ceilings and condense, feeding the perpetual mist. They are nearly hairless, save for a fringe of stiff, reed-like filaments around the crown of the head that wicks water away from the eyes, and perhaps heavy, sensitive eyebrows that channel the steady drip from the ceiling into the designated weeping corners of their personal niches.

Their eyes are large, dark, and lid-heavy, adapted to the 1.5 luminescence—the filtered gray light from the angled ventilation shafts and the smoky yellow of fat lamps. They see best in the gloom; the surface world, when they emerge through the snorkels, is painfully bright, a bleached and burning place. They navigate primarily by touch and vibration. Their fingertips are ridged, almost corrugated, sensitive to the subsonic hum of the clay—a low resonance that seems to emanate from the hill itself as it expands and contracts with moisture. They can feel the hill breathe through their palms pressed flat against the walls: a slow systole and diastole of pressure and damp that tells them which passages are safe, which are closing overnight, where the water is rising, where the geometry has shifted in the dark.

They dress in layers of woven reed and clay-plastered fiber, garments stiff with dried silt that crack and flake as they move with a sound like dry leaves, protecting them from the wet that would otherwise soak to the bone. They go barefoot, their feet broad and splayed, the toes slightly webbed, gripping the stone slabs of the raised floors and the slippery rims of the drainage channels.

Their society is organized not around blood but around maintenance. A household is a work-gang of three to seven individuals bound by the shared responsibility of a section of wall, a length of channel, a ventilation chimney. They sleep in shifts, not from necessity but because the chambers are too small for all to rest at once, and because someone must always be listening, always feeling for the slump, the crack, the change in the hum. The "breathing of three households" that one can hear through the shared walls is not metaphorical; they press their ears to the blue clay and listen to the wet respiration of their neighbors, a sound like lungs filling with fluid. This is their intimacy. This is their privacy, in a place where sound carries and walls are thin.

They are experts in the behavior of young, unweathered sediment. They can read the moisture content of a wall by taste—licking the surface to see if it is too wet, too dry, or just right for the crushed-shell plaster they mix weekly. The renewal of the plaster is a ritual act. Every seven days, teams of Plasterers move through the galleries with baskets of spoil lashed to their backs, scraping away the slumping surface with stone adzes, slapping on fresh blue clay mixed with crushed shell, smoothing it with flat river stones until it gleams wet and new. They chant while they work, a low, thrumming vocalization that matches the frequency of the hill's hum, and in doing so, they believe they are helping the hill to breathe, keeping its airways clear, participating in its slow exhalation.

Their technology is of the hafted stone. They knap flint with obsidian edges using antler hammers, creating tools sharp enough to shear through the sharp-edged silt. They have no metal; when they find a nodule of iron pyrite in the clay—a glint of fool's gold—they treat it as a relic, a gift from the hill's dreams, and haft it into a wooden handle as a charm against the weirdness. They make needles from the leg bones of the pale, eyeless fish that drift in the deepest sump-pools, and with these they sew the reed-matting that lines their sleeping platforms, creating a barrier between their warm bodies and the cold, mineral-heavy water that seeps up through the stone slabs.

They store grain in clay-lined pits, fighting a constant war against saturation, and they render fat from the sparse fauna they hunt on the surface—stunted, salt-tolerant shrubs, wiry grasses, and the occasional bird that mistakes the ventilation snorkels for reeds. The smoke from their clay-lined hearths is precious; it is vented carefully through the chimney-shafts, for it is one of the few dry things in their world, and its yellow flame is the only warm light in the luminous gloom.

But most of all, they are geologists of the intimate. They understand that they do not live in a city built upon the land, but in a process, in the land itself. The hill is dynamic, alive at 1.7, and they are its maintenance crew, its symbiotes, its nervous system. When a wall slumps—a distant chunk followed by the slap of clay and the tamping of earth—they do not despair. They dig. They carve new chambers deeper, where the clay is denser, where the water runs black and still, where the air is thick with condensation and the temperature drops to the cool extreme, where the walls seem to pulse with a rhythm that matches their own heartbeats.

Here, in the deepest levels, they have learned to accept the weirdness: the way water flows upward in narrow capillary channels, defying gravity, pooling on ceilings before dripping down; the way straight passages become curved from week to week, round chambers becoming ovoid, as if the hill is dreaming and rearranging itself in its sleep. They do not fight this instability. They flow with it, redirecting the interior rivers, carving new sumps as old ones silt up, opening passages that closed overnight, always moving downward and inward as the upper levels become too unstable, too wet, too alive.

They believe—if belief is the right word—that the hill is gestating something. Or that they are gestating inside it. That this packed, wet, breathing dark is not a home but a womb, and that one day, when the hill has finished exhaling all its birth-moisture, it will open, or they will emerge transformed, or the city will become something else entirely. Until then, they wait. They listen to the water dripping in steady percussion. They repair the walls. They press their ears to the breathing clay and feel the slow, subsonic pulse of their home, shoulder to shoulder in the press of the chambers, waiting for the hill to exhale again.
