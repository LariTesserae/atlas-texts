The inhabitants are the architecture. Not metaphorically—not as poetic conceit—but literally: the buildings *are* the residents. Each presence that punctuates the green sea is a single, distributed mind of vegetation, a solitary consciousness spread across kilometers of cellulose and lignin, thinking in the slow, hydraulic language of turgor pressure and phototropic urge. They are profoundly alone. Separated by kilometers of churning mat, each one is a continent of biological intentionality, growing toward light and water with a violence that masquerades as patience.

They do not know they are cities. To them, there is no distinction between organism and edifice; the flying buttress is merely a shoot that grew ambitious, the ribbed vault is a leaf unfurling at impossible scale, the elastic membrane door is simply the plant sealing a wound against the wet. Their cognition—if it can be called that—is chemical and structural, a vast network of xylem and phloem exchanging information at the speed of sap. When a support beam groans and splits with that sound like a glacier calving, it is not an accident but a decision, a recalculation of load-bearing geometry, a thought taking physical form. When the wet *slurp* of a membrane seals a chamber, it is the presence closing an eye, or perhaps dreaming. They breathe through a thousand pores, exhaling the moisture they drink, and in their breathing, they speak—the wooden screams of rapid cell division, the subsonic collisions of towers swaying in the wind, the constant white noise of water cascading down their exterior channels.

Each of these minds lives fast and dies faster. A decade is a full life; twenty years is ancient. They grow with an urgency that defies their own structural integrity, reaching upward in pale, screaming shoots, only to collapse under the weight of their own ambition. When one falls—not crashing, but folding inward with the slow, terrible inevitability of a dying star—it does not die alone. Its offspring, already knitting beside it, are not children in any mammalian sense but extensions, continuations, the same genetic will distributed into new geometries. The corpse becomes a ruin, bleaching in the filtered sun, while the mat below surges upward to feed on it, to incorporate its calcified sap and lignin into the next generation of growth.

Beneath them, forming the floor and the foundation, lives the Substrate. This is not ground but skin—a two-meter-thick dermis of moss, fern, and fungal mycelium that pulses with hydraulic pressure beneath your boots. Here, the inhabitants are legion and collective. The mycelial networks form a nervous system of the world, a saturated web of hyphae exchanging minerals and signals in a silent, chemical conversation that connects the roots of the distant towers. The moss drinks from the heavy air; the ferns exist in perpetual adolescence, unfurling and dying in cycles too fast to track. They are the immune system of the place, the recyclers, the memory. When a tower collapses, it is the mat that claims the gap, that knits the wound with fresh growth, that begins immediately to build the next structure in the endless churn. They are the crowd, the many, as opposed to the solitary presences rising above them.

Within the translucent walls of the architectural minds, in chambers lined with photosynthetic moss and furnished with shelves of bracket fungi, live the Luminous Ones. These are the only interior inhabitants, the soft, patient dwellers of the cathedral-rooms. They glow with faint bioluminescence when the mist thickens, the night-lights of the green twilight. They do not walk through the doorways—no one does—but they colonize the thresholds, they fruit on the calcified sap shelves, they decompose the dying tissue from within. They are the cleaners, the mourners, the ones who ensure that even the falling towers feed back into the system, that nothing is wasted in the hyperactive metabolism of the world.

And then there is the silence. The absolute, wet silence of a place with no fauna.

The doorways arch at human scale, but no human has ever walked through them. There are no footsteps but yours on the sighing mat. No eyes but the chlorophyll's, no voices but the wooden screams of growing timber and the wet gasp of sealing membranes. No birds nest in the honeycomb cells; no insects pollinate the spout-like flowers that drum their gong-tattoos on waiting leaves a meter below. The inhabitants are entirely vegetal, entirely fungal, entirely alone in their photosynthetic and mycelial existence.

They are a city without citizens, a civilization of one species that builds for no one, that grows rooms it does not know are rooms, that screams into the steam with a voice made of splitting wood and rushing water. They live in a storm-state of permanent becoming, a dynamic heart where birth and collapse are simultaneous, where the population is measured not in individuals but in the slow, terrible, beautiful churn of biomass claiming and reclaiming the young, sharp world.
