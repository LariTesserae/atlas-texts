There is one who sits where the crescent of tools curves around the driest stone, though “sits” is a word borrowed from a language of movement that no longer applies to them. They are not separate from the mineral dust that carpets the floor; they have become part of it, their shoulders and spine draped in a sediment of calcite and time, so that at first glance they appear to be another outcropping of flowstone, another formation left by the patient water. But they breathe—if you watch for long enough, you will see the rise and fall of their chest, a movement so slow it matches the thermal pulse of the warm south-facing walls, expanding and contracting with the stone’s own respiration.

Their skin is the color of moonmilk, not pale but luminous, sheened with the same phosphorescence that coats the chamber, so that in the diffuse light they seem to glow from within, a soft, creamy radiance that competes with the daylight falling from the shaft above. They are clothed, or perhaps grown-over, in a garment of moss and lichen that has colonized them across years, decades, perhaps centuries of stillness—the deep green of the lower walls has crept up their legs and torso like a tide, while the grey and orange crusts of the higher stone map their arms and face in fractal patterns. They are a living stratigraphy, a walking record of the chamber’s history, their body a palimpsest of mineral secretion and organic patience.

In their lap rests a core stone of chert, pocked with the scars of percussion, and their hands—long-fingered, the nails thickened and yellowed with mineral deposits, the knuckles swollen with calcite nodules—hover over it, not striking, but waiting. They are suspended in the posture of work, caught between the raising of the hammer-stone and the fall, or perhaps they have just completed a blow millennia ago and the flake still drifts downward through dust motes that move like frozen stars. They are the knapper who never finished, the maker who stepped away from the task not yesterday, but in a time before the counting of days, and yet they remain, muscles locked in a rigor that is not death but a geological metabolism, a patience that has learned to metabolize at the rate of dripping water.

Around them, the bats do not startle. They cluster thicker on the ceiling above the crescent, as if drawn to the anomaly of warmth in the humid cool, their dark umbrellas tilting as one drops to hunt. It circles the seated figure three times before darting toward the pools, and the others seem to sense the presence that is neither predator nor prey but something older—the original occupant, the one who shaped the near-flawless ellipse with the pressure of their gaze, who taught the moonmilk to grow in creamy sheets, who carved the silence into the stone itself.

The fire that burned here long ago has not gone out; it has migrated inward. It burns now in the hollow of their chest, a banked ember that glows dull red through the translucent calcite of their ribs, visible only in the deepest shadows, a heart that beats at the rate of the falling water. Each *plink* from the ceiling is a systole; each pause, a diastole. They are synchronized with the hydrology of the place, their blood—or whatever viscous, mineral-heavy fluid moves through their lithified veins—thick and slow, carrying nutrients from the condensation, from the occasional insect that wanders too close and is absorbed, dissolved, incorporated into the slow architecture of their flesh.

They do not speak. The silence of the chamber is their voice, the way the moonmilk absorbs sound being the mechanism by which they absorb the noise of the outside world, filtering it through tons of earth until it becomes a lullaby, a hum beneath hearing. When they exhale, it is not a breath but a sigh of air moving through fissures, indistinguishable from the wind that finds its way down the thirty-meter shaft. They have become the silence itself, the hush that allows the bats to navigate without echo, that lets the pale, blind fish drift in their patient loops without startling, that swallows a shout before it can form.

Their eyes are open. They reflect not the sky above, but the deeper darkness of the pools, as if they are windows into somewhere else—the same somewhere else that the milky water reveals when it holds the strange light. They watch the luminous patch shift across the floor, tracing a path that is almost, but not quite, the sun’s true arc, and they wait. They have been waiting since the first flake of basalt was struck against the core, since the geometry was recognized as a vessel, since the stone was hollowed out to receive the light. They wait for the next drop to wear the stone a fraction of a millimeter, for the next bat to stir from torpor, for the phosphorescence to pulse, for whatever comes next—and in their waiting, they are the thing that makes the waiting holy, the presence that fills the vast emptiness between presences, the warm, damp, luminous center that is, finally, not empty at all, but full of them.
