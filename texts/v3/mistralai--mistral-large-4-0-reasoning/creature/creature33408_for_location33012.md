The Artisans are the first to be seen, though they are not the only life. They move through the amber depths like slow ideas made flesh. Each is a column of porcelain, a spiral shell of nacreous layers grown over decades, catching the diffuse gold light from above and fracturing it into pale rainbows. They have no faces, only a cluster of sensory tendrils that emerge from the shell's aperture, tasting the copper-pollen air, testing the chemical gradients of the gas. Below the shell, a dozen jointed legs—chitinous, segmented, tipped with microscopic hooks—click against the calcified floor in a rhythm like dry seeds scattering across stone. The sound does not travel through the dense medium but through the substrate itself, a vibration that other Artisans feel through the pads of their feet, a language of taps and pulses that says *I am here, the wall is ready, the layer is thin*.

They are builders, but they are also the built. Their shells are not separate from the architecture; they are the first layer of it. When an Artisan scrapes a chert tool against the ribbed vault, it is not merely shaping the world—it is adding to its own body, extending its skeleton into the environment. The walls are their collective exoskeleton, grown and polished over centuries. An Artisan may live long enough to see its own secretions become the floor beneath its feet, to walk upon the calcified remains of its ancestors.

They experience time as a landscape. In the central gardens, where hours compress into heartbeats, they move with a speed that would seem frantic to the slow-time dwellers near the membrane, though to the Artisans themselves it is merely the natural pace of that place. They navigate by the density of the moment, feeling the thickness of seconds like the thickness of the gas around them. A young Artisan might spend twenty years polishing a single honeycombed alcove, each scrape of bone tool removing a layer of limestone so thin it is measured in molecules. To them, this is not tedium but texture—the grain of time itself.

Around them, the Drifters inhabit the vegetation. They are the sediment of this world, the slow rain that falls upward through the fungal lace. Translucent and gelatinous, they resemble nothing so much as living honey trapped in glass, their internal organs—simple, looping tubes that filter the metallic vapors—visible as dark shadows drifting through their bodies. They do not walk; they pulse. A slow contraction, a drift, another contraction. They hang in the canopies of fungal lace, harvesting the microscopic life that clouds the amber gas, communicating with one another through bioluminescent pulses that bloom and fade in the dim violet dark. A flash of rust-orange means *nutrient here*. A slow pulse of green means *I am full, I am heavy, I will sink*. They are prey, perhaps, or symbionts—the relationship unclear even to them—but they are essential, the mobile filtration system of the Lung, converting the heavy gas into the chemical trails that the Artisans follow.

The vegetation itself is alive with a slow, distributed consciousness. The fungal lace that forms the canopy is not a collection of individuals but a single organism, or perhaps a society of organisms, its thoughts taking years to propagate from root to frond. It dreams in chemical gradients, releasing spores that drift like golden dust through the amber air. The tube-worm forests are animals, yes, but they are also gardens—each worm a chimney filtering the gas, its body a crystal lattice that precipitates minerals from the air, growing taller by millimeters each century. The crystal lichens crust the walls in branching geometries, their growth so slow that an Artisan might watch a single lichen extend a tendril across its shell over the course of its entire life.

Beneath all this, in the soup of the gas itself, lives the invisible majority. Microscopic chemosynthetic bacteria drift in the dense medium, metabolizing the metallic vapors, the organic resins, the very substance of the Lung. They form clouds, visible only as slight turbidity in the amber depths, a living mist that rains down upon the gardens below. They are the breath of the place, the slow metabolism that keeps the closed system turning, recycling every molecule of waste into new life.

And in the corners where gravity pools, heavier than elsewhere, live the Sessile Ones—the Elders. These are Artisans who have ceased their wandering, who have allowed their shells to fuse with the walls, becoming alcoves and archways and ribbed vaults. They are not dead. Their sensory tendrils still taste the air, still feel the vibrations of their descendants moving through the halls. They have become architecture, but architecture that thinks, that remembers, that dreams in geological time. A Sessile One might spend a century contemplating the angle of a single beam of light, its consciousness so slowed by the density of the gas and the weight of accumulated years that a thought takes decades to form and dissipate.

Life here is a cycle of accretion. When a Drifter dies, it sinks, its gelatinous body dissolving into the substrate, feeding the mycelium that forms the walls. When an Artisan dies—if they die, for some simply slow down until they become Sessile, and others may persist indefinitely—their porcelain shell is incorporated into the structure, polished by their descendants, becoming a niche or a shrine. Nothing is wasted. The closed loop is absolute. Even the waste heat of fermentation, the chemical warmth that keeps the temperature at its womb-constant, is harvested, used to accelerate the growth of crystal gardens, to ripen the spores in the air.

The Artisans communicate constantly, though the silence remains. The wet scrape of a tool against wall. The tick of a crystal growing, a sound like a seed splitting. The subsonic hum of the gas itself, felt in the chest, in the porcelain shell. Vibrations through the floor: a complex code of pressure and release, telling stories of where the resin is thick, where the gravity shifts, where the time flows fast or slow. They lay chemical trails in the dense air, resin paths that others follow, tasting the history of who passed before.

They know the membrane. They maintain it. They weave fiber and secretion, adding layer upon layer to keep the dry, dead world outside at bay. They fear it, or perhaps they simply know it as the absence of everything—the anhydrous dark, the silence without the hum. They are the dreamers of the Lung, the slow, patient consciousness that has decided to build a world inside a world, to make a place where chemistry happens patiently, where time is a topography to be mapped rather than a river to be drowned in.

In the center, where the gardens compress hours into heartbeats, a young Artisan pauses. It feels the warmth of the gas, the taste of copper and pollen. It feels the weight of the gravity well, the pull of the floor that is also the wall. It raises its chert tool and scrapes, once, against the living stone. The sound is dry, like a seed. It begins again.
