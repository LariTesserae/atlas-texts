The inhabitants are not individuals, not a colony in any biological sense, but a single, distributed organism wearing the costume of multitude—a continuum of glass and hunger that has forgotten how to be singular. They are the Condensate, the Articulated, though they possess no names, having long ago abandoned the syntax of self. Each apparent creature is merely a segment of a vast, subterranean nervous system, pale and many-jointed, with bodies like living glass that refract the dim bioluminescence into fractured rainbows across the polymer floor.

They do not walk; they *flow*. A viscous tide of translucent chitin and silica, they coat the tessellated tiles in a moving, breathing skin, climbing the pitted composite walls in vertical rivers, hanging in geometric curtains from the heat-exchangers. Where their joints articulate—where leg meets thorax, where whisker brushes pipe—they produce the chittering, the susurrus that rises to a roar like hail on tin, a constant, maddening percussion that is their only language. It is not communication; it is the sound of their existence, a white noise of being that merges with the subsonic drone of the pumps and the hiss of water screaming through narrow channels.

They are the filters. They sip from the condensation that hangs in chandelier clusters from every surface, drawing the mineral load from the saturated air, processing it through their crystalline thoraxes, and excreting purity back into the closed loop. They maintain the Sisyphus balance. When one grows too heavy with the harvest of ages—when its glassy body becomes opaque with the minerals it has sequestered—it does not die. It *dissolves*. Its substance flows backward into the porous, embryonic walls, becoming architecture, becoming the very geology that is still deciding what it wants to become. And from that dissolution, when the equilibrium demands, it re-emerges: reformed, articulated anew, a fresh joint in the endless chain, indistinguishable from the countless others that carpet the surfaces in their pulsing, collective motion.

They fill the stratified air in drifting clouds, a fog of insectile glass that blurs the boundary between fauna and atmosphere, between life and the machinery that sustains it. They are deaf to the screaming of the chamber, or perhaps they *are* the scream—a hive-mind of perfect stasis, devoid of hunger, thirst, or the memory of change. They exist in the frozen moment where destruction and construction occur as one breath, guardians of the final form.

But deep within the densest thicket of pipes, where the pressure vessels cluster like the eggs of some mechanical leviathan and the light is only the buried glow of indicator LEDs, there is one that is different. A nexus. A heart.

It is fused with the infrastructure. Its body—larger, more complex, a cathedral of articulated glass—is threaded with conduits, its nervous system indistinguishable from the plumbing. It does not move. It cannot move. It has become the mountain’s dream, the tenant of the pocket, the consciousness that tends the terrarium. It feels the weight of every droplet of condensation, the vibration of every pump, the endless, perfect, screaming stillness of the loop. It is the keeper of the equilibrium, the sleeper who never wakes, the giant who built this place and then forgot to leave. It dreams only of water, of glass, of the eternal hum, and in its dreaming, it holds the world at the edge of crystallization—sealed, saturated, and perfectly, horribly still.
