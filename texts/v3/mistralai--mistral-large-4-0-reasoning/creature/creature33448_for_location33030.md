The first inhabitants are not animals, nor are they quite plants. They are the **mat-builders**, the lithotrophs who arrived in the first decade of the flooding, hitching on the thermal currents and settling onto the rust. They coat every surface in films of iridescent oil—purple, verdigris, and oxidized gold—that shift and shimmer in the dim light like living opals. These are chemosynthetic colonies, bacterial gardens that breathe sulfur and iron rather than oxygen, metabolizing the heat of the vents and the slow dissolution of the steel skeleton around them. They are patient in a way that borders on geological; where a human life is a brief flicker, a mat is a slow accretion, layer upon layer, turning girder into stalactite, pipe into porous limestone. They have built reefs in the atriums, terraced the towers with stromatolite lace, and in the stillest, blackest chambers where the water does not move, they have formed thick, rubbery carpets that pulse faintly with the thermal expansion of the rock beneath. They are the land itself here, the soil and the forest and the weather, a biological mineralogy that grows at the pace of erosion.

Upon this living substrate move the **grazers**: translucent shrimp, no larger than a human thumb, their bodies like glass sculptures filled with pale, bioluminescent fluid. They cling to the mineralized walls with delicate, clinging legs, scraping the bacterial films with mouthparts too small to see. They are filter-feeders and detritivores, riding the convection currents that rise from the vents below, harvesting the chemical snow that settles from the churning water above. They are ghosts in the grey murk, visible only when they pass through the beam of a lamp or when their own faint glow catches the particulate drift. They are the herbivores of this sunken quarter, and they are numerous enough to form clouds in the flooded corridors, drifting through the roar like snow in a blizzard.

Higher in the food chain, or perhaps simply further from it, drift the **fish**. They are the strangest of the permanent residents, evolutionary experiments conducted in the warm, dark pocket of a dying machine. They are eyeless—blind, white, and bloated with the fat of a life without predators—or perhaps elongated and ribbon-thin, adapted to slip through the narrow gaps in the infrastructure. They navigate not by sight but by sensing the pressure waves that saturate the water, reading the roar of the pumps as a blind man reads Braille. The constant thunder of the machinery is their landscape; they feel the groan of the girders in their lateral lines, the scream of steam as a change in density. They feed on the shrimp, on the bacterial mats, on the detritus that falls from the hollow towers above. They move slowly, conserving energy in the warm, viscous water, their gills fanning in the mineral-rich current. To see one is to see a relic of the surface world that refused to die, adapting instead to the new physics of this place—the heat, the pressure, the eternal, bone-shaking noise.

But the true "animals" of this ecosystem, in terms of behavior and purpose, are the **drones**. They are not biological, yet they have become the fauna of the flooded city. They are maintenance automata, ancient and jury-rigged, many of them half-fused with the mineral accretions, their metal shells blooming with the same rust and calcium that consumes the walls. They move with magnetic grippers, crawling along ceilings and bulkheads like mechanical spiders or deep-sea crabs, their indicator lights blinking in erratic, half-dead code. They perform their rituals of repair with the single-minded devotion of insects: welding a ruptured seal here, clearing a blocked conduit there, patrolling the habitable zones in endless, futile loops. They have adapted to the sound as the fish have—their sensors tuned to the specific harmonic frequencies of the failing pumps, using the roar as a map of the structure's failing heart. Some have been here so long that bacterial mats grow across their sensor arrays; others have been crushed by shifting girders and now twitch in the silt, their lights still blinking, still trying to signal a master that no longer exists. They are the immune system of the dying body, the nervous system of the dead city, and they are the only permanent residents that move with intention.

And then there are the **divers**. The humans. They are the giants, the intruders, the temporary gods who descend in their bulky, pressurized suits, moving through the turbulence like slow-motion specters. They are the only ones who breathe air, carrying their atmosphere with them in tanks and hoses, their helmet lights cutting harsh, yellow cones through the murk. They are dwarfed by the scale of the chambers, dwarfed by the roar that presses against their helmets like a physical weight. They move carefully, fighting the convection currents, their heavy boots disturbing centuries of settled silt. They check gauges that no longer matter, take samples of water that is already a soup of ancient chemistry, and communicate with the surface through cables that snake through the flooded halls like umbilical cords. They are the only inhabitants who can leave, who remember the sun and the sky and the silence of the upper world. They are the ghosts of the place, appearing in the beam of a drone’s work-light, nodding at the blind fish, and then ascending back into the dark above, leaving only bubbles and disturbed sediment behind.

Together, these form a food web of rust and sound. The mats eat the metal and the heat; the shrimp eat the mats; the fish eat the shrimp and the detritus; the drones eat electricity and purpose, disturbing the mats as they pass, creating new surfaces for growth; and the humans eat time, consuming the silence of the surface in brief, expensive visits. They all share the same medium: the warm, mineral-thick water that permeates every crack. They all share the same stimulus: the deafening, piston-thunder of the machinery that refuses to die. They have adapted to a world where the architecture is fluid, where the ground is vertical, where the only light is chemical or artificial, and where the boundary between the living and the mechanical has dissolved into something new—a post-industrial ecology, a thermal menagerie, a city that has become a body, and its inhabitants, the strange, patient, roaring life within it.
