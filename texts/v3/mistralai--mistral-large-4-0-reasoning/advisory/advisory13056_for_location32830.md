You do not prepare for this place by packing lighter. You prepare by accepting that you will be **wet**, and that the wetness will not be water alone but a condition of being—a saturation that reaches your marrow before your boots touch the peat.

**First, you prepare your sternum.**

The roar arrives before the sight of it, a subsonic frequency that settles into your chest cavity like a second heartbeat. You will need vibration-dampening insoles—gel or cork composite, something that absorbs the 30-RPM thrum of the impellers before it rattles your teeth loose. Your pack should have a harness system that distributes weight across your hips, not your shoulders; the vibration will fatigue your trapezius muscles within the hour if you let it. Bring anti-nausea medication, not for seasickness, but for the low-frequency disorientation that comes from standing in a field of pressure waves. Some travelers wrap their torsos in elastic compression bandages, not for injury, but to give the vibration something to push against—a feeling of solidity when the world is humming.

**You dress for digestion.**

The air is not merely humid; it is *participatory*. It presses condensation onto your skin the moment you step inside the green rampart. You wear a suit of waxed canvas or modern breathable hydrophobic weave, but you know it is a temporary truce. Your clothing will become a second skin, heavy with moisture, smelling of chlorophyll and iron oxide within minutes. Pack three complete changes of base layers—merino wool if you have it, something that insulates when damp. Do not bring cotton; it will rot against your body in this heat.

Your boots must be tall, reaching mid-calf at least, with soles that grip on rust-slicked grating but release from sucking peat. The ground is a floating mattress, a raft of sphagnum that rises and falls with the pumps' cycles. You will feel it sigh beneath your weight—methane and hydrogen sulfide escaping from the anaerobic depths—and you must train yourself not to startle when the earth burps. Bring gaiters that seal at the knee; the peat water is acidic, tannin-dark, and teeming with microorganisms that would colonize your skin given half a chance.

**You protect your hands with paranoia.**

The iron here is not painted; it is lacquered in moisture and blooming with rust the color of dried blood. Every railing, every pipe, every conveyor housing is a tetanus reservoir. You wear cut-resistant gloves with nitrile coating—not for the machinery (which moves with terrible patience, slow enough to see coming), but for the *edges*. Rusted iron flakes. It cuts. Bring a trauma kit specifically stocked with antiseptic that can handle wet wounds, and ensure your tetanus vaccination is current. In a multiverse, you cannot assume medical facilities share your immunology.

**You prepare your lungs for a world without animals.**

The absence of fauna is not peaceful; it is a warning sign written in silence. The air is thick with spores—fungal, algal, bryophyte—suspended in a medium of steam and exhaust. You wear a respirator with a P100 filter and a moisture-trap valve, but you know it will clog. Bring three spares. The humidity is so complete that breathing feels like inhaling warm soup; your lungs will work harder to extract oxygen from the saturated air. Pace yourself. The machinery roars at a frequency that drowns out the subtle sounds of respiratory distress, so check your pulse manually, often.

Carry a portable gas detector. Not for industrial toxins—those are visible in the steam—but for the pockets of marsh gas that collect in low areas, and for oxygen displacement in the narrow alleys between the pump-house and the control tower. The vegetation is total; photosynthesis runs rampant in this wet heat, but decay runs rampant too, and in the still corners, the air can turn thin.

**You calibrate your eyes for green twilight.**

The light here is diffuse, filtered through ten meters of living wall and steam, casting no shadows—only a uniform, green-grey luminescence that makes depth perception treacherous. Bring a headlamp with a red-light mode (to preserve night vision when the canopy thickens) and a UV flashlight. The algae on the control panels, the moss in the mortar—they fluoresce under UV, revealing slip hazards invisible in the normal spectrum. Your pupils will dilate in this dimness, making you sensitive to the sudden glare of wet metal reflecting what daylight penetrates the haze.

**You pack for temporal drift.**

Time moves differently here—not magically, but physiologically. The constant roar, the slow rotation of massive flywheels, the millimeter-per-day growth of the vegetation create a sensory environment where minutes stretch and hours compress. Bring an analog watch with a physical second hand. Digital displays blur in this humidity. Set alarms not by time, but by sensation: *when the steam from your own breath stops condensing on the air, you have been inside too long.* The place is small enough to hold in a single glance—eighty meters, a complete world—but it is dense enough to feel infinite. You will lose your sense of scale. Mark your route with chem-lights or biodegradable ribbon tied to the exposed pipes; the green-tinted reflections will make every corridor look identical.

**You prepare your mind for the digestion.**

This is the hardest preparation. You are entering a place where the boundary between the built and the grown has dissolved. The buildings are being eaten—willows threading roots through brick like neural pathways, moss carpeting masonry in velvet drifts. It is not hostile, but it is *hungry*. You will feel small, not because the structures are vast, but because they are patient. The machinery operates with geological breaths, steam hissing in rhythmic cycles that suggest a metabolism.

Bring a grounding object—something from your home universe, something with hard edges and no organic matter. A metal coin, a plastic card, a photograph laminated in polyester. When the green light and the roar make you feel like you are dissolving into the peat, into the rust, into the chlorophyll, hold it. Touch it. Remind your nervous system that you are a traveler, not a nutrient.

**You plan your exit before you enter.**

You will leave covered in spores, your clothes stiff with dried peat and algae, your skin pruned and possibly colonized by opportunistic fungi. Pack a decontamination kit: a spray bottle of diluted bleach or hydrogen peroxide, antifungal powder, and a complete change of clothes sealed in a vacuum bag. The silence when you step back through the green wall—after the roar, after the wet heat—will be deafening. Your ears will ring. You may feel a profound, inexplicable grief, as if you have left a womb. This is normal. The place is a cathedral, and you have been standing in its nave, listening to the organ pipes of industry while the moss sang its slow hymn.

Do not stay until dark. The description says the light is normal, diffuse, but in this basin, with the water table flush and the vegetation ten meters thick, night will come early and absolute. The machinery will still roar, but you will not see the rusted grating beneath your feet, the vines thick as a man’s thigh wrapping the control tower, the duckweed islands drifting in the channels like green icebergs.

And when you leave, listen for the sigh of trapped gas one last time. It will feel like the place exhaling, relieved to be rid of you.
