To step inside the thorax of a continent-sized god that has not yet finished dying is to enter a place that is simultaneously a cathedral, a hospital, and a coffin. It is a settlement built within grief. Before you descend through the dark aperture where the spine once connected—before your boots touch the three-meter raft of peat that swallows the sternum—you must prepare not just your body, but your perception of time, sound, and scale. This is not a destination you visit; it is a condition you endure.

### 1. The Wardrobe of Impermanence
Leave behind everything that shines, clicks, or resists moisture. The air here is a 1.9 on the scale of wetness; it is a saturated, pressurized damp that will find every seam, every pore, every unguarded orifice of your clothing. Synthetic fabrics will fail—they will trap the cold against your skin while the mineral sweat of the ribs soaks through, creating a clammy, hypothermic embrace. Instead, wear layers of **boiled wool** and **waxed canvas**, treated with rendered fat or pine resin. The inhabitants favor materials that can rot gracefully, that will eventually feed the moss; your clothing should be prepared to become substrate. Your boots must have **felt soles**, not rubber—rubber thuds and damages the living carpet, while felt muffles your tread and allows you to feel the subtle tremors of the settling corpse beneath your feet. Bring a **hood of undyed linen** that you can pull tight over your head; the rib-moths are drawn to the warmth of mammalian breath, and while they are harmless—they will brush against your face like cold rice paper—their touch is unsettling, a reminder that in this ecology, the boundary between visitor and nutrient is permeable.

Crucially: **carry no metal.** Not a belt buckle, not a knife, not a compass. The mineral condensation that weeps from the porous bone is aggressively corrosive to ferrous metals, and more importantly, the settlement operates on a pre-metal, tactile technology. Metal is foreign here, anachronistic, potentially offensive. Craft or trade for tools of **bone and antler**—a walking staff of cured rib-bone will serve you better than any trekking pole, its grooves fitting your palm like a memory. If you must carry a blade, let it be knapped flint or chert, wrapped in leather.

### 2. The Physiology of the Damp Cold
The temperature paradox will disorient you: the air is glacial, hovering just above freezing, while the water that pools on the floor is strangely warm, a lingering fever from the creature’s final metabolism. This creates a microclimate of rising vapor that fills the chamber with a slow, drifting fog. You will be cold and wet simultaneously, yet you will be tempted to touch the warm pools. **Do not.** That water is not merely warm; it is *nutrient-rich*, thick with dissolved minerals and the cellular debris of a dying titan. It is a bath of liquefied geology. If you must cross, use the walkways. If you fall, the warm water will shock your system even as the cold air chills it, a dangerous thermal whiplash that can incapacitate you in the peat.

Your breath will mist constantly. Practice breathing through your nose, shallow and slow, to conserve heat and to avoid inhaling the bioluminescent spore-dust that drifts from the shelf fungi. Carry a **moss-filter mask**—a pad of living sphagnum held over your mouth and nose with fiber cord. The spores are not toxic, but they are seductive; inhaling too deeply will make you drowsy, attuned to the slow tempo of the place, and you may find yourself unable to leave.

The bone-worms are everywhere. They are translucent, segmented, eyeless, and silent, pulsing across the peat in synchronized waves. They eat calcium through their skin, processing the creature’s skeleton. You might fear they will turn to your bones, but they are specialized; they consume the *dead* calcium of the titan, not the living. Still, wear **thick leather gloves** when handling the walkway ropes or the bone-tools. Their touch is cold and slightly adhesive, like wet parchment, and while they will not harm you, the sensation of them crawling over your skin—should you kneel or fall—will linger in your nervous system long after you leave.

### 3. Light in the Velvety Dark
There is no sun here. The chamber is roofed by mummified tissue, desiccated hide that blocks the sky. The only illumination is bioluminescent: the faint blue-green pulse of the shelf fungi, the glow of cultivated moss-pots, the occasional flash of a spore-sac releasing its dust. To carry a flame—a torch, a lantern with oil—is to introduce a violence into a place of smokeless, patient fires. The inhabitants burn dried puffballs and compressed peat in the central plaza, fires that smolder without smoke, but even these are sacred and controlled.

Instead, cultivate a **moss-lantern**. Before you enter, gather a clay pot or a hollowed gourd, fill it with living sphagnum moss from a similar damp environment, and seed it with a fragment of bioluminescent fungus. Tend it like a ember. It will glow with a soft, greenish pulse, enough to see the creaking walkways and the black mirrors of the pools, but not enough to blind you to the deeper dark. Learn to see in the velvety gloom. Your eyes will adjust, but slowly. Do not rush. The ribs curve overhead like ivory arches; in the bioluminescent half-light, they seem to breathe, expanding and contracting with the settling of the corpse. Let them.

### 4. Navigating the Ghost of a Mind
The settlement is sparse, a scattering of reed huts and peat platforms connected by walkways lashed with fiber rope. But these walkways are not arbitrary. They follow the grooves of the creature’s ancient nervous system, the fossilized paths of nerves etched into the bone. The "streets" of this city trace the ghost of a mind.

You must learn to read these paths. **Do not step off the walkways.** The floor is not earth; it is a three-meter-thick raft of sphagnum and mycelium, a spongy peat that has swallowed the keel and the sternum. It is unstable, shifting, visibly thickening as the moss grows into the cracks of the bone, prying them wider. The creature is still dying, still collapsing inward, and the city adjusts to this slow tectonic violence. The walkways creak as they settle—a low, groaning sigh. When you hear that sound, **freeze.** The architecture is negotiating with the decay of its host. Wait for the creak to stop, for the wood to find its new angle, before you move. To rush is to risk a platform collapsing into a warm, black pool, or to step into a sinkhole of peat that will swallow you up to your waist in seconds.

Carry no map. The place is dynamic; yesterday’s path may be a chasm today. Instead, learn to listen. The dominant sound is the *plink* of water dripping into the pools, irregular and sparse. But beneath that is the creak of wood, the soft whisper of membrane against moss—the bone-worms—and the silence between. The inhabitants move with a slow, deliberate grace, attuned to the millimeter-by-millimeter settling of the corpse. Match their tempo. If you hurry, you will be obvious, a violent intrusion into a place of quiet negotiation.

### 5. The Etiquette of Silence and Symbiosis
The settlement is primitive, pre-metal, manual. Tools are carved from bone—needles, awls, scrapers—hand-crafted and passed down. The inhabitants (whoever they are—perhaps descendants of the creature’s parasites, perhaps something else entirely) live in a closed loop. The vegetation feeds the fauna, the fauna die and feed the vegetation, the water cycles through all of them in a constant, condensing rain.

You are a pollutant in this system. Prepare to be **minimal.** Bring no waste that cannot be composted into the peat. If you must excrete, do so in a designated area away from the pools; your waste will be incorporated into the raft, and in this place, nothing is truly discarded. Bring **offerings**—not gold or gems, but **dried puffballs** or **compressed peat cakes**, fuel for their fires, or **seeds from the outside world** that will never germinate here, left as a gesture of respect for the boundary between the living world and this pocket of dying divinity.

Speak, if you must, in a whisper. The mist muffles sound, but the silence is heavy, expectant. The inhabitants may communicate through vibration—tapping the bone, feeling the creak of the walkways—or through the placement of moss-lanterns. Observe. When you enter the central plaza, where the ribs converge toward the fossilized spine, remove your hood. Bow, if you know how, to the aperture where the spine once connected. You are standing in the heart of a god that is still, geologically speaking, bleeding out. Treat the grief in the architecture with reverence.

### 6. Psychological Preparation: The Intimacy of Scale
This place is intimate despite its height. From any point on the mossy floor, you can see the far wall, the opposite curve of bone, the dark aperture. It is a contained microcosm, a settlement-scale chamber. But the scale is deceptive. The ribs curve overhead like the vault of a cathedral built from ivory and grief. The darkness above is absolute, velvety, pressing. You will feel simultaneously enclosed and exposed, as if inside a ribcage that is also a womb, a coffin, a lung that has stopped breathing but has not yet realized it.

The fauna will overwhelm you. The rib-moths are a blizzard, a silent snowfall of wings, eyeless and pale, drifting through the mist like pale ghosts. They are overwhelming in their numbers, yet they produce no buzz, no flutter. When they land, they do not stay; they dissolve, melting into the moss to become part of the substrate. Watch this happen. Understand that here, death is not an end but a phase change. You may feel the urge to swat at them, to assert your separateness, but resist. Let them land on you. Let them brush against your skin. Feel the cold damp of their wings. It is a reminder that you are temporary, that you too will feed the moss.

The water cycle is hypnotic. Evaporation from the warm pools rises to the cold bone, precipitates back down in a closed, indoor weather. You will be caught in this rain—a constant, patient dripping that is neither warm nor cold, but the temperature of the creature’s final fever. Let it fall on you. Do not try to stay dry. Dryness is a foreign concept here.

### 7. The Temporal Dislocation
Time moves differently inside the thorax. The creature is still dying. The moss grows *visibly*, thickening, budding new fungal shelves overnight. The peat shifts underfoot. The settlement adjusts. A walkway that was secure in the morning may have sagged by evening, re-lashed by invisible hands during the night.

You must learn to move in **geological time.** Do not plan your visit in hours; plan it in days, or weeks. Bring **calcium supplements**—not for your bones, but to offer to the bone-worms if they seem particularly interested in you, a gesture of shared hunger. Bring **patience.** The silence is not empty; it is full of the slow, grinding tectonics of the corpse settling, the soft, wet sound of the moss growing, the whisper of membrane against membrane.

When you stand in the plaza, surrounded by the sparse glow of moss-lanterns, looking up at the curving ribs that vanish into the dark, listening to the dripping water and the creak of wood, watching the silent, overwhelming drift of pale wings through the mist—you will feel small. Not diminished, but *specific.* A single, warm, brief interruption in the quiet, teeming, overwhelming stillness of a god’s final exhalation.

Prepare to be changed. The peat will cling to your boots. The bioluminescence will linger in your afterimages. The grief of the ivory arches will settle in your chest like a second heartbeat. When you finally climb out through the dark aperture, back into the light of your own universe, you will carry the damp cold in your lungs, and the silence in your bones, for a long time afterward.
