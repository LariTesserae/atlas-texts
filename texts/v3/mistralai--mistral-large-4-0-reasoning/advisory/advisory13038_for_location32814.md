You do not prepare for this place with a checklist. You prepare for it with a kind of reverence—the way you might prepare to enter a cathedral that is also a machine, or a wound that is still being stitched shut. This is a place that was born yesterday and is already being eaten by something older than the rock. To step inside is to stand in the moment between human intention and geological patience.

Here is how an ordinary body survives it.

**The Wardrobe of Wet**
Do not bring cotton. It will betray you within the hour, becoming a cold, heavy second skin that never dries. Instead, dress in layers that anticipate saturation: a thin merino or synthetic base that clings without chafing when damp, topped by a breathable waterproof shell that you will seal at every cuff, collar, and ankle with gaffer tape. Your boots must be neoprene-lined and rated for standing water, with soles aggressive enough to grip black, slick concrete that shines like obsidian. Wear them with wool socks, but carry a second pair in a dry bag—you will need them when the first pair becomes a swamp.

Your hands require particular cruelty. The water here is not merely cold; it is *treacherous*. In chambers where the air is frigid, you will find pools of supercooled liquid—water held below freezing, trembling, waiting. If your bare skin touches it, it will flash into ice instantly, bonding your flesh to the surface with a scream of frostbite. You must wear thick neoprene gloves, the kind divers use, taped at the wrists. Test every puddle with a metal probe before you step near it. Assume every reflective surface is a trap.

Cover your head with a hood that seals around your face, and bring a balaclava. The air is so saturated that every breath pulls moisture into your lungs, and the cold will ache in your teeth. Your eyelashes will freeze. Your beard, if you have one, will become a lattice of ice. Accept this. Do not wipe your face with your gloves; you will only smear condensation and mineral dust into your eyes.

**The Silence Within the Roar**
This place is loud in a way that erases sound. The pumps the size of cottages, the reciprocating hammers, the hiss of compressed gas, the groan of ventilation shafts—these combine into a constant, cathedral-deep drone that does not fade with distance but only changes texture. It will vibrate in your sternum until you feel your organs humming in sympathy.

You must protect your ears before you protect anything else. Industrial-grade silicone earplugs, inserted deeply, covered by electronic earmuffs that clamp tight. But even with protection, you will lose the ability to locate sounds. The echo here is wrong—it arrives from angles that do not match the source. Do not rely on hearing to navigate. Do not shout to your companion; the roar will swallow your voice whole. Instead, carry a strobe light or a chem-light wand to signal across the concourses. Establish a visual language: three flashes means *stop*, two means *danger*, one steady pulse means *follow me*.

**The Architecture of Light**
The facility provides light the way a dying star provides warmth—sparse, jaundiced, insufficient. Sodium vapor lamps cast amber pools that die within meters. LEDs flicker blue-green through the mist. Most of the place exists in a regional darkness where distance becomes a pressure against your eyes.

Bring more light than you think you need. A headlamp with a minimum of 1000 lumens, wide-beam, with a red-light setting to preserve night vision in the absolute dark. Carry two backup lights. Attach chem-lights to your pack—green for your right side, red for your left—so that in the fog, you can tell which way is up and which way is forward.

But light here is unreliable. The pools of water reflect ceilings that do not exist, angles that do not match the corridors, geometries that suggest the facility is larger below than above, or folded in ways that hurt to look at. **Do not trust reflections.** Wear polarized lenses if you have them, but even then, avoid staring into the black mirrors on the floor. They are showing you the space as the water sees it, and the water is old and patient and remembers when this cavern was empty.

**The Gravity of Scale**
This is not a building. It is a valley. Corridors stretch for hundreds of meters into darkness. Machines stand alone in cavernous halls where your voice will not carry. The human mind cannot hold this scale; it will fracture into panic or trance.

You must tether yourself to the small. Lay a physical trail behind you: a rope of phosphorescent cord, unreeled from a pack, anchored to heavy machinery every fifty meters. Place glow markers at every junction—color-coded, numbered, so that you can find your way back through the fog when the dim lights fail and the darkness presses against your eyes.

Travel in pairs, never alone. The buddy system is not for company; it is for sanity. When the corridors begin to angle wrong—when the fresh concrete seems to lean, when the frost grows in hexagonal patterns that align with the pumps as if the cold is thinking—you need another pair of eyes to confirm that the wall is, in fact, perpendicular to the floor. If you begin to feel the weight of the vastness, the sense that you are walking toward a horizon that recedes forever, stop. Sit on the wet concrete. Count your breaths. The scale will not shrink, but you can shrink yourself to fit inside it.

**The Chemistry of Touch**
Everything here is wet. The condensation beads on metal and runs in rivulets. The floor is a shallow sheet of runoff, slick and black. The air itself feels like liquid.

Waterproof everything. Your phone, your map, your food—sealed in dry bags within dry bags. Electronics will fail; the moisture finds every seam. Carry paper maps laminated in plastic, and a pencil that writes on wet surfaces.

The water is also *active*. It is being pumped, pressurized, chilled. Steam rises from tanks in chambers where the air is frigid—touch that steam and you will scald. Supercooled water waits in others. And the water is etching the concrete, spreading hairline cracks like neural networks, staining the fresh surfaces with mineral veins. **Do not lean against the walls.** The concrete is young, still curing, and the water is teaching it to crack. Watch your footing. The cracks are trip hazards, and worse, they weep moisture that freezes instantly on contact.

**The Breath Between Drops**
The air is saturated—100% humidity, hovering just above freezing. It will feel like breathing through a wet cloth. It pulls moisture from your lungs with every inhale, and you will cough, constantly, a wet, rattling cough that echoes in the vast chambers.

Bring a respirator with particulate filters, but know that it will fog immediately. Practice breathing slowly, through your nose, to minimize the moisture exchange. Do not hyperventilate; the cold and the damp will steal your heat faster than you can generate it.

Hydrate, but not from the taps. The water here is industrial, cycling through filtration towers and processing tanks. It may be pure, or it may be carrying the minerals of the deep aquifers, or the chemicals of the machinery. Bring sealed water, and ration it. You will lose moisture through your breath in the dry cold, even as the world around you drips.

**The Rules of the Reclaiming**
This place is being digested. You are a morsel in a throat. Move with this knowledge.

Do not follow the frost. It grows in geometries too regular, too deliberate, aligning with the machinery as if organizing itself. It may lead you in spirals, or into chambers where the air is thick with fog that obscures the lights entirely. If you find yourself in fog so dense you cannot see your hand, stop. Tie off your rope. Wait for it to shift, or backtrack along your line.

Do not interact with the drones. The crab-like maintenance machines with blinking amber lights are not hostile, but they are *hungry*—for data, for maintenance, for the warmth of your body heat registering on their sensors. Let them pass. Do not follow them into the dark.

Do not eat the fungus. The pale, luminous blooms in the condensation seams are beautiful and slow-growing and probably toxic in ways that will take days to kill you.

Do not stay too long. The cold will get into your bones. The damp will rot your gear. The scale will wear down your sense of self. Establish a base camp near an entrance shaft, stocked with dry clothes, a thermal blanket, and a heat source—a small camp stove, used with extreme caution in the humid, potentially explosive atmosphere of the deep.

**The Exit Strategy**
The way out is up. Far up. Through shafts that groan with forced air, through levels where the temperature rises and the water drips from overhead pipes.

Mark your ascent as obsessively as your descent. The corridors may shift in your absence—the water moves things, the cold warps the fresh concrete, the weirdness grows. When you return, the angles may be wrong, the reflections may show you a ceiling that has collapsed or a floor that has opened.

But if you have prepared—if you are wrapped in wet-proof layers, if your ears are protected from the cathedral roar, if your path is lit with chem-lights and your rope is tied to your waist, if you have not touched the supercooled pools and have not trusted the mirrors—you will climb back into the world above.

And you will carry the taste of it with you: metallic, wet, the chill of deep stone that has never known sun. You will feel it in your lungs for days, the memory of a place that was born yesterday and is already returning to the water.
