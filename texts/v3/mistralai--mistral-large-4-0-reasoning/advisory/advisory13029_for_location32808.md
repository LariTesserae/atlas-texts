You are going into a held breath. Prepare accordingly.

**First, the Body: Sealing Against the Dry**

This is not a desert. A desert promises eventual rain, the mercy of dew, the possibility of water hidden in root and cistern. Here, humidity is *absolute zero*. The air is a mineral-laden sauna that will crack your throat and make your eyes water with a paradoxical, burning weeping—your body will try to cry, but there is no moisture to spare. You must arrive already sealed.

Wear a full-body environmental suit with a closed-circuit rebreather, or at minimum, a moisture-barrier underlayer beneath heat-resistant outer fabric. The air tastes of sulfur and ancient dust; it will coat your skin in a crust of mineral salt within hours. Without a seal, you will desiccate from the inside out—your sweat will evaporate instantly upon contact with the air, offering brief cooling before the dehydration begins in earnest. Bring more water than you think you need; treat this as a vacuum, not a desert. There are no oases. There is no condensation on the walls. The only "moisture" is the fleshy, dripping profusion of the lithovores themselves, and you must not drink from them—their chemistry is chemosynthetic, alien, and their weak acids are actively etching the stone around them.

Your eyes require goggles with internal moisture reservoirs or a sealed visor. The dryness will abrade your corneas. The airborne particulate—fine mineral dust and luminous spores—will turn the air into a glowing haze during dust surges, reducing visibility to arm’s length. Your respirator must filter both the mineral load and the biological spores; the fungi release them explosively, a sound like distant fireworks or wet applause, and you do not want to inhale a cloud of bruised-green bioluminescent matter into lungs already stressed by the heat.

**Second, the Ears: Armor Against the Screaming Stone**

The dominant feature of this place is acoustic. The mineral shell expands and contracts in thermal cycles you cannot see but will feel in your bones—a violence that sounds like distant artillery, like the popping of giant knuckles, like lithic heartbeat amplified through a hollow chamber. This is the loudness of a place with no fauna; the silence of absent animals makes these geological noises unbearable in their clarity.

You need active noise-canceling protection rated for continuous low-frequency percussion, or at minimum, heavy industrial ear protection combined with bone-conduction communication headsets. Sound travels strangely here: you could shout across the full diameter and hear your voice return from the opposite wall, but the echo is swallowed immediately by the heat—a disorienting acoustic behavior that means your voice carries strangely far yet dies strangely fast. The subsonic thrum of the mycelial networks will vibrate in your sternum, a chemical-exchange hum that bypasses your ears entirely and settles in your marrow. Prepare for sensory contradiction: your ears will be assaulted by the stone’s percussion while your chest vibrates with silent biological frequencies.

Do not remove your hearing protection to listen. The wind through the geode’s vents sounds like a pipe organ played by a subterranean giant; it is beautiful and it will damage you.

**Third, the Feet: Treading on a Living Grind**

The ground is not ground. It is a crust of white and ochre tessellated plates that shift and grind against one another as the thermal mass of the rock pulses. The stone is *screaming*—literally cracking, popping, adjusting its geometry with a violence that will be felt through your soles. You need flexible, ankle-supporting boots with vibration-dampening soles, but also with tread aggressive enough to grip plates that are sliding against each other in slow, grinding percussion.

Watch your footing during thermal contractions. When the shell cools—a cycle you cannot predict but will feel as a sudden drop in the oppressive heat—the plates will contract and shift. A step placed on a tessellated plate may find that plate grinding sideways into its neighbor, dropping you into a new crack. The architecture is sparse; vast empty spaces separate each structure, creating a density of almost nothing per volume. You will be walking for long stretches across this unstable, singing floor with no handholds, no landmarks, only the curved wall rising around you like the inside of a throat.

**Fourth, the Eyes: Navigating the Purple Half-Dark**

Light here is twilight-permanent. The only illumination is the dim, bruised-green bioluminescence of the fungal caps, pulsing in rhythms that might be breathing. It is insufficient. Shadows stretch long and the heat itself seems to shimmer in visible, wavering sheets—thermal distortion that will make distance judgment impossible.

Bring a portable light source, but understand that it will compete with the bioluminescence, creating a strobing effect as the fungi pulse. Use infrared or ultraviolet settings if available; the mineral dust catches light strangely, and the spores glow in the dark, turning the air itself into a luminous particulate soup during dust storms.

More critically, prepare for **vertical ambiguity**. The gravity feels slightly wrong—perhaps it is the heat playing tricks on your inner ear, or the impossible curvature of the walls. The settlement is terraced into the curved interior surface like barnacles; you will be walking on walls that feel like floors, or floors that curve upward into ceilings. Your proprioception will fail. Use a gyroscopic harness or at least a tether line clipped to your belt and anchored to a fixed point—a carved pillar, a fungal stalk—when navigating the terraced architecture. The plaza could hold a thousand souls, yet you will find yourself alone with the geometry of absence; the curvature makes it easy to walk in circles, to believe you are moving forward when you are actually traversing a curve that returns you to your starting point.

**Fifth, the Mind: Preparing for the Paradox**

This is a garden and a ruin simultaneously, and neither state is aware of the other. You will see lushness without moisture—dense, three-dimensional mats of sulfur-yellow and rust-crimson draping over stone, towering fungal stalks rising from plazas, vegetation that moves constantly in thermal currents. But you will hear no birds, see no tracks, feel no heartbeat but your own. The silence of absent fauna makes the geological percussion unbearable. You are in a world that grows lush without rain, in the heat, in the dark, and it will feel *wrong* in a way that has nothing to do with physical danger.

Prepare for temporal disorientation. The vegetation creeps across the ancient stonework at a pace visible over days. The buildings are weathered, settled, their original carvings smoothed into abstraction by millennia of thermal weathering. You are walking through deep time. The architecture was built to be reclaimed—its porous surfaces provide purchase for the lithovores, its hollow interiors trap heat—so the boundary between the grown and the built has dissolved. You will feel the patience of geological time pressing against your human urgency.

The heat presses against your skin like a physical weight. It is not merely temperature; it is a density, a pressure, as if the air itself has mass and is leaning on you. Your inner ear will struggle with the low elevation—this bowl of stone sits pressed down into the earth’s crust—and the curvature of the walls. You may feel as if you are falling upward, or that the ceiling is the floor.

**Sixth, the Chemistry: Respecting the Lithovores**

Do not touch the vegetation. These are not plants that drink; they are lithovores, chemosynthetic colonies drawing sustenance from the mineral substrate and infrared heat. They secrete weak acids that etch the walls, accelerating erosion that has already spent millennia softening the architecture. That acid will burn your skin. The fleshy mats drape over every surface like a tide of organic matter that has learned to swim in dust; they will brush against you as you move through the narrow spaces between structures.

The spores are luminous, but they are also particulate matter. During dust storms—cycles of heat surges and mineral particulates, not rain—the air becomes a glowing haze. Your suit must be sealed against this. The fungi pop as they explosively release spores; the sound is like wet applause, but the cloud they release will coat your visor, your filters, your skin.

There is no metal to glint in the dark. The built environment is hand-carved stone and hardened fungal resin. Bring non-sparking tools only; though there is no oxygen-rich atmosphere for combustion, the mineral dust and sulfur compounds create an unpredictable chemical environment.

**Seventh, the Logistics: Self-Containment and Exit**

This is a sealed pocket, a micro-cosmos within a geode the size of a mountain. There are no resources. No water to drink, no animal to hunt or befriend, no metal to mine, no wood to burn. You are entirely self-contained. Your supplies must include:

- **Water**: More than you think. The absolute-zero humidity means you will lose moisture through respiration and evaporative cooling even while sealed, and any breach in your suit will be catastrophic.
- **Cooling**: Phase-change undergarments. The heat is physical; you will need active cooling to prevent heat stroke, as the thermal mass of the rock radiates constantly from all directions—the walls, the floor, the air itself.
- **Navigation**: A tether line, gyroscopic stabilizers, and a mapping drone if possible. The curvature of the walls makes visual navigation treacherous. The vast empty spaces between structures mean you can become lost in a plaza the size of a city block, disoriented by the purple half-darkness and the shifting shadows of the fungal towers.
- **Communication**: Visual signals. Sound is unreliable—swallowed by the heat, masked by the stone’s percussion. Pre-arrange hand signals or light pulses. If you are traveling with others, stay within sight; the echo will deceive you about distance.

Mark your entry point obsessively. The wind sweeps the streets, carrying fine mineral dust and luminous spores. A dust storm can reduce visibility to zero, turning the glowing haze opaque. The ground shifts beneath you. The walls curve away in all directions. You need a physical tether back to the entrance—a rope, a glow-stick trail, a beacon—because the architecture offers no natural markers, only the geometry of absence.

**Eighth, the Soul: What to Expect When You Arrive**

You will descend into a bowl of stone sitting in the dark. You will feel the heat immediately—a dry, mineral-laden weight that makes your throat crack and your eyes water. You will hear the stone scream—a constant, loud percussion of geology, the thermal expansion and contraction of the shell, distant artillery and giant knuckles popping, the world’s lithic heartbeat.

You will see the lushness—the coral-reef density of the lithovores, the bruised-green pulse of the fungal caps—and you will feel the wrongness of growth without water. You will walk on tessellated plates that grind and shift beneath your feet, feeling the violence of the thermal cycles in your bones. You will navigate by bioluminescence and your own light, on walls that curve into ceilings, in a gravity that feels slightly wrong.

You will be alone, though the plaza could hold a thousand souls. There are no tracks in the dust, no scuttling, no heartbeat to disturb the heavy air. Only the vegetation moves, swaying in thermal currents, creaking in a dry, rattling chorus. The mats of growth creep across the stonework, consuming the architecture that was built to be consumed.

When you shout, your voice will travel across the full diameter and return from the opposite wall, then die, swallowed by the heat. You will hear the wind howl through the vents like a pipe organ. You will feel the subsonic thrum of the mycelial networks in your sternum. You will taste sulfur and ancient dust.

It is a pocket of stone the size of a held breath. Treat it with the reverence of a tomb, the caution of a furnace, and the wonder of a garden that should not exist. Bring your water. Seal your skin. Protect your ears. Anchor your soul. And remember: the boundary between the grown and the built has dissolved here. You are walking through a place that is simultaneously a city and a compost heap, a ruin and a garden, and it is patient. It has been waiting for millennia, and it will wait for you to leave, or to be reclaimed.

Do not stay too long. The heat is a physical weight. The stone is screaming. And the silence of the absent animals will, eventually, become unbearable.
