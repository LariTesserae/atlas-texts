You do not prepare for this place as you would prepare for a desert, or a vacuum, or even a world where the sky is wrong. You prepare as you would for entering a dream that has not yet decided it is a dream, a construction site where the blueprints are still wet ink, a conversation where the grammar is being invented as you speak. This is a pocket where reality is negotiable, where the laws of physics are written in pencil rather than ink, and where the ground beneath your feet is not ground at all but a factory in gestation, a machine learning to be a mountain.

Here is how an ordinary human—one made of finalized physics, of solid bone and decided-upon atoms—must prepare to visit the Embryonic Basin.

### 1. The Suit of Finalized Laws
Your first preparation is not environmental; it is ontological. You must wear a **Reality-Anchored Exoskeleton**, not merely a pressure suit. The air here is thin (2.1) in a way that presses down like a hand, and scalding (2.7), thick with ozone and the scent of hot metal. Standard atmosphere is insufficient. You need a sealed environment rated for thermal extremes and chemical toxicity—sulfur, iron vapor, superheated silicates.

But the suit must do more. It must be a **bubble of completed physics**. The local reality is embryonic (0.2), still under construction, still deciding what it wants to be. Your suit must carry its own gravity (1.0 G, non-negotiable), its own thermodynamics (heat flows from hot to cold, entropy increases, your blood remains liquid), its own causality. It should hum with a low-frequency **consensus field**, a reminder to the local pencil-written laws that *you* are ink, that you are finished, that you will not dissolve into the mist of the basalt pistons.

The faceplate should be polarized against the high-altitude glare (2.4), but also tinted to filter the specific wavelengths that make shadows pool in corners that do not exist. You must not trust your eyes here. The light falls wrong; shadows stretch toward their sources. Your suit should project a **reference grid** onto your visor—straight lines, right angles, true shadows—so your brain has a solid reality to hold onto when the local geometry begins its slow, febrile dreaming.

### 2. The Temporal Harness
Time flows unevenly in this basin. In the center, a minute passes; at the rim, an hour elapses. This is not metaphor. It is a gradient of temporal viscosity. You must wear a **Chronometer with External Beacon**, a device that does not merely tell time but *broadcasts* your personal timeline into the local fabric. A tether—physical, quantum-entangled, or metaphysical—must connect you to a fixed temporal anchor outside the pocket, perhaps a chronometer left at the ridge, or a beacon orbiting the depression.

Set your suit’s internal clock to **slow-time mode**. Move as if every step might take you across an hour of external time, because it might. Carry **time-dilated rations**: food that accounts for the possibility that you will age years while your companion ages minutes. More importantly, establish **synchronization protocols** with any companions. If you split up—and you should not, but if you must—you must agree that you will check your beacons every thirty seconds of *your* time. If your beacon shows your companion has jumped ahead by six hours while you experienced only minutes, you must not follow them into the center. You must wait, or retreat, because the pocket is trying to tear you apart temporally.

Wear **temporal stabilizers**—small devices that pin your personal timeline to a narrow band, preventing you from experiencing the “memory pressure” of the stone, that sensation of walking through pistons and feeling your molecules pass through solid matter as if it were mist. Without these, you risk becoming temporally smeared, existing in multiple minutes at once.

### 3. The Feet and the Floor
The ground is not ground. It is a factory. Basalt pistons rise and fall in cycles measured in decades; granite bearings crystallized yesterday; conveyor belts of slow-moving slurry—thick as cold honey, black as crude, composed of molten silicates—carry nutrients across the basin floor.

Your boots must be **phase-locked**. The stone here is permeable to human matter in a way that feels like remembering a dream. You can walk through the pistons, but the pressure is wrong—a dream-pressure that tries to convince your atoms they are already liquid, already gas, already part of the machinery. Your boots must emit a **solidity ping**, a subsonic pulse that tells the local geology: *I am here. I am solid. Do not incorporate me.*

You must also navigate the thermal extremes. The ground shifts between scalding heat and cooling expanses of slag and obsidian glass. Your boots need **adaptive thermal soles**—retractable crampons for the glass, heat-dissipating plates for the slag, and insulated pads for the slurry belts. You must never stand still. The lithovores’ roots drill into the machinery below, tapping the industrial heat like parasites on a generator. If you stand still too long, warm and stationary, you may find copper filigree and quartz crystal beginning to investigate you, growing toward your heat signature at the speed of continental drift. You will not feel them arrive. You will only notice when your boots are fused to the floor, when your suit’s thermal readings spike as something begins to drink your warmth.

### 4. The Silence and the Marrow
The silence here is absolute, except for the subsonic grinding of stone against stone. This vibration is not heard; it is felt in the marrow. It is the sound of the earth building itself, of basalt pistons grinding against granite bearings, a rhythm measured in decades but felt in your teeth.

You need **osseous dampeners**—not earplugs, but vibration absorbers that line your suit’s interior, protecting your bones from the resonant frequency of the basin’s construction. Without them, the grinding will loosen your teeth, rattle your organs, and eventually convince your skeleton that it is still deciding what it wants to be—perhaps limestone, perhaps steel, perhaps something that has not been named yet.

When the thermal winds pass through the lithovore forests, the copper filigree and quartz crystal fronds chime like distant glass bells (1.2)—mostly quiet, resonant only when you press your ear to the cooling stone. **Do not press your ear to the stone.** The resonance will not be pleasant. It will be the sound of your own blood crystallizing, of your thoughts slowing to geological time. If you must listen, use a contact microphone, and keep the gain low.

### 5. The Lithovores and the Non-Aqueous
There are no animals here (0.4). Only the lithovores (1.8)—lush in the way of deep-sea vents, dense and tangled, teeming with impossible structure. They are non-aqueous chemists, metabolizing sulfur, iron, and thermal radiation. They resemble ferns of copper filigree, orchids of solidified mercury and vitrified sulfur.

They do not drink (water 0.5). They do not need liquid. They photosynthesize using heat, converting thermal radiation into solid structure in a process that violates thermodynamics. They are patient (0.9), growing at the speed of accelerated continental drift. A single frond might take a century to unfurl.

**Do not touch them.** Their biochemistry is alien. They are metallic, crystalline, sharp. A brush against a quartz frond could slice through your suit’s reality-anchoring fabric. A touch of solidified mercury could poison your suit’s seals. More importantly, they are tapping the machinery—the same machinery that is building the basin. If you stand among them, you become part of the nutrient cycle. Your suit’s heat signature will attract their roots. They will not attack you; they will simply incorporate you, slowly, over decades, drilling into your thermal core, treating your body heat as a generator to be parasitized.

Wear **thermal camouflage**—a suit coating that mimics the temperature of the surrounding slag, so you do not appear as a warm anomaly in their thermal landscape. Move through their pockets with the reverence of a diver moving through a coral reef made of razors and living batteries.

### 6. The Negotiation of Reality
The weirdness (2.8) is the true architecture. Reality is negotiable here, and the negotiation is ongoing.

You must practice **reality-check rituals**. Every ten minutes, touch your suit’s interior wall, feel the solidity of the fabric, count your fingers, recite a poem from your home universe, something with a fixed meter and rhyme. The place will try to convince you that you do not need your suit, that the air is breathable, that the heat is merely a sensation, that time is an illusion. It is not lying; it is *negotiating*. The laws here are raw (0.2), coming into being, written in pencil.

Carry a **consensus object**—a stone from your home world, fully formed, weathered, old. Hold it when you feel yourself becoming mist-like, becoming uncertain. Let it remind you what finished geology feels like. Let it be your anchor when the basalt pistons try to convince you that you can walk through them permanently, that you can become part of the machinery, that it would be peaceful to dissolve into the slurry and be carried across the basin floor, black and thick as cold honey.

### 7. The Exit Strategy
This pocket is only a few kilometers across (0.4), hidden between ridges of raw, unweathered stone. It is small, but it is deep in its strangeness. You must know your exit before you enter. Mark the rim—the place where an hour passes for every minute in the center—as your temporal safety line. Do not cross into the center unless you have a **temporal rescue beacon**, a device that can pull you back across the time gradient, or a companion who will wait at the rim and reel you out by a rope that exists in both time-streams.

Remember: the lithovores grow at the speed of continental drift, but the basin itself is building structure. The conveyors carry nutrients. The pistons grind. In a century, this place will be different. In a thousand years, it may be a mountain, or a city, or a god. Do not stay long enough to find out. Visit as a tourist visits a construction site—briefly, respectfully, and with an absolute determination to leave before the foundation sets around your feet.

### 8. What It Will Feel Like
When you stand in the basin, you will feel the thin air pressing down (2.1), the scalding heat (2.7) against your suit’s thermal layer, the ozone in your recycled breath. You will see the high-altitude glare (2.4), white and sharp, but the shadows will crawl toward the light like frightened things.

You will feel the dream-pressure when you step near a basalt piston, the sensation of your molecules negotiating with stone, passing through as if it were mist.

And you will feel, above all, the profound patience of formation. This place is not finished. It is a machine being born, a garden being planted in the cooling skin of a world. You are a fixed point in a fluid reality, a completed sentence in a paragraph still being written. Hold your shape. Anchor your time. Do not let the pencil rewrite you into something else.

Walk carefully. Breathe your own air. Remember your name. And when you have seen enough—when you have witnessed the industrial ecology, the metallic ferns drinking heat, the factory-floor of the world—turn back toward the rim, where time moves faster, where the stone is finished, and where you can be solid again.
