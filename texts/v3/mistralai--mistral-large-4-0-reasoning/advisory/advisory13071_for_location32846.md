You do not pack for this place. You *acclimate* to it.

Begin three weeks before arrival—if you can find a high-altitude bog or a coastal fog forest, go there and stand in the mist until your skin stops trying to dry. Your body must learn that wetness is not a condition to be escaped but the baseline state. The air here is a contradiction: thinned by elevation, yet saturated to the point of precipitation. Your lungs will struggle to extract oxygen from water-heavy atmosphere while your skin drowns in it. Practice breathing slowly, diaphragmatically, in a steam room or hot shower until you can maintain calm respiration when your throat feels lined with wet wool.

**The Garments**
Forget waterproofing as you understand it. Nothing here dries; the concept is as foreign as fire would be to a deep-sea fish. Instead, dress to function while perpetually damp. Merino wool is your first skin—it insulates when soaked and resists the rot that will claim cotton within hours. Over this, wear oilskin or pine-tar-treated canvas, but understand that your outer layer will never be dry; it will be a second, breathing skin of saturated fiber.

Acquire, if you can trade for it before arrival, a cloak of the local manufacture: woven water-reed or felted moss, dyed with lichen into the dull greens and umbers of the bark. It will not keep you dry—nothing will—but it will keep you *warm*, and it will signal that you understand the place’s rules. Bring three such cloaks; you will need to rotate them as they mildew.

Your feet require special treason. The ground is peat—a springy, black, methane-breathing mat that swallows boots. Wear wide-soled felt boots, hobnailed if possible, or construct snowshoe-like frames to distribute your weight across the sinking earth. The trees themselves are slick with algae; every handhold is treacherous. Wrap your hands in waxed leather strips, not gloves, so you can feel the grain of the wood while maintaining grip on surfaces that weep sap and condensation.

**The Kit of Impermanence**
Metal is precious and cursed here. Bring iron or steel only as trade goods—salt is worth more than gold, and a simple knife blade will buy you weeks of hospitality—but keep your personal tools in stainless steel, titanium, or obsidian. Everything rusts within a season; a carbon-steel blade will be orange with corrosion before you finish your first meal.

Your fire-starting kit must be paranoid: ferrocerium rods in waterproof containers, stormproof matches in nested tins, and a vial of hand sanitizer (alcohol gel burns even when damp). But expect to fail. The natives keep their fires perpetually smoky and struggling; you will too. Learn to cook with hot stones wrapped in wet moss, or eat cold.

For navigation, forget maps. There is no horizon—only near and nearer, a vertical labyrinth of living architecture. Bring 200 meters of bright cordage, dyed with something synthetic that will not fade in the perpetual gloom. Tie it to your belt and let it trail behind you as you move through the catwalks; when the fog rolls in—and it will, swallowing the upper walkways in minutes—you will follow your own bright thread back to shelter. A compass may work, but the magnetic field at this altitude and latitude may be strange; rely instead on the sun’s diffuse, shadowless path and the growth patterns of the moss, which always favors the wettest, northern side.

Carry a breathing mask—not for particulates, but for the midges. They rise in clouds thick as smoke, a 2.1 loudness of wings that will coat your face, enter your nostrils, and drown in your ears. A simple cloth soaked in vinegar or eucalyptus oil, tied over nose and mouth, will help, though it makes breathing in the thin air harder. Accept the trade-off.

**The Body’s Maintenance**
Your skin will macerate. It will turn white and wrinkled within hours, then begin to break down. Before you go, harden your skin with tannins—soak in strong tea or oak bark. Pack antifungal powder (tolnaftate or clotrimazole) in quantities that would seem excessive on Earth; you will dust your skin folds twice daily. Zinc oxide barrier cream for your groin and armpits, where friction and moisture will create sores that fecter in the damp.

Your ears will fill with water. Bring alcohol-based ear drops and use them religiously; swimmer’s ear in this perpetual humidity becomes systemic infection with alarming speed. Your eyes will stream with condensation; bring saline drops and a microfiber cloth that you keep in a sealed bag—though it will never fully dry.

Pack broad-spectrum antibiotics and oral antifungals. A scratch from a thorn or a bite from a territorial bird will not heal; it will become a weeping, green-tinged wound within a day. Wound care is paramount: clean everything with boiled water (cooled in the mist), and carry honey or sugar to pack into injuries—both have antimicrobial properties that work even in this saturated world.

**The Mind’s Preparation**
This place assaults the sensorium. The sound is not silence broken by noise, but a continuous 2.1 roar: water rushing down bark, wood creaking in the wind, the screaming chorus of frogs and birds, the drone of insect wings. You will not sleep without earplugs, yet you must remain aware enough to hear the creak of a failing lashing or the warning cry of a resident.

The swaying will make you seasick. The bridges are vines lashed between living towers; they oscillate in the wind that funnels through the high valleys. The entire settlement rocks like a ship. Take motion-sickness medication, but learn to move with the sway—plant your feet wide, bend your knees, let the platform move beneath you rather than fighting it. The trees grow visibly; a walkway you crossed yesterday may sag lower today, or a new platform may have been lashed in overnight. The architecture is alive, constantly being eaten by rot and repaired by hand. You must develop a tolerance for impermanence, for the sense that the ground beneath you—whether peat or wooden platform—is temporary, negotiable, breathing.

Prepare for claustrophobia and its inverse simultaneously. The scale is intimate—you can see the whole settlement from any point, a cathedral interior of three or four hectares—but there is no horizon, no sky to look up to, only the pale, diffused light filtering through the cloud layer that lives at ground level. It is like being inside a lung, or a wasp nest. Some travelers find it womb-like and comforting; others panic at the lack of escape vectors. Know which you are before you arrive.

**The Approach**
Do not arrive at midday when the mist is thickest, nor during a squall. Wait for a moment when the horizontal rain pauses and the wind drops to a groan. Approach from the bog, from below, where the managed platforms meet the wild root-mass. This is the boundary—a gradient, not a wall—where the fuzzy line between civilization and wilderness shifts with the seasons.

Announce yourself with gifts: salt, a stainless steel blade, or iron nails. The inhabitants live in permanent maintenance; they value labor and durable materials above all else. Offer to help lash a new platform or harvest peat. Do not enter a hollow—a carved chamber in a trunk—without invitation. The trees are their homes, their cathedral, their lungs; treat them with the reverence you would give a church.

Learn quickly to read the water. Every surface channels it: rivulets down bark, collection troughs carved by primitive hands, aqueducts of hollowed logs carrying runoff from roof to roof. The air is heavy enough to drink—literally; you can wring moisture from your cloak and drink it—but carry a gourd to collect the drizzle. The water is mineral-laden, tasting of stone and peat. It is safe, but it will never be cold; it carries the temperature of the place, just above shivering.

**The Rhythm of Survival**
You will learn to move slowly. Everything here is slow—the growth, the rot, the drying (which never happens). But the weather shifts in minutes. A bank of fog will swallow you whole; a squall will turn the paths into waterfalls. You must be able to find shelter in thirty seconds, to know which hollow is nearest, which platform is stable.

Sleep in a hammock, never on the peat. The ground breathes methane and wet rot; it is a floating mat that shifts with the rain. Suspend yourself between two trees, above the sinking earth, and accept that you will wake damp, shivering, to the sound of the settlement groaning in the wind.

When you leave—if you leave—you will carry the wet with you. Your gear will be moldy; your skin will be paper-soft and peeling. You will quarantine everything. You will crave dry air, hot sun, the smell of dust. But for a time, you will miss the roar, the sway, the intimacy of a world where the boundary between human and tree, between architecture and organism, has dissolved into a single, breathing, dripping cathedral at the edge of the clouds.

Prepare to be changed. This is not a place you visit. It is a place that soaks into you, and you will carry its humidity in your bones long after you have climbed down from the clouds.
