You do not prepare for this place with checklists. You prepare by surrendering the assumption that preparation is sufficient.

**First, you must learn to breathe absence.** The seam is the width of a held breath—not an invitation to hold it, but a requirement that you *empty* yourself. Before you press your shoulders through the crack, you must exhale completely, letting your lungs collapse to nothing, because the moment your body crosses the threshold, the air does not leak out; it is *taken*, all at once, leaving you instantly submerged in a medium that is not quite water. You will need either gills that can parse the heavy stratum—the water that has grown old with duration—or a rebreather that circulates the saturated mist from the ceiling pocket, though that air tastes of breath and sleeping things. Train yourself to extract oxygen from pressure rather than gas. The water presses against your eardrums not with the violence of depth, but with the intimacy of a womb; learn to equalize by relaxing your jaw, by accepting the pressure as a handshake.

**Second, you must armor yourself against youth.** The stone here is not weathered; it is *new*, fractured in planes that catch phantom light and return it as absence. If you brush the walls, you will cut yourself on the violence of the place's recent birth. You need a suit that is not merely waterproof but *cut-resistant*—flexible chainmail, perhaps, or a membrane of woven carbon that transmits the pressure of the water against your skin while preventing the sharp geometry of the rock from finding your blood. But do not seal yourself completely. The vegetation—those hair-thin filaments that grow in a thicket so dense the water is merely the spaces between them—will comb through your hair, cling to your skin with a viscosity that is almost affectionate. You must feel this touch, or you will lose your orientation. The suit should be a second skin with pores large enough to feel the cold spots where the phosphorescence runs in reverse, emitting dark instead of light, but small enough to keep the spores out. The water is 2.6 parts packed with drifting seeds and shed membranes; if they enter your lungs or your blood, you will become part of the forest, rooted in the heavy layer, feeding on the minerals of the young stone.

**Third, abandon sight.** The darkness here is subtractive—it does not merely lack photons, it absorbs the concept of illumination. You will not see your hands. A flashlight will not help; it will only make the surrounding water seem bright by contrast, disorienting you. Instead, you must navigate by *cold*. The bioluminescence of shadow will register against your skin as cold fingers drifting past you in the dark. Learn to read the temperature gradients. Learn to map the room by the pressure of the water against your palms, by the way the vegetation brushes your knuckles, by the acoustic tomb of the heavy stratum below—press your skull against the gelatinous floor and listen to the grinding hum of rock compressing, of time becoming more solid, more *real*.

**Fourth, prepare your gravity.** The heavy water at the bottom is authoritative; it defines down. You can stand upon it, but your boots will sink to the ankle, and the surface will ripple outward in concentric rings that take minutes to dissipate. You will need weighted boots with soles like snowshoes—broad, flat, patient—so you do not break through into the older, colder water beneath. But beware: in one corner, the heavy water climbs the wall, defying the lighter water above, creating an overhang of liquid stone you must swim beneath. In another, it recedes, leaving a pocket of breathable air suspended at mid-height, not at the ceiling. The vegetation grows toward this dryness like a magnet. You must be able to swim in directions that do not exist in three dimensions, to trust that the post leaning at an impossible angle—falling upward, sideways, into a geometry that excludes mercy—is telling the truth about the local physics.

**Fifth, steel your mind for the slow.** Time here is layered. The water at the bottom tastes of centuries, metallic and heavy; the water at the top tastes of recent rain, sweet and immediate. You will swim for subjective hours to cross a space the size of a closet. A single bubble, if one forms, will take an hour to rise three inches. The vegetation sways in a current that completes one oscillation every twenty minutes. You must eat before you enter, but bring no food you expect to digest quickly—your metabolism will slow to match the place, or you will starve in the eternal present of a room that has only just been born and is already ancient. Carry a time-anchor: a ticking watch, a song, a memory of your mother's voice. The heavy water compresses, becoming more floor, more ground, more real, while the world outside forgets this pocket ever existed. You may lose track of which layer of water is yours.

**Sixth, respect the artifacts.** The three river-smooth stones arranged in a triangle that makes no geometric sense—do not touch them. They are water-worn but not local, placed by hands that existed before the crack opened or will exist after it closes. The carved post with spirals that rotate when viewed peripherally—do not look at it directly, or you may find yourself leaning with it, falling into the non-direction it indicates. The doorframe standing in the lightest water near the ceiling, leading nowhere, or leading into a recursion of water-filled rooms each darker than the last—do not step through. It is a throat, and this place is already a womb, a tomb, a mouth. If you must investigate, tie a thread to your wrist—a thread of silver, or of your own hair—and let it trail behind you in the current, though the current moves so slowly you may not reach the other side before the thread rots or you forget which end is yours.

**Seventh, prepare for the creature.** You will swim for hours before you see it: flat, pale, eyeless, the size of a dinner plate but thin as a leaf, with too many joints arranged in a symmetry that suggests a geometry excluding mercy. It glides along the surface of the heavy water as if it were a meadow, grazing on the filaments at the speed of a growing icicle. It will not react to you. It has never known anything but this dark, this pressure, this slow time. Do not fear it, but do not follow it. It is a warning of what patience becomes when it has no end—what you might become if you stay too long, flattened by the weight of duration, eyeless because there is nothing to see, jointed for a locomotion that requires no hurry.

**Finally, know how to leave.** The seam will still be there—a vertical crack no wider than a held breath—but the heavy water is compressing, hour by hour, becoming more solid, more authoritative, until it may seal the fissure from within. When you are ready to go, you must rise, but not upward—*toward* the ceiling pocket where the air is indistinguishable from mist, from breath, from the exhalation of something sleeping. You must find the place where the water is lightest, least patient, newest. Then you must hold your breath again—not because you need to, but because the seam requires emptiness, a vacuum for your shoulders to pass through. Exhale. Press through. Let the air run out of you all at once, and step back into the world that has forgotten you, carrying with you the metallic tang of centuries on your lips, and the cold that pools in your palms like a memory you cannot name.
