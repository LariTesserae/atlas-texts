You are not preparing to visit a place. You are preparing to be *gestated* by it—to be briefly held in the palm of a mountain that has not yet decided what it wants to be. This is not tourism. This is trespass into a birth canal. Treat it accordingly.

**I. The Acoustic Self (What You Wear)**

Do not bring a standard wetsuit. The water here is hungry for shape; it will try to fill the negative spaces of your body, to wear you like a glove it intends to keep. You need a suit of *texture*—something covered in microscopic ridges, fractal scales, or a lattice of tiny hooks. When the water thickens around your stillness, it must find purchase on chaos, not smoothness. Think of yourself as a reef, not a swimmer.

More critically, you must wear sound. The roar is structural—the acoustic scaffolding preventing the embryonic chamber from crushing itself into solid stone. Your suit must be lined with piezoelectric filaments, with resonant membranes that sing when the currents move against them. You are not dampening the noise; you are *contributing* to it. If you enter silent, you risk becoming a mute point in the symphony, a node of stillness that the mountain interprets as contraction. Bring a tuning fork the size of your forearm, struck against your sternum every thirty seconds. Let your bones ring. The water conducts geological creation through vibration; your skeleton must participate.

Thermal protection must be chemical, not electrical. Batteries behave strangely where time moves in eddies. Use exothermic packs—calcium chloride, magnesium sulfate—strapped against your core, replaced before they cool. The chill is not merely temperature; it is carried by convection currents that loop with "geological patience." You will be cold in your marrow while your skin is submerged. Accept this. The cold is the mountain remembering it was once magma.

**II. Temporal Anchors (What You Carry)**

Time here is a current, not a river. You need ballast. Carry a mechanical pocket watch—brass gears, no battery, wound tight against its own spring. When you drift near the walls where the water moves slower, viscous, time will accelerate or decelerate around you. The watch is your lie detector. If its second hand stutters or races, you are in the boundary layer. Flee immediately toward the center, the laminar flow, where time passes normally.

Also carry a stone tool of your own making. Not for use—for offering. The ledge holds hammerstones from yesterday and from a century ago, indistinguishable because time pools here. When you arrive, you must leave a mark that is *anachronistic*. Carve a symbol that does not exist in your era. Knapp a flake from a stone that geologically should not be in this mountain. This is your visa. It tells the water: *I am a visitor from a time that is not this time. I will not stay.*

**III. The Threshold (Entering Through the Curtain)**

You do not enter through water. You enter through *suspended rain*—a curtain of falling water that hangs in the air above the pool, roaring but never touching down. This is a membrane between the hollow mountain (density 0.3, vast and empty) and this pocket of wetness.

Swim upward into the curtain. The transition will disorient you—you will pass from dry air to falling water that does not fall, to the roaring suspension below. Equalize your ears *continuously*, not in pops. The mountain flexes; the chamber breathes. You will feel pressure changes against your eardrums like a cool squeeze that never releases. If you feel sudden, crushing pressure, the mountain is contracting. The sound will intensify. This is normal. This is the chamber holding itself open against its own desire to become solid.

**IV. Movement in the Memory-Water**

The water has memory. If you hold your hand still, it thickens into a gelatinous sleeve that preserves the shape of your fingers for minutes after you move. This is not a hazard to avoid; it is a warning. Stillness = aging. In the boundary layer near the walls, minutes become hours. You could grow old while holding your breath.

Therefore: **move constantly, but move *centrally*.** Stay in the laminar flow, the center of the chamber, where the water churns in slow, violent gyres but time runs true. Swim in spirals that match the rising silver bubbles—they ascend in hesitant columns, and they mark the safe currents. Do not drift. Do not pause to admire the mineral fingers of sulfur and iron oxide that grow and retract on the walls (dynamic 2.7). If you stop to watch them, the water will begin to learn your silhouette, to sculpt you into its memory.

The turbulence is weather. The cold seeps rise in mushroom clouds of methane and brine. Swim through them; do not avoid them. They are the breath of the chamber. But beware the floor—a slope of scree and pumice, unstable, shifting underfoot with every surge. Do not stand. You are a swimmer here, not a walker. The rock is glass-sharp tachylite and obsidian. Any contact will slice.

**V. Perception Under Impossible Physics**

Your eyes will lie to you. Light refracts in geometries that do not exist; shadows fall upward, pooling in corners where no darkness should be. Do not trust your visual depth perception. Bring a sonar pinger, but know that sound behaves strangely here—you will hear bubble-collapses three seconds before they happen, a reverse-reverberation that makes the roar feel prophetic. When you hear a sound that has not happened yet, do not turn toward it. It is an echo from the future, not a threat from the present.

The aragonite needles will hang from the ceiling like frozen lightning, crystallizing in pulses that match your heartbeat. Do not panic when you feel your pulse in the stone. The water is precipitating new rock to the rhythm of your blood. Keep your heartbeat steady, if you can. A racing heart might cause the mineral growth to become frantic, unstable.

**VI. The Ledge and the Artifacts**

To reach the ledge where the tools rest, you must swim through the curtain of suspended water again—this time horizontally, through the wall of rain that roars but never touches down. It is possible. The water is less liquid than breathing suspension; it will part for intention.

On the ledge, you will see the stone tools: graywacke hammerstones, flint scrapers, bone hafts lashed with sinew. They are fresh. They are ancient. Do not touch them unless you are prepared to pay the temporal toll. One tool is from yesterday; one from a century ago. If you touch the old one, you may age. If you touch the new one, you may regress. They are markers left by others—architects of stone and bone who swim through solid rock (the hollow dark beyond, density 0.3) and visit this pocket briefly, leaving their marks.

Leave your own tool. Place it beside theirs. Build a cairn of three stones if you must, but do not build higher. You are marking your brief passage, not claiming territory. The water remembers visitors, and it does not like to be remembered for long.

**VII. The Exit Protocol**

You are not supposed to be here. You are supposed to be here only briefly. The "weirdness" (2.2) is a countdown. When you feel the water beginning to *keep* your shape—when the gelatinous sleeves around your limbs feel permanent, when your reflection in the impossible shadows starts to lag behind your movements—you must leave.

Do not stay to see the pale, eyeless fish drift by. Do not watch the moss colonize the crack. Do not let the mineral precipitate (calcite and silica drifting down from walls still being born) settle on your shoulders like snow. The mountain is rising, groaning, adjusting to its own uplift. The sound you feel in your teeth is the acoustic energy of creation. You have heard the mountain's heartbeat. Now you must go before it decides to close its fist.

Swim back to the curtain. Pass through the suspended rain. Equalize as the pressure drops. Surface into the hollow mountain—the cathedral of air and silence where the wind screams a different, drier song.

Leave your tuning fork behind. Let it ring in the water after you are gone, contributing to the structural roar, keeping the pocket open for the next brief visitor—the next trespasser in the birth canal of the earth.

You will carry the memory of this place in your bones, a sympathetic pitch that resonates when you hear thunder. That is the souvenir. The stone tools stay. The cairn stays. You go, and you do not return, because the water has learned your shape, and it is hungry, and it does not like to be remembered for long.
