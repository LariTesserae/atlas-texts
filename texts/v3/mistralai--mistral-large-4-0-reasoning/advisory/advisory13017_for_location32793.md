You are going to a place where time has been arrested, where the air is a soup of minerals and moisture, and where silence is not an absence but a substance. An ordinary human body—warm, decaying, noisy, impatient—is a foreign object here. To survive, you must prepare to become less like yourself and more like the moss: patient, saturated, still.

Here is how you prepare.

**First, understand the cold.** This is not the dry cold of a mountain peak or the bite of wind. This is the cold of a lung filled with glacier water, the cold that clings. It is humid, mineral-heavy, at the exact edge of freezing where water refuses to decide between liquid and ice. Your breath will crystallize instantly, turning to a cloud of microscopic shards that hang in the light like diamond dust. Do not breathe through your mouth. Train yourself, before you arrive, to breathe only through your nose, slowly, filtering the air through a damp cloth. Bring a respirator—not for poison, but for the iron. The air tastes of rust and wet stone; it is saturated with metallic particulates that will coat your tongue and, if inhaled deeply, may line your lungs with a film of mineral. Practice breathing shallowly. The cold is not trying to kill you, but the moisture will find every pocket of warmth in your body and pull it out, drop by frozen drop.

**Prepare your clothing for saturation, not insulation.** You do not want down or wool—these will absorb the perpetual condensation and become waterlogged, heavy, and cold. Instead, wear waxed canvas, oiled leather, rubberized fabrics. You want a shell that sheds the dampness while allowing you to breathe without sweating. The moisture clings to everything; your clothes must be the one surface it cannot claim. Wear layers that you can remove in the "pockets of warmth"—those rare thermal anomalies the description mentions—but expect to be mostly cold. Your extremities will suffer. Bring chemical hand warmers, but also practice the discipline of keeping your fingers still. Movement generates heat, yes, but heat generates sweat, and sweat in this environment is a death sentence.

**Bring light management for the Photic Constant.** The light there is brutal—high-latitude, snow-bright, constant. There are no shadows that move, no dusk, no dawn. Your circadian rhythm will shatter within hours. Bring a sleep mask that seals completely, light-proof, and practice sleeping in bright environments before you go. Wear tinted goggles, not sunglasses—side-light must be blocked, because the illumination comes from above in thick columns, and the glare off the white walls and ice-rimed shell will snow-blind you. The dust motes hang suspended, never settling; they will catch the light and create a permanent glittering haze. Your eyes will strain. Bring lubricating drops. Prepare for a headache that does not go away.

**Train for the Silence.** This is not quiet. Quiet is the absence of sound. This is silence—a vacuum, a pressure, a thing that has weight. The moss blankets every surface, absorbing all vibration. The creatures have evolved to be mute. You will hear your own heartbeat, the rush of blood in your ears, the creak of your joints, the microscopic pop of your breath crystallizing. It will be deafening. You must learn to move without sound before you arrive. Practice walking on moss—it is thick as velvet and slippery, and every step can be a crunch if you are not careful. Wear soft-soled boots, felt or moss-lined. Learn to place your weight slowly, letting the vegetation accept you rather than compressing it. If you make noise, you will shatter the equilibrium. The inhabitants—if you can call them that—may not react, but the silence will notice you. It will mark you as a disruption. Bring earplugs, but not blocking ones. You need to filter the overwhelming internal noise while remaining aware of your own footsteps. Find a balance between hearing your heartbeat and not going mad from it.

**Prepare psychologically for the Density.** There is no empty space there. No room to spread your arms without touching wood, moss, or stone. No corner to retreat to. The city is compressed, vertical, packed tight against the living walls. You will brush against architecture, against ferns, against creatures with fur frosted at the tips. You must overcome claustrophobia, but also prepare for the opposite—the vertigo of intimacy. Every breath you take is shared. Every step displaces something. You are not a visitor; you are an insertion, a foreign body in a saturated system. Practice sleeping curled tight. Practice eating in cramped spaces. Learn to find comfort in contact rather than privacy. The architecture has no metal—only timber, fiber, stone. Do not bring metal objects if you can avoid it. Not because they will rust (the cold arrests decay), but because metal rings against stone, and the sound will carry through the moss like a bell. If you must bring metal, wrap it in cloth. Silence your zippers, your buckles, your keys.

**Understand the Stasis.** This is the most dangerous aspect. Time does not pass there. The moss does not grow, but it does not die. The wood does not rot. The water hangs suspended. Your body, however, will try to continue its normal processes. Your cells will divide, your hair will grow, your nails will lengthen, your wounds will heal—but the world around you will not change to accommodate this. You may find yourself aging while the place remains identical. Or worse: the equilibrium may try to enforce itself on you. The description says the erosion is "patient"—the slow wearing down of edges. Do not assume this applies only to stone. Do not assume your skin will be exempt. Trim your hair before you go. Cut your nails short. Bring no wounds, no infections, no biological processes that require change. You are entering a climax community; you are not part of the climax. You are a perturbation.

**Prepare your senses for the Green.** The vegetation is impossibly alive—viridian, saturated, deep. But it does not grow. It simply *is*. This is a hallucination of life, a frozen moment of perfect verdance. Do not touch it. The moss is thick as velvet, but it is also a blanket that muffles, that absorbs, that might—if you press too hard, if you try to pluck a fern—reveal that it is not growing from soil but from something older, something that has been there since before the tree became hollow, before the cyst formed. The liverworts spread in fractal patterns; do not trace them with your fingers. The patterns are not decoration. They are maps, or warnings, or the frozen neural pathways of something vast that dreams beneath the roots. Bring gloves, but not for warmth—for separation. You need a barrier between your skin and the green, because the green is hungry for contact, for friction, for the warmth of a human hand. It wants to wear you down, patiently, like the river stone walls.

**Pack for a place where nothing decays.** Your food will not spoil, but it will also not digest properly if you are not careful—the cold slows metabolism, and the silence slows the mind. Bring high-calorie, easily digestible food. Fats, sugars, dense nutrients. Avoid fiber; your gut will be sluggish. Bring water, but know that any water you carry will bead and hang, suspended, refusing to flow down your throat. Drink slowly. The iron taste in the air will make you thirsty, but drinking too fast will chill your core to dangerous levels. Bring a thermos, but fill it with something warm, not hot. The contrast between your internal heat and the external cold is already extreme.

**Prepare for the Fauna.** The moths have wings like lichen. The beetles are armored in iridescent blue. The small mammals have fur frosted at the tips. They are everywhere, yet they make no sound. They stir so slowly that their movement is imperceptible—a geological patience. Do not try to catch them. Do not try to feed them. They are not pets; they are preserved moments, taxidermied by time but still breathing, still blinking with a slowness that will make you question your own haste. If one lands on you, do not flinch. If a bird nests in your hair—and it might, for the warmth—do not move. Let it. You are a tree now, a warm, stupid, temporary tree. Accept the frost on your eyelashes. Accept that you are being incorporated into the equilibrium, however temporarily.

**The Departure Problem.** Most importantly: know that you may not be able to leave. Or rather, you may leave, but you may not be the same. The place is hidden, embedded, a room-sized universe. To exit, you must find the fracture in the ice-rimed shell, the canopy above. But the light is constant, the angle fixed. There are no shadows to navigate by, no sun-path to follow. Bring a compass, but know that magnetic fields may behave strangely in a geological cyst. Bring a rope, but know that you may need to climb through the canopy, through branches that have been polished smooth by centuries of contact. Mark your path not with stones—they are all the same, worn to river-stone smoothness—but with something that will not decay. Plastic? It might be the only thing that outlasts the moss. Or bring a spool of bright thread, something that contrasts with the green and grey. Tie it to the architecture as you go. Let it trail behind you like a lifeline, like a breadcrumb, like a prayer.

**Finally, prepare your mind for the Unchanging.** You will want to change. You will want to grow, to learn, to mark the walls with your knife, to leave a sign that you were here. Resist this. The place has forgotten to pass time; do not remind it. Do not carve your initials into the wood—the adze marks there are ancient, deliberate, part of the infrastructure. Do not pick a flower—there are no flowers, only the pale ferns unfurling in corners, and they do not reproduce, they simply persist. Do not speak. The silence is a language, and your voice will be a violence.

You are going to a place that is a single, enormous form—a tree or a cyst—low in a valley that holds the cold close. You will be inside it, drinking iron air, breathing ice crystals, listening to the absolute quiet of a world that has stopped becoming and begun merely being. Prepare to be still. Prepare to be saturated. Prepare to be, for a pocket of time that may not exist, perfectly preserved.
