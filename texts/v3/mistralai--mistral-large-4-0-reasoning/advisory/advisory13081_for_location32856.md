**A Field Manual for Surviving the Gloam: Preparation for the Thick Dark**

You are not entering a place of shadow. You are entering a place where shadow has weight, where the absence of light is not a void but a *substance*—warm, humid, and alive with particulate press. To visit the Gloam is to drown slowly in air that has forgotten how to be thin. An ordinary human traveler must prepare not merely with gear, but with a fundamental reorientation of what it means to exist in a medium. Below is the accumulated wisdom of those who have walked these plateaus and returned—changed, if not broken—by the weight of the dark.

---

### **I. The Respiratory Apparatus: Learning to Breathe Water-Air**

The atmosphere of the Gloam is a colloidal suspension. Every cubic meter holds not just oxygen, but mineral dust, fungal spores, steam particulates, and the microscopic debris of a chemosynthetic biosphere that has never known sunlight. The air presses against your skin like a held breath because it is, in many ways, liquid—dense, saturated, and resistant to expansion.

**What to bring:**
- **A closed-circuit rebreather with chemical scrubbers.** Do not rely on open filtration. The spore-load is so high that ordinary masks clog within minutes, and the mineral content of the steam can corrode lung tissue. You need a system that recycles your breath, scrubbing CO₂ and replenishing oxygen from a canister, while filtering out the biological and chemical particulates that would otherwise colonize your alveoli. The suit should be positive-pressure; the external medium is thick enough that you must actively push your breath out, and the seal must be absolute. Any gap in the seal will fill with the ambient press.
- **A secondary "snorkel" for emergencies.** The rebreather will fail eventually—batteries die, seals degrade in the perpetual wet. Carry a portable scrubber mask that can filter the ambient air for short periods (fifteen to twenty minutes) should you need to flee a collapsing structure or a spore-storm. But treat this as a desperate measure, not a solution.
- **Nasal and sinus rinses.** The moisture is so absolute that condensation will form in your nasal passages even while wearing the mask. The minerals in the water are harsh. You must be able to clear your passages without breaking the seal of your respirator.

**Physical conditioning:** Practice breathing against resistance. The Gloam’s atmosphere behaves like shallow water; your diaphragm will fatigue. Train with weighted breathing exercises. Learn to take slow, deep, *heavy* breaths. Panic in this environment means hyperventilating against a medium that pushes back.

### **II. The Acoustic Environment: When Sound Becomes a Weapon**

In the Gloam, sound is not vibration traveling through air; it is *pressure* moving through a dense fluid. The hiss of geothermal vents, the groan of shifting machinery, the drumming of rain on fungal caps, and the resonant calls of megafauna do not merely reach your ears—they press against your eardrums, your chest cavity, your bones. Left unprotected, the ambient noise floor of the high plateaus will rupture your eardrums within hours, and the low-frequency roar of the wind in the canyons will induce nausea, disorientation, and eventually, hemorrhaging.

**What to bring:**
- **Active noise-canceling (ANC) earmuffs with integrated communication.** Passive foam plugs will not suffice. You need electronic cancellation tuned to the specific frequency spectrum of the Gloam—the low, subsonic rumble of thermal vents, the high-frequency shriek of spore-storms, the rhythmic *thrum* of automated drills. The ANC must be adjustable; you need to dampen the dangerous frequencies while allowing through the bioluminescent clicks of insect-analogues (which may warn of predators) and the thermal hiss of approaching fauna.
- **Bone-conduction communication headsets.** Shouting is useless; your voice will not carry through the dense medium, and the attempt will damage your throat. You need to speak directly into a throat-mic and hear through vibrations in your skull. This bypasses the air entirely.
- **A "silence protocol."** Learn to move without sound. The fauna hunt by echolocation; the dense air carries their clicks for miles. Every footstep, every brush of fabric against wet stone, is a dinner bell. Practice the "Gloam shuffle"—slow, deliberate, rolling footfalls that minimize acoustic signature. Your boots must have soft, moss-like soles, and you must walk as if the ground itself is listening.

### **III. Visual Management: Navigating the Bioluminescent Haze**

There is no sun. The light parameter is zero. Yet the world glows. The flora emits a soft, pulsing blue and green; the geothermal vents provide a dull, subterranean fire; the industrial ruins flicker with stubborn, oily light. This is not illumination; it is *bioluminescent camouflage*. The glow is patchy, shifting, and it creates shadows that lie. The erosion has carved the stone into impossible geometries—arches that support nothing, rocks balanced on points—and in the flickering half-light, these become optical traps.

**What to bring:**
- **Multi-spectrum vision gear.** Your eyes will adapt to the dark, but they will be betrayed by the sudden flare of bioluminescence. You need goggles with:
  - **Thermal imaging (infrared).** This is non-negotiable. The fauna are warm-blooded; they glow in the thermal spectrum. Predators skim through the mist hunting by heat-signatures. Without IR, you are blind to the primary threat. You must see the heat of your own body to know what the predators see.
  - **UV and low-light amplification.** The bioluminescence is partly in wavelengths invisible to the naked eye. You need to see the full spectrum to understand where the fungal forests end and the industrial ruins begin.
  - **Polarized lenses.** The constant moisture and condensation create glare that is disorienting. Polarization will cut through the haze of condensation and allow you to see the true shape of the karst topography—the sinkholes, the collapsed cavities, the knife-edge decay of the stone.
- **A "darkness anchor."** Carry a small, intense, white-light source that you can use to "reset" your perception. When the impossible geometries begin to swim before your eyes—when the horizon curves wrong and the air pools like water in mid-air—flash the light. It will recalibrate your sense of up and down. Do not use it for navigation; use it for sanity.

**Visual discipline:** Never trust a silhouette. The Gloam is full of vestigial eyes—useless, atrophied things on creatures that navigate by heat and sound. They remind you that sight is a lie here. Move by thermal signature and echolocation (see below), not by what you think you see.

### **IV. Thermal Stealth: You Are a Beacon in the Dark**

The creatures of the Gloam have never known cold or light. They are warm-blooded, translucent-skinned, and they hunt by thermal sensing. To them, you are a lighthouse—a screaming, bright beacon of mammalian heat in a world of absolute dark and ambient warmth. Standing near a geothermal vent is suicide; the predators congregate there, skimming through the mist like elongated shadows, waiting for prey to wander into the heat.

**What to bring:**
- **A thermal masking suit.** Not merely insulation, but active cooling. You need to match the ambient temperature of the Gloam—warm, yes, but not *hot*. Your suit must circulate coolant (perhaps drawn from the mineral-rich water, filtered and chilled) to keep your surface temperature within a degree or two of the surrounding air. Any heat signature above background will attract attention.
- **Heat-dampening blankets/tarps.** When you must stop—to rest, to repair gear, to set up camp—you must hide your thermal signature. Wrap yourself in reflective, heat-absorbing materials. Bury yourself under the fungal mats if possible; the chemosynthetic flora generate their own heat, and you can blend into that thermal noise.
- **Movement discipline.** The large herbivores are slow, but the predators are fast when they strike. Move with the patience of the erosion that shaped this place. Stand still for long periods. Let your body heat equilibrate with the air. Never run; running generates heat and sound.

### **V. The Wet and the Karst: Footing on a Knife-Edge**

The ground is marsh-edge and sinkhole. Water weeps from stone, condenses on every surface, and falls as perpetual rain. The karst topography means the ground is honeycombed with collapsed cavities. You will not sink into mud; you will fall through air into steam.

**What to bring:**
- **Pneumatic or spring-loaded boots.** Standard hiking boots will fill with water instantly and become anchors. You need boots with sealed bladders that can handle the pressure of the dense atmosphere, and soles that can grip wet, weathered stone that is "balanced on the knife-edge of decay and endurance." Consider crampons or grippers for the vertical faces of the mesas.
- **A probe staff.** Before every step, test the ground. The sinkholes are invisible under the fungal canopy and the perpetual mist. A collapsing cavity will drop you into hot, mineral-rich steam and possibly into the automated machinery below. The staff should be collapsible, lightweight, and thermally insulated (it will get hot).
- **Skin protection.** The water is not pure H₂O. It is mineral-rich, possibly acidic or alkaline, and teeming with chemosynthetic microorganisms. Your skin will be wet constantly; the condensation never dries. You need a full-body suit—sealed, but breathable (see Respiratory Apparatus). The suit must be made of materials that do not degrade in perpetual moisture: certain synthetic polymers, treated fabrics. Avoid anything that can rot, rust, or feed the fungal growth. The moss and filamentous plants will try to grow on you; your suit must be smooth, sealed, and perhaps coated with a biocide (though this will wear off).

### **VI. Navigation Through Impossible Geometry**

The horizon curves away into condensation. The erosion has created arches that support nothing, rocks balanced in configurations that defy the thick gravity. Water pools in mid-air; it falls upward in thermal currents near the vents. Visual navigation is impossible. The "impossible geometries" are not just optical illusions; the local physics may be subtly wrong.

**What to bring:**
- **A sonar/echolocation device.** Since the fauna navigate by echolocation, you must too. A handheld or helmet-mounted sonar will map the thermal vents, the sinkholes, the industrial structures, and the large fauna in real-time. It will create a "sound-picture" of the world that bypasses the lying visual spectrum.
- **A gyroscopic compass and inertial guidance.** Magnetic compasses may behave strangely in the Gloam’s atmosphere and geothermal fields. You need a system that knows where it is relative to your entry point, independent of visual landmarks.
- **Physical markers.** Leave a trail. Biodegradable, glowing markers that you can follow back. The spore-storms will erase visual tracks; you need chemical or thermal trails that persist. Perhaps UV-reactive paint, or heated stones, or simply a cord unspooled behind you (though this risks entanglement in the dense vegetation).
- **A "reality anchor" journal.** Write down what you see. The strangeness is subtle at first, then overwhelming. When the rocks seem to balance wrong, when the water seems to flow uphill, write it down. Check your notes. If the notes change when you are not looking, you are experiencing the Gloam’s subtle oneiric influence—the sense that the place itself is dreaming. Do not trust your memory.

### **VII. The Industrial Ghosts: When Machines Wake**

The geothermal refineries are automated, old, and being digested by the lush growth. They persist—engines turning, lights flickering—but they are not safe. The machinery shifts in the wet earth. Pipes snake across the plateau like veins; they may be pressurized with superheated steam. The structures collapse into marshes with a "wet, final sigh." Treat every industrial site as an active hazard.

**What to bring:**
- **A hard hat with a face shield.** Not for falling rocks (though those exist), but for steam bursts, for oil spray from failing machinery, for the sudden collapse of a rusted catwalk.
- **A gas detector.** The refineries belch steam and possibly other industrial byproducts. The "oily glow" of their lights suggests hydrocarbon combustion or leakage. Monitor for carbon monoxide, hydrogen sulfide, and unknown industrial chemicals.
- **Climbing gear and rappelling equipment.** The plateaus are high; the mesas float above seas of cloud. You may need to ascend or descend vertical faces. The rock is weathered smooth—offering poor handholds. Bring pitons, cams, and rope that can handle the humidity (nylon absorbs water and loses strength; consider spectra or dyneema).
- **An "industrial silence" protocol.** When approaching a refinery, power down all electronics. The automated systems may be dormant but responsive. A sudden sound or light might trigger a maintenance routine—a drill grinding into bedrock, a vent releasing pressure, a robotic arm sweeping through the space where you stand. Move slowly. Listen for the rhythmic, patient hunger of the drills. When you hear it shift, freeze.

### **VIII. Biological Hazards: The Swarm and the Translucent**

The fauna are abundant, strange, and warm-blooded. Swarming colonies of insect-analogues click and chatter in the fungal understory. Massive, slow herbivores have translucent skin revealing the heat of their blood. Predators are low, elongated, and skim through the mist hunting by thermal vent signatures.

**What to bring:**
- **Insect repellent and netting.** The swarms are not merely annoying; they may be parasitic, or they may carry spores and microorganisms. A fine mesh over your respirator intake, and repellent that works in high humidity (most degrade quickly in saturated air).
- **A thermal decoy.** A device that emits a heat signature stronger than your own, which you can place away from your position to draw predators. Perhaps a chemical heater, or a container of warm mineral water from a vent.
- **Echolocation dampeners.** Since they hunt by sound, you must be silent. But you also need to hear them. Use directional microphones that can isolate the clicks of the swarms from the ambient roar. Learn the difference between the chatter of herbivores and the hunting clicks of predators.
- **A first-aid kit for thermal burns and chemical exposure.** The geothermal water is scalding and mineral-rich. The fungal spores may cause allergic reactions or infections. Pack broad-spectrum antibiotics, burn gel, and chelating agents for heavy metal exposure (the steam is rich in minerals).

### **IX. Psychological and Existential Preparation**

This is the most important section. The Gloam is not merely a place; it is a *condition*. The darkness is a medium, thick and warm. It presses against your skin. It fills your lungs (even through the mask, you will feel its weight). The sound is physical. The light is wrong. The geometry is impossible.

You will experience sensory deprivation and sensory overload simultaneously. The absolute dark, broken only by bioluminescence, will play tricks on your mind. The "vestigial eyes" of the fauna suggest that sight is a failed evolutionary experiment here; you will begin to doubt your own eyes. The heavy air will make you feel like you are drowning, or like you are being held in a warm, dark womb. The isolation—the scattered, lonely industrial ruins, the vastness of the continent—will press on your sanity.

**Mental preparation:**
- **Meditation and breathing exercises.** Learn to be comfortable with the sensation of pressure, of being submerged, of the dark. Practice in sensory deprivation tanks.
- **A communication schedule.** Establish regular contact with your base or ship. The Gloam is vast; you can walk for days and see nothing but wild. The industrial marks are "scattered, lonely things." You will feel abandoned. The radio link is your lifeline to the reality of human civilization.
- **A "touchstone" object.** A physical object from your home universe—a photograph, a stone, a piece of metal—that you can touch to remind yourself of solid geometry, of thin air, of sunlight. When the rocks begin to balance impossibly, when the water pools in mid-air, hold the object. Feel its weight. Know that gravity still works on it.
- **Accept the dream.** The description says the fauna navigate as if "the Gloam itself is dreaming them into being." You may begin to feel that you, too, are being dreamed. Do not fight it. Move through the dream with the patience of the erosion, the persistence of the machinery. You are a temporary eddy in a vast, dark engine.

### **X. The Departure Protocol**

Leaving the Gloam will be as disorienting as arriving. Your body will have adapted to the thick air; the thin air of your home universe will feel like vacuum. Your eyes will be adapted to the dark; daylight will be blinding. You will have to relearn how to breathe, how to hear, how to see.

**Before you leave:**
- **Acclimatize gradually.** Spend time in a depressurization chamber, slowly reducing the pressure and humidity around you.
- **Purge your gear.** The spores are in your suit, in your hair, in the crevices of your boots. You must sterilize everything before returning to your ship, or you will carry the Gloam’s biology with you—a chemosynthetic ecosystem that might colonize your home.
- **Document the strangeness.** Write down the impossible geometries. The thermal signatures of the predators. The rhythm of the drills. You will forget. The Gloam is a place that erases memory, replacing it with the press of dark and the weight of wet.

---

### **Final Intuition: The Nature of the Visit**

To visit the Gloam is to accept that you are not a visitor, but a *nutrient*. You will be breathed by the air, heard by the fauna, seen by the thermal predators, and eventually—if you stay too long—absorbed by the machinery and the moss, becoming part of the "established, weathered balance." The machines grind on without human overseers. The ecosystem is in constant motion, a vast engine running on heat, water, and time.

Go slowly. Breathe heavy. Mask your heat. Listen to the pressure. Do not trust the light. And remember: in the Gloam, the dark is not the absence of light. It is the presence of something else—something warm, and wet, and patient, and hungry.

**Survive the medium. Respect the dark. Return changed.**
