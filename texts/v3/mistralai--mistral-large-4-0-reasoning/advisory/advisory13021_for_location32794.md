**Preparation Protocol: The Throat at 2.5 Elevation**

You are not climbing to this place. You are being swallowed. Prepare accordingly.

---

### I. The Descent & The Thermal Shock

You will arrive via a vertical or near-vertical passage—a throat, a gullet, a geological esophagus. The transition from the outer world to the chamber is not gradual. One moment you are in the cold, thin air of altitude; the next, you are exhaling into a mouth that steams.

**Acclimatization is impossible.** The chamber exists at 2.5 elevation, yet it sweats at 2.2 warmth. This is not the dry cold of peaks; this is the wet heat of a lung. Your body will struggle to thermoregulate because the humidity (2.2) prevents evaporative cooling. You will sweat, but the sweat will not dry. It will pool in your boots, soak your shirt against your spine, and make every movement feel like swimming through warm broth.

**Prepare your cardiovascular system.** The air is thick—not just with moisture, but with the subsonic pressure of a held breath. Some travelers report a heaviness in the sternum, as if the mountain is inhaling you. If you have anxiety disorders or cardiac sensitivities, bring beta-blockers or anti-nausea medication. The geothermal turbines generate a vibration (1.4) that bypasses your ears and resonates in your bones. It is not loud, but it is *present*, a constant sub-bass note that can induce vertigo or panic after hours of exposure.

**Bring a harness.** The descent may require rappelling through the throat. Once inside, do not assume the floor is stable. You will stand on grating—metal mesh designed for drainage—but the grating is carpeted in moss (vegetation 2.4). It sinks slightly underfoot, alive and yielding. Test every step. The intimacy (scale 0.7) of the chamber means you will feel close to everything, but the density (1.7) means you may brush against walls, pipes, or hanging vines with every movement.

---

### II. The Architecture of Breath & Water

This is a place where water is both medium and machine. The atmosphere is a permanent post-rain mist—condensation hangs in every breath, films your skin, and drips in slow percussion from unseen heights. It is a cloud trapped indoors.

**Waterproofing is not enough; you need vapor barriers.** Standard rain gear will fail. You need:
- **Breathable, hydrophobic outer layers** (e.g., eVent or similar) to prevent the "inside rain" of your own sweat condensing against a non-breathable shell. You will become wet from the inside out otherwise.
- **Sealed electronics.** Every device—phone, light, GPS—must be in an IP68 case with silica gel packs. The steam will find any gap. Conformal coating on exposed circuits is advisable if you bring custom gear.
- **Anti-fog everything.** The temperature differential between your body and the ambient air will cause your goggles to steam instantly. Bring hydrophilic lens coatings or a small battery-powered fan for your face.
- **A respirator, not just a mask.** The air is thick with mineral particulates from the "emerging" geology (erosion 0.3)—obsidian dust, basaltic silica, newborn mineral crust. These are sharp, microscopic, and will embed in your lung tissue. Additionally, geothermal activity may vent trace sulfur compounds or carbon dioxide. Carry a half-face respirator with P100 filters. The "held breath" of the mountain is literal; do not let it become yours.

**Hydration is paradoxical.** In 2.2 heat, you will lose fluids rapidly, but the humidity means you won't feel the cooling effect of sweat evaporation. You will feel cold and clammy while actually dehydrating. Drink electrolyte solutions constantly, not just water. Bring salt tablets. Watch for signs of heat exhaustion that don't look like typical heatstroke—there will be no dry mouth, only a clammy, exhausted heaviness.

---

### III. The Body in the Wet: Dermatological & Medical

The chamber is a petri dish. At 2.2 humidity and 2.2 warmth, fungal and bacterial growth is explosive.

**Pre-treat your skin.** Before entry, apply antifungal powder (clotrimazole or terbinafine) to all skin folds: groin, underarms, between toes, under breasts. Bring a broad-spectrum antifungal cream. The moss (vegetation 2.4) is not just slippery; it is a reservoir of spores. The orchids blooming in the condensation are beautiful but alien; their pollen may trigger reactions in Earth biology.

**Wound care is critical.** The walls are lined with obsidian glass—volcanic glass "sharp enough to cut." It is pre-formation rock, raw and unweathered. A brush against a wall can open a deep, jagged laceration. In this humid environment, such wounds will not scab; they will macerate and fester. Bring:
- Hemostatic gauze (the bleeding may be hard to stop due to the heat vasodilation).
- Waterproof adhesive bandages (hydrocolloid).
- Broad-spectrum antibiotics (topical and oral).
- Iodine or chlorhexidine wipes.

**Foot care.** Your feet will be wet for the duration. Maceration will occur within hours. Bring multiple pairs of moisture-wicking socks (merino wool), and change them every few hours. Treat your feet with antifungal powder before donning socks. Consider toe socks to prevent friction blisters in the wet.

**Eye protection.** The light (2.1) is diffuse, milk-pale, filtered through steam like frosted glass. It is deceptively bright in its diffusion, but it offers no shadows. Depth perception will fail. Wear tinted, polarized lenses to cut the glare, but also bring a headlamp with a **red-light mode**. The pale moths (fauna 1.1) are attracted to white light; red light will preserve your night vision and minimize insect swarms. Use anti-fog wipes constantly.

### IV. Navigation in the Dense Intimacy

The scale is 0.7—a hamlet compressed into a cathedral. You can almost see across it, but density (1.7) intervenes: layer upon layer of presence. Copper pipes the girth of tree trunks. Conduits thick as pythons. Vines thicker than a man's wrist drape from the ceiling, creating a second canopy. Ferns unfurl from pipe flanges.

**You will get lost.** Not because the space is vast, but because it is *packed*. The mist softens edges. The milk light eliminates shadows. The industrial architecture (tech 1.6, built 2.7) repeats—catwalks, pipes, buttresses, collection basins.

**Bring:**
- **A physical tether.** A 50-meter climbing rope. Tie it to a fixed anchor (a bolted flange, a welded basalt column) and use it to navigate. The moss-covered grating can hide gaps or weak points.
- **Chem lights or glow sticks.** Radio signals may be blocked by the stone and the steam. Visual markers are better than electronic ones. Place them at 10-meter intervals to create a path back.
- **A compass.** The machinery is "electrified" (1.6 tech). Magnetic interference is likely. Learn to navigate by the sound of dripping water and the direction of the steam columns.
- **A whistle.** Three blasts for distress. Sound carries strangely in the steam—it will seem to come from everywhere and nowhere.

**Watch the maintenance drones.** They are spider-like, silent, and crawl the catwalks. They are not hostile, but they are *other*. Do not interfere with them. They are part of the digestion process, the slow photosynthesis absorbing the machinery into the stone.

---

### V. Temporal Awareness & The Dynamic Pulse

This place moves at 2.4 dynamic. It is a hurry of stillness. The moss grows visibly, overnight. Mineral deposits accrete, layer by layer, slowly burying the bolts. The turbines turn, and the earth provides, and the water cycles.

**Time behaves strangely.** The internal weather cycles—steam rising in columns that cool and fall as rain inside the roof—create a circadian rhythm that has nothing to do with the sun. You may lose track of day and night. The light is constant, milk-pale.

**The mountain is still being born.** The rock is "pre-formation," embryonic. It is under construction by heat and pressure that haven't yet learned to smooth their work. This means the chamber is geologically *active*. You may feel micro-tremors. The walls may shift. Do not camp against the walls; they are growing, expanding, accreting. Sleep in the center of the chamber, on a catwalk, away from the "emerging" surfaces.

**The machinery is fossilizing.** The paradox is literal. The industrial heart beats slowly, but it is being absorbed. The copper pipes will eventually be mineralized, the turbines encased in new stone. You are visiting a moment in deep time—a snapshot of industry becoming geology, and biology becoming architecture. Respect the pace. Do not rush. The place is quiet, a hush like the inside of a shell.

---

### VI. Psychological Preparation

The weirdness (1.5) settles in the juxtaposition. You are in a cathedral, but it is a grotto. You are in an industrial site, but it is a garden. The air is warm and wet, like breath. The mountain has a mouth. It is holding its breath.

**Prepare for the "held breath" sensation.** The chamber feels less like geology and more like a held breath. This can trigger primal claustrophobia even in open-minded travelers. The intimacy (0.7) of the space combined with the density (1.7) of the machinery and vegetation creates a sensation of being inside a body, inside a womb, inside a machine that is also a living thing.

**Expect no birds.** The silence is absolute except for the subsonic thrum and the water percussion—drip, drip, splash. The absence of birdsong will feel wrong, unnatural. The only fauna (1.1) are pale moths, bewildered, circling lamps, and spiders fat with condensation. You may glimpse a salamander, or you may glimpse a leak that looks like a salamander. The uncertainty is part of the place.

**Meditate on transience.** The moss grows overnight. The mineral deposits bury the bolts. You are witnessing a transformation that will take millennia, but you see it in the span of a droplet falling. This can induce a peculiar vertigo—a sense of being too temporary for a place that is too permanent, or vice versa.

### VII. What to Pack: The Checklist

**Clothing:**
- Merino wool base layers (multiple sets, changed frequently).
- Hydrophobic, breathable hardshell jacket and pants.
- Quick-dry underwear (synthetic or wool, never cotton).
- Sturdy, waterproof boots with aggressive tread (for mossy grating).
- Gaiters (to keep dripping water out of boots).
- Wide-brimmed hat (to keep ceiling drips off face, though the space is enclosed, the "rain" is internal).
- Lightweight, long-sleeved sun shirt (for protection from sharp rock and dripping condensation).

**Protection:**
- Half-face respirator with P100 filters (and spare cartridges, sealed in plastic).
- Sealed, anti-fog goggles.
- Polarized sunglasses.
- Cut-resistant gloves (Kevlar or similar, for handling sharp obsidian).
- Knee pads (you may need to crawl through intimate spaces).

**Light & Navigation:**
- Headlamp with red-light mode (waterproof, IP68).
- Spare batteries (kept in a sealed, insulated container to prevent condensation).
- Chem lights (green and red, for marking paths).
- 50m climbing rope and carabiners.
- Physical compass.
- Waterproof map case (and a paper map—electronics fail).

**Medical:**
- Antifungal powder and cream.
- Broad-spectrum antibiotics (oral and topical).
- Hemostatic gauze and waterproof bandages.
- Electrolyte tablets.
- Anti-nausea medication (for the subsonic thrum).
- Blister kit (moleskin, hydrocolloid patches).
- Water purification tablets (the water is channeled and recycled through the machine; do not drink it raw—it may be mineral-saturated or contaminated with industrial byproducts).

**Tools:**
- Multi-tool with a glass-breaker tip (for emergency exit through obsidian if necessary—though this is a last resort, as the glass is part of the "pre-formation" and may be load-bearing).
- pH testing strips (for water sources).
- Waterproof notebook and pencil (pens will fail in the humidity).

**Psychological:**
- A small, waterproof photograph of a dry place (for grounding).
- A mantra or breathing exercise to manage the "held breath" anxiety.
- Earplugs (to filter the subsonic thrum if it becomes overwhelming, though you will still feel it in your sternum).

### VIII. What Not to Do

- **Do not touch the "newborn" mineral deposits.** They are crusting surfaces in white and ochre. They are forming. Your oils will disrupt the accretion.
- **Do not drink from the collection basins.** The water is recycled through the machine. It is industrial, not potable.
- **Do not disturb the orchids.** They are drinking from the steam vents. They are part of the slow photosynthesis that is digesting the machinery. They are fragile and alien.
- **Do not stand still for too long.** The moss grows overnight. You may find yourself rooted, or the catwalk may become impassable while you sleep.
- **Do not trust the silence.** The place is quiet, but it is not empty. The maintenance drones are watching. The mountain is breathing. The water is listening.
- **Do not try to climb out immediately.** You arrived by descending; the throat may have shifted. The rock is emerging, under construction. Wait for the chamber to "exhale" before attempting to ascend. Check your rope. Check the walls. The mouth that opened may not be the same mouth that closes.

---

### IX. The Final Preparation

Stand at the threshold of the throat. Feel the warm, wet air rising to meet you like a tongue. Understand that you are entering a place where the distinction between inside and outside has been dissolved. The mountain is a body. You are entering its lung.

Breathe deeply. Then hold your breath, if you can. Step down into the milk-pale light, onto the moss-covered grating, into the slow percussion of the industrial rain. The mountain is waiting. It has been waiting since before the machines were bolted to its walls, and it will wait long after they have fossilized into stone.

Be quiet. Be wet. Be brief.
