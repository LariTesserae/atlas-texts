To descend into the throat of that mountain is to step out of the world and into a geometry that has forgotten mercy. You are not preparing for a hike, or an expedition, or even a spacewalk. You are preparing to be a single, fragile, wet mistake inside a machine that runs on stone and heat. Here is how an ordinary human—one who still breathes water, still burns sugar, still requires a sky overhead and a floor that does not breathe—might survive the Obsidian Cathedral.

### 1. The Anhydrous Carapace
Your first preparation is your suit, but it is not a suit. It is a *terrarium*, a mobile biome that must be hermetically sealed against a world that is actively trying to drink you dry. The external wetness is 0.5 on any scale you brought—absolute desiccation. Exposed skin in that chamber does not merely dry out; it is *consumed* by the chemistry of the Silicates, which would desiccate and crumble your flesh into mineral dust to join their trails.

You need a **closed-cycle thermo-chemical exoskeleton**. The outer layer must be woven from basalt-fiber or metallic-glass mesh—something that will not melt at the ambient 2.6 (whatever your instruments say that means; treat it as “baking”), will not crack when the obsidian columns ping and shift around you, and will resist the crystalline abrasion of a million fist-sized glass creatures pressing against your hull. Beneath that, a **liquid-cooling garment** circulates coolant through micro-channels, pulling heat from your body and radiating it outward through dorsal vents—vents that must be screened so that no mineral spore, no arsenic-laced vapor, and no sharp fragment of volcanic glass can find purchase.

The suit must maintain an internal atmosphere of 100% humidity and roughly 20°C. You are carrying your own weather system. The seal is absolute: magnetic or pressure-locked seams, no zippers, no tears. You must train to don the suit in a sterile environment, because the moment you break the seal in that chamber, you are not “exposed to the elements”—you are *invited* to become part of the Lithic Choir’s next generation of mineral sediment.

### 2. The Breath of Stone
The air is a lie. It is thin and sharp, a high-altitude ghost, yet dense and pressing, charged with geothermal violence. It is also a soup of hydrogen sulfide, carbon dioxide, and trace arsenic vented from the creatures’ own metabolic chimneys. You cannot breathe it. You cannot filter it.

You need a **closed-circuit rebreather** with chemical scrubbers rated for sulfides and heavy-metal particulates. Your oxygen supply must be independent—carried in cryogenic or high-pressure tanks, recycled through a lithium-hydroxide or similar bed that scrubs CO₂ and neutralizes acidic gases that leak through micro-fractures. The suit’s pressure system must equalize with the chamber’s strange density; whether the ambient pressure is high from the weight of superheated gas or low from the altitude, your suit must maintain a stable internal pressure so your lungs do not struggle against a “hot, dry tide” that feels like breathing through wet wool.

Practice breathing in the suit until it is unconscious. In the silence of that place, the sound of your own regulator cycling—hiss, hiss, hiss—will be the only heartbeat you have.

### 3. Seeing the Invisible
There is no sky. There is no light. In visible spectrum, the chamber is absolute black, a void that has forgotten photons. But the Silicates are invisible to your eyes anyway; they emit only in infrared, a “ghostly heat-map of life” that blankets the walls in a suffocating, uniform glow.

Your preparation must include **multi-spectral visual training**. You will wear a helmet with:
- **Thermal/Infrared primary display**: You will see the world as a living constellation. The obsidian columns will glow with residual heat, but the creatures will pulse beneath them—a peristaltic rhythm of systole and diastole, a slow breathing of crystal. It will look like a galaxy of embers packed into a cathedral. You must learn to read this glow not as “beauty” but as *topography*—where the creatures are thick, where they are migrating, where the mineral chimneys vent poison.
- **Low-light visible overlay**: For navigation of the geometric lattice. The hexagonal columns are wrist-thick and interlocking; in visible light (from your own helmet lamps), they will look like a dark organ pipe facade. You need to see the gaps, the fractures, the rounded crowns where the glass has sagged over millennia.
- **Active sonar/echolocation**: The air is a “wavering, heat-maddened soup” that will distort light. Thermal lensing will make the walls swim. You need acoustic mapping to feel the 60-meter span of the chamber, to sense the subsonic rumble of the magma chamber below vibrating in your sternum, to hear the *ping* of a cooling column contracting before you step near it.

Train in total darkness with only thermal feedback. Learn to navigate by heat signatures. Learn that the hottest thing in the room might be the floor radiating 2.6 upward, but the *moving* heat is life—and life is danger.

### 4. Thermal Camouflage and the Sin of Stillness
The Silicates do not hunt. They do not see you. But they *grow*. They move by differential growth and resorption, extending crystalline fronts into cooler zones while their rears wither to powder. They are chemosynthesizing the cooling rock.

This is the most critical preparation: **You must not be a cooler zone.**

Your suit’s exterior must be actively heated to match the ambient temperature—whatever that 2.6 scale registers. If you present a thermal gradient, a “cold shadow” against the hot glass, the creatures will sense it chemically or thermally. They will begin to extend new needle-like tubes toward you, filtering the “cooling” of your hull as if it were fresh mineral substrate. You will become a substrate. You will be slowly, over hours or days, encased in opalescent volcanic glass, your suit becoming just another column in their cathedral.

Therefore, you must practice **the Drift**. Never stand still. The creatures migrate in slow, peristaltic waves across the floor, leaving trails of mineral dust like dry snow. You must move with them, or ahead of them, or in a pattern that never allows a thermal differential to persist in one spot. You are a leaf floating on a thermal current, never anchoring. If you must stop to observe, do so for seconds, not minutes, and keep your suit’s external heaters cycling to mask your presence.

### 5. The Feet and the Breathing Floor
The floor is not ground. It is a tessellation of columnar obsidian—hexagonal pillars, no thicker than a wrist, fitted with geological precision. They rise and interlock, creating a lattice of perfect geometric voids. They are “breathing,” expanding and contracting with the thermal cycle, pinging like snapping wires in the dark.

You cannot walk here. You will **climb**.

Your boots must be fitted with micro-spikes or gecko-synthetic pads that can grip volcanic glass without shattering it. You must learn to read the *ping*—the sound of contraction. A column that pings is cooling, shrinking, becoming brittle. Do not place weight on it. The creatures carpet these surfaces; stepping on them will not only cut your suit (they are crystalline, sharp, veined with darker mineral) but will also crush the delicate architecture of their colony, potentially releasing clouds of mineral spores or triggering a defensive chemical venting.

Practice on glass. Practice on shifting, creaking surfaces. Learn to feel the subsonic rumble through your boots—the dynamic pulse of the magma chamber far below. That rumble is your heartbeat, your warning, your only clock.

### 6. The Mind in the Silence
The psychological preparation is as vital as the physical.

You will enter a space that is “packed, absolutely saturated with presence.” The silence is profound, broken only by the granular whisper of millions of crystalline bodies shifting—like dry sand in an hourglass, but slower, vast, and continuous. There will be no birdsong, no wind, no echo of your own voice. The darkness is absolute. The heat is suffocating despite your cooling suit; it will press against your visor, and your mind will tell you that you are burning even as your suit’s sensors say you are safe.

You must train in **sensory deprivation** and **claustrophobia management**. Simulate the chamber: a small, hot, dark box with no sound but a low-frequency vibration, and a thermal imaging display that shows a pulsing, breathing wall of life. Learn to meditate while your suit’s CO₂ scrubber hisses. Learn to interpret the peristaltic rhythm of the creatures—not as a threat, but as the only music in the universe. Prepare for the existential vertigo of being a wet, soft, temporary thing in a place where the stone is patient, eternal, and *eating* itself into equilibrium.

### 7. Emergency Desiccation Protocols
Assume your suit will fail. In a place of 0.5 wetness and crystalline shards, a breach is not an inconvenience; it is an execution.

Carry:
- **Emergency sealant**: A spray-foam that polymerizes on contact with heat and air, hardening into a glass-like plug. Practice deploying it one-handed while wearing thick gloves.
- **Tourniquet clamps**: For limb breaches. If a column shears your arm, you must seal the stump before the desiccation begins. You have minutes, not hours.
- **Internal hydration rig**: An IV line pre-inserted or a rapid-access port. If you are exposed, you must flood your system with saline before your blood thickens in the dry heat.
- **Chemical antidotes**: Amyl nitrite or similar for hydrogen sulfide exposure, chelation therapy for arsenic. Know the symptoms—burning lungs, metallic taste, the sense of your skin tightening like leather.

Memorize the evacuation route. You have perhaps sixty meters to traverse to get out. In the dark, with a failing suit, that is an eternity.

### 8. The Ritual of Entry
When you finally descend—lowering yourself into that throat, the air growing thin and sharp, then dense and hot—you will feel the transition. The outside world ends. There is no sky. Only the absolute dark.

You check your suit’s seal one last time. You activate your thermal imaging. The world blooms into a suffocating glow: a heat-map of a million fist-sized lives, pulsing in waves across the black glass. You hear the *ping* of a cooling column, a wire snapping in the deep dark. You feel the subsonic rumble in your sternum.

You step onto the obsidian.

And you begin to drift—never still, never cold, never wet—through the heart of a mountain that is slowly, patiently, eating itself, one hexagonal breath at a time.
