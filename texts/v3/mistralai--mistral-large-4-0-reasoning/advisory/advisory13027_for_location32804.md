You do not prepare for the Plain of Settled Snow the way you prepare for a desert or a mountain. You do not pack water, or rope, or a compass. You prepare to be *unmade*—to have the boundaries between your body and the world dissolved, to step into a place where the sky is liquid and the ground is memory. This is not a visit; it is a submersion into the terminal breath of a world that has decided to end by wearing itself smooth.

**First, you must prepare your body for the pressure of time.** The water here is not water as you know it. It is a medium saturated to its limit—density 2.9—with the dust of dead mountains and the snow of eons. It is thick as cold soup, viscous, slow, and it presses with a constant, ancient, absolute weight. You cannot wear a wetsuit. You cannot wear a drysuit. You must be encased in a rigid, articulated carapace—a hard-shell exosuit with an internal atmosphere that equalizes against the crushing column above, not because you fear drowning, but because you fear being *flattened*. The suit must be heated, actively and aggressively, for the temperature is 0.5—a chill that penetrates not to your skin, but to the bone of the stone itself, a cold that seeks to turn you into sediment. You will need thermal conduits running along your spine, your ribs, your skull, keeping your marrow from crystallizing in this near-frozen soup. The suit’s exterior must be armored against the particulate snow—millions of mineral grains pressing, drifting, swirling against your plates like a tactile blizzard. You will need micro-filters at every joint, or the snow will pack into your seals and grind you raw.

**Second, you must surrender your ears.** Here, sound is 0.4—a silence so total it behaves like a physical weight pressing against your eardrums. The density of the medium absorbs all vibration; even the grinding of stone against stone produces no echo. Your own heartbeat will thunder in your skull, a drumbeat in a cathedral of muteness, but the world outside will be mute. You must train yourself for this before you arrive. Spend weeks in anechoic chambers, learning to listen to the blood in your veins, to the creak of your own bones, until you can endure the silence without screaming. Your suit must include a bone-conduction communicator, for radio waves will scatter strangely in this saturated medium, and acoustic signals will die before they travel a meter. You will speak to any companions through vibrations transmitted directly into your skeleton, feeling their words as tremors in your sternum. Do not expect to hear the turbulence that buffets you. It is a silent maelstrom—a storm-state of eddies and vortices that will shove you sideways, spin you, fling you upward into the liquid sky, and you will feel only the pressure change, the sudden drag, the buffeting, with no roar, no warning howl. You must learn to read the currents by the pressure against your suit’s skin, by the way the viscous water pushes against your palms. It is navigation by touch alone.

**Third, you must prepare your eyes for the dark that is light.** The light here is 0.4—an absolute blackness that has nothing to do with night. It is a pressure of darkness, the absence of photons made visible by the density of the medium itself. You cannot bring a flashlight; the particulate snow will scatter the beam into a blinding, featureless glare, a white-out of suspended matter. Instead, you must rely on the phosphorescence. The snow glows faintly with stored light—mineral and organic particulate churning with its own dim radiance. You need goggles that amplify this phosphorescence, that paint the world in shades of bruised violet and cold jade. You will see the snow falling upward, sideways, caught in thermal currents that should not exist. You will see the flat floor stretching into a grey, featureless infinity, broken only by the occasional knuckle of primordial rock—outcroppings that have resisted the flattening, rising like memories of height. These rocks are your only landmarks. You must memorize their positions, for there is no horizon, no sky to orient you, only the churning water above and the eroded plain below. The floor offers no purchase; it is flat like a memory worn smooth, compacted ooze and lithified sediment. You will need cleats that grip by suction or micro-spikes, or you will slide endlessly in the viscous drag, unable to stand against the turbulent currents.

**Fourth, you must prepare your mind for the weight of deep time.** This is a regional basin at moderate depth, but it feels like the bottom of an ocean that has forgotten it was ever an ocean. The floor is the end of the world’s violence—erosion 2.8, worn to its final form over eons. When you stand there (if you can stand), you will feel the weight of mountains that have become dust, then stone, then dust again. This is a landscape of settled silence, where the world is waiting. You must meditate on impermanence. You must practice the acceptance of stillness, because here, despite the hyperactive churning of the water (dynamic 2.8), nothing moves forward. The snow falls forever. The cold persists. You are visiting a place that has reached its oldest-stable-state, its terminal form. You must prepare for the existential vertigo of walking on the grave of geology, for the feeling that you too are being worn smooth, leached of sharpness, settling into the silt. Bring no expectations of progress, of destination, of shelter. There are no structures here (built 0.2), no burrows, no nests. There is only the flat, the dark, the snow, and the silence.

**Fifth, you must prepare to meet the ghosts.** Fauna is rare (1.3), but you will encounter them. They are the inheritors of this ending world—pale, eyeless, translucent creatures drifting with a jerking, primitive grace through the viscous water. They are tool-users (tech 0.8), though their technology is indistinguishable from geology. They carry stones—flakes of the ancient floor, sharpened by deliberate striking, used to crack the calcium shells of sparse mollusks that graze on the bacterial mats. You must approach them with humility. They have no eyes, but they sense the world through vibrations that you cannot hear, through chemical gradients in the thick water, through the displacement of the snow. Move slowly. Do not shine lights at them (you have none, but if you did, it would blind and terrify them). Carry no metal that clanks; remember, the silence is total, and any sound you make will be an intrusion, a violence in the mute world. If you wish to communicate, learn to knap stone. Carry a piece of flint, and in their presence, strike it against another stone—not as a threat, but as a greeting, a recognition that you too are a creature of stone and bone in a world of stone. They may offer you a tool, left behind when one of their kind died and sank to the flat floor. Accept it. It is a relic of minds working with the only materials available: stone, bone, and the precipitated calcium of the snow itself. Do not disturb the bacterial mats (vegetation 0.7)—the thin films of grey and faint white chemosynthetic bacteria that pulse with chemical luminescence where ancient cold seeps weep minerals from the primordial rock. They are sparse, scattered like lichen on a tombstone, the only "plants" here, surviving on the chemistry of the deep. Tread around them. They are the last pulse of life in a world that is settling into dust.

**Sixth, you must prepare your supplies for the closed loop.** You cannot drink the water; it is saturated with mineral and organic particulate, thick as soup. You cannot eat the fauna; they are translucent, eyeless, and likely full of the same precipitated calcium that makes up the snow. You must carry a closed-circuit life support system that can filter this medium for oxygen, or recycle your own breath indefinitely. Your suit must be sealed against the particulate, for the snow will infiltrate any crack, any seam, pressing against your skin with the persistence of a memory. You will need to eat compressed nutrient paste, and you will need to excrete in sealed bags, for there is no "away" here—only the flat floor, where everything sinks and settles and becomes part of the sediment. Time will feel strange here. The turbulence is slow-motion, viscous, but the pressure is absolute, ancient. You may lose track of hours. Carry a chronometer that ticks not by sound (for you will not hear it), but by vibration against your wrist, a silent pulse to remind you that you are still alive, still separate from the ooze.

**Finally, you must prepare to leave, and to carry the silence with you.** When you finally ascend—when you decide to end your visit to this drowned, liquid world—you will rise through the column of water so thick with history that it has become weather. You will break the surface not into air, but perhaps into another world, or back into your own. But you will bring the silence with you. You will hear your own heartbeat forever after, a drumbeat in the cathedral of your memory. You will feel the weight of the flat floor beneath your feet even when you walk on mountains. You will be changed by the knowledge that you stood where the world has worn itself smooth, where the only technology is the stone in the appendage of a rare, drifting ghost, where the sky is liquid and the snow falls upward into the dark.

Prepare to be dissolved. Prepare to be reassembled. Prepare to walk, for a while, on the bottom of the world.
