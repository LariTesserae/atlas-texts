You do not "visit" Vorn. You surrender to it. The village does not decay; it *transitions*, and you are a guest in a metamorphosis. To survive—and to truly see—the ordinary traveler must prepare not just their body, but their perception of time, sound, and solidity.

Here is how you prepare.

---

### I. Physiological Conditioning: The Double Penalty

**The Altitude-Cold Paradox**
You are heading to a high-altitude basin where the air is thin *and* the water is glacial (8°C, possibly colder at depth). These two factors conspire against you. The cold will steal your breath faster at altitude because your blood is already struggling to oxygenate in the thin air. You will feel hypoxic not from depth, but from the sky.

- **Train for "cold hypoxia."** Spend weeks doing cold-water immersion (lakes, not pools—natural water has a different, more honest chill) while doing cardio at simulated altitude (hypoxic masks or mountain running). Your body must learn that when the chest tightens, it is not drowning; it is the mountain and the lake both demanding your oxygen at once.
- **Build the "slow heart."** This place moves at 0.1x speed. Train yourself to hold your breath not for sport, but for *patience*. Practice floating motionless in cold water until your heart rate drops into the 40s. You will need this when the hum of the architecture begins to vibrate in your sternum and you feel the urge to panic-swim toward the surface.

---

### II. The Equipment: Dressing for a Cathedral

**The Suit**
Wear a **drysuit**, not a wetsuit. At 8°C, a wetsuit will fail you in twenty minutes; the cold seeps through neoprene like regret. Underneath, layer merino wool—it retains insulating properties even when damp from condensation. But bring a **thick hood** and **five-finger gloves** with sealed cuffs. The water is "heavy" with mineral content; it conducts cold faster than pure water. Your extremities will go numb first. Accept this. Numbness is the price of admission.

**The Breath**
Carry **two independent gas sources**. This is a freshwater lake, so your buoyancy calculations are different (freshwater is less buoyant—you sink faster). More importantly, the water is stratified with lenses of varying mineral density. These layers can create false thermoclines and, in rare cases, pockets of lower oxygen saturation near the silt beds. A rebreather is ideal if you have one—it allows you to move silently, which matters here—but standard open-circuit is acceptable if you are disciplined about your bubbles. *Do not* let your bubbles rise through the kelp canopy; they will disturb the light shafts and startle the minnows into clouds that will blind you.

**The Light**
Bring a **focused beam** (not a flood). The ambient light is a green-gold twilight, perpetual and dim. You need to pierce the darkness of the plaza (the 30-meter-deep heart of the valley) where no light reaches. But use it sparingly. The village is adapted to low light; your bright beam is violence. Use red-light mode if available. The blind salamanders are sensitive to pressure waves and sudden illumination, even if they cannot see you.

**The Tool**
Carry a **dive knife** with a serrated edge, but not for fighting—for *cutting*. The kelp is thick, freshwater varieties that grow in columns from the rooftops. It can entangle. The petrifying wood, black and coal-like, is deceptively buoyant but brittle. If a beam collapses and traps you, you will need to cut your way out of lashing reed that has turned to something like wire.

**Communication**
Bring a **through-water communicator**, but expect it to fail. The village is a pipe organ; the low-frequency drone (felt in the sternum, heard as a sub-bass hum) will drown out electronic signals. Learn **hand signals for silence**. You will need to communicate "stop," "I am listening," and "ascend" without sound. The stratified water layers will distort your voice even if you shout—sound bends strangely here, as if the water is arguing with itself about which direction is up.

---

### III. Psychological Preparation: Time is a Local Custom

**The Temporal Drift**
Time moves slowly in Vorn. A dropped stone takes ten seconds to settle. The kelp grows a centimeter a month. When you enter, your watch will lie to you. Subjective time dilates. You may feel you have been swimming for hours when only minutes have passed, or vice versa. You may experience a profound, meditative calm, or a creeping dread of the endless.

- **Set a "touchstone."** Before descending, fix a physical anchor in your mind: the exact weight of your tank, the texture of your glove fingertips. When the hum begins to rearrange your sense of duration, return to this anchor. Do not trust your internal clock. Trust your depth gauge and your air supply.
- **Prepare for the "Loud Silence."** This place is paradoxically noisy and silent. The architecture drones; the shrimp click; the fish susurrate. But there is no human sound, no wind, no birds. The silence is *full*—a pressure against the eardrums. It will feel like the village is listening to you. It is. Do not be ashamed of your breathing. It is the only honest sound you bring.

**The Cathedral Effect**
You will feel small, but not in a frightening way—in a *sacred* way. The houses are planters now. The streets are beds of weed. You are swimming through a liturgy. Prepare yourself to be moved. Many travelers report a profound sense of peace, a desire to stay forever in the suspended animation of the cold water. Resist this. The water preserves, but it does not nourish. You are not meant to stay.

---

### IV. Entry Protocol: Piercing the Skin

**The Surface Mat**
You cannot simply dive in. The surface is a floating mat of duckweed, water lilies, and fibrous peat-moss—a skin that blocks direct sunlight. It is thick, possibly half a meter deep in places.

- **Approach from the edge of the basin**, not the center. Find a gap where the mat is thinner, perhaps where a current from the surrounding mountains breaks the surface.
- **Descend vertically**, using a line if possible. Push through the mat with your hands. It will feel like breaking through wet felt. Once under, look up: you will see a green ceiling, dappled and breathing. This is your roof now.
- **Do not kick.** Your fins will stir up the silt from the valley floor, reducing visibility from twenty meters to zero. Move with **frog kicks** only, and slowly. You are a ghost entering a dream.

---

### V. Navigation: Reading the Ruins

**The Urban Plan**
The village is legible, but only if you learn to read it as a diver. The central plaza is a clearing of pale silt—**avoid it**. The silt is thirty meters deep in the valley's heart, a black velvet that suggests depth without end. It is soft. You can sink into it, and the cold, anoxic mud will suck at your boots. If you must enter the plaza, use a guideline. Tie it to a stone pillar on the perimeter. Do not trust your sense of direction; the stratified water layers will bend your perception of straight lines.

**The Streets**
Swim the "streets"—twenty meters wide, lined with two- and three-story houses. The water here is clearer, filtered by the moss. Watch for the **silicifying wood**. The beams are black, dense, heavy, yet buoyant in their decay. They look solid, but they are transitioning—half-organic, half-geologic. They can crumble without warning. Do not grab them for stability. Use the stone walls, which are velvet-smooth, rounded by centuries of water.

**The Tower**
The tallest spire rises to within a meter of the surface (three meters of water above it). Its top stones are encrusted with green. This is your shallowest point, your emergency refuge. But beware: the green fringe is algae that has grown thick. It can be slippery. If you need to surface for air (if your gear fails), you must break through this crust. It will feel like breaking skin.

---

### VI. Biological Interface: Meeting the Gardeners

**The Salamanders**
You will encounter blind, white salamanders, endemic to this valley. They drift in the deep water near the plaza. Their movements are languid, deliberate. They are *tending* the gardens—clearing silt from doorways, nibbling algae from window ledges. They are the maintenance crew of the metamorphosis.

- **Do not touch them.** They are pale, eyeless, and sensitive to pressure changes. Your touch could damage their delicate skin.
- **Do not shine lights on them.** Use red light or none.
- **Observe.** Watch how they work. They move with a patience that will make you ache. They are preserving the ruins not out of sentiment, but as substrate. You are witnessing a symbiosis older than memory.
- **Do not feed them.** You will bring nothing they need. They are part of the cycle.

**The Flora**
The walls are carpeted in moss three centimeters thick—a sponge that drinks water and exhales oxygen. You may feel tempted to touch it. Do so gently, if at all. It is alive, and it is the lung of the village. The kelp grows from rooftops in columns reaching for light. It sways in currents so slow they seem to move only when you blink. Swim through it, but do not grasp it. It is not rope; it is a forest.

**The Fauna**
Schools of pale minnows (*Leuciscus* or similar) move through the streets in silver clouds. They will part around you. Let them. Giant water beetles crawl over the petrifying wood—prehistoric, armored. They are harmless. Newts with orange bellies hunt in the moss. They are curious but not aggressive.

---

### VII. The Stratification: Diving Through Glass

The water is layered. Temperature and mineral salinity create lenses that bend light strangely. When you swim through them, the world will appear to ripple and distort, as if viewed through old glass.

- **Trust your instruments.** Your depth gauge, your compass, your line. Your eyes will lie to you when you cross a thermocline. The village may appear to tilt, or distant walls may seem impossibly close.
- **Move slowly through layers.** A sudden change in density can disorient your inner ear, causing vertigo. If you feel the water "thicken" around you, stop. Float. Let your body adjust.
- **These layers are also acoustic traps.** Sound behaves strangely here. The hum of the architecture may seem to come from everywhere and nowhere. Do not follow sounds. Follow your line.

---

### VIII. Emergency Protocols

**Structural Collapse**
If a beam of petrifying wood cracks (it will sound like a gunshot underwater), **do not swim upward.** The collapse will create a silt cloud that reduces visibility to zero. Swim *sideways*, along the wall, until you are clear. Then ascend.

**Silt Entrapment**
If you sink into the plaza silt: **do not struggle.** The more you kick, the deeper you sink. The silt is anoxic and cold. It will preserve you, but it will not release you easily. Drop your weights. Spread your arms and legs to distribute weight. Wait for buoyancy to work (the water is heavy, but you are heavier). If you have a lift bag, deploy it. If not, wait for your buddy (you should never dive here alone) to pull you out by your harness.

**Cold Water Exhaustion**
At 8°C, you have maybe 30-60 minutes of useful function before hypothermia degrades your cognition. Watch for the "umbles"—stumbling, fumbling, grumbling, mumbling. If you feel the cold as a *warmth* (a dangerous sign of severe hypothermia), ascend immediately. The surface is only 3-20 meters away, depending on where you are.

**Altitude Sickness**
When you surface, the thin air will hit you like a wall. You may feel dizzy, nauseous. Sit on the shore (if there is shore—the basin may be surrounded by cliffs). Do not exert yourself. Let your body readjust to the oxygen-thin atmosphere.

---

### IX. The Departure

When you leave—when you must leave—do not rush. Swim slowly to the surface mat. Place your hands on the underside of the vegetation and push up. Break through. Gasp the thin, cold mountain air.

You will carry the hum in your sternum for days. You will hear it in shower water, in traffic. You will dream of velvet-smooth stone and black, buoyant wood. You will want to return.

Do not.

Vorn is becoming. It is not a museum; it is a chrysalis. Each visit disturbs the balance, stirs the silt, accelerates the petrification. You are a brief, warm intrusion into a cold, patient process. Let it remain a memory—green, gold, and humming.

Prepare to be changed. Prepare to be preserved. Prepare to let go.
