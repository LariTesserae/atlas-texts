You do not prepare for this place by packing for a climate. You prepare for a *condition*—a slow, wet unraveling of the boundaries between your body and the world.

**The Suit is Your Skin**

You cannot wear ordinary clothing. The humidity here is not moisture; it is an atmosphere that seeks entry. You need a sealed, positive-pressure suit—not for vacuum, but for *infiltration*. The shrimp-things are small enough to find any gap, and they are legion. They will not bite, not exactly, but they will coat your visor, your seals, your boots, until you are walking through a writhing second skin. The suit must be pale-colored, reflective, because the light here is a bruised, permanent twilight and dark colors absorb heat you cannot afford to carry. It must have a cooling loop, because the air is thick with geothermal heat that presses against your chest like a hand. The condensation will bead on the outside of your suit within minutes, dripping in a rain that never quite reaches the ground, and you will feel the chill of evaporation against the synthetic fabric, a constant, clammy reminder that you are the only dry thing in a wet world.

**Your Feet Will Betray You**

The shoreline is a lie. There is no edge, only a gradual surrender where concrete becomes crust becomes soup. You need boots that seal at the knee, with soles that grip on mineral slick and bacterial film—that oily, faintly iridescent patina that coats everything. The ground where it rises is a crust of evaporated salts, sharp enough to file skin, baked into shapes that will twist an ankle. Where it sinks, it sinks into water that is warm as blood, thick with suspended minerals, and alive with movement. You must carry a probing staff, not for balance, but to test depth. One step off the apron and you may find yourself thigh-deep in a lagoon that was, seconds ago, solid ground. The shrimp will swarm your boots instantly, a silent, clicking carpet, and you must learn to walk through them without flinching, because flinching means falling, and falling means submersion in a broth that is not water but chemistry.

**Your Lungs Must Learn a New Language**

The air is steam and haze, a vault of diffused light that smells of hot metal, sulfur, and the particular mineral tang of boiling stone. You need a respirator with filters rated for particulates, heavy metals, and whatever volatile compounds the ancient factories still leach into the humid air. Breathing here is work—the air is dense, saturated, and you will hyperventilate if you do not consciously slow your rhythm. The sound will disorient you: a low thrum felt in your sternum rather than heard, the hiss of pressure valves, the groan of thermal expansion in rusted girders. You need ear protection that filters the white-noise hiss of steam while allowing you to hear the *clicking*—that soft, endless percussion of chitin against chitin, the sound of billions of eyeless creatures moving in the shallows. It is almost below hearing, a vibration in your teeth, and if you listen too long, you will swear you can feel them moving inside your skull.

**Your Eyes Need Shelter**

The light is a Turkish bath, a greenhouse at noon, a world where the sun never burns but never truly shines. It is uniform, pale, shadowless. Your depth perception will fail. You need a visor with polarized lenses, not for glare, but for the mineral haze that blurs all edges. You need a headlamp—bright, focused—because the dimness is deceptive; the factories are vast, hollow skeletons, and the spaces between them are depths of warm, steaming dark. The light will play tricks. You will see movement where there is none, and fail to see the rusted walkway that ends abruptly over a discharge channel until your boot is already past the edge.

**Water is Everywhere and Nowhere**

You will thirst. The heat is oppressive, wet, a fever-dream of humidity that makes your sweat useless. But you cannot drink the lagoon. It is geothermal, mineral-saturated, filtered by extremophiles and polluted by industrial runoff. You must carry every drop, sealed in a thermal flask, and you must ration it with religious precision. Your suit will have a condensation reclamation system—drinking your own sweat, distilled through filters—but it will not be enough. You will need electrolyte tablets, because the heat will pull salts from your body faster than you can replace them. When you piss, you will do so into a sealed waste unit. To release it here is to feed the cycle, to add nutrients to the broth that breeds the swarms. You are a contaminant in a closed system, and you must behave like one.

**The Swarm is a Weather System**

You must prepare psychologically for the *multitude*. Not danger—these things are harmless, patient, translucent—but for the *scale*. They drift by the billion. They coat the stilted legs of factories in writhing carpets. They swirl in the discharge channels, filtering, breeding, dying in cycles so fast they blur into a single organism. When you stand still, they will settle on you. You will feel them as a faint, tickling vibration against your suit, a million tiny impacts. It will trigger something primal, a revulsion deeper than disgust. You must train yourself to stand motionless among them, to let them crawl over your visor, obscuring your vision with their pale, many-jointed bodies, and not scream. They are silent, but their movement is a susurrus, a whisper of chitin, and in the thick, humid air, it will seem to come from inside your own ears.

**The City is a Skeleton**

The infrastructure is vast, sparse, and *porous*. Concrete has gone soft, leaching lime. Steel has rusted to powder. You cannot trust any surface. Walkways on stilts span the lagoons, but the stilts are driven deep into warm mud that may not hold. Test every girder with your weight shifted slowly. The factories are empty—machines dismantled, rusted into the landscape—but they groan. Thermal expansion will make the metal scream as the geothermal pulse heats it. You will hear cracks that are not structural failure but the earth breathing. You need a hard hat, not for falling debris, but for the psychological weight of standing under structures that are actively decaying, that are being digested by heat and time.

**Time Feels Different**

The light never changes. It is always twilight, always the pale glow of a sun diffused through steam. You will lose track of hours. The swarms live fast and die faster, a pulse of life that makes human time feel geological, slow, patient. You need a chronometer, and you need to check it often, because the disorientation is profound. The sound—the thrum, the hiss, the clicking—creates a trance. You may find yourself walking in circles, or standing still for hours, watching the steam rise from a crack in the concrete, feeling the warmth seep up through your boots.

**What to Bring**

- **A white flag or beacon**: Not for surrender, but for visibility. In the dim light, among the mineral flats, you will become invisible. You need to mark your path with reflective tape or glow-sticks, because the steam will swallow your footprints before you take the next step.
- **Antiseptic wipes**: For when you must remove your gloves. The bacterial films are alien, extremophile, indifferent to human skin but potentially hungry. The mineral crust will cut you; the water will infect the cuts.
- **A journal with waterproof paper**: To record the patterns of the steam vents, the safe paths through the crust, the times when the discharge channels run hottest. This place changes. The "gradual drowning" is literal—the shoreline migrates with the geothermal pulse.
- **A signal mirror**: Because sound is absorbed by the humidity. If you need to call for extraction, light will travel where sound dies.
- **Patience**: You cannot rush here. The heat, the humidity, the psychological weight of the swarms—they demand a slow, deliberate pace. Haste means falling into the lagoon, means heat stroke, means losing your mind to the clicking dark.

**What to Leave Behind**

Leave your expectations of *shore*. Leave your sense of *edge*. Leave the idea that land and water are separate things. Here, they are a gradient, a breath, a decision the earth has not yet made. You are visiting a place that is post-vegetative, indifferent to green things, a chemistry set left out in the heat. You are a brief, warm, wet intrusion in a world of minerals and multitudes.

When you finally stand on the concrete apron to leave, you will be coated in salt, in condensation, in the memory of a billion small, pale things. Your suit will drip. You will carry the thrum in your sternum for days. And you will understand that some places are not meant to be visited, only witnessed—from the safe distance of a sealed visor, watching the steam rise into a sky that never clears.
