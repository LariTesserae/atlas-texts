You do not simply *enter* this place. You surrender to it. Preparation begins not with gear, but with the acceptance that you will be the only soft thing in a world of glass and grinding stone, and that the world will not notice you. It will merely adjust around you, as water adjusts around a stone—slowly, and with the weight of tons.

**The Body, First**

You must prepare your flesh for a contradiction: heat that is simultaneously dense and thin. The air is scalding, yet it presses less against your skin because you are high in the spine of the world. Acclimatize to altitude sickness before you arrive; your blood will thin, your breath will come shallow, and the heat will feel like it is rising from inside your own skull. But do not acclimatize to *wet* heat. This is desert-dry, volcanic-dry, the kind of heat that sucks the moisture from your eyes and makes your sweat evaporate before it touches your skin. You will need three days of dehydration training—learn to recognize the moment your body stops producing sweat, because that is the moment you begin to cook from within.

Train your sternum. The sound here is not merely loud; it is a physical substance. Millions of mandibles grinding volcanic glass create a low-frequency roar that travels through basalt, through your boots, up your legs, and settles in your chest like a second heartbeat. Practice sleeping with a subwoofer pressed against your ribs. Learn to feel vibrations in your teeth without flinching. You will need to hear your own thoughts in a place that screams constantly.

**The Shell**

Your clothing must be a compromise between armor and mobility. The walls are raw volcanic glass—obsidian-sharp, angular, fractured into geometries that have not yet learned softness. A single fall, a stumble against the wall, and you will be shredded. Wear a suit of woven ceramic fiber over chainmail or scaled aramid plates. The creatures themselves are armored in mineral deposits; you must match them, not out of aggression, but so that when you brush against the writhing mat of their bodies, you do not become the soft thing that gets sliced open.

The suit must be sealed. Not just against cold, but against the dust. The mineral dust here never settles; it hangs in the dimness like ash suspended in honey, a particulate soup of silica and sulfur that will coat your lungs and grind them to scar tissue within hours. Your respirator must be positive-pressure, with a HEPA cascade and a chemical scrubber for sulfur compounds. Carry three days of filtered air minimum—there is no photosynthesis here, no plant to exhale oxygen for you. This is a chemosynthetic world; you must bring your own breath, bottled and precious.

For thermal regulation, you need a closed-loop cooling system that does not rely on evaporative sweating (you have no water to spare) but on phase-change materials or thermoelectric pumping. The temperature sits at the edge of human endurance, shimmering the air into visible waves. Your suit's interior should stay at 20°C while the exterior faces 60°C or more of dry, radiant heat rising from cooling magma below.

**The Eyes and Ears**

You will need goggles that filter the harsh spectrum of bioluminescence—creatures flickering in the dark with cold, alien light—while allowing the dull red glow of cooling magma to illuminate your path. The contrast will be extreme: black glass absorbing light, then sudden, pulsing luminescence from the living walls. Your pupils will struggle. Bring infrared capability, but also a very narrow bandpass filter for the bioluminescent wavelengths, or you will be blinded by the "stars" of their bodies.

For sound, carry active noise-canceling protection that does not merely mute the industrial roar, but *translates* it. The vibration carries information—the shift of a thermal gradient, the movement of a large specimen adjusting its bulk against the glass. You need to feel these things in your fingertips, not just hear them, or you will step into a path that has not yet opened and be crushed by tons of slow-moving biomass.

**The Movement**

The entry is a fissure no wider than a shoulder. Practice crawling through tubes while wearing a bulky, heat-resistant suit. Learn to move sideways, to flatten yourself, to slide through the throat of the world. Once inside, you will find no floor that is not occupied. The creatures carpet the ground in a writhing, clicking mat, packed so tightly there is no empty space. You cannot walk; you must *swim* through them, or climb.

Bring magnetic climbing gear—the walls are basalt, iron-rich, and the mineral-plated creatures themselves may provide handholds if you are careful. But move slowly. The colony shifts its weight on a timescale of hours. A leg extends. A mandible opens. You might find yourself pinned against the glass by a wave of biomass adjusting to a new thermal gradient. Do not panic. Do not struggle. The creatures are indifferent to you; they do not look up. They will eventually shift again, and you will be released. Panic is how you get ground into the ore.

Your boots must have soft, wide soles to distribute weight—you do not want to puncture the mat and find yourself ankle-deep in superheated gas venting from respiratory slits. You also need crampons for the glass, but they must retract instantly. One false step on obsidian, and you are bleeding into a place that has no concept of blood, only of ore.

**The Mind**

Prepare for the psychology of deep time. These creatures operate on a timescale of hours and days. A wave propagates through the mass like a glacier calving. You will feel impatient, then you will feel small, then you will feel the urge to scream into the roar and feel nothing. Resist this. The place is not hostile; it is *industrial*. It is grown, not built, and you are a foreign object in a smelter.

You must also prepare for the darkness. There is no sunlight, only the faint red pulse of magma below and the flickering bioluminescence of the inhabitants. Your eyes will adapt, but your mind may not. Bring a headlamp with a very dim red setting—white light will startle nothing (they do not react), but it will ruin your night vision and make the glass walls into mirrors of your own insignificance.

Learn to eat without hunger. There is no food here, only chemistry and heat. You will carry nutrient paste, and you will eat it mechanically, because the body burns calories maintaining temperature in the heat, and because the mind needs routine in a place where time moves differently. Drink your water ration with religious precision. In this place, water is not life; it is a finite resource that evaporates from your skin before it touches the ground.

**The Protocol**

Do not bring weapons. They are useless against creatures that process stone for a living and do not recognize you as prey or predator. Bring instead a *sample kit*—vials lined with Teflon, because the excretions of these creatures are pure mineral deposits, veins of refined metal lining the walls. You may be the first to collect them.

Do not bring a communicator that relies on radio waves; the mineral dust and volcanic glass will scatter the signal. Use a wired tether, or acoustic signaling through the rock itself—press your ear to the wall and listen for the groaning of the colony shifting. You can communicate with other travelers this way, tapping out patterns that travel through basalt faster than sound travels through air.

**The Return**

When you leave—and you will leave, because no human can stay in the edge of endurance forever—you must decontaminate. The dust will be in your suit's seals, in your hair, in the creases of your skin. The sulfur will have stained your filters. You will carry the vibration in your sternum for days after, a phantom grinding that makes your teeth ache when you try to sleep in a quiet room.

But you will also carry something else: the memory of a place where biology and geology have fused into industry, where the world is still young enough to remember its own birth, and where you were the soft, temporary thing passing through the machine. Pack accordingly. Move slowly. Breathe carefully. And remember: the dark closes behind you like a throat swallowing, but it will open again, eventually, to let you out.
