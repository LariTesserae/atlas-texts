You will not be visiting a place; you will be *entering a metabolism*. The High Hollow does not care that you are a traveler from elsewhere, that your ears are calibrated for speech and your eyes for horizon lines. It is a cathedral of industry that has decided to grow a garden, and you are a guest in its lungs. Prepare accordingly.

**First, your body.** The elevation will bite the moment you step through the airlock—thin, cold, faintly blue air that tastes of metal and distance. Even though the Hollow itself is warm, you must carry altitude sickness medication, supplemental oxygen for the transition zones, and a pressure-regulation mask that seals well enough to handle the sudden density change when you enter. Inside, you will sweat; the air is damp in pockets, industrial-warm, thick with the respiration of compost and geothermal exchange. Dehydration will ambush you here despite the humidity—the air is working, it pulls moisture from your skin even as it beads on the walls. Drink constantly. Your clothing should be layers you can shed: breathable, quick-drying, but with a waterproof outer shell that can handle the warm drizzle that falls only in the central court. Wear boots with thick, vibration-dampening soles; the terraced floor is printed ceramic and sintered metal, and you will feel every pump cycle through your feet if you are not prepared for it.

**Your ears will betray you.** The roar is not merely loud; it is a *floor*, a steady-state ocean with no shore. Speech dies here. Bring industrial-grade hearing protection—not the foam kind, but active noise-canceling muffs with bone-conduction capability—because you will need to feel the room’s heartbeat in your sternum to navigate it. The Hollow communicates through vibration: the fauna are mostly deaf to the airborne shriek and speak through taps, tremors, and pressure pulses in the floor. Learn to read the substrate. When the great ventilation lungs inhale for hours and then exhale, you will feel it as a shift in pressure against your eardrums, a slow tide. Do not fight it. Move with the breath of the machines.

**Communication is tactile.** Since the noise never drops to a level where words survive, pack a personal vibration-transmitter—a device that converts your voice into subsonic pulses you can press against the floor or walls to send to companions. Learn basic light-signaling: the electric lamps here provide a steady spectrum for growth, and you can modulate a flashlight or wearable LED to cut through the roar. Touch is currency. The moss is soft, the slag pebbles are rounded and warm, the conductive vines are pulsing with current beneath their living tissue. Do not grab the vines; their stamens are electrodes, and the flowers open to expose circuitry. Carry insulated gloves, but keep your fingertips sensitive—you will need to feel the difference between a warm heat grille (safe, where the geckos sun themselves) and a live conduit (dangerous, veined through the composite walls).

**Time will dilate.** This is the strangeness that is quiet rather than spectacular. A storm lasts minutes, but it belongs to a water cycle that turns over years. The central rain follows a slow-moving ellipse beneath the ceiling plates, shifting across the court over days. Moisture sometimes climbs upward along the warm copper rails, defying gravity through charge and capillary action. Do not stand beneath the hanging planters when the thermal columns rise; droplets detach and appear to ascend before they are caught and rerouted. The gantries move across the atrium once an hour with a sound like a slow door in a very large throat—sixty minutes to cross the void. Learn their schedule. Do not be in the center when the maintenance platform descends. The blind warm-water fish turn in the floor basins; they are keyed to the machines’ long breath, feeding during intake, mating during exhaust, sleeping when the great fans draw down. Move slowly. The erosion here is not ruin but an established state—edges are softened, metals carry stable patinas, biofilms have reached equilibrium. Respect the pre-weathered twins; when a panel is replaced, it is replaced with something that already looks ancient. Do not bring shiny new objects. They will scream against the settled aesthetic. Pack dull, matte, pre-aged gear if you can.

**Your eyes will need recalibration.** The light is ordinary—flat, even, diffused by membranes, supplemented by a steady electric spectrum. There is no gloom and no glare, which means no shadows to read depth by, no golden hour to mark time. The place is visually calm, which is deceptive. The vegetation is lush and none of it is wild: thick vines drape the gantries, ferns crowd every niche, roots grow through aeroponic lattices rather than soil. The green layer is dense at the surfaces, almost overgrown, but it respects the center. Do not wander into the middle of the room expecting emptiness; it is a void, yes, but it is *held*. Stand in the center and look up: the ceiling grid is far away, normal-lit, beaded with condensation. Look down: the basins hold their small seas. Around you, the walls are thick with life—pale moths rise around the lamps and vanish into the dark, beetles with translucent iridescent wing-cases move across the walls, snails trace the glass. They are vibration-oriented. Do not stomp. Do not shout (you cannot anyway). Do not expect to be alone in the void; the pale motes of spores hang there, and the machines breathe around you.

**Psychologically, prepare for contradiction.** You are in a small room at extreme elevation—a hamlet-sized machine-garden where everything is constructed, yet everything is slightly overgrown. It is small enough to hold (you can stand near one wall and see the opposite wall) and empty enough to feel like a sky that has been roofed over. The middle is almost nothing; most of its volume is air. This will disorient your sense of scale. The outside is cold and lethal; the inside is a factory-biome producing fresh water, biomass, compressed air, spores, ceramic feedstock, and steady heat. You are inside a living machine that farms itself. The noise is deafening, but the changes are glacial. A machine’s failure is not an event; it is a weather pattern.

**Pack for the specific dangers:** a compass that works on magnetic fields (the composite walls may confuse GPS), a headlamp with a red filter (to avoid disrupting the fauna keyed to light cycles), a water filtration system (the condensation is pure but the biofilms are established, not sterile), and a journal that you write in by touch—braille or tactile ink—because you will not be able to hear yourself think, let alone speak.

Most importantly, pack patience. Time here is measured in pressure and patience. Do not rush to see the "sights"; there are none, only systems. Watch the water weep from pipe joints. Feel the thermal expansion groan in the walls. Wait for the hourly gantry to pass. Stand in the center when the warm drizzle prepares overhead, and feel the room breathe around you. You are not visiting a landscape. You are standing inside the exhalation of a world that has been itself for a long time, and it will continue to be itself long after you have crossed back through the airlock into the thin, cold, blue dark.
