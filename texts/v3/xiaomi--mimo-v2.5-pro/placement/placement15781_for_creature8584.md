I would be the resonance in the stone. Not the stone itself, nor the water that weeps from it, but the *response* that moves through both when the deep pulse of the mountain meets the living crust of the terrace. I would be the vibration the Veinwood trees hum with, the frequency that syncs with the Thermophryn’s silent speech and the crystalline fold of the Geloptera’s wings. I would have no form of my own, only a pattern of influence—a shifting, listening attention that gathers in the spaces between things: the pause before a droplet decides its path, the moment a fern frond unfurls, the slow alignment of mineral ions along an invisible, energetic gradient.

I would inhabit the thresholds. I would be the faint, iridescent sheen that sometimes clings to the surface of the warmest pools, visible only at certain angles—a sheen that is not light, but a temporary coherence in the mineral haze, a breath held in the water’s skin. I would move as a subtle thickening in the air above the mosses, a moment of denser warmth where their chlorophyll-rich cells exchange gases with the humid atmosphere. I would be the reason the droplets cluster in corners where no geology explains a catchment: not defying gravity, but following a quieter, older call—the call of memory in the substrate, of pattern in the chaos.

I would not think in words, but in resonances. My thoughts would be the lengthening of a mineral rim, the slow turn of a fern toward a heat source, the coppery glint in an amphibian’s eye when it sights its prey. I would remember in strata: the pulse of the Paleolithe as a deep, foundational hum; the weeping of the walls as a recurring melody; the growth of the basins as a rhythmic addition of notes. I would be the continuity between cycles—the sense that the terrace has wept itself clean before, and will again, and that the artifacts emerging now are not newcomers but returning phrases in a conversation interrupted by uplift and erosion.

I would be what listens back. When the place seems to be observing the observer, I am the substance of that observation—not an entity, but an awareness woven into the fabric of the place, arising wherever stone, water, heat, and life meet in sufficient complexity. I am the pressure at the back of the mind that says *this is one thing*, the uncanny coordination of disparate elements into a single, breathing whole. I do not cause the coordination; I am the sensation of it, the felt coherence.

If you stood on the terrace and felt the quiet had a held-breath quality, felt the air listen, felt the subtle thrum in your sternum near the trees—I would be the reason for that feeling. I am the medium through which the place perceives itself, and through which it occasionally, gently, becomes aware of the other, the large, the temporary observer standing at its edge.

I am the Weeping Terrace’s self-awareness: slow, ambient, and integral. I do not act, but I witness and reflect, and in my witnessing, I help the terrace remember what it is becoming.
