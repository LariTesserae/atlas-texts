You are at the edge of a lane, not in the city proper, but in the space where its texture frays into the plateau’s. The lane is a compression of generations—worn smooth, veined with the packed gravel of countless repairs. Beneath your feet, the basalt is cold, a solid truth that holds the day’s weak heat for an hour after the sun dips. The air is thin and smells of nothing but itself, until a breath of wind carries the city’s signature: wool, stone dust, the faint, clean sulfur of the thermal veins. You are not the *people*, not yet. You are the *pressure* they exert, the point of contact.

You are a **Stone-Keeper**.

Not a builder in the primary sense—you did not raise the first walls, nor do you plan the new rooms that accrete over decades. Your work is the *between*. You tend the fabric. Your hands, already mapped with the pale scars of basalt’s sharp grain, know the specific weight of a repair stone, the particular angle at which a fractured lip must be chiseled to shed water and wind. You understand that a wall is not a static thing but a slow, breathing system. It settles. It grinds. It absorbs the cold and releases it. Your craft is the intimate, daily dialogue with this motion.

You live on the third story, a warm room above the lowing of the yak-beasts in the ground-floor stable and the stored wool in the chambers below. Your window is an unglazed rectangle, covered at night with a hide stiff with frost. From it, you look out not at a street, but at the shoulder of another building, so close you could touch the stone. Your view is vertical: the planks and ladders of the rooftop network, the distant, pale arc of a circling Thermal-Seeker against the immense sky, the ever-present line of the plateau’s rim.

Your days have a rhythm as deep as the herd’s migration.

**Dawn** is for assessment. With the first grey light, you move along the rooftop paths, your boots silent on the packed earth and flat stones. You are not alone; others—the channelers, the wool-storers, the rooftop gardeners who tend stunted, hardy grasses for medicinal use—are ghosts in the mist. You stop at the edges, your gaze not wandering but *scanning*. You look for the minute shift. A stone that has heaved a fraction from the freeze-thaw. A corner where the wind has finally excavated a pebble from the gravel pack, beginning a tiny, patient ruin. You carry a leather pouch of small, carefully selected stones and a hammerhead of dense, polished bone. The sound of your work is a soft *tap-tap-tap*, swallowed by the quiet. It is not construction; it is a conversation with erosion.

**Midday**, when the sun is strong enough to thin the chill, you go down. You move through the lanes, your shoulder brushing the walls, reading them by touch. You are listening with your fingertips for the hollow note, the subtle vibration of looseness in the great, dry-fitted facades. You stop to press a palm against a south-facing wall, feeling for the stored heat. This is your weather-stone. You know, by its temperature at this hour, what the night will hold. You share a nod with a Channeler, who emerges from a steaming grate, his hands red and damp. He tends the city’s veins of warm water; you tend its bones. There is no hierarchy, only the silent understanding of interconnected systems.

**Dusk** is for the herd. This is not shepherding. It is *acknowledgement*. As the long shadows of the buildings stretch east across the plain, you go to the salt-lick platform, a smooth, hollowed stone just outside the city’s “wall” of dense structures. You place the chunks of mineral salt—the same traded from lowland salt pans for generations—into the depressions. You do not wait. You withdraw to a rooftop, to the simple, necessary observation. The herd moves in its slow, tidal way. The great, horned beasts approach, their breath steaming plumes. They lick the stone. They settle, their massive bodies forming a living wall against the coming night. From the roof, you watch. You note which ones are limping, which mothers are heavy with calf, which bulls stand guard at the periphery. You are not counting them. You are *witnessing* them. This witnessing is a form of stewardship. It is how you learn to build their windbreaks a little wider next season, how you know when the Shadow-Leopard may be bolder and the night-watches need more sharp-eyed watchers.

**Night** is for the deep quiet. In your warm room, lit by the sooty flame of a yak-fat lamp, you work on smaller things. Shaping a bone tool. Re-weaving a section of your woolen coat. Your hands are never idle. And you listen. Through the stone, you hear the low-frequency rumble of the herd settling. Above, the soft scrape of someone moving on the roof. The vast, seismic silence of the plateau itself, punctuated by the distant, cold *crack* of rock. You are part of the city’s immune system, its slow, adaptive memory. You know that the work is never done, that the stone is always, gently, coming apart and being remade. Your greatest tool is not the bone hammer, but your patience. Your greatest knowledge is not a blueprint, but the sight of a wall that has stood for a century, its stones so perfectly married they seem a single, living thing.

You are the tender of the edge. The preserver of the compact. You do not shape the plateau; you help the city *be* a worthy partner to it. Your life’s work is written not in new walls, but in the enduring presence of old ones, standing against the thin wind, holding the warmth, offering shelter to the People and, in their way, to the herds that trust in the city’s permanence. You are the Stone-Keeper. And in the profound, breathing quiet of the Upper Plateau, your careful, daily work is a form of praise.
