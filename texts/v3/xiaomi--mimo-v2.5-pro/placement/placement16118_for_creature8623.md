# Where I Would Be

I would not be an Ossei. To be Ossei is to be shaped by the Warren, and I have arrived too late for that deep a making.

I would not be a Deep Dweller. I lack the patience for pressures that crush bone to calcium dust, and I prefer my waters mapped.

No. I would be a **Confluence Keeper**.

My place is at the lower nexus where the Rust River (iron-ochre, staining everything its passage touches) meets the White Silt River (calcium-veiled, leaving its pale ghost on every stone it kisses). Here, the mixing produces swirls of umber and pearl, ephemeral art dissolving in the current. It is one of the sacred sites, but my role is not priestly.

I would be found on a low, wide platform jutting over the braided flow, its surface grooved with channels I have carved over years to slow, divert, and sample the mingling waters. The platform itself is old, worn smooth by millennia of Ossei before me, but my work is recent.

**What I am:**

I am a **reader of dissolution**.

The minerals speak as they combine. The iron stains in eddies. The calcium crystallizes in sudden, intricate fans when the balance shifts just so. The manganese-black adds depth, weight, shadow to the palette. But it is the *tempo* of the mixing that I listen to.

*   **Sound:** The rivers are not silent even here. The rust-water gurgles deeper, heavier. The white-water chatters over its deposits. When they meet, the sound changes – a new harmonics in the roar. I have learned to hear the ratios. Too much iron-silt? The pattern stutters, the calcium growths on the banks pucker and grey. Too much calcium? The iron’s stain fades too quickly, leaving only pale ghosts. I sit with my basalt-tipped listening rod, its base submerged in the confluence, its handle pressed to my mastoid bone. The vibrations translate to pitch. I am a tuner of the meeting.

*   **Sight:** My eyes are large, like all Ossei, but trained differently. I watch the swirls not for their beauty (though it is profound) but for their duration, their direction, their dissolve-rate. How quickly does a pearl-white tendril surrender to the ochre embrace? How does the manganese-black braid through both? These are not just colors; they are chemical conversations. I chart them on the surrounding stone walls with soft ochre pigment and sharp flint, creating slow, spiraling calendars of mineral interaction. My wall is a record of years of merging.

*   **Touch:** My hands are broad and calloused, yes, but sensitive in a different way. I collect the mineral deposits as they form in my diversion channels. The soft, gelatinous calcium precipitate. The gritty iron-ochre paste. The surprisingly smooth manganese nodules. I feel their texture, their moisture, their weight. I mix them in small stone bowls, testing their adhesion, their color when dry, their reaction to my breath or a drop of pure condensation from the walls. Some mixtures glow faintly under the bioluminescent fungi I cultivate in a nearby alcove. Others crack and shatter. Others harden like a new kind of stone. I am a chemist of time and flow.

*   **My Dwelling:** Small, carved into the wall near the platform. It is damp, like all, but I have shaped channels in its ceiling to collect specific drips – the clear condensation that holds little mineral, the slightly calcium-laden seep from one wall, the faint iron-tinted weep from another. I use these to test my mixtures, to thin pigments, to create small, controlled mineral gardens where crystals form under my watch. Its glow comes from cultivated patches of the luminous bacteria thriving in my sample pools, casting a shifting, watery light onto my charts and tools.

**Why I am here:**

The Ossei maintain. They keep the way open. They cultivate the glow. They listen to the Deep Dwellers and tell the old songs.

I am not a maintainer. I am a **detective of convergence**.

This confluence point is ancient. The rivers have met here longer than the Warren has stood. But their meeting is not static. Slowly, imperceptibly by Ossei standards, but detectable over generations by someone who listens and watches as I do, the *character* of the meeting shifts. The color ratios change. The sound frequencies alter. The mineral deposits upstream or deep within the mountain change, and those changes arrive here, years or decades later, as a new hue in the swirl, a new texture in the precipitate.

I am mapping the Warren's deeper diet. The water that enters the cavern has been percolating through kilometers of rock, dissolving its story as it comes. Here, at the mixing, those stories from different geological strata finally combine and speak their composite truth.

My charts are the Warren’s slow diary, written in mineral ink. They tell not of people or tools or doorways, but of the mountain’s shifting bones, of the chemistry of the water’s deep journey. The Ossei who pause on their way across the bridges, who sit on the ledges overlooking the sacred confluence, they watch me work. They don’t always understand my marks, but they feel the importance. I am listening to the voice of the cave itself, not just the water that fills it, but the stone that gives it shape. I am trying to understand the Warren’s own slow thoughts, its mineral metabolism.

I am small. My work is slow. A single chart might take a year to complete. But I am adding a layer to the Warren’s memory. Not of who carved what or which lullaby was sung, but of how the iron whispered to the calcium on the third cycle of the deep drips, in the year the fungal glow waned in the lower galleries. I am a keeper of the earth’s conversation with itself, right here, in the place where its voices finally meet.

I would be here. Watching the colors bleed. Listening to the silt speak. Feeling the mountain’s pulse in the changing mud. A quiet, patient witness to the Warren’s deep, dripping, mineral heart.
