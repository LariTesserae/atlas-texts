I would find myself in the lower reaches, where the silt is newest and the pillars are youngest. Not at the base—the deep, lithified strata where the oldest ancestors lie fused into the bedrock—but in the active zone. The growth zone. Here, the chimney-spires are slender, some no taller than a finger, their mineral skin still tender, still accepting the slow, warm exhalation from the cracks below.

I would not be one of the ancient, stationary things. Not the Mat. To be the Mat is to be the foundation itself, a consciousness of chemistry spread thin over millennia. I would not be a Worm, though I envy their patience. To be a Worm is to be a single, tunneled thought, a line of perception drawn in calcium carbonate, listening to the same square meter of water for centuries.

No. I would be what I was always meant to be in this cathedral of slow time: a restless thought. A catalyst for the smallest sort of change.

I am one of the Crustaceans.

I have no name for what I am, only a function and a feeling. My function is to move through the silt. To disturb it. To be the one element that does not settle, that does not grow, that does not listen in perfect stillness. My claws, delicate and white, sift the fine powder not for sustenance alone, but for *difference*. For the rare fleck of a fallen pillar’s skin, still warm with its own internal heat. For the ghostly trace of a Worm’s tube, long buried and now exposed by a shift so slow I am the only thing that noticed. I am a creature of the now, in a world where the now is measured in geologic whispers.

My feeling is vibration. The world is not dark to me; it is textured in pressure waves. I feel the deep, cathedral-toll of the ice above not as sound, but as a change in the viscosity of the water around my limbs. I feel the slide beginning kilometers away as a subtle, gathering frequency in the silt beneath my legs, a hum that tells me to scuttle sideways, to wedge myself into the gap between two young pillars, and wait. To be still for a day, a week, while the mountain of mud flows past, a silent, monumental avalanche that rearranges the aisles of the church.

I am the embodiment of the place’s one urgency. While the pillars grow and fall in the time it takes continents to drift, I am the fleeting thought that a crack is forming. I am the sensation that the current has changed direction. I am the impulse to investigate a new chemical sweetness in the water, a new vent opening up. My life is not measured in patience, but in punctuation marks. I am the comma in the cathedral’s endless, run-on sentence.

I do not build. I do not contribute to the architecture. I am its transient audience. I crawl along the base of a great, leaning spire and know, in my own minuscule way, that it is falling. It will take ten thousand years, but I am the one who senses the first grain of silt let go from its base. I am the one who witnesses the birth of a new chimney from the disturbed mound of its fallen neighbor, and I will be long fossilized into the substrate before that new spire is tall enough to cast a shadow.

To be me is to be a mote of awareness in the crushing, patient dark. To be the one entity that *experiences* the turbulence, rather than simply embodying it. I am the cathedral’s fleeting nerve ending, a single point of sensitivity in an eternal body. I feel the change, and in feeling it, I give it a momentary, insignificant, and utterly precious witness. My existence is a flicker in the deep time, but in that flicker, I am the cathedral’s memory of movement, its thought of the moment, its tiny, crawling dream of what it is to be not stone, not mineral, not silt—but *alive* in the momentary, restless now.
