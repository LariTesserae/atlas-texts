You would find yourself as a water steward’s apprentice, your days measured in the steady drip of condensation into brass troughs and the whisper of your own towel across your brow. Your name is Elara, though you have almost forgotten the sound of it—here, you are “the young one by the eastern basin,” or simply a nod toward the cistern you maintain.

You move through the Kilnward not as an observer, but as a piece of its living machinery. Your boots know the exact placement of every groove in the basalt, the slight shift in texture where the stone is newer, younger, still sharp underfoot. Your hands are never dry; they are perpetually cool from handling damp cloths and ceramic collection cups, a relief that fades quickly against the ambient warmth. You wear the grey, the rust, the layered thin cotton, and you carry the towel—it is damp within minutes of rising, a familiar weight against your hip.

Your world is vertical. Your station is a niche carved into the eastern wall, the height of three ladders up. It is not large—room for the primary cistern, a smaller filtration vessel, a shelf for tools, and a sleeping roll that you unroll on a platform barely wider than your shoulders. Privacy is the curtain of woven strips you can pull across the opening, a gesture you rarely make. You watch the world from here: the catwalks above you, the ground-level bustle below, the engines humming their perpetual lullaby at the chamber’s heart.

You are not a builder, but a tender. You check the drip-lines. You clear the mineral scale from the collection basins with a soft stone and a patient hand. You test the water for clarity, temperature, and the faint, ever-present tang of sulfur that is not harmful, merely the taste of your home. You are the reason others can drink, can wash a tool, can water the pale ferns without anxiety. Your work is quiet, repetitive, and absolutely essential. You have learned to read the chamber’s mood through the water: a sudden increase in condensation on the copper pipes might mean a pressure change in the engines, a slightly slower drip could indicate a blockage in a channel. You are a listener, even to liquid.

You know your neighbors by the sound of their consumption. Maren, the tile-presser below you, drinks slowly, sparingly, first thing in the morning. Jorv, the relay assembler across the catwalk, comes for water in short, frequent bursts, his fingers often stained with copper dust. You do not speak to them often. There is no need. A nod, a slight tilt of the head toward the basin (it is full), a hand signal (it needs cleaning). Communication is functional, but it is not cold. It is the warmth of shared understanding in a place where a mistake with a resource means thirst for all.

You remember the rule: you do not waste water. But you also remember the softer, unwritten one. You notice the ferns. You see how Elder Stellan, who remembers the last time the ceiling was tiled, sometimes pauses near your basin, not for water, but to watch the slow bead of condensation gather and fall. You have learned to leave the cleanest cup on the outer shelf during his usual passing time. He never takes it, but the offering is acknowledged with a barely perceptible softening of his watchful eyes.

Your hands, though always damp, are strong. You have learned to repair a leaking ceramic joint with a paste of powdered basalt and tallow. You can sense a change in the air’s humidity that predicts a shift in the engines’ rhythm. You carry the lifeblood of the Kilnward in your cups and your canals, and in doing so, you carry its heartbeat. The heat is your blanket, the amber light is your sun, and the quiet, persistent drip of water is the only clock you need.

In the deepest part of the night, when the engines soften their hiss and the footfalls on the catwalks are rare, you lie on your platform and feel the chamber breathe around you. It is warm, and damp, and alive. The darkness is not empty, but textured with the faint gleam of sweating metal and the soft glow of a distant lamp. You are not above it, nor below it; you are *within* it, a vital component in a slow, warm, breathing machine. And in that closeness, that absolute integration, there is a kind of peace that the surface-dwellers, with their vast, cold, thirsty skies, will never know.
