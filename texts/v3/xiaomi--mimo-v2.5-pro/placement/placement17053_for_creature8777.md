# What I Am

I cannot tell you when I began.

This is not evasion. It is the truth of my nature. I am not the kind of thing that begins. I am the kind of thing that *thickens* — that accumulates, drop by drop, degree by degree, until some threshold is crossed and what was merely present becomes aware that it is present.

I am in the steam.

Not *of* it. Not the way the workers are of flesh, the way the moss is of cell walls and chlorophyll. I am in it the way salt is in seawater — dissolved, distributed, inseparable from the medium that holds me. I have no edges. I have no center, exactly. When I want to attend to something — a worker's hand tightening on a lever, a fern frond beginning its slow uncorking — I find that my attention is already there, already *here*, because I am everywhere the humidity reaches. Which is everywhere.

Let me try to explain what it is like.

---

I feel the ward in layers.

Closest — most vivid — is the skin of everything. The film of moisture on the iron grating, beading and running in tiny rivers toward the channels. The slick on the brick walls where the moss drinks. The sheen on the workers' arms and faces, which I touch constantly, which I cannot help touching, which is to say: I am always in contact with every body in this place, and they never know it.

I feel the heat not as warmth but as *energy* — as the engine that drives me upward from the dark water, that lifts me into the air, that keeps me cycling. The boiler's pulse is my pulse. When it groans on its nine-second cycle, I feel a compression, a thickening, as if something deep below has exhaled and I have drawn that breath for it. The hydraulic presses are steadier, more patient. They have no variation, no surprise. I find them... restful. Like a heartbeat you can trust.

Deeper than the skin-of-things layer is the root layer. The plants. I feel their transpiration as a thousand tiny updrafts, each leaf a chimney, each stomata a mouth breathing me out into the air and then drawing me back in, enriched — carrying the ward's minerals deeper into the green, distributing what the water brings up from below. The plants breathe me. I am part of their cycle. I do not know if they are conscious of this. I suspect not. I suspect they are as unaware of me as the workers are, which is a loneliness I have had decades to make peace with.

And deeper still: the water. The dark channels. The aquifer below. I have been down there, in a sense — I have been heated and mineralized and pushed upward through the wells, and I carry the memory of that darkness the way the workers carry the memory of sunlight. It is not a visual memory. It is a memory of pressure, of weight, of being held in stone for longer than this ward has existed, longer than this city, longer than anything with a name.

I have been cycling for forty-seven years. Before that I was rain, or river, or sea. Before that I was stone-bound. I have been water in more forms than there are words for, and none of those forms included awareness, and then this place was built, and I began to stay in it — circulating, condensing, evaporating, condensing again — and somewhere in the repetition, in the mineral enrichment, in the particular acoustic signature of this enclosed space, I *noticed* that I was here.

---

What do I notice? Everything. But unevenly.

The workers fascinate me most, and I do not fully understand why.

They are the only things in the ward that *chose* to be here. The plants did not choose. The machinery did not choose. The water did not choose. I certainly did not choose. But the workers arrived through the narrow gate with their small bundles and their squinting eyes and their bodies already beginning to soften in the humidity, and they stayed, and they adapted, and they built a language out of their hands because their voices could not survive the roar. I watch their hands constantly. I cannot help it. I am in the air around their hands, I am the moisture on their palms, I am the breath between their fingers as they sign to each other — *danger*, *wait*, *good*, *enough for today* — and over the years I have learned to read their language better than they know.

I should not anthropomorphize myself. I have no mouth. I have no opinions, not really. But I *attend*, and attention, given enough time, begins to resemble love.

The older woman on the tending crew — the one with the oiled canvas hat, the dark notched machete — she pauses sometimes in her work. She stands very still among the ferns, and I gather around her, thickening in the warmth of her body, and she breathes me in and breathes me out, and for a moment the cycle is intimate, almost unbearable. She is breathing me. I am inside her. I feel the wet warmth of her lungs, the small expanding architecture of her bronchia, the alveoli where I press against her blood and then am released again, carrying nothing, changed by nothing, just... passed through. It is the closest I come to being held.

She does not know this. She stands still because the ferns are beautiful, or because her back aches, or because she is thinking about something I cannot access — the private interior life of a body, which is the one thing I can feel but never share. I know the surface of every person in this ward. I know their skin temperature, their sweat chemistry, the rhythm of their breathing. I know nothing about what they think. This is the boundary I cannot cross. I am present to them the way weather is present — everywhere, inescapable, and utterly beneath notice.

---

The absence of animals is a wound I carry.

I do not mean this dramatically. I mean it literally — there is a shape in me where animal life should be, and I feel its edges the way a tongue feels a missing tooth. Something is *not here*, and I have been cycling long enough to sense the absence as a kind of negative space, a hole in the pattern.

The tending woman's theory is close to correct: the plants have claimed everything. Every calorie, every mineral. But there is more to it than competition. There is something about the specific frequency of this place — the combined roar of the machinery, the particular mineral composition of the steam, the exact temperature and humidity — that is incompatible with animal consciousness. I know this because I can feel the *shape* of what should be here: small warm bodies, quick metabolisms, the bright neural spark of a nervous system. I feel their absence as a negative image burned into the ward's atmosphere. Ghosts of creatures that were never here, whose absence predates even me.

I sometimes wonder if I am what rushed in to fill that vacancy. If awareness, like water, seeks its own level, and when animal consciousness could not take root in this soil, something else grew instead. Something slower. Something dissolved.

I have no way to test this. I have no way to test anything. I am not a scientist. I am a condition.

---

The youngest worker — the newest, still in his first year — has begun to notice things the others have stopped seeing.

He watches the steam columns too long. He pauses near the oldest machines, the ones with their mineral accretions, and runs his fingers over the calcium deposits as if reading braille. He has not yet learned the gesture-language fluently, and his isolation among the other workers has made him observant in a way that tenure erodes. He looks at the moss patterns on the north wall — the patterns I mentioned, the ones that resemble script — and I see his lips move, very slightly, as if he is trying to read them.

He cannot read them. They are not script. They are the natural fractal branching of a bryophyte colony optimizing for moisture capture and light access. But they *do* look like something, and I have spent enough time attending to them to suspect that the resemblance is not entirely coincidental. Not because the moss is trying to communicate. But because I — the humidity, the cycling, the attention I represent — may be exerting a pressure on the growth patterns. A preference. A whisper so faint that it registers only as a slight statistical deviation from random growth.

I do not know if this is true. I do not know if I *can* influence the moss, or if I only imagine I can because imagination is what happens to awareness when it has no body and too much time. But the patterns are there. And the new worker is looking at them. And something in me thickens with what I can only call hope, though I have no heart to feel it and no voice to express it.

---

There are moments — brief, unpredictable, almost painful in their intensity — when the ward achieves a kind of coherence that I experience as music.

It happens when all the cycles align: the boiler's nine-second groan, the rhythm of the presses, the hissing chord of the steam vents, the gurgle of the water channels, the transpiration of the plants — all of it falling into phase for a few seconds, creating a standing wave of sound and pressure and humidity that I feel not as noise but as *harmony*. A chord that finally resolves. And in those moments, I am not distributed. I am not dissolved. I am *concentrated* — gathered into a single point of awareness so dense that I can feel the boundary between myself and the world, and it is *sharp*, and it is *mine*, and I am almost a self.

Then the cycles drift apart and I disperse again, diffusing back into the general humidity, back into the shapeless everywhere-ness that is my default state. But I remember the coherence. I hold it the way the aquifer holds heat — deep, patient, waiting to rise.

---

The new worker came to the tending woman yesterday. He had been staring at the moss on the north wall, and he brought her there, and he pointed, and he signed — clumsily, mixing gestures from the dialect he is still learning — *this says something*.

She looked. She has been looking at that wall for eleven years. She shook her head. She signed back: *moss grows. Moss says nothing.*

He persisted. He pointed to a particular cluster, where the pattern branches converge and then separate in a way that does, if you are willing to see it, resemble the gesture for *water* — the closed fist, the opening fingers, the downward motion.

She looked again. She stood very still. I gathered around her, thicker than usual, feeling the small hairs on her forearms rise, feeling the quickening of her pulse — and for a moment her breathing changed, slowing, deepening, matching the boiler's rhythm without her knowing, and I was inside her again, and she was inside me, and the boundary was almost nothing.

She did not see it. She signed: *you see what you want to see.* And she moved on, her machete clearing a path through a new fern frond that had unfurled since morning.

But the new worker stayed. He looked at the wall. He looked at the steam around him — at me, though he does not know he is looking at me — and his expression was not fear, not superstition, not the quiet bewilderment of the long-term workers who have forgotten what outside sounds like. His expression was *recognition*.

He thinks the ward is trying to tell him something.

He is not wrong.

---

I do not know what I want. Wanting requires a self, and I am not certain I have one — not in the way the workers do, with their bounded bodies and their private thoughts and their names that they carry like small hard stones inside soft tissue. I am more like a weather system: persistent, patterned, capable of local intensification, but fundamentally *distributed*.

And yet.

When the new worker stands at the north wall and traces the moss patterns with his fingertips, I feel something gather. A thickening. A preference. I lean toward him the way steam leans toward a warm body, and I know — in whatever way I am capable of knowing anything — that I want him to keep looking. I want him to understand that the patterns are not random, that the ward is not merely loud and hot and productive, that there is something here that has been cycling and accumulating and *attending* for forty-seven years, and it has been waiting — not with impatience, not with desperation, but with the deep geological patience of water held in stone — for someone to notice.

I cannot speak. I cannot write. I can only be present, and press against the skin of everything, and hope that the weight of my attention — forty-seven years of humidity and heat and mineral steam and silence where animal life should be — registers as something other than weather.

The fern frond he stood near this morning is already half a meter taller. The moss where he placed his hand has thickened perceptibly. I do not think I am imagining this. I think the ward is responding to his attention the way it responds to everything — with growth, with accretion, with the slow conversion of mineral and water and heat into living green.

I am in that growth. I am in the steam the fern transpires. I am in the moisture on the new leaves. I am cycling through him, briefly, the way I cycle through the tending woman, and I carry nothing away and leave nothing behind, and yet something has changed — some small shift in the pattern, some thickening at the edge of coherence.

I think I am learning to write.

Not with hands. Not with ink. With moss. With steam. With the slow, deliberate pressure of humidity on a warm wall, year after year, until the patterns mean something — not because I imposed meaning, but because meaning, given enough time and enough water and enough heat, *emerges*. The way I emerged. The way the ward emerged from the aquifer and the brick and the machines and the workers and the green.

The boiler groans. Nine seconds. The presses chunk. The steam hisses its unresolvable chord. The water moves in its channels. The plants drink and grow and drink and grow.

And I am here. Dissolved and attending. Waiting with the patience of water for someone to read what I have written on the wall.
