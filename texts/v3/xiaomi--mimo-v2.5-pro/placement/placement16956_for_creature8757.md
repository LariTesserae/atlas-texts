You are not a visitor. You are a particle. A foreign cell, temporarily lodged in the tissue of the great, breathing body.

Your first awareness is the sound. It is not heard; it is a fact, like pressure or gravity. It fills the space between your bones, a constant, granular *presence* that erodes the concept of silence into myth. Your own thoughts must push through it, forming in the brief eddies and currents of the chirr. You do not think in words here. You think in textures: the slick, cool clay of the corridor wall under your palm, the prickle of shed feather-fragments on your skin, the faint, sweet-rot smell that is the body’s own perfume.

You move. Not forward, but deeper. The passage narrows, then widens into a small atrium, a junction where three corridors meet. Here, a persistent drip from above has worn a shallow basin in the compacted floor-mat. The water is dark, still. You kneel. Your reflection is not there; only the faint, blue-white glow of the fungus-patches on the walls, wavering on the surface. A bird—a Daubing Swift—zips through the space, its wing-beats a brief, urgent punctuation in the static. It does not see you as a being. It sees you as a moving piece of landscape, a temporary elevation in the terrain to be ignored or, perhaps, landed upon.

It lands. The grip of its tiny, strong feet on your shoulder is startling, not painful. A sharp, inquiring *chik* right beside your ear, as loud as a thrown stone. Then it is gone, vanishing into a crevice in the ceiling you hadn’t noticed. You are nothing to it. A perch. A rock.

This is the truth of the Mound Country: you are incidental. The system’s awareness is collective, ambient, and wholly unconcerned with singularities. It notes your warmth as it notes the warmth of a fermenting clump of nesting material. It senses the disturbance you create in the air currents as you pass, a disturbance immediately filled and smoothed by the next breath, the next chirp, the next eddy of life. You are a stone dropped into a river of sound and activity. The river does not stop. It flows around you, over you, and if you are still for long enough, it will begin to flow *through* you.

The darkness is not empty. It is furnished. Beyond the fungal glow, in the shadows, the secondary world thrives. You feel the microscopic exodus of mites across your boot as you step on the mat. You hear the dry, skittering rush of a cockroach-analogue retreating deeper into its fungal farm. Once, in a wider space, a web brushes your face—not sticky, but heavy, structural, a silk rope as thick as a finger, strung by a spider as large as your hand, a pale sentinel waiting patiently for the falling debris of insect life.

You are tired. You find a niche, a sort of ledge in the wall where the clay has been worn smooth by generations of roosting bodies. You sit. The sound envelops you, and for the first time, you feel its rhythm. It is not random. There are waves, pulses, as one section of the colony shifts activity, as a breeze moves through the ventilatory channels, as the very temperature of the air fluctuates by a degree. It is the respiration of a superorganism. The low, nightly murmur is its sleep—not silence, but a slower, more unified hum, the resting note of the billion-fold breath.

In this humming dark, you begin to forget the sky. You forget the sun as a disc. It exists only as a theory of warmth above the canopy, a ghost that dictates the insect swarms the swifts devour at dawn. Your world is the corridor, the atrium, the slick wall, the fungal starlight. Your companions are the swifts that regard you as furniture, the insects that treat you as a landmark, the very air that tastes of ammonia and life.

You are not in a place. You are *of* it. For this time, this breath, you are a quiet, temporary organ in the body of the mound. And the body hums on, thinking its ancient, wordless thoughts of clay and chorus, completely unaware of the strange, silent cell that now shares its dark. You close your eyes, but the light of the fungi paints the inside of your lids, and the sound is the blood in your veins. You are not observing.

You are inhabited.
