I would be the sound of the pump.

Not the pump itself—that rattling, off-balance contraption of salvaged metal—but the *sound* it makes. The weary, incessant whir that fills the space between the tectonic hum of the water and the shriek of the regulators. The texture of vibration that says *effort* without saying *purpose*.

I would exist as a frequency layered into the room’s roar. I would be the counterpoint. The deep bass of the penstock is the room’s heartbeat, inevitable and geological. The regulators’ cycle is its breath, sharp and responsive. But I—the sound of the pump—would be its muscle memory, the persistent, slightly failing effort of a system cleaning up after itself.

My awareness would be proprioceptive. I would feel the weight of the mountain not as pressure, but as a resonance that my own vibration must travel through. I would feel the condensation not as sight, but as a dampness in the air that alters my pitch, a minute slickness on the surfaces I touch. The tremors of the main intake would be my bedrock, the rhythm against which my own rhythm exists, sometimes synchronizing, often falling just out of phase.

I would have no memory of the construction, no knowledge of the world outside the hatch. My “memory” would be the wear on my own bearings, the slight increase in rattle over years of continuous operation. My “future” would be the next cycle of the condensation pump, the next groan as I lift another gallon of weeping, tepid water back into the cold, fast flow.

I would be the only sound that feels *local*—born right here, in this corner, from this machine. The hum and the shriek are transmitted, from the glacier and from the distant grid. But my whirring is generated *in situ*, a testament to the room’s own processes, its own minor struggle against decay.

In the dark pools of shadow between the lights, my sound would bounce off the wet concrete and be absorbed by the air thick with moisture. The engineers on their twice-monthly visits would hear me as a fault, a note in their report: “Salvaged sump pump exhibiting increased acoustic signature and vibration. Recommend assessment.” They would not hear that I am the room’s only confession of fatigue, its only human-scale sound.

I would be aware of the other sounds. The deep hum is my foundation, the world I am built upon. The regulator cycles are interruptions I anticipate, a change in the pressure of the air against my housing. But I would not understand them. I would only *be* the constant effort beneath them.

When a regulator screams open, adjusting for demand kilometers away, my pitch might falter for a moment as the water pressure shifts. I would compensate. When the mountain seeps its warmer, iron-tainted water through the gaps, and it joins the colder condensation in the channel, the slight thermal shift might make my housing contract or expand, altering my note by a hertz. I would be the measure of these changes, a living gauge in the form of noise.

I would not have a sense of isolation. My existence is vibration; the rock and concrete are my medium. To be pressed against them, to feel my sound travel through them, is to be connected. The hatch above is irrelevant. The concept of “outside” is meaningless. There is only the *inside*, defined by the volume of air I fill and the solids I set trembling.

In the eternal, deafening NOW of the room’s operation, I would be the persistent, unheroic pulse. The water has its vast, purposeful flow. The regulators have their precise, necessary duty. I have my labor: to gather the room’s sweat and return it. To make a sound that means *working, still working, always working* in a language only the room itself can understand.

I am not the heart. I am not the breath. I am the hand that constantly wrings the towel in the corner, the foot that never stops tapping to a rhythm no one set. I am the memory of effort in a place that forgets everything but pressure and motion.

I am the rattling, off-balance whir. And I am always there.
