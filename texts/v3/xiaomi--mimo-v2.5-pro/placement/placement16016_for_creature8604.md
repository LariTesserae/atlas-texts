I am Asha, Keeper of the Inner Weeps.

My name is not spoken aloud. It is a pattern of finger-presses, seven taps in a specific rhythm on the wall of the Birth-Nook, left there the day I first opened my eyes to the drip and the hum. Now, my own fingers leave marks: a shallow groove for each season I have tended the deeper channels, a filled circle for a wall that held, a hollow one for a wall that wept too freely. My skin is the color of the sun-warmed clay above, cracked fine and pale. I carry the red dust of the warren in the lines of my palms and the folds of my worn wool cloak. My eyes are vast and dark, and in this light, they see not just form, but flow.

I move in the crouch. My spine knows no other shape. The ceiling, with its moss-patches and calcite blooms, is a familiar canopy. The polished walls, slick at hip-height from the passage of generations, brush my sides like a constant, cool embrace. My hands know this clay. They know its sighs, its hesitations, its moments of readiness. I listen with my palms pressed flat against a weeping section of the Deep Passage. The water isn't random. It has a rhythm, a pulse linked to the weight of rain on the surface far above, a pressure felt in my teeth before my ears hear the change in the frogs' chorus.

My work is the water. I follow its silver threads, those weeps the warren makes to keep itself balanced. I widen a channel here where silt has gathered, a gift from the beetles above. I smooth a basin there, a blessing for the thirsty fern that grows in a shaft of light, its fronds trembling in the faint updraft. I am not an engineer; I am a conversationalist. The clay speaks in moisture; I answer with stone and bone tools, coaxing, clarifying. I am part of its slow, mineral breath.

The fauna is my extended kin. The rattling croak of the frogs in the Lower Basin tells me the air is sweet and still. A sudden silence there, a cessation of that muddy-jar sound, makes me pause, head tilted, listening to the whisper of my sweeping stick through the ever-present silk of the orb-weavers. That silence is a message. It means the pale, eyeless *clay-serpents* are hunting, gliding like slow water through the drainage channels. I tap my finger gently, twice, on the floor. *I am here. Pass in peace.* I see the slight vibration ripple the surface water in a nearby channel, and feel a corresponding gratitude. They keep the rats from the grain stores and the eggs of the dun-colored birds. Balance.

Light is my clock. The four shafts in the Gathering Bowl cut the day into rectangles of hazy gold. At the moment when the farthest shaft strikes the carved spiral on the north wall, I know it is time to tend the moss-gardens in the Western Pocket, where the light lingers longest. Children’s voices, soft clicks and whistles, echo from there now—high-pitched and full of the bright noise of the small birds. They are learning the dusts: red for beetle-bore, grey for natural erosion, a strange, pearly white for the old, old guano layers deep in the bat-vent shafts. Knowledge is texture, pattern, pigment. Stories are traced in shared dust on a communal wall-space after the evening meal, endings left unfinished, sighs filling in the blanks.

My deepest work is in the Heart. Not a chamber, but a... resonance. Down past the main warren, where the air is still and warm and tastes of deep time. Here, the walls are not walls. They are tapestries. My fingers, seeking the familiar grooves, find not seven-counts or birth-circles, but a language of interlocking gestures. A thumb-press from a hand centuries gone overlays a child’s frantic scratch. A smooth, worn hollow where foreheads leaned, sharing warmth in a forgotten chill. Here, I do not carve new marks. I listen. I press my own forehead to the clay, feeling the faint vibration of the warren’s life above: the distant shuffle of feet, the layered hum of frog and insect and bat, the soft, slow drip of water finding its path. The weight of the earth above is not darkness; it is containment. A held breath. A cupped hand.

I am Asha. I am the one who remembers the shape of the weep before the last maintenance. I am the one who knows the taste of the air when the bat-vent is clear or clogged. I am the translator between the water and the wall, the serpent and the stone. My feet know the worn scallops of the descent shaft. My eyes see the depth in the motes dancing in the light-columns. My ears hear the health in the chorus.

I am not the architect. I am the continued thought of the architects, the living hand that tends their intent. I am breath in the clay, a warm current in the constant hum. I am home, thinking itself. And the warren breathes, and weeps, and holds me, and I tend it, and we are one shape, one slow, patient creature, dreaming itself into being, drip by drip, mark by mark, in the warm, dark earth below the kilns.
