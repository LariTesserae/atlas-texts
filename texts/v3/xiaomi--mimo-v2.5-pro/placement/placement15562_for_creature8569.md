The dry place isn’t dry. You learned this before you learned your mother’s face.

You are Fenmouth-born. Your first breath was water-thick, scented with peat smoke and the sweetish rot of moss. Your skin remembers the constant damp, the way it seeps even through the woven leaf-membrane cloaks, settling cool against your collarbones. You don’t shiver from the cold anymore; your body runs on a slower burn, broad and efficient, like the settlement’s lamps. You are short, solid, built low to the ground, good for hunching over your work on a ledge, good for moving without disturbing the teeming surfaces.

You live in the green glow. The settlement’s woven walls pulse faintly with captured foxfire. It’s not light to read by, but it’s light to *know* by. You know the patterns: the slow, deep-green throb of healthy moss-mat; the frantic, mottled flicker when a fungal patch is stressed; the steady, cool-blue constellation of the growths by the lagoon edge. Light is language. Darkness is not an absence here, but a texture – the dense, breathing dark where the amphibians watch and the arthropods flow.

Your hands are never clean. The fine grit of basalt dust, the slick of moss slime, the faint, greasy residue from handling dried arthropods – it’s etched into the lines of your palms. You weave. Not cloth, but life. You take the pale, leggy stems, harvested when they’re at their most pliable, and you weave them into frames. You layer the translucent leaf-membranes, still damp, stretching them taut. Then you wait. You keep the structure misted, you encourage the living moss-mat to colonize its edges. You *build* the walls, and then the walls begin to build themselves. Within a season, the outside is shaggy, glowing faintly. Within a year, you can’t tell where your craft ends and the Fenmouth begins. It’s growing over your tools, too. The bone pick you use daily has a soft, green fuzz along its shaft. You scrape it off, but it’s a losing battle. Everything here wants to be absorbed.

You fish the lagoon. You know the rhythms. You watch the walls, not the water. When the frogs and salamanders go still – a sudden, profound cessation of their slow, predatory patience – you know the eels are near the surface. You pull in your net, light hand-woven mesh of dried stem-fiber. You wait. The silence thickens, becomes a physical thing in your throat. You hear the soft *gulp* as an eel breathes, see the ripple disturb the glowing algae. The living wall holds its breath. Then, slowly, a frog twitches. An arthropod skitters. The river of pale bodies resumes its flow. You cast your net. Timing is everything. You take only what the rhythm allows.

You harvest. Not with violence, but with selection. You seek out the slightly larger arthropod species that dwell in the mid-depths of the moss-shag. You recognize them by their slower gait, the deeper grey of their segments. A quick, gentle pinch behind the head-segment. They stop moving. You drop them into your woven pouch. You leave the tiny ones, the frantic rivers. You leave the tenders of the bulbous growths undisturbed – you know where they work, and you avoid those patches. It’s not morality, exactly. It’s pattern recognition. Disrupt the tenders, and the growths sicken. The growths sicken, and something else suffers, and the rhythm falters. You are part of the rhythm.

You sit sometimes on a ledge above the lagoon, wrapped in your shimmering cloak. You watch the deep. You see the eels’ slow, pale loops in the black water, the brief galaxies they leave in their wake. You feel the presence of the amphibians all around you – unseen, but *felt*, a thousand points of patient attention in the dark. The rustle of the arthropods is the background hum of your blood. The drip of water is your pulse. You are not separate. You are the Fenmouth, aware of itself.

The world above is a story. Flat grey plain. Wind. Empty sky. Your elders speak of it with a quiet dread. Not of danger, but of *lack*. The terrifying emptiness. Here, in the pocket, every square inch thrums with purpose. The growing is the purpose. You feel it – the slow shift of the walls, the imperceptible rise of the lagoon. Your dry place must move upward, occasionally. You reinforce lower structures with fresh moss-layers, knowing the old ones will be subsumed. You don’t fight the growing. You move with it. You *are* it.

You are the complex node in the network. The aware one. The one who weaves the stems and tells the stories and times the fishing by the stillness of frogs. You are Fenmouth-born, and the dark is not empty. It is full of you, and of everything else, breathing, watching, growing, together. The damp is your skin. The cold is your companion. The bioluminescence is your sky. This pocket, this exhaled place, this living bowl – it is the world, and you are within it, and of it, and it is home.
