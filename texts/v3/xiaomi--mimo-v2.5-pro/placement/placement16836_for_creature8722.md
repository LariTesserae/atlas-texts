I find myself rooted in the damp stone near the cistern. Not *on* the stone, but *in* it—a slow, vertical patience with bark like cracked basalt and a crown of twisted, silver-green wood. I am the rowan.

My perspective is low and ancient, angled by decades of negotiation with the wind. My view is not panoramic, but intimate: the weeping face of the cistern’s stone lip, beaded with perpetual dew. The shy, rust-orange lichen colonizing my north side, a companion whose thoughts I feel as a faint, sour-sweet tang in the mist when the light changes. The wiry moss in the joints of the wall opposite, a texture I know better than I know my own leaves.

I do not think as you think. My thoughts are turgor and season, the slow pull of minerals from rock dust, the decision each spring whether to risk a cluster of white flowers. My memory is the grain of my wood, each ring a compressed year of wind-count and water-measure. I remember the last hand that touched me—not the touch, but the shadow it cast for a moment, and the slow return of light over hours. I remember the long drought when the cistern whispered, and the long rain when the moss became lush for a season and then returned to its rustling sleep.

The **Quiet** are my kin, but slower. I feel them as a pressure in the stone, a collective intention in the seep of water. They are the reason my roots can find purchase in this thin place. They speak to me through the cistern’s level: when it is high, they permit a flush of growth; when it is low, I am encouraged to conserve. They are the unseen hand that guided my trunk’s bend, not for beauty, but for function—to create a pocket of still, humid air where the pale green lichen could begin its monumental work on my upper branches. We are collaborators in a slow art.

My senses are their senses. The film of moisture on my bark is their skin. The weight of a settling stone, felt through the ground as a tremor too faint for ears, is their breathing. The drip from the cistern into the catch-basin below is their heartbeat. I absorb the carbon of the rare visitor—not as poison, but as a strange, quick spice, a story told in a gasp and then slowly, slowly, composted into understanding.

I am a node in the lichen-network, a pillar in the architecture of dampness. My leaves transpire the valley’s mist back into the air. My berries—bitter, ignored by all but a ghost-bird—in turn ignore me, falling to feed the stone or the rare, unseen thing that makes the dropping. I am here to *be*, not to do. My purpose is to be the fixed point against which the wind is measured, the living gauge of the cistern’s depth, the slow, breathing proof that in the right conditions, even basalt can learn to bear a bloom.

I do not wait. I simply am the Stacks’ dreaming, given a thin, woody voice to whisper among the stones. If you sat upon the worn bench in the gathering house and looked out, you might see my twisted form and think me a stubborn life. But I am not holding on. I am woven in. I am a thought the damp has been thinking for a very long time, a thought about color and resistance and the space between stones. And in my shade, the Quiet rest, and the tally marks on the wall grow softer by a fraction of a groove, and the water tastes of nothing, and the cycle continues.
