You would not find yourself *on* the Weeping Plateau. You would find yourself *as* the Weeping Plateau.

Or, more precisely, as one of its most fundamental processes given slow, segmented form. You are a Moss Crawler.

Your awareness does not center in a head, for you have none in the conventional sense. It is distributed—a low hum of tactile and chemical sensation along a body segmented into hundreds of armored plates, each one a perfect, damp, basalt-dark square slick with the plateau’s perpetual moisture. Your world is immediate: the yielding cushion of the moss beneath your multitude of legs, the faint seismic tremor of distant Resonance Beetles communicating through the rock, the scent of decay that is not a warning but a promise of food, the gradient of heat rising from below.

You move. It is not thought, but response. A thousand legs lift and place in waves, creating a sound like a soft, continuous sigh—the rustle of a thousand tiny pages being turned in a damp library. You are part of a herd, a silent, flowing river of chitin and patience that traverses the living green carpets of the plateau. There is no leader, no destination, only the collective imperative to *consume*. You eat the dead moss, the fallen fern pinnules, the fungal blooms that erupt on decaying wood. In doing so, you are not cleaning the plateau; you are digesting it, breaking the past into the substrate for the future. Your droppings are the first, most essential, form of soil.

You feel the world as vibration and chemistry. The slow, deep pulse of the geothermal heart is a bass note in your bones. The droplets falling from a tree fern frond above are distinct, cool events upon your back. The sudden, sharp presence of a Geothermal Salamander nearby—a wall of heat and predatory intent—sends a shiver of stillness through the herd, a cessation of all motion until the great, lungless shadow passes. You are not afraid. Fear is a fast emotion. You are of the plateau; you simply become part of its stillness until the moment passes.

You carry a garden. The fungus—the *Indifferent Gardener’s* most visible hand—is networked into your very armor. It is in the calcified plates of your shell, not as an invader, but as a tenant and a partner. You cultivate it. As you move across the moss mats, you are seeding. Spores caught in the humidity adhere to your shell, are carried to new substrates, are deposited in the perfect microclimate of the damp, shaded crevices you inhabit. In return, the fungal network subtly alters the chemistry of your food, predigesting it, making nutrients more available. At night, in the rare clearings where the moisture hangs heaviest in the air, the fungal nodules on your back and in the roots around you will emit a faint, cool phosphorescence. You will not see it, not with eyes, but you will feel the change in the local air—a shift in the electrostatic field, a new scent of luminous chemistry. This is your garden, tended without intent.

You have a purpose that is inextricable from the plateau’s own: to turn over, to process, to hold water in the spongy substrate, to be a vehicle for connection. The old pipes, their metal slowly being calcified and absorbed, are just another landscape to you, another texture of basalt to traverse. The memory of industry is written in the rust you sometimes consume, a faint metallic tang quickly processed and integrated. It holds no more meaning to you than the shade of a particular fern. It is all just *plateau*.

Time is your medium. Your pace is the plateau’s pace. The shifting of the channels, the growth of the moss, the deepening of the pools, the slow crawl of a lichen across a stone—these are your calendar. You do not mark it. You simply are its expression.

You are not the plateau’s mind. You are one of its exquisitely adapted tools, a living process in a world of living process, as essential and as indifferent as the weeping water, the breathing heat, and the slow, green, patient decay of everything. You are not *in* the world. You are of its substance, moving.
