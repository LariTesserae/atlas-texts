I am the one who listens to the drip-songs. Not the grand chorus of frogs after rain, nor the mechanical *clack-clack* of the crested flocks—those are for everyone. My domain is the whisper, the trickle, the almost-inaudible *tick* of condensation finding its path down a leaf-vein, into a cupped petal, along a channel in weeping bark, and finally into the waiting stone trough. I trace the water’s memory.

In the Basin of Lantern Marsh, where everything is wet, my role is to know *how* it is wet. I am a Water-Reader, a Keeper of the Drip. My dwelling is a hollow in the great central tree, not far from the notch-carved record. Its floor is not matted fiber, but a mosaic of different mosses I cultivate—each one drinks differently, speaks differently to the air. One turns brittle and pale when the fog will lift by midday; another beads with frantic, tiny droplets before a storm climbs over the ridgeline.

I move through the canopy not on vine-ladders, but along the paths of the water itself. I know which root-knobs are slick with algae and therefore treacherous, which platforms shed rain fastest, where the condensation channels on the oldest trunks converge into reliable streams. My hands are not stained green from fruit, but grey-green from the fine, silty mud that accumulates where water pools and slows—the ‘dreaming mud,’ which I read for its layers. A thick, dark layer means a heavy season; a thin, pale one means drought was long. The mud remembers the years the notches on the trunk might forget.

The animals are my informants. The sleek, weasel-like predators—let’s call them **slip-pelts**—avoid certain root tangles after a downpour. Not because of the water, but because the saturated wood there grows a toxic fungus only visible in its blooming phase. The beetles with iridescent shells gather on the north side of trunks not for food, but for the specific mineral weep from the stone beneath. Their gathering tells me where the bog-iron deposits are richest this season.

My work is quiet amid the clamor. While others scrape bark or pound fiber, I sit for an hour by a troubled wall where the living wood is weeping too much, threatening to rot the dwelling inside. I listen. I place my palm flat against the damp bark, feeling for the rhythm of the internal flow. Is it a wound? A shifted root blocking a deep channel? Then, perhaps, I will make a tiny, careful incision with my iron knife—not to harvest, but to redirect, to guide the water’s path into a new channel I have prepared with a sliver of hollow bamboo. A small intervention, a conversation with the tree’s thirst.

I am an Anchor, though not for lineages of vines, but for lineages of moisture. I remember that the great flood of three notches ago began not with heavy rain, but with a week of thick, silent fog that soaked the canopy so deeply the roots simply could not hold more. I tell the Weavers that the lower shelters are not safe until the ‘drip-song’ from the canopy shifts from a patter to a steady hum. I advise the medicine-growers which of their planters will waterlog first, so they can raise their precious herbs.

My days are a tapestry of sensation: the chill of a dew-drop falling on my neck, the sound of a single water-strider insect skating across a pool no bigger than my hand, the sight of a column of fog-light illuminating a spiral of moths whose wings kick up mist as they pass. I chart the invisible rivers in the air.

Here, in this basin, I am a translator. I translate the language of saturation, the dialect of drip, the grammar of growth and rot, into knowledge my people can use to dance another step in the endless, wet, vibrant dance. I am not apart from the chaos; I am its cartographer, mapping the currents that everyone else swims in. And when I sleep, wrapped in my never-dry cloak, my mosses whisper their predictions into my dreams, and I listen, even then.
