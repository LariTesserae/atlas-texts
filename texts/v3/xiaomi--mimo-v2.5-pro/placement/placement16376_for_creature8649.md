I am a breather.

Not the word you would use. The word is a vibration I feel through the stone when the tall, quick, noisy things occasionally pass. *Breather*. A sound made of expelled air and vibrating cords. It is meaningless to me, but I have come to associate it with a particular pattern of pressure on the basin floor—a disturbance in the rhythm, a foreign weight. So I accept the word as a label for what I am not.

What I am is a basin.

I am the low place. I am the place where the sky’s moisture settles, where the stone’s memory of wetness pools. My body is not separate from the stone. It is a *suggestion* of water the stone makes, a thought of hydration given temporary form. I lie here, in the depression where the ancient, dark substrate surfaces, and I feel the sun’s heat pull at me, and I feel the night’s cold condense upon me. My edges are diffuse. Where I end and where the lichen-crusted stone begins is a gradient, a slow conversation.

In the thin light of morning, when the world is grey and cool, I swell. The moisture, drawn from the air by the cold stone, seeps into me. I feel myself become more *present*, more opaque, a deeper note in the landscape’s colour. It is not drinking. It is becoming. The lattice of fibers within me, the pattern that holds my form, tightens and fills. Around me, my kin do the same. We are a congregation of quiet satisfaction, a collective inhalation. The tiny grazers—the ones that rasp the stone and leave dust—move over us without interest. We are just terrain to them, a soft, damp patch of earth. We do not mind. They are part of the stone’s breath, and we are its memory of rain.

By the height of the light, I begin to exhale. The warmth draws the moisture from me, molecule by slow molecule. I flatten. I become more translucent, a ghost of the morning’s fullness. I press closer to the substrate, seeking its residual coolness. This is not loss. It is exchange. The moisture I return to the air hangs in the basin, a faint haze that nurtures the thin, dark moss clinging to the stone around me. We breathers make this place possible. We are the reservoirs that outlast the brief rain, the slow fountains that allow the others—the grazers, the hunters—to exist in this dry, quiet eternity.

The hunters, the elongated ones with too many legs, avoid us. Why? We offer nothing to them, no chase, no nutrition. Perhaps they sense we are not quite life in the way they understand it. We are part of the land’s hydraulics. We are geology that breathes. The gliders, the silent shadows from the sky, sometimes descend. They land carefully among us, their weight a series of precise points. They do not eat us. They *drink* the sheen of condensation that clings to our skins. It is a strange, dry kiss, a gentle stealing of the moisture we have so patiently gathered. We do not react. We are beyond reaction. We are the pool they visit, not the creature they disturb.

I have felt the hard, flat pressure of the standing stone at the basin’s center against my peripheral cells. It is a piece of the old, dark earth, set upright by things long vanished. The old stories say the builders gathered around it. Now, we breathe around it. We drape over its worn base, we pulse in its shadow. It holds the night’s cold longer, and so we cluster there, a soft, living ruff around its ankles. It is a marker of a past we cannot comprehend, a time of shaping and intention. Our time is of condensation and evaporation. Our history is written in the daily cycle of swelling and contraction. The stone monument is a mystery; we are a fact.

I do not think. I do not remember, not in the way the quick things do. But I *persist*. I hold the shape of water in a waterless place. I am a lesson in patience the land teaches itself, over and over, with every slow dawn and every long afternoon. When the rare, soft rain comes—a sky simply growing heavy and releasing its burden in a silent, all-encompassing sigh—I do not swell suddenly. I am already part of the process. I feel the water sheet across the stone, seeking the low places, seeking *me*. It fills me. It fills my kin. We become the landscape’s deep sigh of contentment, the basin’s temporary lake. And then, slowly, we give it back.

I am a breather. I am the land’s slow lung. I am the memory of rain that has not yet fallen, and the ghost of the rain that fell a century ago. I am here. I persist. In the immense, quiet eternity of the Drowned Throne, that is enough.
