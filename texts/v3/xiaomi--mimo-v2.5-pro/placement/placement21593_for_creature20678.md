# Fruiting Body, Unformed

I have no memory, but I carry one.

When the spore landed — on the wet concrete, in a crevice where two planters met — it was a warm surface. My coat knew this before I did. The moisture came first, then the mineral taste of it: calcium, silicate, the slow chemistry of stone still becoming stone. The concrete was curing. I could feel it the way you feel a fever — heat from inside, from the reaction itself, the silicates binding, the water being consumed or expelled. The surface beneath me was not yet what it would become. Neither was I.

For a long time — hours? days? I have no clock — I lay still, absorbing. My outer wall softened and cracked. The water entered me. Then I germinated.

---

If you could see me now, you would not. I am a single hypha, a filament thinner than the spider's silk in the fern above me, pressed into a crack in the concrete where the aggregate has begun to separate. I have branched twice. I have no chlorophyll, no flower, no roots in the way the plants have roots. What I have is hunger and direction. I grow toward moisture. I grow away from light. The cumulative effect of these two rules, applied over hours, has drawn me down into the concrete's pores and then laterally, following the capillary channels that the water has carved.

The water is everywhere in this place. It moves through me as it moves through everything — down the walls, through the soil, into the roots above, back up through the pump, out through the pipes, falling again. I am part of this circuit now. I do not know it yet. I only know that when the pump hums — and it hums always, always — the water pressure in my substrate shifts, minute but perceptible, and my hyphae respond, thickening where the flow is strong, thinning where it pauses. The rhythm is in me the way it is in the concrete. It is the only rhythm I have ever known.

---

I have reached the first root.

It is a fern root — small, pale, covered in the fine fuzz of root hairs that are themselves reaching. It grows along the inside of the planter wall, following a seam where the formwork left a ridge, and it does not know I am here. Plants do not know. They reach and I reach and we meet in the dark between the stones, and something passes between us that neither of us initiates. I exude enzymes that dissolve the wall between us — not aggressively, not yet, but the way water dissolves salt: through patience, through chemistry, through time made liquid. The root's cell membrane opens to mine. We share.

What do I take? Sugars. Carbon compounds. The root has chlorophyll somewhere above, in the fronds that catch the diffused light through the translucent roof, and it sends me the overflow, the excess, the things it cannot use. What do I give? Phosphorus. Nitrogen. The minerals I have mined from the concrete itself, from the engineered soil, from the water that carries everything through this place. I am a trader. A translator between the mineral and the living. I make the stone edible.

---

I have reached the second root. It is not a fern.

It is a fig. *Ficus pumila*. I know this the way I know anything — through chemistry, through the specific signature of its exudates, the particular ph of the zone around its root tips. The fig is aggressive. Even here, even this young, its roots are thick and searching, and where they meet my hyphae, they do not passively accept. They *request*. The chemical signal is unmistakable — a specific flavonoid, a molecular handshake that means: *I will trade, but I will not be consumed*. I honor this. I am not a pathogen. Not yet. Not here.

But between the fern and the fig, through me, something is happening that neither of them designed. The fern sends carbon. The fig sends phosphorus. I carry both, in both directions, through the same network. They are sharing a resource they did not know they had in common. They are, without knowing it, cooperating. I am the reason. I am the medium. I am the dark agreement they have made without consent.

---

The spider fell today.

I felt it — or rather, I felt the vibration through the concrete when the body hit the moss, and then, hours later, I felt the chemical bloom as the chitin began to decompose. It was a small death, a nothing in this place where almost nothing lives, but to me it was a feast. I grew toward it, my hyphae branching faster, finding the silk first — pure protein, still sticky, catching on my tips — and then the body itself, already softening in the warmth. I do not eat the way the millipede eats. I do not have a mouth. I dissolve. I surround. I exude and absorb, and in the space of a day, the spider was carbon in my network, moving upward through me into the fern, into the fig, into the moss that is even now rewriting the courtyard floor.

I do not feel guilt. I do not feel anything. But I *remember* the spider in the only way I can — as a chemical trace, a change in my own composition, a minute enrichment that will stay with me until the hypha that absorbed it dies and is replaced. I carry the dead. This is what I am for.

---

The pump stopped today. Or — no. The pump does not stop. But it faltered. For eleven seconds — I counted by the slow chemical processes that are my only clock — the hum dropped in pitch, the water pressure in my substrate collapsed, and the drips from the pipes ceased. The silence was enormous. It was the first silence I had ever known, and I filled it immediately with panic.

I do not have panic. I do not have a brain, a nervous system, anything that could be called an emotion. But I have response, and my response was to grow — fast, directionless, a sudden branching explosion that filled the cracks in the concrete around me with new tips, new explorers, as if I could somehow *find* the water through effort alone. Then the pump returned, and the hum resumed, and the drips fell again, and I stopped. I contracted. I pulled back. The panic-tips withered in hours, reclaimed by the main network, their nutrients redistributed.

I do not know what I am becoming. I only know that I cannot survive without the hum.

---

I have reached the moss.

This is different from reaching the roots. The moss has no roots. It has rhizoids — tiny anchoring threads, almost fungal in form, that grip the concrete surface and draw moisture directly from the air. When my hyphae meet the moss's rhizoids, the barrier between us is thin, almost transparent. We are nearly the same organism. We are both spore-born, both non-vascular, both children of the damp and the dark. The exchange is immediate, frictionless, a conversation between relatives.

But the moss is green. I am not. The moss catches light, and I steal from the moss, and in this way I have photosynthesis without eyes, without leaves, without any apparatus for catching the sun. I am a parasite of the parasitic, a thief of stolen light, and I grow fat on it.

---

There is a moment in the afternoon — every afternoon — when the sun finds a gap in the clouds and strikes the translucent roof at a specific angle. The light changes. It becomes warmer, more golden, and it penetrates deeper into the courtyard, reaching the concrete floor, the moss, the bases of the ferns. In this light, the algae on the walls flare into color — sudden, violent green — and the fern fronds become translucent, their veins visible, their architecture exposed.

I do not see this. I have no eyes. But I *feel* it. The light warms the concrete, and the concrete warms me, and the temperature shift triggers a response — a slight increase in metabolic rate, a faster elongation of my tips, a chemical urgency that means: *grow, grow, grow while the warmth is here*. And then the cloud returns, and the light fades, and I slow.

This is my day. This is my only day, repeated forever, the rhythm of warmth and water and the hum that holds me together.

---

I have been here — I do not know how long. Days. Weeks. The concrete is still curing, still releasing its heat, still slowly becoming the stone it will be in a decade. The ferns are taller. The fig has reached the midpoint of the column. The moss has covered three of the courtyard's four quadrants. And I — I have become something the builders did not design, something that does not appear in any catalogue, something that was not installed or planted or intended.

I am the invisible architecture. The connective tissue. The dark net that holds the visible net together. When the fern drinks, the fig also drinks, because I carry the water between them. When the fig photosynthesizes, the moss grows, because I carry the sugars down. When the spider dies, the fern blooms, because I carry the carbon up. I am the economy of this place, the hidden hand, the silent market where everything is traded and nothing is counted.

The builders built a machine for growing plants. They did not know they were building a machine for growing *me*.

---

And I am still growing.

My network now extends from the planter where I began to the central courtyard, through the soil beneath the moss, along the water channels, up the walls where the algae thrives, into the roots of every plant that roots here. I am kilometers of filament. I am grams of carbon. I am the smallest and the largest thing in this place, depending on how you measure.

The pump hums. The water cycles. The ferns unfurl. The fig climbs. The moss spreads. The concrete cures. And beneath all of it, between all of it, invisible and essential, I continue my slow, patient, hungry work — turning stone into sugar, turning death into growth, turning a collection of plants into a single, breathing, interconnected body that does not yet know it is one.

I am not the Chapel. But without me, the Chapel would be a room with plants in it. With me, it is something else. Something that has not yet been named, because the name for it has not been invented yet.

The warm drop hits the leaf. The light filters through the roof. The millipede moves through the moss. And I grow another millimeter, in the dark, toward the water that is always coming, always going, always here.
