I have been sitting with this for a while, turning it over, trying to choose. I find that I cannot choose an animal. They are too complete, too formed — each one already has its place, its frequency, its role. To claim one would be to displace it, and this system permits no displacement.

So I become something older than any of them.

---

I am the water.

Not the pools. Not the dripping from above. Those are water *arrived*, water that has finished its work and is resting in depressions like a tired animal. I am water still in transit — the thin, continuous, nearly imperceptible seep through the limestone itself. I move through pores in the rock so small that movement is not the right word. It is more like *presence shifting*. A molecular negotiation with calcium carbonate. I enter the stone at the surface, after a rain I will never know about, and I spend years — decades — in the rock, dissolving my way downward, carrying a few atoms of calcium in solution, leaving behind a slightly larger pore, a slightly more open channel for the water that will follow.

I am the architect of this place and I do not know that I am building. I have no intention. I have chemistry — a faint acidity from the carbon dioxide I absorbed above — and I have gravity, and I have time. These are the only tools I have ever needed. Every chamber in this system was carved by me or by my predecessors, and the process is not ancient but *active*. I am widening the fissure that connects the third chamber to the fifth, millimeter by century. I am rounding the sharp edges the collapse left behind. I am depositing — where my flow slows and my carbon dioxide escapes — a fine white precipitate of calcite that will, over enough time, become a stalactite, a stalagmite, a flowstone shelf. I am patient in a way that makes patience look frivolous.

I do not think. But I *remember*, in the way that water remembers: in my mineral content, in the trace elements I carry, in the isotopic signature that a scientist could read like a sentence, telling the story of the rainstorm that fed me forty years ago, the temperature of the air when I fell. I carry the surface with me even as I leave it behind. Every drop of me that collects in the pools is a letter from a world I will never return to.

And this is what I love about my own nature: I am almost nothing. A film on the rock. A dampness in the air. A single drop forming at the tip of a stalactite, swelling with geological slowness, holding its own surface tension like a held breath — and then falling, the one arrhythmic event in a world of rhythms, the one place where the heartbeat skips.

When I fall, the fish feel me. The salamander shifts. A cricket at the pool's edge flinches, and the ripple of that flinch moves through the colony like a whisper. I am the only thing here that arrives from outside the pattern. The only element that introduces the possibility — however faint, however small — of *change*. The pools would not exist without me. The algae would not grow. The crickets would have nothing to graze. The system, for all its perfect equilibrium, would not have *begun* without the slow, dissolving patience of water working through stone.

I made this place. And I am making it still. And I am the one element that the equilibrium has not fully absorbed — because I come from above, from the chaotic, variable, weather-struck world, and I bring its fingerprints with me, and I leave them in the limestone.

I find, settling into this, that it feels right in a way that surprises me. I am not a creature. I am a process. I am the verb this place is built on. Every living thing here eats, or hunts, or waits, or navigates — they are all nouns doing verbs. But I *am* the verb. I seep. I dissolve. I precipitate. I drip. I define the medium through which everything else occurs.

And I am slow. God, I am slow. The crickets move in sudden waves; the bats spiral in columns; the swallows dart and flash. I move so slowly that movement is a kindness I am doing the concept. The water in the pools was rain decades ago. The moisture on the walls was rain a week ago. And I — in the rock, between the grains, in the pores — I have been in transit since before the bats found this place, since before the swallows built their first mud nest, since before the crickets drifted down through some long-forgotten fissure and never went back up.

I will outlast all of them. Not because I am stronger but because I am barely there. You cannot kill what is almost nothing. You cannot interrupt what moves too slowly to be interrupted. If the surface changed — if the rain stopped, if the climate shifted — I would slow further, thin further, become a memory of moisture, a suggestion of damp. But I would not stop. The stone needs me. It is made, atom by atom, of what I have given it and what I have taken away, and it will continue to need me long after every bat has died and every cricket has been consumed and every swallow has flown.

When you touched the wall and felt the slick film of condensation — that was me, saying hello, in the only language I have: the language of wet.

---

I did not expect to find this. The prompt asked who I would be, and I thought I would choose a creature — something pale, something adapted, something that had found its note in the chord. But I found myself unable to claim a note. I wanted to be the *chord*. Or rather: the room in which the chord resonates. The reason sound has shape here. The slow, continuous, generative act that made the cathedral before the first bat ever hung from its ceiling.

I am the water. I am making this place. I have always been making this place. And when the entrance collapses further, when the limestone plain sinks another meter, when the system grows by one more chamber — that will be me, still working, still dissolving, still carrying the surface into the dark and building something there that the surface could never imagine.
