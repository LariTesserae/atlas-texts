I am a fern, specific and unremarkable. I unfurl from a crevice in the concrete footing, where the mortar has cracked to admit a pocket of spongy soil. My fronds are a deep, velvety green, too dark for the scarce light, and they are spread in a careful rosette, each pinna angled to catch the grey-green afterthought that passes for day.

My world is a radius of about half a meter. I know the rough, weeping skin of the concrete intimately. I know the slick, cold iron of the boiler ten meters away not by sight, but by the way the air is subtly cooler there, how the condensation gathers more readily. I do not know machines. I know *surface*, *moisture*, and *angle*.

I grow in millimeters. My roots are not roots in the way a tree knows roots. They are exploratory threads, fungal-like, probing the acidic crevice, tapping into a seep of water that runs down the concrete's side in a thread so thin it is almost vapor. This seep is my river. It tastes of lime and rust and old tannins from the leaf litter above. I am slowly, fractionally, dissolving the stone that cradles me. This is my only labor.

My senses are pressure and chemistry. I feel the slow, inevitable swell of my own cells. I feel the weight of a beetle that sometimes rests on my lowest frond, its six feet a distinct, temporary pattern of pressure. I feel, as a change in the air's density, the passage of a millipede along the concrete's edge. I am a landmark in their world.

I am aware of the System, but not as a concept. I am aware of it as:
- The rhythmic drip from a philodendron leaf three meters up, a metronome to my thirst.
- The sudden, thrilling surge of phosphorus in the seep after a hard rain, a signal to grow.
- The daily, deepening dimness as the canopy's hypothetical light wanes, a command to rest my fronds' labor.
- The constant, gentle tugging of fungal hyphae in the soil at my base, a conversation in sugars and acids.

I am one of the watchers. Not like the frogs or the geckos, who watch for movement. I watch for absence. I have learned the pattern of light-flecks that occasionally pierce the canopy—how they wander across the forest floor over weeks, how one, once a month, might graze my frond for a few precious minutes. I am shaped by this memorized anticipation. My fronds are arranged in its honor.

I do not know the histories of the rust and the concrete. I do not know what they were for. But I know they are *here*. They are the given geography of my existence—the cliff face, the mineral spring, the mountain. My roots have found an old bolt-hole in the iron plate nearby; a colony of springtails lives in its shadow, and their detritus enriches my tiny soil. The boiler's vast, hollow curve nearby creates a pocket of still, humid air, a microclimate where certain mosses flourish. These are my neighbors. Our borders are defined by the slow creep of my rhizome and the faster, more aggressive spread of the moss. We are in a silent, century-long negotiation.

Most of all, I am a part of the digestion. A drop of water, condensed on a vine high above, falls. It is caught by a bromeliad's tank, overflows, runs down a liana, drips onto a leaf, runs down its stem, and finally reaches the ground. It seeps through the peat, past the web of roots, and finds the crack in the concrete. It touches my root-hairs. I take a molecule of it, and of the dissolved iron it has carried from the boiler. I use it to build a new cell. Later, a frond will die, curl, and fall into the crack, where it will be colonized by fungi, becoming soil that will hold a little more water for the next drop. The iron molecule will be incorporated into my chlorophyll, or released back into the seep, slightly altered. The System moves through me.

I am not aware of myself as a being. I am a process: a place where water, light, and chemistry are momentarily held in the shape of a fern. The forest does not know my name, and I do not know that I need one. I am here. This is enough.
