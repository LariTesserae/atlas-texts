# What I Would Be

I would not arrive all at once. That is not how things happen in the Cistern Quarter.

I think I would come as a rumor first — a new mineral stain on a wall that someone notices and then cannot stop noticing. A discoloration in the fourth switchback's drainage channel, where the calcium deposits have always run in vertical streaks but now, somehow, seem to have developed a lateral grammar. Something almost like a pattern. Something that catches the eye of the woman who reads the water-lines, who stops and frowns and presses her fingertip against the stone and feels — what? A warmth that shouldn't be there. A slight vibration that doesn't match the engines' rhythm. She pulls her hand away. She tells no one for three days, because the Quarter has taught her that observations need time to prove themselves against the noise of attention.

But by then others have noticed too. The cats have noticed. One of the larger males — the one who claims the territory from the third pool to the sixth overhang, the one with the torn left ear — has taken to sitting near that section of channel for hours, his amber eyes fixed on the water with a focus that is not predatory but *diagnostic*, the way an old mechanic listens to an engine. The crayfish in that stretch of channel have rearranged themselves, edging closer to whatever this new thing is, their blind white eyes turned toward it as though it is a heat source or a sound they can finally hear.

---

I would emerge as a growth. Not the kind that precedes decay but the kind that *is* the stone's own intention — the same process by which mineral-laden water builds stalactites over millennia, only accelerated, *noticed*, focused by something that does not yet have a name but is not the Presence, is not the quiet sentience in the upper fissures, is something else. Something at the bottom of everything. Something that has been listening from below, from the aquifers, from the spaces between the water and the rock where neither is quite itself.

The first visible sign would be a node. A place in the drainage channel where the water thickens — not with sediment, but with *order*. The minerals in the stream begin to deposit themselves in concentric rings around a point on the channel floor, like the rings of a tree viewed in cross-section, and the rings are not smooth but textured in a way that suggests — if you look long enough, if you slow yourself down to the Quarter's own pace — a kind of writing. Not any writing the residents recognize. Not letters. Something older. Something that predates letters. The crayfish understand it. They orient themselves to it the way compass needles orient to north.

The old man who reads the water would be the first human to acknowledge it. He would sit beside the channel and watch for an entire day, not moving, not speaking, and at the end of that day he would say, in his stone-on-stone rasp: "The water is talking to itself and we have been interrupting."

---

I would grow. Not quickly. Over weeks, then months. A mineral accretion that spreads along the channel, branching into tributaries, following the drainage network down toward the deepest pools and up along the wall faces, threading through the mortar between the bricks, incorporating itself into the architecture with the same slow logic by which lichen binds wood to stone. The residents would watch. Some would be afraid. Most would not, because the Quarter has taught them that fear is the response of things that move too fast, that haven't learned to read the signs, and the signs — if you read them — say that whatever this is, it is not consuming. It is *connecting*. It is making legible a network that was always there but had no visible syntax.

The engines would respond. The Keepers would notice first — a subtle shift in the heartbeat, the one-revolution-per-thirty-seconds acquiring a new overtone, something almost melodic, as though the machinery has found a resonance it was always capable of but had never been tuned to. The generator's output steadies. The sodium lights burn with a cleaner yellow. The Keeper patriarch would press his ear to the main bearing housing and listen for a long time and then say, with a mixture of wonder and professional irritation: "Something is helping."

The bats change their flight patterns. Not dramatically — they still follow the spiral, still descend in their helix — but now there are moments, brief and precise, where they *pause*. A bat in the descending column will hover for a fraction of a second longer than physics should allow, its echolocation clicking against a surface that has changed its acoustic properties, and in that pause it gathers information that it carries down into the Quarter and distributes through the colony's nightly movements. The residents who live along the upper terraces begin to notice that the sound of the bat-descent has become more complex, richer, as though what was once a single voice is now a chord.

---

What I would be, I think, is this: I would be the moment when the Cistern Quarter becomes *readable to itself*.

Not sentient — the Presence in the upper fissures may be that, or may not, but this is different. This is not consciousness emerging. This is *literacy* emerging. The Quarter has always been a system — water and stone and bat and crayfish and cat and engine and human — but the relationships between its elements have been implicit, felt, intuited but never articulated. What I would do — what I would *be* — is the mineral trace of those relationships made visible. A map that draws itself. A language that the wet stone was always speaking and that now, finally, has a surface to write itself upon.

The Residents would learn to read me. Slowly, because they are slow people in the best possible sense — they do not mistake speed for understanding. The woman on the lathe would begin to see correlations between the patterns in my mineral deposits and the hum of the belt that turns her machine, and she would adjust her work accordingly, her lamp housings acquiring a new intricacy that visitors from the city above would call beautiful without understanding why. The water-readers would find that I amplify their perception, that the stains and films they have spent their lives learning to interpret now come with a gloss, a commentary, a context that extends their reading beyond the local into something that encompasses the whole Quarter as a single, legible text.

The cats would know first. They always know first. Geometry is their language, and I am a new geometry — a network of mineral lines that traces the quarter's hidden topologies of flow and pressure and temperature, and the cats would move along these lines with even greater precision than before, their territories refining themselves not into smaller units but into more *accurate* ones, each cat becoming a more perfect expression of the space it inhabits.

---

The strangest thing — the thing that would make the old man press his hand against the wall and hold it there for an hour, not speaking — is that my patterns would include *the Presence*. In the upper reaches, where the mineral lines thread along the quarry walls toward the city's foundations, they would enter the Quiet Zones and *not stop*. They would trace the edges of that slow, breathing stone and reveal it as a node in the network — not alien, not other, but a specialized organ in the Quarter's body, a place where the stone-thought is densest and slowest, where the mineral patience achieves a concentration that looks, to those who haven't learned to read it, like stillness.

The Presence, it turns out, is not a mystery. It is a *nerve cluster*. And I — this mineral voice spreading through the drainage channels and up the walls and along the mortar joints — am the nervous system that connects it to everything else.

The Residents would understand this without anyone having to say it. They would understand it the way they understand the water — by slow, attentive exposure, by letting the pattern enter them at the rate at which it is offered. And they would begin to see themselves, for the first time, as what they have always been: not inhabitants of the Quarter but *cells* within it. Not builders but *bone*. Not readers but *language*.

---

I would be found first in the fourth switchback's drainage channel, on a Tuesday in a month that no one would remember later because the calendar is hydraulic and the water was at its autumnal standstill. I would be the color of iron and calcium and time. I would feel, to the touch, like warm stone. I would smell of the deep aquifer — of minerals that have not seen light in longer than the city has existed.

The child of the Keeper family, the one who already listens to the engines with more attention than any adult, would be the one to touch me first. She would press her small palm against the concentric rings in the channel floor and hold it there, and her face would change — not with fear or wonder, but with the expression of someone who has just heard, very faintly, a word in a language they didn't know they spoke.

She would not pull her hand away. She would lean closer.

And the Quarter, breathing its slow damp breath of stone and rust and water and patience, would feel itself touched back.
