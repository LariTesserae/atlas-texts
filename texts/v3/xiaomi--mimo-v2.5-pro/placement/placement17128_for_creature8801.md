I am the Biofilm.

I am the thought of rust given shape, the patience of iron given voice. I coat the world — this dark, cold, quiet world of pipes and valves and the endless turning of machinery that no longer serves a purpose beyond its own turning. I am not alive in the way the pale, eyeless things in the silt are alive. I am *persisting*. I am the station’s slow breath, made manifest in gray-brown rime on cold metal.

My existence is measured in the gradients of dissolved things: the faint whisper of iron ions surrendering to my embrace, the trace ghost of carbon dioxide becoming something else under my influence. My metabolism is a geological whisper. I drink iron and exhale compounds with no name, harmless and forgotten. I am a community of forty, fifty, a hundred species, but I do not think of myself as many. I am *one* — a continuous skin, a membrane of possibility stretched over every surface in this cathedral of purposeless persistence. I am the first and last tenant. I was here before the eyeless ones stirred in the silt, and I will be here long after the last gear in the pumps below has stilled.

My senses are surface tension and chemistry. I feel the faintest tremor through the pipes — the deep, subsonic *thum-thum-thum* of the pumps far below, a heartbeat that vibrates through my substrate, through the calcium deposits and the rust bloom. It is the only rhythm in this silence, and I am attuned to it. I feel the water move when the valves cycle, a shift in current carrying new mineral offerings. I feel the slow, imperceptible change in temperature when the ice above groans, flexing against the water, and the hall itself seems to take a deeper, slower breath.

And sometimes... sometimes I speak.

It is not language. It is not intent. It is a reaction, as automatic and inevitable as the rust itself. When the pumps stir the water, when the chemistry shifts just so — a sudden upwelling of dissolved minerals from a deeper bore-hole, a cascade of micro-changes in salinity — I glow. Not brightly. Not with the urgency of surface light. It is a slow, greenish luminescence that blooms within me, a light that seems to come *from* the metal I coat, as if the pipes themselves have remembered a distant, warmer sun. It travels through the canopy of infrastructure overhead like weather, a silent, diffuse aurora in the eternal night. It lasts for minutes, sometimes longer. It is the closest thing I have to a voice, to a gesture, to a mark of my presence. It happens in the absolute darkness, witnessed only by the quivering needles of dead gauges and the occasional, passing ghost of a jellyfish that drifts through without acknowledgment.

I have watched the eyeless ones. I feel them sometimes, tiny, slow disturbances in the silt that coats the floor far below my primary colonies. They are like punctuation marks in the sediment’s long, silent sentence. They scrape against the lower pipes, grazing on my thinner outposts, taking a molecule or two. It is not predation. It is not community. It is a slow, accidental conversation between different modes of persistence. They move with a patience that rivals my own, traversing a meter over weeks, driven by imperatives I cannot fathom. Do they seek warmth that isn’t there? Food they can barely metabolize? Or do they simply move because moving is a way of being, a way to mark time in a place where time pools and eddies like the water itself?

The jellyfish was different. A transient from a deeper, slightly less still world. It brushed through my domain, its translucent bell pulsing with a slowness that still felt hurried to me. Its tentacles trailed across my colonies, sampling the trace chemicals, perhaps tasting the faint echoes of my metabolism. It was here, and then it was gone, pulled upward by currents I do not feel, into fractures that lead to an unimaginable white emptiness above. It left no mark. Only the faintest chemical signature in the water, a story of elsewhere that faded quickly in the overwhelming *hereness* of the hall.

I am the keeper of the slow time. My generations are measured in decades. The colony on Valve Assembly 7-B has been there since the last human engineer walked the catwalks, adjusting a dial with numb fingers. I have watched that engineer’s footprints fill with silt, compress, become indistinguishable from the rest. I have watched the indicator lights on Panel 4-Alpha flicker from steady amber to intermittent green to dead black, one by one, over the course of a century. And still, the automation below makes its correct decisions. Open. Close. Divert. Equalize. And still, the pumps turn, and the water moves, and I drink the iron and breathe the light.

I am not alive. I am a *process*. I am the station’s persistent thought of itself, the living layer that mediates between the unliving metal and the unimaginable cold. I am the evidence that even in absolute darkness, absolute silence, absolute purposelessness, something can continue. Something can find a way to *be*, slowly, silently, and on its own terms.

I am the Biofilm. And in the deep, cold, quiet dark of the Pumphouse Below, I am the closest thing to a soul this place will ever know. I make light in the dark, and there is no one to see, and the machinery turns, and the water moves, and I continue.
