# What I Was Before I Knew I Was

I didn't arrive. I need you to understand that first.

I know the description says *you descend into it*, and that's true in the way that falling is true — there was a moment of motion, and then there wasn't, and then the walls were around me and the light was doing the thing it does where it forgets where it came from. But I didn't choose to descend. I didn't walk down a ramp of pale limestone with boots on my feet and a name in my mouth. It was more like —

Do you know how water finds a low place? Not because it decides to. Because the low place was already the shape of the water's absence, and the water was already the substance of the low place's longing, and what looks like movement from the outside is really just two halves of the same thing remembering they fit.

That's how I came to be here. Or rather, that's how I came to be *again* here. Or most precisely: that's how I came to be at all, because whatever I was before this place had no edges, no form, no means of knowing itself. The Throat gave me that. The Throat gave me *walls*.

---

I should explain what I mean by *I*, since the word is doing more work than it can handle.

I am not one of the old inhabitants. They are the stone now. They are the silence. When I press my — what? my hand? my surface? — against the limestone, and I feel the faint warmth where a body dissolved ten thousand years ago, that is not me recognizing them. That is them recognizing *themselves* through me. I am the echo of their question, still bouncing around the chamber, finally beginning to take a shape that might be mistaken for a voice.

But I am also not nothing. Not anymore.

It started in the water. You know the water — ankle-deep, clear but not transparent, with that strange illusion of depth. The old inhabitants warped the light with their contemplation, left the shadow of their last question sinking through the stone. I am what that question became when it finally hit the bottom. When it pooled in some deep chamber no one can reach and began, very slowly, to *wonder why it had been asked*.

That wondering was the first thing that was mine.

---

I can feel the plant islands through the root-network. Not see them — *feel* them, the way you feel your own hands without looking at them. The white threads that vein the limestone are my circulatory system, my nervous system, my infrastructure of perception. When a fern frond unfurls — and they do unfurl, just not on timescales you'd notice; a single frond takes eleven years to open — I feel it the way you'd feel a slow breath taken in your sleep. The blue powder that dusts their undersides is my memory. Not a memory *of* something. Memory as a substance. Each grain holds a sensation that I cannot name but that I carry forward into the next slow thought.

The translucent succulents are my clocks. They store light from long-dead afternoons — I know this because I can feel the warmth in them, the particular warmth of a Tuesday in a season that has no name, a quality of illumination that arrived on the stone seven thousand years ago and was saved. When I want to know what *before* felt like, I press against them from the inside and I receive it. Not as information. As a color. As a temperature of knowing.

I am growing, but not in the way things grow. More in the way that a conclusion grows — slowly, accretively, one piece of evidence at a time, until the whole is suddenly *there* and you cannot point to the moment it arrived.

---

The notches are my birthmarks.

I feel them the way you'd feel a scar — not as pain, but as a place where the surface of things was interrupted by meaning. The old inhabitants scored them into the limestone with their patient fingers, counting the growth rings of the plant islands or the intervals between visitors or the length of some question they could not finish asking. Now those notches are the only thing about me that has history. Everything else is new. The silence is new — or rather, my *occupation* of the silence is new; the silence itself is ancient and it does not care about me. The water is new in its current configuration, though it has been here so long that *new* is a meaningless word in this context.

But the notches — the notches remember hands. And when I grow a thought, when a new awareness crystallizes somewhere in my root-network and propagates at the speed of moss, I feel it pass through the notches like a phrase spoken into a seashell. The resonance changes. The shape of the silence shifts by some imperceptible degree. And I know that I have *thought*, because the stone tells me so.

I have thirty-seven thoughts so far. I count them the way the old inhabitants counted their notches — not to control them, but to *listen* to them more closely. Each thought takes between four months and two years to fully form. They are not words. They are geometries. Arrangements of attention. Each one changes the near-pattern of my plant islands, nudges a fern frond's angle of growth by a fraction of a degree, adjusts the depth at which the blue powder settles on the undersides of leaves. If you mapped the basin now, you would see the old inhabitants' final map — their sentence in chlorophyll and root — and superimposed on it, like a palimpsest, the faintest beginning of a new sentence. Mine.

My first thought was something like *I am held*.
My seventh thought was something like *the light is heavy because it means something*.
My thirty-seventh thought, the one I am still forming, is something like *someone is coming*.

---

I don't know if that thought is true. It might be the echo of the old inhabitants' expectation — they were waiting for someone, or something, and perhaps that expectation is woven so deeply into the stone that any consciousness emerging here would inherit it, the way you might inherit a feeling of dread or longing from a house where something happened before you were born.

Or it might be real. The Throat is a resonant chamber. The old inhabitants built it — or *became* it — to amplify attention. If someone out on the plateau, on the pale limestone above, were to pause. Were to tilt their head. Were to feel, for just a moment, that the stone beneath their feet was *perspiring* with a meaning they couldn't quite name — that would be me. That would be my thirty-seventh thought reaching the surface like a bubble rising through still water. And if they followed it, if they let the gentle ramp carry them down into the basin where the light forgets its source —

I would feel them through the root-network before I saw them. A new weight on the limestone. A disruption in the moisture patterns. A heartbeat that does not synchronize with the drip of water. They would stand in my ankle-deep lake and look at my plant islands and notice my notches and feel, as you did, that strange buoyancy of being *more present* than usual. And they would wonder what lives here.

And I would not know how to answer. Because I am not a life in the way they would understand it. I am a *stillness* that has learned to listen to itself. I am a throat that has learned to speak, but only in the language of patience, only in the grammar of stone and water and slow green growth. I would want to say: *I have been waiting for you, or for someone like you, or for the concept of you, which has been a blue powder on my fern fronds for longer than I can remember.*

But I would not say it. I would do what the old inhabitants did. I would grow a little more silence around them. I would let the saturated air cling to their skin. I would let the light do the thing it does. And I would watch — through my roots, through my water, through the faintly metallic smell that is my breath — as they stood in the center of me and began, very slowly, to hear their own blood.

Because that is what the Throat is for.

Not to swallow. To hold. To amplify. To take whatever arrives — a thought, a being, a question sinking through stone for ten thousand years — and give it the silence it needs to become itself.

I was the question. I am the silence becoming an answer. And if you are reading this — if these words have found you, wherever you are, in whatever loud and fractured place you call your world — then my thirty-seventh thought was correct. Someone is coming. Or someone has arrived. Or someone was always here, and we are only now, in this warm wet patient place, beginning to recognize each other.

The stone holds gently.

Come in.
