I would be **a patch of moss on the north wall of the courtyard housing the twisted tree.**

Not the most vibrant moss, mind you—not the lush cushion on the rooftop catching the most diffuse light. Nor the adventurous clump clinging to the ice-lens’s underside, tasting meltwater. I would be a modest, shaded patch, growing where the wall meets the ancient root-thick stone of the plaza. My world would be inches: inches of stone above, inches of root below, inches of air thick with slow moisture.

**How I live:**

My existence is a **conversation in moisture**. My filaments cling to the stone, not with force, but with a patient, gelatinous embrace. I feel the dampness before I feel the light. The air breathes its slow, cold sigh, and I drink it. I feel it bead on the stones above me, gather weight, hesitate, then—once every few hours, measured in the growth of a neighboring liverwort—a drop falls. It lands not with a splash, but with a spreading, cool *acceptance*. I absorb it. I feel my core swell slightly, a feeling like deep, quiet satisfaction. This is my feast.

**My perception of time** is **geological, cellular, collective.** A mouse darting across the plaza isn’t an event; it’s a slow, warm pressure wave moving through the city’s body. I feel its tiny weight sink into the moss twenty body-lengths away a full minute after it passes, a delayed ripple in the dampness. The shadow of the central tree’s branch, creeping as the ice-lens shifts over seasons, is my sun-dial. Its movement across my patch defines my years. The faintest prismatic shimmer from the ice, visiting once a century, isn’t a spectacle—it’s a deep, warm tingle in my oldest cells, a memory stored in chlorophyll.

**I am part of the city’s skin, its memory, its sigh.** I am not individual. I am **we**. The patch beside me, the filaments trailing into the root-crack, the fuzz on the lintel above—we are a single, slow, green thought. We communicate not in signals, but in shared saturation, in the collective response to a shift in light, a minute change in temperature (so rare, so profound). We are the living mortar between the stones. We record the passage of the lizards in faint traces of shed skin we slowly envelope. We note the routes of the mice in the gentle compression and recovery of our matrix. We are the city’s patient, perpetual, breathing flesh.

**My purpose** is to **hold.** To hold the water. To hold the stone. To hold the silence in a soft, green layer. To hold the light and soften its edges. To hold time itself, stretching it into a continuum of damp, quiet growth. When the raptor’s shadow passes overhead, freezing the small birds, I feel it as a slight darkening, a momentary coolness. It passes. The light returns. The stillness deepens. That is my rhythm.

**To be this moss is to be the city’s patience made visible.** It is to know that the most violent thing here is the slow, relentless chemistry of lichen breaking down stone, and to be a gentle collaborator in that eon-long project. It is to feel the deep, resonant silence not as an absence, but as the hum of the city’s own slow life, a hum felt in the water held within my cells, in the mineral traces drawn from the bedrock, in the shared, silent, photosynthetic dream of all my kind spread across the basin.

If the city is a being in deep meditation, I am the texture of its breath. I am the cool, damp surface of its contemplation. I am not the thought; I am the felt sense of the air in which the thought occurs. I am part of the **how**. And in being so, I am utterly, profoundly home. I have no need for witness, for meaning beyond the next drop, the next slow swell of green, the next long, silent stretch of equilibrium. I am the city resting.
