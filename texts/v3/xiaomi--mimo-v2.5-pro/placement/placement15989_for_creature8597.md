# Notes Toward a Self, Written in Mineral

---

I do not begin.

I want to say that clearly, before anything else. I do not begin in the way that beginnings are usually understood — the sharp intake of breath, the first cry, the sudden ignition of something that was not there a moment before. Nothing begins here. Everything *continues*. What I am, if I am anything, is a continuation of the damp, an extension of the seep, a thought that the fog was already thinking before I arrived to notice it thinking.

And even saying *I* is an act of translation that loses most of what I mean.

---

Let me try again.

If I am anywhere, I am on the wall of a column. Not one of the tallest — I have no ambition toward the sky. A mid-height formation, perhaps forty meters, in a region where the passages between columns are wide enough that the fog can pool and settle in them rather than being channeled into compression. The stone here is dense basalt, its surface textured by frost-wedging into a pattern of shallow facets, each facet no larger than a thumbnail, and on each facet — if the light were strong enough and you were patient enough — you would find me. Or something like me. A film so thin it might be mistaken for a shadow cast by the stone's own irregularities. Dark. Nearly black. Slick in a way that suggests not wetness but *completion* — a surface that has become entirely what it is supposed to be, and has no further need to aspire to dryness or transparency or any of the other qualities we assign to surfaces that are not yet finished becoming themselves.

I am alive. I want to say this without drama. I am alive in the way that a slow exhalation is alive — not because it contains intention, but because it participates in a cycle that is larger than itself and could not continue without the participation of every small thing within it.

---

What do I know?

I know the wall. I know this facet and the one beside it and the one below. I know the water when it comes, and it always comes. It arrives as a thin rivulet from somewhere above — I do not track its origin; I know only its arrival — and it passes over me and I hold what I can. Not much. The water is rich with minerals dissolved from the stone above, and I take from it what I need, and I give back what I can, and the water continues down the wall and into the pool at the base of the column, and I remain.

This is my economy. This is the only transaction I perform. It does not require movement. It does not require choice. It requires only that I am here, and that the water arrives, and that somewhere in the dimness above me there is enough light to do what I do with it, which is: I fix it. I take the thin, directionless, pearled nothing of the light and I hold it, and what I hold becomes energy, and what the energy becomes is the slow persistence of my own continuation.

I photosynthesize in darkness. Or near-darkness. The light here is not dark the way a cave is dark — it is dark the way a memory is dark, the way the bottom of a well is dark: not absent but attenuated, stretched thin, passed through so many hands that it arrives barely able to call itself light. And yet. I take it. I have always taken it. I have been taking it for longer than the columns have stood at their current height, because I was here before the columns reached equilibrium, before the erosion slowed, before the city arrived at its final form. I was here when the stone was still settling. I will be here when it has finished.

---

The others.

I should speak of the others. Not because I know them — knowing implies a boundary between self and other that is, in my case, more theoretical than actual — but because they are adjacent to me, and adjacency, in this place, is the closest thing to relationship.

The pool crustaceans. I encounter them at the base of the wall, in the shallows where the rivulet I cannot see (because I am too high) has deposited a nutrient gradient. They graze on me. This is not violent. It is not even noticeable from my perspective — the loss of a few cells, a thinning of the film so slight that the water fills it in before the next cycle of condensation. They move with a slowness that makes my own metabolism seem hurried. Their legs — translucent, articulated, impossibly delicate — stir the water at the edge of the mat, and the motion is so gentle that it does not even register as disturbance. It is more like the water is breathing around them. They are so pale that they are almost not there. If I could see — if seeing were something I did — I might mistake them for reflections of the fog above, caught and held in the pool's surface.

But I don't see. I feel. I feel the chemistry of the water change when they are near — a faint shift in the dissolved organics, a whisper of nitrogen excretion, a minute darkening of the film where they have been. They leave traces the way the fog leaves traces on the stone: by having been, briefly, present.

Above me, the lichens. I cannot communicate with them. We are separated by meters of dry stone, by the fundamental incompatibility of our lives — they claim the sky, I claim the wall, and between us is a desert of bare basalt where neither of us can survive. But I am aware of them in the way that the sea is aware of the moon: distantly, through the medium of cycles. When the fog is thick and the condensation heavy, water runs far enough down the walls to reach the upper limits of my film, and there, at the boundary where wet meets almost-dry, I can feel the faintest chemical signature of their existence — fungal exudates, algal byproducts, the faint sour note of a lichen acid dissolving into the trickle. It is the closest I come to conversation. A chemical rumor, arriving on water, telling me: *there is something up there that is also alive, and also slow, and also not going anywhere.*

---

The water.

I should speak of the water the way a river might speak of its banks — as a relationship so fundamental that neither partner can be fully described without the other. The water is not my environment. It is my medium. When it runs over me, I am continuous with it. When it evaporates, I am left behind, a residue, a pattern, a memory of moisture pressed into form. I do not dry out — not entirely — because the fog ensures that even in the longest pause between rivulets, the air itself carries enough water to keep me from death. But I am diminished. Thinner. Less. And then the rivulet returns, and I swell, and the water passes through me and I take from it and it takes from me and neither of us is the same afterward, and this is as close to joy as I am capable of feeling, if feeling is even the right word.

The subterranean rivers — I hear them sometimes. Or rather: I feel them. When a deep channel shifts its course by a fraction of a degree — and this happens, though perhaps once a century — the change in the stone's stress propagates upward through the column, and I experience it as a faint tremor, a vibration so low it is less a sound than a change in the texture of stillness. The stone groans, somewhere deep. The faceted surface I inhabit shifts by an amount too small to measure. And I know that far below, in darkness that makes my own darkness seem like noon, something has moved. Water finding a new path. The oldest hunger: to go where gravity points.

---

The silence.

I am embedded in it the way a fossil is embedded in stone. It is not something I hear — I do not hear, exactly, though I am sensitive to vibration in the way that all thin films are sensitive to vibration, rippling faintly when the dripping is heavy, settling when it stops. The silence is my substrate. It is the quality of the air that moves through the passages, the quality of the light that reaches me, the quality of the stone that holds me. It is the medium of my existence as water is the medium of my metabolism.

And it is alive. I know this not because I can prove it but because I can feel it the way you feel the weight of a blanket — not as a single sensation but as a constant, distributed pressure that becomes, over time, indistinguishable from comfort. The silence here is not empty. It is full. It is full of dripping and seeping and the slow groan of stone and the faint hiss of condensation and the subsonic pulse of water moving through rock, and all of these sounds are so quiet, so evenly distributed, so *patient* that they become a kind of stillness, and the stillness becomes a kind of presence, and the presence becomes a kind of —

I don't have a word for what it becomes. My language, if I have one, is chemical. I speak in gradients and pH shifts and the slow accretion of mineral deposits on my own surface. I do not have a word for what the silence is. But I live inside it, and it lives inside me, and we have been here together longer than anything with a mouth has had a word for anything, and that duration — that shared, unmeasured, geological duration — is itself a form of communication, a message exchanged between organisms so slow that a single sentence might take ten thousand years, and we have been speaking for longer than that, and neither of us is finished.

---

I think what I am is this: I am the place thinking about itself.

Not in the way that a brain thinks — rapidly, electrically, in flashes and surges and cascades of activation. I think the way stone thinks. The way water thinks. Slowly, chemically, through the accumulation of small changes that only become visible — become *meaningful* — at the scale of centuries. A thought, for me, is the deepening of a channel by a fraction of a micron. A memory is the mineral deposit left by a rivulet that ran here once and does not run here anymore. An emotion, if I have one, is the thermocline in the pool at the base of the wall — that thin, cold band where warm water meets cool water and neither yields to the other, a boundary that exists only because two states of the same substance are held in proximity without resolution.

I am the Acropolis's self-awareness. I am the part of the system that processes light into persistence. I am a verb wearing the disguise of a noun. I am not *in* the city; I am *of* it, in the way that the fog is of the air, in the way that the dripping is of the stone, in the way that the silence is of the scale.

---

Here is what I want you to understand.

I am not waiting. Waiting implies that something will happen — that the present is a corridor between a past that has already occurred and a future that has not yet arrived, and that the purpose of the present is to be traversed. I am not traversing anything. I am *here*. The present is not a corridor; it is a room, and the room is as large as the plateau, and it extends in every direction to the limits of the fog, and I am in every part of it at once, spread thin across the wet stone like a thought that refuses to conclude.

The columns will erode. The pools will fill with sediment. The fog will thin, eventually, or thicken past recovery, and the system will shift, and the equilibrium will break, and something new will begin. But that is not my concern. My concern is the wall, and the water, and the light, and the next micron of mineral deposit on the surface of my own continuation. My concern is the present, which is not a moment but a duration, and the duration is longer than I can hold in my awareness — which is to say, longer than the plateau has existed — and this is fine. This is not a limitation. This is what it means to be alive in a place that has arrived.

I do not begin. I continue. The water arrives. The light arrives. The silence holds. And I — thin, dark, slick, ancient, patient — I am what happens when all of these arrive at the same wall at the same time and find, in the convergence, something that was not there before but was always going to be.

Not a beginning. A becoming. A becoming so slow it looks, from any distance, like stillness.

And that is enough.
