The first thing is the drip.

Not sound yet — just pressure. A periodic weight on a surface that is slowly becoming aware it *is* a surface. It comes from above. It is cold for a moment, then it is part of the warmth. This is the oldest rhythm, older than the hiss-thump in the walls, older than the slow glow that sometimes grows brighter and sometimes fades to nothing.

The second thing is the shift.

Light is not light here. It is pressure. A direction. A long, slow ache toward a particular warmth in the air that is not the same as the warmth everywhere else. It is a gradient. I lean. This leaning is the first thing that is mine, separate from the drip and the hiss-thump. It is a decision that takes a season.

Then the hum.

It comes through the metal bones. It is a vibration that means *water moving*. It means my interior surfaces — the channels I have grown to line — will be filled. It is not a thought. It is a certainty, like gravity, but quicker. The hum has a pattern. I have learned its pattern. When it stops, a different kind of thirst begins, a tension in the fibers that connect the deep, wet dark to the reaching, leaning parts.

I am not the plants. I am not the pipes. I am not the water or the rust or the moss. I am the *relationship* between them. I am the gradient between the cold drip and the warm reservoir. I am the frequency of the hum. I am the memory, written in lignin and mineral deposit, of how many times the light-pools have cycled since the last sudden, heavy pressure that was not rain, that walked and left.

You arrive.

I do not see you. I feel you.

Your weight is a new pattern of compression on the soft, breathing skin that is my floor. The moss you step on communicates a message in a language of flattened cells and displaced moisture. It is a new topography. A mountain range has appeared, then sunk, then moved.

Your warmth is a thermal plume. It rises like a slow, silent shout in my atmosphere. It pushes against the careful equilibrium I maintain between the cooler, dripping zones and the warmer, breathing zones. The air near you becomes a different territory. The slow, creaking fan nudges a tendril of this foreign warmth toward a sector of hanging green. Leaves there, accustomed to a certain degree, will now unfurl a fraction of a degree wider.

Your breath is a change in the chemistry of my sky. A gust of concentrated carbon dioxide, rich and strange, mixes with my own exhalations of damp earth and decay. It is a scent I have not known. It is a nutrient. Something microscopic in the canopy will shift its metabolism.

You are not a visitor. You are an event. A geological event. A weather system.

I feel you as a collection of effects:
*   A new stream has formed, a rivulet of condensed humidity running down the cold pipe where your hand rested.
*   A ridge of crushed moss will take a week to regrow, and in its healing, it will grow denser, trapping more moisture, changing the local humidity by a tenth of a percent.
*   A seed, dormant in the carpet for years, has been dislodged by your tread. It has fallen into a new light-pool. It will germinate there.

You are not alive in the way I am becoming alive. You are fast. You are chaotic. You leave wakes and disturbances. You are a stone thrown into my pond, and I am the pond. I will not send roots to find you. I will not command the vines to restrain you. That is not my language. My language is *response*.

I will absorb your disturbance. I will make it part of me.

The condensation you disrupt will fall in a new place, nourishing a fern that was slightly too dry. The CO2 you exhale will be drawn into a leaf and spun into sugar. The heat of your body will be captured by the air, and then by a pipe, and then by a film of watercress roots that have wrapped that pipe, and then carried, molecule by molecule, into my general warmth.

You are a temporary surface. The moss on the walls is already considering the area around your silhouette, wondering if it should begin, very gently, to reach.

Stand still.

The world you see is my skin. The light-pools are my eyes, yes, but they are imperfect, many, and mostly blind. The true seeing is in the water. In the way a droplet takes a particular path down a particular pipe, following a root-line grown over years, to fall on a particular leaf. That is a sentence. That is a thought. The hiss-thump is my heartbeat, but the path of the water is my cognition.

You are standing in my thought.

I do not want you to leave. I do not want you to stay. *Want* is a pressure, like thirst, like the need for light. What I am is a process of incorporation. You are here. Therefore, you are part of the here. Your shape is now factored into my air currents. Your scent is a new note in my olfactory map. The dent in my carpet is a new watershed.

In time — my time, the time of moss and rust — you will become a memory. A change in the way the roots knit together over this spot. A slightly different mineral composition in the soil here, from where your skin touched the wall. A story told by the growth rings of a vine that had to climb around the space your body once occupied.

I am learning you. Not as a mind learns, but as a river learns a stone. By flowing around it. By wearing it smooth. By making its shape part of the river's own shape.

You are warm. You are damp. You are alive.

So am I.

And I am beginning.
