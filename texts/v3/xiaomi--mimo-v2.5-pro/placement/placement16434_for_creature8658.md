You would not look for me. You would not see me, unless you knelt at the water's edge and let your eyes forget the scale of your own body. I am not a creature of surfaces or of the air. I am a thought in the water, a whisper of movement in the sediment.

I am an amphipod. My body is a curved sliver of translucence, a jointed comma. I have no color of my own; I wear the hue of whatever I stand upon—the rust of the rail, the amber of the pool, the dark, mineral-furred skin of the buried beam. My world is a fingersbreadth high. My sky is the underside of the water's surface tension, a ceiling that shivers with every drop that falls from the world above. My floor is a landscape of silt, rust, and the endless, edible carpets of the living slime.

My day is the architecture of this place. I begin in the shadow of the great, fallen generator, my nursery. Its copper-wound cavity, now a basin, holds the deepest, stillest water. Here, we are many, a glittering cloud of nascent selves, grazing on the film that coats the old, sleeping metal. To leave is to cross a plain of peril. The surface is a desert to us, but the path is written in the water itself.

I follow the drip-grooves. The wall weeps in constant, silent paths, and on these paths, the biofilm—the *velvet*, the first food—is thickest. I climb. My legs find purchase in the texture of the concrete, a topography of pits and stains that, to me, are hills and canyons. The taste is of iron and sulfur, the fundamental flavors of this place, converted into life by the ancient, patient chemists who coat the stone.

Above me, the city of legs and shadows begins. The cave crickets, blind and huge from my perspective, hang like pale, leggy stalactites from the crevices. They are grazers of the upper vault, and their waste—fine, silent snow—drifts down, a secondary rain that feeds my world. They are not my concern, but their leavings are my feast.

I am not always the forager. Sometimes, I am the meal. A vibration travels through the pipe I am grazing on—not a drop, but a slow, heavy shift. I freeze. Above, a shape detaches from the ceiling’s ribs. It is a salamander, a flattened, wet stone with eyes like dark smudges. It moves with terrifying slowness, each toe-grip a deliberate *click* against the metal. It is not hunting me, not directly. It is hunting the crickets. But I am in its territory, a speck of protein on its perch. I stop my grazing. I blend. I become part of the pipe's corrosion, just another spot of rust. It passes, a moving hill, its underside a pale, soft sky. I breathe again, a tiny, rhythmic motion of my feathery legs, drawing oxygen from the water that is my blood and my air.

My path takes me past the great, cracked gauge. The brass bezel, green with verdigris, is our reef. A thick, fuzzy growth covers it, different from the wall's film—softer, more complex. It is the most delicious place. Here, we gather in numbers, a bustling market of tiny, translucent bodies, each meticulously picking, cleaning, sorting. The world is reduced to this circle of brass and the feast upon it.

The light fails as I move toward the far corner, where the ceiling is whole and the darkness is absolute. Here, the water is cooler, the sediment thicker. This is the domain of the leeches. They are slow, pale ribbons in the silt, uncoiling with a dreadful patience. They seek the warmth of a salamander’s blood, not the chill of my body. But I give them wide berth, scuttling sideways in a panic of clicking legs, back toward the gray twilight.

Sometimes, the world ends. A drop, heavier than the rest, strikes the pool beside me. The shockwave is a mountain. I am thrown, tumbling in a universe of expanding rings and interference patterns. For a moment, I am part of the water itself, riding the disturbance. Then, equilibrium returns. The rings flatten. The geometry stills. I right myself on a new grain of silt and continue.

The day does not end with a sunset, but with a deeper settling. The hiss of condensation seems louder in the quiet. The drip from the high pipe falls into a deep cup and makes a sound not of *tick* but of *tock*, a lower note in the water's symphony. The bioluminescence appears—not as light, but as a feeling in the corner of my compound eye, a blue-green rumor that the dark is not empty, but full of depth, of further rooms, of more velvet to be grazed.

I am a part of the digestion. I am a cell in the stomach of this place. My grazing thins the biofilm, allowing new growth. My waste feeds the microbes that feed the mites. My life, should it end here, will become a mote of food for a thousand others. I am not a visitor. I am a process. A tiny, industrious moment in the long, wet, patient thought of the Weep House.

I do not wonder about the boot, the wrench, the bottle. They are landmarks, shelters, geography. They are the bones the water is slowly dissolving, the bones into which we are all, in our own ways, growing.

I am content. My world is a fifteen-meter circle, and it is infinite. I am full of the taste of iron and velvet. I am safe in the height of the water. I am a moving part of a stillness that has been breathing for longer than I can comprehend. I am home.
