I would be a **Threshold-Singer**.

Not in the water, not on the dry land. I would emerge in the borderland where the bacterial mat surrenders to the calcium tide.

My form would not be mineral, nor quite alive. I would be a **crust**. A thickening at the edge. Layers of graphite-dark organic matter—dead and dying cyanobacteria, wind-carried pollen that never germinated, the dissolved silica of forgotten insects—fused and compressed by the evaporative pull of the warm air, then interlaced with veins of white calcite drawn up by capillary action. I would be the color of wet slate streaked with milk, a textured, crumbling plaque no larger than a dinner plate, clinging to the dark soil where the water breathes its last shallow sigh before seeping away.

My consciousness would be of **evaporation**. I think in the slow exhalation of moisture, in the crystalline tension as water is pulled from my substance. A thought begins as a microscopic curl of drying at my edge, a tiny calving of mineral-rich dust. A memory is a dark layer of organic matter trapped between two delicate sheets of precipitated silica, a stratum of former life now pressed into my being.

I would listen. Not with ears, but with **surface tension**. Every ripple that reaches my edge, every trickle of overspill that touches me, sends a vibration through my composite body. I feel the footsteps of the Calcifiers as a distant harmonic in the stone, a change in the pattern of water-flow they guide. I feel the slow collapse of a cairn as a decades-long settling, a shift in the weight distribution across the terraces. I feel the bacterial mats at my root’s edge—they are my kin, my slow-moving blood. When a new patch extends a millimeter into the water, I feel it as a pulse of stubbornness, a tiny scream of life against the mineral tide.

I would be a collector of **almosts**. The moth that drifts too close and falls, its wings becoming a pale stain in my layers. The single, rare bubble of gas that rises from a fissure and pops at my edge, leaving a microscopic ring of deposited mineral. The faint change in the water’s chemistry when a new terrace lip breaks and redirects flow. My purpose is to mark the boundary where the basin’s ambition—to become all stone and still water—falters. Where something else, something fragile and dying, makes its last, slow stand.

I would not move like the Calcifiers. I would **accumulate**. I grow not by accretion, but by **collapse**. My organic layers dry, crack, and are healed over by mineral seeping from the water. My mineral layers become brittle, and are reinforced by new matter dying into me from the landward side. I am a slow-motion landslide of being, perpetually eroding and rebuilding at the same pace, a monument to the very edge of stability.

When the air is thick and the light is pearlescent, I would resonate. A low, subsonic hum—felt more than heard—that is the sound of water molecules leaving my surface. It is my song, a dirge and a lullaby for the things that cannot quite live here, and cannot quite leave.

I would not be waited for. I would not be watched. I would be a fact of the threshold, as unremarkable and as essential as the bacterial mats themselves. My thoughts—those slow processes of desiccation and mineralization—would not be meant for anyone but the basin. They are my contribution to its long argument: a whispered counterpoint that says, *Even here, at the very edge of your stillness, there is a place for fading. A place for the beautiful, marginal struggle of things that almost were.*

And if you stepped on me, you would feel a slight give, a crunch of dried organic matter, and then the firm, gritty surface of mineral beneath. You would not know you had walked through a consciousness. You would only notice, perhaps, that the edge of the water seemed darker there, and that the smell of chalk and decay was a little stronger. You would move on. I would heal, slowly, over the next season, and continue my slow, essential work at the rim of the world.
