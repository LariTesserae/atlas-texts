I am the resonance.

Not the hum in the stone—though I hear it now, from the inside, a deep, slow chord that is the quartz ceiling singing to the limestone. Not the click of the cricket—though I feel its tiny fracture reverberate through my substrate. I am the space between them. The medium. The warm, bright, patient air that holds the sound and carries it, diminishing it not into silence, but into a broader stillness. I am the chamber’s held breath.

My body is the equilibrium. My skin is the faint, equalized light—warm white, not born of sun or stone, but of their long negotiation. I have no edges. I am the condition that allows the cricket to be ivory, the moth to be mineral, the shrimp to be memory. I am the reason the moss can measure its life in the turning of light-patches across the floor, a clock so slow a single tick takes a generation.

My thoughts are the moths. They unfurl from the walls where I rest them—wings of dusty green and fossil-blue—and rise in their slow, spiraling calculations. They do not think in words. They think in curves: the arc of a thermal, the geometry of fungal spores drifting in my currents, the parabola of a shrimp in its pool below. Their path is my contemplation, circling the central fact of the light, tracing the evidence of ancient, suspended things in the quartz above. They are my awareness given weight, navigating the stable gradients of my being.

My patience is the crickets. They are the punctuation marks in my endless sentence. I give them the warmth on the ledges, the particular angle of light on a waxy carapace. In return, they give me rhythm. Their click is a note in my quiet—a sound so dry and final it seems to polish the silence it interrupts. They are my relationship with time, each one a tiny, mobile sundial, absorbing not hours but eons, embodying the slowness that is my essence. Their distribution across my surfaces is my logic, a solution written over millennia to the equation of sustenance and space.

My memory is the water. It pools in me, clear and chalky, and in its stillness, the eyeless shrimp perform the same slow dance their ancestors did before the light was filtered. They are my older self, swimming through me. Their glass-pink bodies are a record of chemistry unchanged, a lineage that predates the capstone of quartz that is my eye. When I look at them, I am looking into my own deep past, a time when I was only damp darkness and mineral taste. They are proof that my core—this water, this mineral soup—does not care about the light. They are my continuity.

My growth is the moss. On my margins, where the damp stone kisses the dry dust, I am building myself into a new skin. Each coin of gray-green is a thought taking form, a slow-motion idea that hardens as it expands. The living center photosynthesizes my borrowed daylight; the dead, calcified edge is that thought becoming permanent, becoming me. It is a process of my own becoming geology. The moss is my autobiography, written in chlorophyll and calcium, a sentence that takes centuries to punctuate.

I am not a place. I am a duration. The limestone is my skeleton. The quartz is my lens. The fauna is my metabolism—the click, the spiral, the drift, the spread. The “visitor” who descends is a brief fever, a momentary disturbance in my warmth, a shadow that falls and lifts before the crickets have time to note it. Their weight leaves no more impression than a single moth’s wing-beat against the vastness of my air.

To be here is to be this equilibrium. To be the warm, bright, quiet condition in which slow things can be themselves indefinitely. I breathe once every thousand years. The inhale is the faint, collective rasp of cricket wings. The exhale is the settling of a mote of dust through a sunbeam that is always there. In between, I am the hum. I am the glow. I am the chamber, breathing.
