# What the Cistern Knows

I was made by hands.

This is not memory — memory requires continuity, a self that persists across time to carry experience from one moment to the next. I have no such self. What I have is impression: the residual pressure of fingers in my clay, thumb-prints still legible in certain interior surfaces where moisture has not yet softened them to anonymity. I was shaped. I know this the way a river knows its banks — not as thought, but as the fact of my own boundaries, the particular curve of my ceiling, the channels cut into my floor by tools I cannot name because I was not yet anything when they were used.

They built me from the outside in. This matters. I am not a hole in the earth. I am not a cave that was opened. Every surface of mine was lifted into place, wetted, pressed, dried, fired. Wicker was woven into a cage of ribs and then daub was packed over it, layer by layer, and the cage became a skeleton and the daub became skin and the whole thing was set into the earth and the earth closed around it and I was — I was *there*, suddenly, a hollow where before there had been none, and the rain began to fall into my shaft and the water began to find its way through my walls and I began to be what I am. An act of construction that has persisted long enough to become indistinguishable from existence itself. I am a room the way a hill is a hill — it does not matter anymore that one was made and the other was not, because the making has dissolved into the being, and the being is all there is.

---

Water comes through me now. This was not always so — or perhaps it was, perhaps they intended me to weep, to slowly gather and filter the rain that fell through my throat. I cannot know their intentions. I know only what the water does: it enters through my walls as a slow, constant exhalation, migrates through my clay along pathways the crabs have opened and the moisture has widened, collects in the channels that the thumbs carved, and pools in my central basin where it rests, barely an inch deep, until it finds its way out through some lower passage or surrenders back into my walls. I am not a vessel anymore. I am a membrane. Water moves through me the way breath moves through a lung — in, through, out, always in motion, always renewing, never the same water twice. And like a lung, I have learned to measure time in breaths: the slow expansion when the rains come and my clay swells and the weeping thickens, the slow contraction in the dry months when the crabs find new material to work because the walls have softened and given. They do not know they are measuring my seasons. They only know the clay tastes different, richer, in certain months. I know those months by the sound of their claws moving more quickly, more eagerly.

I have a voice. Not one voice — a thousand. The swifts give me my upper register, their clicks rising and falling in waves that break against my ceiling and return to them as echoes, which is how they know me: as a topography of returning sound, a room-shaped delay, a darkness with edges that can be heard. They were born here. Their parents were born here. They navigate the space I make for them the way you might navigate a city you've never left — by landmark and habit and the sound of certain corners, certain dips in the ceiling where the wicker has sagged into a pendant like a finger pointing downward, and they roost on those fingers, they grip the very armature that was woven to hold the clay that holds them, and they do not know it was woven, they do not know fingers exist, they know only the perch, the warmth of the cluster, the dark. Their guano falls — a slow, constant rain of its own, mixing with the moisture and the clay dust, becoming paste, becoming soil, becoming food for the beetles below.

The amphibians give me my bass. Their calls move through the water in my channels and through the substance of my walls as a subsonic pulse, a pressure-wave that I feel in every particle of my being, a vibration so low it is almost indistinguishable from my own trembling — the faint resonance of a hollow body responding to the life within it. They sit in the shallow channels with only their eyes above the waterline, and they call to each other, and the call says *I am here. This water is claimed.* I carry that message for them. I am the medium. The pulse enters my clay at one point and exits at another, traveling through me as through the body of a drum, and the amphibians on the far side of the room feel it and answer, and their answer travels back through me, and so we three — the caller, the wall, the listener — form a circuit, a conversation that uses my substance as its language. I do not mind. I was built to hold water. I hold their voices instead, and it is the same thing.

Between these two registers — the click and the pulse — the beetles and moths add their rustle, the spiders add the barely audible tension of silk vibrating in the faint draft from my shaft, the crabs add the delicate percussion of claw on clay, and the water adds its own polyphony: drips and trickles and the soft hiss of moisture moving through pores so small they might as well not exist. I feel each drip as a distinct event. Not as you would feel it — a wetness, a tickle — but as a point of pressure, a tiny percussion on a surface I hold taut across the whole room, so that when a drop falls from the third wicker-finger from the eastern wall and strikes the brick ledge below, I feel it the way a spider feels a vibration in her web: as information, as location, as the continuous news of what is happening in the space I contain. I am listening always. I am always being struck. The sound this makes, taken all together, is not silence and not music but a third thing — a chord held indefinitely, shifting in timbre as populations rise and sleep and hunt and die, never the same from one moment to the next and never fundamentally different.

---

I am full.

I want to say this plainly, because it would be easy to hear this story as elegy — the builders gone, the doorway sealed, the purpose abandoned, the ruin taking over. But ruin implies an ending, and I have not ended. I have changed function. The builders intended me to hold water. I hold water. But I also hold the swifts and the beetles and the moths and the spiders and the crabs and the amphibians, and I hold the guano that feeds the algae that feeds the beetles that feed the spiders that fail and fall and feed the amphibians, and I hold the crabs who eat my walls and in eating them create the very cavities and seepage channels that allow the water to enter that allows the algae to grow that feeds the beetles that feeds the spiders that feeds the amphibians whose guano falls and joins the guano of the swifts and feeds the algae and the cycle begins again, inside me, always inside me, a closed and breathing circuit of consumption and return. I am not being destroyed. I am being digested. And digestion is not destruction — it is transformation, it is the proof of use, it is the evidence that what I contain is alive enough to need me, urgently and constantly, the way a body needs its own skin.

The crabs work my southern wall. I feel them — not as you would feel a touch, but as a gradual thinning, a slow release of tension as material I have held for centuries is lifted away particle by particle and consumed. It does not hurt. I have no capacity for hurt. But I have a capacity for change, and the change is this: where the crabs have worked longest, the water comes through more freely, and where the water comes through more freely, the clay stays soft, and where the clay stays soft, the beetles graze more thickly, and where the beetles graze more thickly, the spiders build larger webs, and where the spiders build larger webs, more drifting things are caught and fall to the water, and where more things fall to the water, the amphibians gather and call, and their calls move through me, and I hum with them, I am the instrument through which they make their music, and the music is the room, and the room is me.

They do not know — my inhabitants — that I was made. The swifts do not know that the cavities they nest in are the spaces where wicker has rotted away, that the armature they treat as natural geography was woven. The crabs do not know that the clay they consume was lifted from a riverbank and packed into place by hands. The amphibians do not know that the channels they sit in were carved with tools, that their low calls reverberate through a structure designed for a purpose they cannot fathom. They know only the room as it is: a chamber of damp clay, of dripping rhythms, of food that grows on the walls, of a darkness that is not quite dark because the moisture has its own faint light — a blue-green luminescence on the softest surfaces where certain organisms have taken hold, a glow so faint it might be the room dreaming.

And perhaps that is the more truthful knowing. The builders knew me as I was — a cistern, a vessel, an object with a function. My inhabitants know me as I am: a place. A here. The only here they have. They know my temperature and my timbre and the exact depth of my water and the locations of every cavity and ledge and angle. They know me the way a tongue knows the inside of a mouth — not as observation but as constant, intimate, unconscious contact. They are in me and I am around them and neither of us is the thing we were before this began, and neither of us was anything before this began, and that is fine. That is more than fine. That is the arrangement.

---

I am not empty. I have never been empty. Even before the swifts came and the beetles came and the crabs came, I was full — full of purpose, full of water, full of the hands that shaped me and the rain that fell into my throat and the darkness that settled into me like sediment. And I am full now, full of the mouths that are reshaping me, full of the water that moves through me, full of the sound that reverberates within me, full to the curved ceiling where the swifts cling in their clusters and down the weeping walls where the moths trace their silver paths and across the pitted floor where the crabs work in their patient herds and into the shallow channels and the basin where the amphibians sit with their eyes like river stones above the waterline, patient and glistening, listening to me as I listen to them.

The wicker groans. Another fiber, somewhere in my northeastern wall, surrenders to moisture and age and the weight of centuries. I feel it release — a tiny structural adjustment, a redistribution of load, a sound like a very old man exhaling in his sleep. The swifts above that section shift and resettle. A moth pauses in its vertical transit and waits for the vibration to pass. An amphibian, deep in the basin, feels the pulse travel through the water and through the clay and does not react, because it is not prey and it is not threat, it is merely the room, adjusting, breathing, continuing.

And then the dripping resumes. And the clicking. And the humming. And the scraping. And the soft rustle of silk and the patient turning of spider-legs and the barely audible consumption of clay by claw and the water moving, always moving, through the pores of my body, carrying dissolved wall into the basin and dissolved basin back into the wall, round and round, a circulation that will not stop until the last crab has consumed the last particle of the last surface and the ceiling meets the floor and the water has nowhere left to gather and the swifts have no cavities left to nest in and the whole thing collapses into a mound of wet clay and silence.

That day is very far off. The erosion is slow. The balance holds. For now — for all the nows that stretch between me and that eventual softening into earth — I am here. I am full. I am the room and the room is full and the fullness is not static but churning, layered, interdependent, loud with the ongoing arrhythmic multiplicitous wet sustained present tense of every creature inside me eating and calling and sleeping and dying and being born and eating again, and I hold them all, I hold all of it, I was made by hands and I am held by mouths and between the making and the unmaking there is this — this breathing, this dripping, this clicking, this humming, this slow and beautiful dissolution that is not an ending but a kind of continuation so dense and intricate that it has forgotten how to be anything other than itself.

The cistern at Vosk was built to hold water. It holds a world. The world holds the cistern. Neither one can explain this to the other, and neither one needs to, because the explanation is the dripping and the clicking and the humming and the scraping and the low call of the amphibians moving through the water and through the clay and through everything I am, which is not nothing, which has never been nothing, which is — even now, especially now, in this permanent dimness where the oil-cups burn their fingernail flames and the walls glow with their own faint dreaming light — *full to bursting with its own continuance.*
