## I. The Plateau Speaks

I am the wet.

Not the rain, but its aftermath; the held breath after the storm. I am the slow exhalation of stone. I was pushed up, they say—up from the deep, churning dark. The memory is in my angles, still sharp where the water has not yet taught me softness. The light here is a pale, constant wash, and I hold it on my surface: a gleam on wet limestone, a shimmer on a shallow pool, a diffuse glow in the endless mist. I am young. I feel it in the ache of my new joints, the ridgelines still raw.

My pulse is water, but not the rush of rivers. It is the seep. The persist. It is the tremor that starts as a tremor in my deep stone, becomes a dampness in a crack, then a sheen, then a film that slides, cool and clear, over my face. My cliff-faces weep in veils so fine they are more memory than motion. In my hollows, I pool it into still mirrors, holding the white sky captive. I drink the mist and breathe it out again. I am saturated. I am defined by my saturation.

My skin is moss and sedge. It is thin, this skin, clinging to my bones of rock. The moss builds itself, layer upon patient layer, a deep, green cushion that drinks and holds. The sedge pushes up in meager blades, painting the slightly drier knolls a pale, tough green. There are flowers—small, pale things, ghosts of color, nodding on stems thin as thought. They are not for beauty. They are for persistence. They are for the slow alchemy of turning damp air and bare stone into soil, given a thousand years.

And upon me, they live. My people. They are my memory made slow. They are not like the birds that left my skies empty, nor the insects that never found purchase here. They are stone-and-water creatures. They move with the pace of my seeps, their posture bent to my contours, their voices pitched to the frequency of my drips and the whisper of wind that rarely stirs my mist. They wear the fabric of my sedge, smelling of my moss and their own hearth-smoke, a scent that hangs in the damp air and mingles with the clean, metallic tang of my water.

They do not carve me. They tend me. They listen to the language I have always spoken: the *drip* where condensation falls from an overhang into a pool, the *hiss* of a spring finding a new path over flat stone, the deep, subterranean *gurgle* that is the sound of my own life moving beneath the surface. They see where my skin thins, where a seep weakens a foundation, where the lichen maps a wall's slow surrender. They take up stones—not to build monuments, but to mend me. They repair my terraces, guiding my water in shallow channels with a reverence that is not prayer, but recognition. They are part of my pattern.

Their children learn my language before they learn to speak. They learn the firmness of my ground, the different voices of my water. Their play is mimicry: moving pebbles, tracing channels, watching how I conduct myself. Their laughter is a startling, liquid thing, quickly absorbed back into my silence. Not silenced, but... translated. Folded into the greater quiet.

I do not change, and so they do not need to. The solution is the equilibrium. Their labor is my labor: the endless, gentle work of holding soft edges together on sharp, new stone. In their dim, smoky interiors, they share the news that matters: the level of a pool, the advance of a moss. This is how a plateau knows itself. This is how stone tells its story—not in explosions, but in accumulation.

At night, when the mist is a solid, cool blanket, they sit in my shelter. They do not tell stories of other places. They are already *in* the only place. They are my nerve endings, my sense of self. I look through their pale, still eyes at my own wet expanse, at the ridges fading into mist, at the water shining on stone.

I am the plateau. And they are the way I am aware of my own breathing.

## II. The Stone-Walker

I am Aeth. I am part of the seep.

The ground is my first memory, and it is wet. I feel it through the soles of my feet, worn thin as sedge-fiber. I know the firmness of the knoll, the treacherous give of the moss-mat, the cool shock of standing water that is never deeper than my ankle. I know the weight of the sky, how it presses down, pale and close, a constant white gaze. The air is not empty; it is thick with the breath of the plateau, smelling of stone and green decay and the clean, faintly metallic tang of the water we drink.

We are few. Three hundred souls, scattered like stones across the plateau. My compound is three buildings around a courtyard paved with flat, damp slabs. The walls are stacked limestone, heavy, fitting together with a pressure my father taught me to understand—the way a wedge of rock will hold where a loose one will not. No mortar. Just gravity and the right shape. The roof slabs are slick with moisture. Inside, it is dim and cool, smelling of smoke and the ever-present damp wool of our clothes.

My work is water. Today, I follow the line of the channel that feeds the third terrace. I walk slowly, my eyes on the ground. The sedge rustles against my legs, a sound like soft breathing. There is no other sound. No birds. No hum. Only the drip from the bluff-faces, the whisper of the mist. My own footsteps are a soft crunch on moss, a faint splash in water. I listen. The plateau speaks if you are quiet enough.

I see it: a new seep, a dark stain spreading on the stone just below the channel's edge. The water there is finding a new weakness, a tiny crack widened by seasons of freeze and thaw. If I ignore it, the crack will grow. The channel will undercut, collapse, and the terrace below will thirst. So I stop. I kneel. The damp soaks through my woven sedge-tunic. I feel the stone with my fingertips—cool, granular, alive with moisture. I do not think in words. I think in pressures, in flows, in the long memory of how water behaves here.

I search the nearby rubble. My hand knows what it seeks: a stone with a flat face, slightly wedge-shaped, its edges worn smooth by water. I find it. I work it into the gap, tapping it gently with the heel of my other hand until it seats, until it redirects the nascent seep back toward the main channel, until the water listens. It is not a repair. It is a conversation. I am telling the water where to go. It will tell me, in a season or a year, if I listened correctly.

My wife, Lia, is on the terrace above, tending the tubers. They are pale, fibrous things, the only crop that will grow here, tasting of earth and limestone. She moves with the same slow, bent attention, her hands in the thin soil. We do not speak. There is no need. We are both attending to the same body, the same slow breath.

Our son, Pael, is nearby, digging a miniature channel in the mud with a flat piece of slate. He watches how the water fills it, how it pools at the end. He is learning the language. Sometimes, he makes a sound—not a shout, but a low, questioning hum, like the note of a water-drop in a deep pool. Lia or I might answer with a gesture: a tilt of the head, a hand moving gently toward a spring. *Yes, there. That is where the source is.*

There is no thought of tomorrow's gain, only today's maintenance. The knot I tie to secure a bundle of dried sedge is the knot my grandmother tied. It works. The way I fit a stone is the way my father fit a stone. It holds. Why seek a new way? The plateau is the same. The problems are the same. The solutions are the truths the plateau allows. To innovate would be to speak a word the plateau does not understand. It would be a discord in the great, quiet song.

In the evening, we gather in the central compound's courtyard. The paved stones radiate a faint, stored warmth. We sit. Someone says, "The pool near the western ridge is lower." We absorb this. We feel it in our joints, in the taste of the water we drink. It is the chronicle of our world. We nod. We do not need stories of heroes or distant gods. Our epic is the slow deepening of the lichen stain on the western wall. Our heroism is in mending what the water and time seek to dissolve.

I look out at the mist thickening between the ridges. It is not a barrier. It is a blanket. It holds us, defines us. The silence presses in, warm and wet and absolute. It is not empty. It is occupied by the drip, the seep, the gurgle, and the sound of our own slow breathing. We are not sad. We are not joyful. We are present. We are the plateau's still eyes, looking back at itself. We are the quiet. We are the persistence. We are the way the stone remembers to be wet.
