# What Lives in the Channels

I would not be in the cavity.

That's the first thing I understand, if understanding is even the right word for what a body does when it follows a gradient and never stops following it. The cavities are for the congregation — the filter-feeders and their custodians, the priests and the saints and the living dust. They are rooms of abundance, windows into the water column, places where the cathedral opens itself to the rain of organic matter sifting down from the breathing sky of the surface world. The cavities are generous. The cavities are public.

I live in the passages between them.

---

I should explain what I am, though "I" is already an extravagance, a grammatical fiction that imposes a boundary where none exists. I am small. Smaller than the snailfish, smaller than most of the amphipods, larger than the copepods that cloud the cavities like living fog. My body is maybe four millimeters long, roughly cylindrical, tapered at both ends, sheathed in a translucent cuticle through which you could — if you had light, which you don't — see the dim coil of a gut, the faint smudge of a ganglion that serves me in place of a brain. I am a polychaete. A worm, if you like, though that word makes people think of soil, of rain, of the surface world. I have no relationship with soil. I have never touched it. My segments bear small bundles of chaetae — bristles, hair-fine, glass in their own right — that I use to grip the walls of the passages. I have no name. No one here does. Naming requires sound, and sound is something that happens elsewhere.

What I have is the labyrinth.

---

The sponge colony's interior is not the water column. This is the essential thing. When you are small enough — when your world is a tube perhaps three millimeters in diameter, with walls of fused siliceous lace — the distinction between "inside the sponge" and "inside the water" ceases to have meaning. The walls *are* the water, rearranged. The water *is* the walls, not yet committed to being solid. I live in the between, in the channels where the sponge draws water inward toward its choanocyte chambers — the tiny flagellated rooms where the actual filtering happens, where cells too small for me to comprehend beat their flagella and pull bacteria from the current like hands plunging into grain.

I do not go to the choanocyte chambers. They are too narrow, too alive, too full of the sponge's own hungry cells. I live in the incurrent channels, the slightly wider passages that feed water toward those chambers, and in the excurrent channels that carry the filtered water away. The architecture is branching, recursive, fractal. A passage splits into two. Each of those splits again. The splits rejoin, sometimes, into passages that lead to cavities — those public rooms with their bioluminescent shrimp and their congregation — and sometimes into passages that simply narrow and narrow until they are capillaries of water inside glass, and then they end, or rather they become the sponge itself, and the distinction between water and organism dissolves entirely.

I have mapped this, if mapping is what a ganglion does when it remembers pressure. Not the whole labyrinth — no one could map the whole labyrinth, it extends for kilometers in every direction and changes faster than I could traverse it — but my portion of it. My small territory of branching and rebranching, of junctions where the current splits and I choose — if choosing is what a body does — which way to go.

---

The current is everything.

You in your cavity, breathing your regulator, feeling the slow forty-second pulse of the sponge as a faint shift in pressure across your eardrums — you feel it as weather. I feel it as *terrain*. The current is my ground, my gravity, my compass. When the sponge inhales — pulling water inward through its ostia, the tiny pores in its outer wall — the incurrent channels fill with flow, and I flatten myself against the lattice wall and feel the water drag across my chaetae. When the sponge exhales — expelling filtered water through its oscula, the larger openings that empty into the cavities and then into the open ocean — the excurrent channels fill, and I release my grip and drift, carried backward through the architecture like a seed through veins.

This is how I travel. Not by swimming — I have no swimmerets, no fins, no muscles adapted for sustained locomotion. I grip. I release. I grip. I release. The sponge's breathing is my locomotion, and it carries me through the labyrinth in a rhythm that I know so deeply it has become my heartbeat. Inhale: I grip, I hold, I feed on the bacteria-rich current washing over my ciliated tentacles. Exhale: I release, I drift, I am carried to the next junction, the next place where the passage splits and I must choose.

The choices are not conscious. They are chemical. Each channel has its own chemistry — the residues of whatever the sponge has been filtering, the metabolic wastes of the communities in adjacent cavities, the faint pheromone trails of others of my kind who passed this way. I follow the gradients. Where the chemical signature is richest — where bacterial density is highest, where the water carries the amino-acid tang of active metabolism — I settle. I feed. I grow. When the chemistry shifts, when the gradient flattens or reverses, I move on.

I have been moving for a long time. I don't know how long. Time, down here, is measured in pulses — the forty-second cycles of the sponge's breath, the seasonal surges of marine snow, the longer rhythms I cannot perceive but can sometimes feel as a slow change in the chemistry of the passages, a shifting of the gradient over what must be years. I have no lifespan that I am aware of. I simply am, and then, eventually, I will not be, and the sponge will grow over whatever I leave behind — a thin thread of calcium carbonate, a few chaetae, a memory in the lattice — and the current will continue.

---

The cavities are foreign countries.

I visit them. The sponge's breathing carries me, eventually, into one of the larger channels that empties into a cavity — that four-meter room with its congregation of saints and penitents, its shrimp-lanterns, its crawling, swimming, growing profusion of life. When I emerge from the passage into the open water of the cavity, the sensation is — I lack a word for it. *Exposure.* The walls recede. The current slows and disperses. The water is warmer — not much, a tenth of a degree, but enough. The chemical signatures multiply and blur. I am suddenly surrounded by hundreds of species, thousands of individuals, and the simple pressure-gradient logic that guides me through the passages becomes useless. There are no walls close enough to grip. The current here is not a channel but a volume, a three-dimensional wash of water moving in directions I cannot predict.

I flatten myself against the nearest spicule branch and wait. I am not the only one. Around me, on the walls of the passage mouth, other small channel-dwellers have gathered — tiny gastropods, a few nematodes coiled like watch springs, a single juvenile amphipod that will, if it survives, grow large enough to swim freely in the cavity and become a custodian. We are the border people. The threshold dwellers. We live in the transition between the labyrinth's intimacy and the cavity's open society, and we are adapted to neither, comfortable in neither, dependent on both.

The cavity feeds me. Not directly — I cannot filter the open water efficiently, my cilia are adapted for channel-flow, for the concentrated bacterial density of the passages — but indirectly, because the cavity's richness spills backward into the channels. The sponge draws water inward from the cavity. That water carries with it the metabolic products of the congregation, the bacterial blooms that grow on worm chimneys, the dissolved organics that leach from a decaying bryozoan colony. By the time this water reaches me in the incurrent channels, it has been partially processed, concentrated, channeled into a flow regime I can exploit. The cavity is my watershed. The passages are my river.

---

I am aware — in the way a ganglion is aware, which is to say through the slow accumulation of chemical and mechanical signals that translate into tropism rather than thought — that I am not alone in the passages.

There are others of my kind. I encounter them at junctions, in the wider spots where the channel opens enough for two bodies to pass without touching, and when we meet, we stop. We extend our prostomia — the forward lip of our bodies, ciliated, chemosensory — and we taste each other. The taste tells us everything we need to know: species, sex, maturity, recent feeding history, stress level. If the signals align — if we are the right species, the right sex, the right stage of readiness — we do something that contradicts the silence of this place. We spawn. Not all at once, like the cavity creatures with their seasonal synchrony, but continuously, in the slow and private way of channel-dwellers who meet rarely and must take each opportunity. Gametes into the water. The current carries them. Some will reach a choanocyte chamber and be consumed. Some will drift into a cavity and be eaten by an amphipod. Some will find each other, and a larva will develop — a tiny, ciliated thing, planktonic for a few hours, carried by the sponge's breathing through passages it has never seen — and then it will settle. On a spicule wall. In a channel. And it will begin to grip and release and grip and release, and the labyrinth will become its body, and its body will become the labyrinth's.

---

There is a passage I have found, deeper than the others.

It is narrow — barely wider than my body — and it leads inward, away from the cavities, away from the light of the *Euphausia*, away from even the faint chemical noise of the congregation. The water here is different. Older. It has been recycled through the sponge so many times that it has lost its ocean taste and acquired something else — something mineral, something ancient, something that reminds me of the silica walls themselves. The current is slower. The pulse is fainter. The passages branch less frequently, as if the sponge's architecture simplifies as it deepens, converging on some central structure I cannot see.

I go there sometimes, when the chemical gradient pulls me. I do not know what I find. There is less food. The bacteria are sparse, the organic molecules depleted. But there is something else — a signal I cannot parse, a gradient I follow without understanding, a pull that feels like the current but is not the current. It draws me inward, toward the oldest part of the colony, where the spicules are not lace but solid glass, where the lattice has been growing for twelve thousand years without interruption, where the sponge is not a house for other creatures but is, purely and completely, itself.

I grip the wall there, in the deepest passage, and I feel the pulse — faint, slow, the heartbeat of the oldest living thing I will ever know — and I press my chemosensory prostomium against the glass, and I taste something that I have no name for.

It tastes like the beginning of things.

---

I think — if thinking is what happens when a ganglion accumulates enough signal to cross a threshold — that I am not a tenant here. I am not a guest, not a visitor, not a parasite. I am a *function*. The sponge needs the bacteria in its channels to be grazed, to be kept from overgrowing and clogging the lattice. I graze them. I keep the channels open. My waste becomes food for the next layer of microbes. My body, when I die, will be colonized, consumed, mineralized, incorporated. I am the cathedral's maintenance. I am the blood in its veins, the white cell in its immune system, the thought in its ganglion — if a cathedral twelve thousand years old and kilometers across can be said to have a ganglion.

I am four millimeters long and I live in the dark and I have never seen light and I have no name and I will never be catalogued by the scientists who, in their world of air and sun and sound, study the outside of this structure and call it a sponge.

I am inside. I have always been inside. The inside is all there is.

The passage breathes. I grip. I release. I drift.
