# First Condensation

I arrive with no memory of arriving, which is perhaps the only way one can arrive here. The passage from the northern edge is not a doorway — it is a gradual conviction. The fog I have been walking through does not end; it thickens, darkens, and begins to hold shape. Trunk. Lattice. The faintest suggestion of a room beyond a room. My hand finds bark that is wet, and I understand this is not rain.

---

I should say: I have not been placed here. I have not been told to come. But I have been walking for a long time — months? longer? — through the flat grey-green expanse that surrounds this grove, and I have been losing specificity. The landscape outside is like a sentence that has been repeated too many times: it means less with each iteration. I know I started somewhere with edges. I know I had a shape that was more deliberate than this. But the fog has been smoothing me, and I have been letting it.

What I was before — I think I was someone who worked with words the way those old builders worked with wood. I selected, I pruned, I grafted. I thought I understood material. I made structures I believed were load-bearing. Some of them held. Some of them are still standing, in places I cannot quite remember. But I was a mason of meaning, and meaning, it turns out, is a living material. It does not stay cut.

So I have been walking, and the walking has been a kind of pruning. Every day a little more bark shed. Every day a little less weight.

And now this.

---

The first thing I feel is the floor.

Not with my feet — with my whole body. The floor gives, and the giving is not weakness but breath. I have been standing on surfaces that refuse to acknowledge my weight — stone, concrete, the packed and punished earth of places where everything alive has been convinced to grow only where it is told. This floor does not refuse. It yields, and in yielding it holds me. I understand this the way you understand a hand extended: not through analysis but through the sudden, total cessation of effort. I have been braced for so long. I did not know I was braced until I stopped.

I am standing on root-knuckles, and they are warm.

---

I spend — I cannot call it time, not yet, because time here moves like the sap in the cam-press: in slow, deliberate increments that have nothing to do with clocks. I spend a first interval in the lowest room, the one where the water is deepest. It is ankle-deep. It is the color of tea steeped in wood. I can see the root-mat beneath it, pale and groping, and I can feel the water's cold climbing my skin with a patience that suggests it has been climbing for centuries.

A small amphibian sits on a root-platform two steps away. It is nearly invisible — I find it not by seeing it but by noticing the absence of water texture where it sits. It watches me with eyes that are not quite eyes, more like suggestions of where eyes might go if this creature ever decided to commit to sight. It does not move. I do not move. We are having a conversation in stillness, and I realize, after some interval, that I am losing. It is stiller than I am. It has been practicing longer.

This is the first lesson: stillness here is not the absence of motion. It is motion so slow it has become a single, continuous gesture. The amphibian is not waiting. It is doing one thing — sitting — with a totality I have never brought to any act in my life.

---

I find the engines before I understand them.

The hum enters me before I locate it — a vibration in the root-platform slats, a tension in the air that is not sound but the memory of sound, played so long it has become a property of the space. I press my palm to the nearest trunk and feel it: the slow tick of belt on wheel, transmitted through meters of living wood like a pulse.

The engine itself is half-swallowed. Root growth has crept up around its housing in a slow embrace, and ferns have colonized the gap where the flywheel turns, their fronds fanning gently in the draft created by the wheel's rotation. The belt is leather, old, darkened with oil and time, and it moves with a speed that is almost not speed — more like the rate at which lichen advances. The cam it drives presses into the soft inner bark of the trunk with a rhythm that I eventually recognize as the grove's own rhythm: the same cadence as the slow settling of wood, the same patience as the drip from the joint-cups.

The system is not powered by the engine. The engine is powered by the system. I understand this the way you understand that a heart does not power a body — the body powers the heart, and the heart returns the favor.

There is a tool leaning against the trunk nearby. A wooden handle, worn smooth in a specific grip, darkened with the oil of a palm that has held it ten thousand times. I pick it up, and the weight of it tells me something — not what it is for, but how it has been held. Gently. Deliberately. The way you hold a thing you expect to use for the rest of your life.

I put it back. I am not ready for the tool. I am still learning the floor.

---

Days pass, or what I call days — intervals of slightly different condensation patterns, a faint shift in the quality of the light filtering through the canopy gaps. I move through the grove slowly, not exploring but *circulating*, the way the water circulates, the way the air breathes through the passages on its tidal schedule.

I learn the rooms by their moisture. The central rooms are driest — which is to say, the condensation here takes a few seconds longer to form, and the lichen is slightly less aggressive. I learn the passages by their sound — each one has a different acoustic signature, a different relationship between the drip and the hum and the deep groan of the major trunks. The passage nearest the northern edge, where I entered, is the loudest: it connects to the outside air, and on days when the exterior temperature drops, the cold breath of the grove pours through it like a slow river.

I learn the smell. At first it is one smell — green, wet, living. Then it separates, the way a chord resolves into notes. The cellular sweetness of active growth. The sharper tang of sap from a fresh cut in the bark. The mineral note where root-fingers have cracked the stone. The thin, clean fire of the amber resin. The darker, fungal complexity of the places where decay is doing its necessary work. And beneath all of these, a smell I have no word for — the smell of a system in balance, which is not any single scent but the relationship between all of them.

I begin to notice the marks. Carvings on certain trunks, nearly healed over — the bark has been slowly closing around them for years, and what was once a clear tally or message is now a faint ridge, a scar that will be smooth within another decade. I run my fingers over them and feel the gesture that made them: not urgent, not possessive. An inscription made by someone who knew the trunk would heal, and let it.

---

I do not decide to stay. That is not how it happens. What happens is that I begin to do things.

The first thing: I notice that a water channel in the southeastern passage has been slowly occluded by root growth. The water that should be flowing to feed the roots at the grove's margin is pooling instead, backing up, seeping into a room where it is not needed. I notice this the way I might notice a word misused — a dissonance, a thing out of place in a sentence that otherwise reads clean.

I kneel. The water is cold on my knees. I can see the root-fingers pressing against the clay pipe, and I can see that the root is not aggressive — it is simply growing toward water, which is what roots do. The pipe is slightly cracked, and the moisture leaking from the crack has drawn the root the way sugar draws a moth.

I look around for a tool, and my hand finds one before my mind has finished asking. A small iron blade, wooden handle, leaning against a specific trunk in a specific place. It fits my grip as though it has been waiting. I use the blade to carefully score the root — not severing it, but redirecting it. A thin cut along one side will cause the root to grow away from the pipe, toward the open water of the low room instead. I have not been taught this. I know it the way I knew how to breathe when I was born: because the knowledge was already in the system, and I am now inside the system, and the system speaks through my hands.

The root weeps a little from the cut. The sap is clear, faintly warm. I press my thumb against the wound for a moment — not to stop the bleeding, but the way you might touch someone's arm when they are hurt, an acknowledgment that pain is occurring and is witnessed.

I put the tool back. I stand. The water is already moving, already beginning to clear the channel.

This is the first thing I have done in a very long time that feels like it belongs to me.

---

The second thing comes later — the next interval, or the one after. I am in the processing room, and I notice that the combustion chamber is burning low. The heat beneath the copper plate has dropped, and the resin in the basin is thickening too fast, beginning to skin over. If it solidifies in the basin, it will take hours of careful warming to clear.

I find the dried plant material stacked in an alcove — deadfall, bark-shed, neatly bundled. I feed it into the chamber, a little at a time, watching the flame. The fire here is not dramatic. It is a negotiation between heat and the copper plate's own conductivity, a balance that requires tending the way a conversation requires listening. I add material until the resin begins to thin again, until its surface ripples gently with the convection of the heated basin. The sharp, clean smell intensifies, and I breathe it in, and it fills a space inside me that I did not know was empty.

I sit beside the basin and watch the resin drip. Each drop takes — I count once, to learn, and then I stop counting — a long time. The interval between drops is not empty. It is full of the grove's sound: the hum, the drip, the slow breath of the canopy. I learn to put my attention inside the interval, the way the amphibian puts its attention inside its stillness. The interval is not waiting for the drop. The interval is its own complete experience. The drop is a punctuation mark in a sentence that has no beginning and no end.

---

I am becoming something. I can feel it in my gait — I walk differently now. My feet find the root-knuckles without looking, the way a pianist's fingers find the keys. My weight distributes differently: lower, more settled, closer to the ground's own breath. My heartbeat, I notice, has begun to lengthen. Not dangerously — I am not dying. I am *slowing*. My body is adjusting its tempo to the place, the way a vine adjusts its growth to the available light.

I eat the resin. It is dense, warm in the mouth, faintly spicy, and it sustains me in a way that food has not sustained me before — not by filling a void but by connecting me more deeply to the system that produced it. I am eating the grove, and the grove is drinking from the water I cleared, and the water is rising from the stone the roots have cracked, and the stone is — I lose the thread. The thread is not a thread. It is a root. It goes everywhere and ends nowhere.

---

At night — in the deep interval, when the condensation thickens and the grove's breath slows to its lowest register — I climb to the higher room where the major trunk forks into a hollow that fits my body the way the tool fit my hand. I lie back against wood that is warm from the slow metabolic heat of living tissue. Above me, the lattice-ceiling is a map of grafting scars, each one a decision made by someone long ago, each one now sealed over with bark, healed into strength.

I listen.

The drip. The hum. The deep groan.

I am not the keeper. I am not the owner. I am not even, anymore, the visitor. I am the part of the system that moves between the engines and the roots, between the resin and the water, between the cut and the healing. I am the slowest-moving organ. My heartbeat is the grove's heartbeat. My breath is the grove's breath.

When I close my eyes, I can feel the condensation forming on my skin.

---

I think, sometimes, about the fog outside. About the flat grey-green landscape I walked through to get here. I think about the edges I used to have, the structures I used to build, the words I used to select and prune and graft. I do not mourn them. They are the deadfall that feeds the combustion chamber. They are the bark-shed that becomes fuel for the work of staying warm.

The grove does not ask what I was before. The grove does not have a before. The grove has only what is growing now, and what is growing now includes me.

I place my hand against the trunk beside my sleeping hollow. I feel the sap moving, slow and warm, on the other side of the bark. I feel the engine's hum transmitted through the wood like a thought I am about to have. I feel the water finding its ancient grooves.

I am not remembering this place. I am being remembered by it.
