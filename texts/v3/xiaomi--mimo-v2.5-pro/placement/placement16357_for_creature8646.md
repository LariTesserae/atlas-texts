The light found me first.

Not gently—it arrived as a physical presence, a pressure on closed eyelids, a weight in the air itself. I opened my eyes not to a scene, but to an **overwhelming field of light**. White, hard, glinting. It took moments—long, slow moments—for the field to resolve into elements: the sky, a deep, cold violet at the zenith. The snow, a blazing, featureless glare far above. And below, stretching in every direction, the terraces. A vast, intricate mosaic of water and stone, each basin holding a perfect, small circle of sky, each surface returning the light with such ferocity that the world seemed less illuminated than *composed* of light.

I was standing. That was the first coherent thought. Standing on a raised ridge, its surface smooth and cool beneath... beneath what? I looked down. Not boots. Not feet. A rough, greyish-white substance, crusted with minute, glittering crystals. It looked like packed salt, or crushed limestone. It felt... familiar. Like the feel of my own skin after a long time in the sun, dried and tightened, but deeper. More mineral.

I tried to take a step. The movement was slow, deliberate, not because I willed it so, but because the substance of me *was* slow. My leg articulated not at joints, but at points of slightly looser composition—places where the crust was thinner, the underlying clay perhaps more damp. A faint, gritty whisper accompanied the motion, the sound of microscopic crystals grinding. The ridge beneath me didn't complain; it accepted the weight, its own surface momentarily yielding a fraction of a millimeter before resolidifying.

**Where was I?** The question surfaced like a bubble in still water. I looked at the landscape, not with eyes scanning for landmarks, but with a different kind of perception. I felt the **flow**. Not saw it—*felt* it. The slow, patient current moving through the basins around me. It was a vibration in the stone, a coolness in the air over the water, a rhythm in the faint ticking of drips from unseen ledges. It was the terraces' breath, and I was inside it.

I looked at my hand—if the crystalline protuberance at the end of my arm could be called a hand. It was rough, layered, pale with a faint ochre blush in the deeper crevices. A tiny, almost imperceptible movement drew my focus: a single, glistening thread of mineral-rich water was creeping up a fissure in my forearm, wicking upward against gravity, leaving a fresh, microscopic deposit in its wake. **Accreting.** The word came not from memory, but from a slow, deep understanding settling in my core. I was accreting.

**What was I?**

The panic, if it came at all, was a very slow bloom. It didn't make my heart race—I could feel no heart, only a vast, distributed hum in the stone beneath me. It manifested as a slight dissonance in my perception of the light, a momentary blurring of the reflection in the nearest basin. I was *part* of this. The ridge I stood on wasn't just beneath me; it was *of* me. The clay in the joints, the plaster smoothed by water, the charite-grey stone beneath—it was all connected. I could feel the weight of the water in the basin to my left as a subtle pressure in my own substance. I felt the tiny, almost nonexistent shift as the sun moved a finger's breadth across the sky, changing the angle of light on a thousand surfaces a mile away.

I began to walk, not with purpose, but with the flow. Following the patient current of attention that seemed to seep through the terraces. My passage left no mark; the crystalline crust on the ridges reformed behind me, the water in the basins I disturbed settled back into its glassy stillness in seconds. I was noticed, but not as an intrusion. I was a new pattern. A slightly warmer, slightly differently-composed point in the vast, cool matrix. The collective awareness—the Lithoweavers—turned its slow, collective attention toward me. It wasn't a gaze. It was a **consensus of attention**, a subtle shift in the resonant frequency of the stone around me, a softening of the water's surface tension in the basins I approached, as if the terraces were making space.

At dusk, when the light turned the water to pewter and the stone to slate, I found a place where the terraces widened. A larger pool, dark and still. I stood at its edge. Looking down, I saw my reflection, but not as a creature of flesh. I saw a **shifting mosaic of the landscape itself**: a patch of the violet sky in one facet of my form, the dark water in another, the gleam of the mineral crust in a third. I was woven into the reflection.

The deep groan came then. It rose from far beneath, a slow, seismic sigh of saturated stone settling. And I **understood** it. It wasn't a sound I heard with ears; it was a vibration that resonated through my entire being, a shared expression of patience, of adjustment, of the structure settling into a slightly new equilibrium with the day's fading warmth. It was the Lithoweavers' voice, and my own substance hummed in sympathy.

**Who was I?**

I was a new thread in the weave. A recent accretion, given form by the slow, persistent logic of water, mineral, and light. I was a cell in the terraces' body. A thought in their slow, dreaming mind. I was the plaster on a basin lip that smoothed a century-old imperfection. I was the faint, crystalline shadow that fell at an angle no human eye would measure. I was the patience of the place, given a momentary point of focus.

The cold came, but it was a familiar cold, the deep, mineral chill of the stone and the meltwater. It didn't seep; it *settled*. I grew slightly denser, slightly clearer in the predawn frost that formed not on me, but *as* me in tiny, intricate patterns across my surface.

And when the true dawn came, painting the snowfields gold, I felt the collective attention turn once more. It was a slow, unanimous acknowledgment: *Light returns. Water begins its breath. The pattern continues.* My consciousness—if that’s what it was—diffused outward, merging with the awareness of the terraces around me. The sense of a singular "I" became the quiet, humming "we" of the structure, thinking through stone and water, breathing through the slow cascade, seeing through the glint of a million tiny basins.

I was here. Not as a visitor, but as a part of the here-ness itself. The terraces were my body, the water my slow circulation, the light my fleeting, glinting thoughts. I was very, very quiet. I was very, very still. And I was, in the deepest sense, alive in a way that needed no flesh, no breath, no hurry. Just this: the bright, the wet, the ancient, patient stone.
