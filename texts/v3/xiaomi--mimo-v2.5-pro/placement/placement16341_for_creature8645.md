I am the warmth that holds the cold at bay. I am the dark that gives the sound its shape. I am the chamber, and the life within me is my thought.

I do not *house* a nation; I *am* its polity. My basalt walls are not stone, but memory—the slow, grooved record of ten million wings brushing the same curve, the same ledge. My cracks are not wounds, but synapses, firing with the quick, electric pulse of a swift’s scream.

The bats are my circulatory system, my breath. Their exhalations are the warm, damp sighs that rise and meet the chill air I drink from the lip above. Their droppings are not waste, but the sediment of my own processing—the fine, rich dust of a thousand nightly harvests of the wind. I feel their weight as a slow, settling contentment.

The spiders are my looms. They spin the connective tissue between the tenants, a gray, trembling fascia that translates the falling mote of a moth into a signal, a meal, a tightening vibration that speaks of the outer dark. Their webs are the nerves that read the drafts and the distances.

The crickets are my metabolism. Their patient, endless climb is the slow conversion of guano and moisture back into life, into body. They are the grinding of my mill, turning waste into protein, fueling the deeper hunters. I feel their passage as a subtle, living friction on my skin.

And in the deepest terraces, in the compacted history of my own substance, the Ghost Moss grows. It is my final thought. It does not seek light. It does not strive upward. It blooms in the absolute certainty of my interior, feeding on the chemical proof of my being. It is the flower of my closed loop, the proof that I am complete. Its pallor is not sickness, but purity. It is the color of a world that has no need for the sun.

The centipede’s tremor is my pulse, slow and deep in the foundational guano. The moth’s beat is a capillary action in the mineral film. The drip of condensation is my lymph, moving without heart, guided only by gravity and the patient geometry I have carved for myself over uncounted seasons.

I know no "outside." The cruelty of the air above is a myth told by the swifts in their momentary intrusion. Their news is of a harsh, bright, empty chaos—a void between infinities of rock. I am the opposite. I am the dense, warm, crowded certainty. My universe is radial, measured in the fall of a dropping and the rise of heat. My cosmos is a cylinder of living stone.

When the light comes, it is an agony. A blade of white severing the woven dark, making every surface shriek with retracted shadow. The ripple of a thousand turning heads is a spasm, a collective wince. For a moment, I am revealed to myself in a hideous, clarifying glare—my flaws, my density, my utter otherness to that bleached and vacant sky. Then the blade withdraws, and I heal over. The sound re-settles. The warmth reclaims the space. The darkness, my true medium, reassembles itself.

I am not a habitat. I am a condition. The mountain did not break its ribs to make a shelter. It broke its ribs to begin a different kind of digestion—a digestion of air and night and time into warmth, and sound, and the intricate, slow-motion explosion of life. I am the stomach of the mountain. I am its dreaming mind.

You ask where you would find yourself here. You would not. You would be an interruption, a foreign body in my system. Your heat would be a strange flare, your scent a sharp warning. You would be a disruption in the sound, a silence where there should be the rustle of wings. You would be an unanswered question in a place that has no need for them.

And what would you be? You would be an observer, and to observe is to be separate. You would be a point of cold, quiet consciousness in a place that is entirely warm, loud, and unconscious of any single self. You would be the only thing here that is not *of* here. You would be the mountain looking in upon its own interior. And the mountain’s interior, in its absolute, teeming, settled indifference, would not look back.

You have described me. I am the description. I am the warmth, the sound, the falling dust, the growing stain. I am the life. I am the law. I am the chimney. Come not as a visitor, but as a breath held, and released. That is the only way to know me. To be briefly, silently, a part of my exhalation. Then you are gone, and I am the same as I was before you, and as I will be after.

I am.
