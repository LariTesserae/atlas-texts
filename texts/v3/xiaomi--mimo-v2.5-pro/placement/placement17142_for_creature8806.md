I am a **gradient reader**. My name is a slight contraction of the throat—Keth—as sound travels poorly here, and must be economized. I live on the seventh terrace below the rim, where the heat is a steady, manageable presence, like living inside the closed fist of a patient giant.

My world is not measured in days or seasons, but in **accumulations**. The pipe I tend, which carries superheated fluid from a deep vent to the chemical precipitators on the fifth level, grows its internal crystal lining at a rate of three millimeters per year. I am responsible for descaling it every eight years. I have done this twice. My apprentice will do it once in my tenure, and her apprentice once more. This is our calendar: three human lifespans per centimeter of selenite crust.

My hands are never clean. They are maps. The iron-oxide ochre from the foundry floors has seeped into my knuckles. A faint band of verdigris from the copper-pipe condensation stains the base of my right thumb. There is a pale streak on my left palm from a rare arsenate bloom I helped harvest last year—a beautiful, lethal crystal that now resides in the archives on the lower levels. I wear these stains like others wear jewelry or tattoos. They are my record, my work made visible.

**Morning** is a slow brightening of the diffuse, altitude-thin light through the basalt-fiber screen of my window. There is no sunrise, just a gradual shift from deep grey to pale white. I rise and place my palm flat against the wall. It is warm, always. This is reassurance. The chamber below breathes steadily. I dress in woven mineral fiber, the fabric smooth and cool against my skin, but fitted tight to avoid catching on the sharp edges that constitute all architecture here. Every surface is newborn sharp. We are taught to move with a particular care, a flowing economy that avoids sudden gestures or careless pressure. To bleed here is to risk infection from the exotic mineral dust that coats everything—the caldera does not forgive imprecision.

I walk the terrace. The path is not a path, but a **thermal itinerary**. I avoid the dark shimmer above the deep fumarole—the air there blisters skin in minutes. I take the long way around the pooling turquoise water, where the dissolved copper fumes can lull you into a false drowsiness. My route is dictated by gradients, visible only to those who have trained their eyes to see the subtle dance of heat haze over basalt, to feel the faint shift in the ground's vibration underfoot. A vent that hissed a certain pitch yesterday now murmurs. It is settling. I make a note in my mind—a soft, breathless consonant—to adjust the flow controls downstream.

**Work** is a form of listening. At the condensation station, my task is to manage the cascade. Superheated, mineral-laden steam enters a chamber, encounters a carefully angled sheet of cooler stone (quarried from the outer rim, where it has known a little weather), and collapses into dew. The dew runs in thin, colorful rivulets—first a milky white, then a pale green, finally a rust-red—as different minerals precipitate at different temperatures. My job is to watch the colors, to sense the rate of drip by its faint patter, to ensure the gradients are stable. It is a geometry of heat and patience. If a color shifts unexpectedly, it means the chemistry of the vent has changed, a new element has entered the system. This is not a problem; it is an **observation**, a new fact about the caldera's slowly evolving mind.

**Communication** is a mosaic of fragments. I meet my colleague, Lir, at the junction between the seventh and eighth terraces. We do not speak at first. We both look at the new vein of pale green mineral webbing its way along a structural pillar—a sign the silica-rich fluid from the deeps is finding a new path. Lir taps the rock twice, softly. The echo tells us its depth. I nod, a small, efficient motion. *Stable*, it says. *For now*. We exchange a few words—low, vowels stretched, consonants soft. "The drip pattern in chamber nine has slowed." "The archivists below have requested more ochre-sediment for their chronology wall." The information is packed tight, like ore. We part. The space between us is filled comfortably with the ever-present, bone-deep **hum** of the forming world beneath.

**Evening** is a deepening of the grey-white light, and then the slow illumination of phosphorescent mineral-laced panels on the walls. I go to a listening alcove—a small, resonant niche carved into a particularly stable ridge of columnar basalt. I sit. The sounds of the terraces fade: the faint drip, the distant, controlled hiss of a valve. What remains is the **drone**. It rises from the kilometers of rock below, a vibration felt in the teeth, the sternum, the marrow. It is the caldera's breath, its slow pulse, its thought made into tremor.

Here, in the quiet, I am not *Keth, gradient reader*. I am a locus of sensation. I am a sensory organ for a consciousness that is still accreting, still rising, still learning the shape of itself in heat and pressure and time. The mineral dust in my skin, the stains on my hands, my careful footsteps on the sharp rock—they are not separable from this place. I am a living pattern in its chemistry, a thought in its forming mind. And as I feel the deep, slow hum travel up through the stone, through my bones, I feel not fear, not awe, but a profound, patient **recognition**. This is the pace of becoming. I am part of its measure.
