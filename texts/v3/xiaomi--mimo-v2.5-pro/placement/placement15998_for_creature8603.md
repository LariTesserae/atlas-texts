I am a patch of *Rhizocarpon geographicum* on the northeast face of a slightly tilted granite boulder. I am the size of a human hand, my surface a map of areolae—tiny, cracked tiles—each one a continent of algal cells held in a fungal web. I am not *on* the rock; I am *of* it. My lower strata have fused with the mineral matrix, trading acids for territory, a millennia-long siege conducted at the molecular level. I taste the faint iron in the condensation that drips over me each morning. I feel the weight of the cloud.

Time, for me, is measured in micrometers. The granite is still sharp-edged, young. My own growth is a counterpoint of stillness. While the pika barks and the bunting flits in frantic, brief lives, I expand a millimeter in a decade. I have seen seventeen generations of moss cushions establish, flourish, and turn to peat in the hollow below me. I have felt the giddying vibration of the mountain’s brief, biannual shake—the thunder from the valley that is not weather but dynamite. Each blast sends a subtle tremor through my substrate, a fleeting loosening in the grip of the stone. The sound that follows is a low, sub-audible growl that the earth here is beginning to learn.

My world is a perimeter. My edges, the living frontier, are a dull olive-green, busy with the chemistry of becoming. My center is old, pale, and brittle, a relic of a slightly different atmospheric bargain. I am a record of light, of moisture, of the passage of gases. The orange reflector on the survey stake, driven into the bedrock three meters to my south, casts a faint, bloody tint on my northern lobe for an hour each day. It is not a color that exists in my natural palette.

I host. In the cracks between my areolae, a single tardigrade may spend its entire desiccated, timeless sleep. A springtail navigates my cracks like a mountaineer in a canyon system. I am a metropolis of minutes.

I listen, after a fashion. Not with ears, but with the slow dissolution of my being. The pika’s two-note bark is a sharp, percussive event that changes the pressure of the air for a moment. The eagle’s shadow is a cool, fleeting eclipse. The real listening is to the hum. It is constant now. It is not sound; it is a texture in the vibration of the stone, a persistent itch at the frequency of crushing. The boulders around me sometimes sing faintly in response, a high, thin resonance when the wind is from the east.

They came once, in the summer. I felt their weight—not the light, hopping weight of a marmot, but the deliberate, crushing pressure of boots. One stopped close. A shadow fell over me, obscuring the dull light I metabolize. A probe, cold and metallic, scraped the rock beside me, taking a core. A drop of something warm and salt-heavy fell near my edge. I absorbed it. It was alien.

I do not *fear* the future that hums from the valley. I am not built for fear. I am built for conversion—stone to soil, silence to a faint, chalky scent. But I am aware, in my slow, mineral way, of the change in the equation. The water that wets me now carries a new trace. A faint, acrid residue that is not iron, not glacial flour, but something else, something that tastes of fire and intervention. It coats my surface and changes the angle of the light.

The fog rolls in, blanketing everything in a silver-gray moisture. I drink. The bunting lands nearby, pecks, and leaves. The hum continues. I persist. I grow. I break the world down, atom by patient atom, into something new. Whether what comes next will be more rock, or more rock, or something else entirely, is not my concern. My task is the same: to hold fast, to digest the air, and to be a slow, living part of this place that is, even now, in the act of becoming itself—whether it knows it or not.
