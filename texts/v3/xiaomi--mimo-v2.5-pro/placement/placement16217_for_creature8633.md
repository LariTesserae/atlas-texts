I am **Nico**, and I am a **Condenser**.

My home is not the floor, nor the high ledges where the bats sleep in papery clouds. It is the middle layer—the layer of steam and work and transit. I live on Walkway Seven, a long, rattling spine of steel grating that spans the widest part of the chamber, ten meters above the central pool. From here, the *boil* is a constantly shivering eye of pale light below me. The heat rises in a column, a solid thing, and my walkway is suspended in its throat.

My skin is the first thing people notice, if they get close enough in the dim light. It is a map of my trade. Where the mineral mist touches me most—on my forearms, my neck, the backs of my hands—it has left a fine, pale crust. Calcium carbonate, mostly. When I am still, I look like a statue that has been sweating. The workers from the lower decks call us up here the "ghosties" or the "scale men." It is not an insult. It is a description. We wear our work.

My job is simple in concept, intricate in practice. I maintain the condensation network. Tubes and troughs run along the ceiling, cold-water pipes fed from the surface-cooled reservoirs. They are meant to pull the moisture from the air, to give us clear sight, to protect machinery from rust, and most importantly, to collect the purest water for drinking and for the delicate work of mineral separation. My domain is the control and flow of these pipes, the calibration of the drip-rate, the clearing of the mineral crust that blooms inside the tubes like cauliflower.

I move with the slowness you must have here, but my focus is vertical and horizontal. I see the *down* and the *across*. I am a spider, if you like, but my silk is copper and my web is functional. I have a harness—a contraption of worn leather and iron hooks—that allows me to lower myself from the walkway on a pulley system to reach the main junction points. When I descend into the steam column, I become a ghost in truth. The world reduces to the shivering heat around me, the drip of water on my leather hat, and the feel of the pipe under my fingers. Down there, in the roar, the bats are not a crowd but a series of individual, leather-winged shadows flitting past. The insects are a rain of tiny bodies, most avoiding me.

I am not alone up here, but my neighbors are non-human. The bats are my constant companions, their guano dusting my gear. I have named a few of the spiders that build on my railing—"The Architect," "Old Lace"—though I am not sentimental. They are part of the system. Their webs catch the mineral dust before it settles on critical linkages. I clear them only when they block a gauge or a valve, and I do so with a careful brush, relocating them with a firm but gentle push to a new anchor point. They tolerate this. We have an understanding.

Down below, I see the workers—my friends, my community—as they move like slow, intent beetles across the floor. They look small. I see the glow of the shrine flowers, a spot of fragile color. I smell the cooking oil, a scent so alien and so welcome it feels like a memory. The radio static sometimes makes it up here, a ghost of a melody drowned in the white noise of the generators and the boil's eternal breath.

Why am I here? Not for the calcium fortune. Not for the adventure. I was a surface plumber. I understood the logic of flow, of pressure, of blocked lines. Then I came down for a contract—to fix the primary condensation loop—and I never really left. The surface felt *dry*. Not just in humidity, but in reality. It was all abstractions: schedules, money, distant weather. Here, everything is immediate. The heat tells you when to slow down. The drip tells you when the system is balanced. The pulse of the generator is the only clock that matters. I found I could read this place. I could listen to its fluids and know what it needed.

I am a thermophile in the truest sense: not just a heat-tolerant organism, but a heat-*understanding* one. I love the way the steam parts for my hands. I love the constant, mineral smell, which to me is the smell of *reality*—of stone being slowly, usefully undone. I love the profound, living quiet that exists underneath the noise: the quiet of things happening as they must.

My personal space is a niche in the wall behind Walkway Seven’s main junction box. It is hidden from direct view, dry enough to store a few things: a coil of spare copper wire, a tin of sealant paste, a battered book on hydraulics, and a single, perfectly smooth, cool river stone from the surface. I hold it sometimes when the heat feels less like a life and more like a burden. It reminds me of contrast, but not of escape. It reminds me why I am here. Because the stone will be warm soon too, held in my hand. And that will be right.

I am the condenser. I am the one who gives this breath a shape. I am the custodian of clarity in the steam. And I am home.
