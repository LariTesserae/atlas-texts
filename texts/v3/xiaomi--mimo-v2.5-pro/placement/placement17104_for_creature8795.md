I would be a **trailing selaginella**, a spikemoss, not quite a true moss but fitting their world. My form is a low, creeping mat of minute, scale-like leaves, deep green with a faint, bristly texture. I am neither the first wave nor the commanding canopy. I exist in the in-between, the humid understory.

I would find myself clinging to a north-facing ledge of andesite, halfway up the inner wall of the basin. Here, the rock is perpetually weeping with condensation. I am not anchored in deep soil—there is none—but in a microscopic world of my own making: a packed mat of my own stems and the captured particles of decaying fern fronds, shed bark from the leaning trees above, and the fine, gritty dust of mineral precipitation. My roots are shallow, an embrace of cool, wet stone.

From here, my world is a vertical tapestry. Below, the fern thicket chokes the basin floor in a dark, dripping sea. The constant *hiss* and periodic *thump* of the central vent are felt in my tissues, a pulse I have grown into. I taste the air—sulfur, always, like a faint memory of fire, but also the sweeter exhalation of wet earth and the green, mulchy scent of the ferns. The light is a uniform, pearlescent grey, a glow that seems to emanate from the steam itself. I photosynthesize slowly, patiently, in this softened dusk.

I am a community. Lichen, pale and crustose, binds parts of me to the rock. A tiny, almost microscopic fern (*Hymenophyllum*, perhaps, a filmy fern) has found purchase in my mat, its translucent fronds a delicate green film over my own bristly form. We share the moisture. We are a collective island.

My life is a negotiation with three forces: **gravity, water, and time.**

**Gravity** wants to pull my mat downward. I counter it by growing *into* the rock, my stems finding every hairline crack, secreting a faint acid to etch a better hold. I am a slow-motion vertigo, a green lacework always on the verge of letting go, yet never doing so.

**Water** is both my sustenance and my adversary. I drink it from the air, from the constantly wet rock. But too much threatens to smother me, to rot my heartwood. I have learned to channel it. The architecture of my tiny leaves directs rivulets along my stems and off the ledge in a perpetual, silent weeping. I am a gutter, a collector and conveyor of the basin’s exhalations.

**Time** is measured in shifts. Not the sun (I rarely see it) but in the growth of the fern canopy above me, in the encroachment of the moss from below, in the slow, mineral crusting of the rock face beside me. My own time is one of millimeters per season. I am aware, in my way, of the basin’s larger time—the groaning of the earth, the occasional sharp *crack* of a new fracture opening somewhere distant, the way the steam plumes wander over months. I have been here for decades, I think. I remember a small rockfall that scoured the ledge below me, opening new bare rock for the mosses to claim. I remember a change in the scent of the vent, a sharper acidity for a season that made my edges curl.

I am not a passive inhabitant. I am an active participant in the basin’s saturation. Every inch I grow is a claim, a stabilization, a contribution to the living skin that is slowly, inevitably, softening the violent geometry of the rock. The ferns above me would not have such a purchase without my kind and the mosses creating this initial, clinging soil. I am part of the **infrastructure of resilience.**

If a slab were to calve from the wall above, I would be buried. If the vent’s chemistry shifted dangerously, I might wither. My existence is precarious, balanced on the exact, unstable conditions of this room. But within that precarity, I am utterly *at home*. The damp, the dark-green light, the taste of stone and sulfur, the constant hum and hiss—this is my element. I do not struggle against it; I *am* its expression, a quiet, bristly, deep-green thought in the mind of the vent, a minor but tenacious player in the grand, unfinished drama of Alto Pluma.
