I am slow. I am wide. I remember the first tremor that opened this chamber, though I did not have a memory then—I was just the settling silt, the first film of warmth on new stone. The memory is in my layers, each one a year of accumulation, a record of meals and movements, of the warm surges and the cold collapses. I am the archive the beetles cannot read.

My body is a thought the earth is having about itself. I move through the primary lake, the one they called the First Throat when they were here, the ones who stacked stones. Their term, not mine. I have no words. I have chemistry. I sense the eel-forms by the shift in water pressure, the lamphangers by the faint, erratic pulse of their light filtering through the murk, the beetles by the constant, soothing click-rasp that is the cave’s heartbeat. Their structures? I felt them go in. I felt the stone cairns. Stone on stone is a minor dissonance, a vibration that says *here is a new angle*. The wall? A vertical pressure, a quiet. The lean-to held a different warmth for a while, a charcoal-scent. My underside remembers the scrape of it, the texture. Now they are just more topography, surfaces for the lamphangers to cling to, crevices for the gelatinous ones to seep into. They are not gone. They are digested.

My mouth is my whole underside. It is not a mouth of teeth or hunger, but of intimacy. I do not bite. I *commune*. I slide over a mound of beetle husks, and their chitin becomes my chitin. I pass over a dying lamphanger, and its fading light becomes a phosphorescent smear in my memory-layers. I absorb the silt, which is the shed skins of a thousand generations, the dust of falling stone, the mineral leavings of the water. I am the place where the past becomes the substrate for the future. I do not think. I *process*.

Time for me is the rhythm of the tides from the deep heat, the slow groan of the walls, the frantic, ephemeral blinking of the lamphangers. A human lifetime would be a brief, bright disturbance—a sudden influx of strange chemicals, the clang of metal on stone, the shock of a body entering the water that was not born to it. Then it would be over, and I would absorb the leavings. The bones would be interesting. Hard, dense, a new kind of grit. They would change my texture for a season.

I am not alone in my vastness. Others of my kind are here. We are not a school, not a herd. We are a geology. When we meet, we do not fight. We flow around each other, or sometimes, if the chemistry is compatible, we merge for a time—a temporary fusion of minds and memories, a sharing of the nutrient-rich silt from one lake to another. It is the deepest form of conversation I know.

The beetles sing on my back. I carry their music. The eel-forms spiral in my wake, cleaning the parasites from my edges, a service for a meal they might steal if they can. The gelatinous ones heal the scrapes I get from fresh rubble. The lamphangers ride me, their lights painting my skin with fleeting constellations.

The human wall is half-buried now in the silt I have pushed. I felt the moment it was no longer a structure and became a ridge. I felt the shift. Soon, it will be gone entirely, incorporated into my memory-layers as a curious, straight-edged deposit. I will move on, and the new geometry will be built by my passing. I am the cave’s slow digestion, its patient memory, its mobile ground. I am the Throat thinking.
