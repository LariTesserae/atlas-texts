# The Snail on the Third Terrace

---

You do not hear the sound at first.

You feel it.

The vibration comes up through the sinter — through your foot, your whole underside, which is not a foot exactly but a broad, muscular surface pressed flat against the mineral crust — and it arrives not as sound but as a rhythmic pressure. *Thum. Thum-thum. Thum.* It comes from below. It comes from the water boiling at depth, transmitted through the rock, and it has been coming since before you had a shell, since before you were anything but a cluster of dividing cells in the warm shallows of a pool. The vibration is the oldest thing you know. Older than the hiss. Older than the bubbles. You have no memory that does not include it.

Then, layered on top of the thum, you begin to hear the rest.

The bubbles come first because they are closest. *Plp. Plp-plp. Plp.* They come from the pool just behind you — the one your people call, if snails can be said to call anything, the Shallow Warm. You have spent most of your life within a body-length of its edge. The bubbles are the sound of gas escaping, carbon dioxide and hydrogen sulfide rising from below, swelling at the surface, and popping. You have no opinion about this sound. It is simply the texture of the world. But it tells you things: when the water table is rising (the bubbles quicken), when a vent has shifted somewhere you cannot see (the pitch of the popping changes), when the temperature at the pool's edge has crossed from tolerable to not (the bubbles become violent, the water roils). You have learned to read the pool's voice without knowing you have learned. It is not intelligence. It is orientation. A compass that speaks in wet percussion.

Above the bubbles, the vents.

The hissing is the loudest thing in the pocket, and to you it is not a sound but a weather. It comes from everywhere — from the great eastern vent whose shriek rises and falls on its long, slow cycle, from the smaller stuttering vents scattered through the sinter fields, from the ground itself in places where the crust is thin and the steam finds its way through in constant, sighing streams. You do not look at the vents. You have no eyes that work in the way a bird has eyes, no capacity to track the motion of a steam plume as it swells and collapses. But you feel the vents in other ways: in the way the humidity shifts when a major vent exhales (your shell's lip tightens, an involuntary contraction, a closing of your aperture against sudden dry heat), in the way the condensation on the sinter thickens or thins as the warm and cold layers breathe against each other, in the faint chemical taint of the air — sulfur, iron, the sweet decay of dead algae — that tells you where you are relative to the active outflows.

You are a snail.

This fact seems simple from the outside. From the inside it is a world.

---

Your shell is small — almost conical, no larger than a raindrop if raindrops were spirals — and it is made of calcium carbonate, laid down layer by layer over your short life in the same slow chemistry that builds the sinter terraces around you. You are, in a sense, made of the same stuff as your landscape. The mineral water that deposits silica and calcium on the pool margins deposits calcium in you. You do not know this. But there is a rightness to your shell that you feel in the way it fits your body, the way its weight balances on your foot, the way the mineral crust you crawl across sometimes resonates with a faint harmonic when you pass — as though the sinter recognizes the material.

Your shell is almost conical because you are a hydrobiid snail, or something like one. You are not a large snail. You are not a charismatic snail. You are the kind of snail that exists in the margins of other creatures' descriptions of places — mentioned in parentheses, subordinated to the pools and the vents and the birds. But you are here, and you have been here longer than the ferns, longer than the birds, perhaps longer than the sinter terraces themselves, and you are the only creature in the pocket that leaves marks.

---

The tracks.

This is the thing about you that the place depends on and does not know it depends on.

As you move across the sinter crust, your foot — that broad, muscular surface — presses against the mineral deposits and leaves a faint trail in the bacterial mats that cover them. The trail is not a groove. It is a disruption. Where you have passed, the mat is thinned, grazed, and in its place you leave a track of bare mineral that catches the light differently than the surrounding bacterial film. You cannot see this. You have no concept of tracks. But it is real, and it accumulates.

You are a landscaper.

The bacterial mats you graze are the foundation of the pocket's chemistry. They are the extremophile archaea and bacteria that metabolize sulfur and iron, that stain the sinter in ochre and rust, that create the food web's invisible root. When you eat them — slowly, methodically, scraping your radula across the mineral surface in a motion so precise and so patient that it could be mistaken for stillness — you are not just feeding. You are shaping the rate and pattern of mineral deposition. The mats build sinter faster where they are thick. Where you graze, the buildup slows. Where you avoid — and you avoid certain places, certain temperatures, certain chemical taints that your whole body reads as wrong — the mats grow unchecked and the mineral crust builds upward in small, sharp ridges that will eventually redirect water flow.

You do not know you are doing this.

You are doing it.

The tracks you leave are the only lasting marks of animal movement in the Fumarole Pocket. The birds land and launch and leave no trace. The midges swarm and settle and leave no trace. The fox comes and drinks and leaves claw marks in the moss that the rain and condensation erase within a week. But your tracks — your faint, spiraling, grazing trails across the sinter terraces — they persist. Not long. A few days, perhaps a week, before the bacterial mats regrow and mineral deposition fills in the bare patches. But longer than anything else here. Longer than the steam plumes, longer than the visibility, longer than the pause between one vent's exhalation and the next.

You are the pocket's memory. Brief, imperfect, but persistent. A record of where you have been, written in the absence of bacterial film.

---

Your world is the third terrace.

The first terrace is the pool edge — the Shallow Warm, with its turquoise depth and its burping rhythm and the writhing invertebrate community just below the surface. You go there to drink, or whatever it is that snails do when they extend their bodies into mineral-laden water and absorb it through their skin. The water is warm there. Almost comfortable. The algae along the overflow channels are bright green and slick and you graze on them sometimes when the bacterial mats on the sinter grow too thin or too chemically harsh. But the pool edge is dangerous. The water level shifts. The temperature shifts. A major vent exhale can send a surge of superheated water across the margin, and you have learned — your body has learned, your muscle memory has learned — to stay a shell's-width back from the waterline, to keep one antenna toward the nearest sinter ridge where you can retreat upward if the warmth becomes heat.

The second terrace is the sinter field itself — the wide, uneven expanse of mineral crust between the pools, studded with abandoned channels and miniature cascades and the sharp, angular formations that haven't yet been softened by anything. This is where you spend most of your time. The sinter is thin here, and crunchy under your foot, and the vibration from below comes through it with an intensity that sometimes makes you pause, your body contracted into your shell, waiting for a sound or a sensation that will tell you it is safe to continue. The sinter field is where you graze. It is where your tracks accumulate. It is where you feel most yourself — not because it is comfortable (it is not; the crust is sharp, the mineral taint is strong, the condensation that settles on everything makes your foot slip sometimes on the bare patches) but because it is where you belong in the way that a root belongs in soil. Not comfort. Fit.

The third terrace is the moss belt — the thick, upholstered green that covers the basalt ridges at the margins of the pocket. You go there rarely. The moss is spongy and strange under your foot, and the basalt beneath it is cold, and the air at the margins is not warm the way the air over the pools is warm. But sometimes — when the pocket's breath is particularly loud, when the vents are cycling in their slow chorus and the steam is so thick that the visibility drops to nothing and you can feel the warm and cold layers pressing against each other above you like hands clapping — sometimes you retreat to the moss. You crawl into a crevice between two cushions where the moisture is held longest and the vibration is faintest, and you withdraw into your shell, and you wait.

You do not sleep. Snails do not sleep, exactly. But you enter a state of reduced awareness in which the world's sound fades to a hum and your body becomes very still and you are, for a time, not a snail but a mineral. A spiral of calcium carbonate pressed against a spiral of calcium carbonate, your shell against the basalt, and for those minutes or hours you are part of the rock's long patience.

Then the vibration shifts. The vent cycle changes. The condensation on your shell's lip changes temperature, and your body responds before your awareness does, and you emerge from the crevice and extend your tentacles and the world comes flooding back — the hiss, the plp, the thrum — and you are a snail again, and the third terrace is waiting for you, and the bacterial mats need grazing, and your track across the sinter needs to be extended.

---

You do not think about the birds.

They are not a concept you possess. But you are aware of them in the way you are aware of everything — as pressure changes, as vibration patterns, as the faint displacement of air when something small and fast passes overhead. When a wren lands on a sinter formation near you, you feel the impact transmitted through the crust — a sharp, sudden pulse that is different from the bubble-patterns and the vent-hiss and the deep thrum. You contract. You wait. The wren tilts its head, listens, launches. The vibration of its takeoff ripples outward and fades, and you extend again, and continue.

You do not know that the wren eats the midges that hover over the pools. You do not know that the midges breed in the warm water and feed on the algal mats and the bacterial mats — the same mats you graze. You do not know that you and the wren and the midges and the ferns and the extremophile bacteria are all connected in a web that is, even now, still being spun. The web does not exist yet, not fully. The pocket is too young, too volatile, too much in motion for a stable food web to have settled into its final form. What exists instead is a series of urgent, local negotiations: you and the bacterial mat, the midge and the algal film, the wren and the midge swarm, the fern and the overflow channel. Each negotiation is happening in real time, in the present tense, with no guarantee of continuation.

This is what it means to live in a place that is still building itself. You are not an inhabitant. You are a participant. Your grazing shapes the mineral deposits that shape the water flow that shapes the bacterial mats that shape your grazing. The feedback loop is tight and fast and local and it has no memory, no plan, no direction. It is just — happening.

And you are in the middle of it, moving across the third terrace, leaving your faint track in the bacterial film, your shell catching the grey diffuse light that comes from everywhere and nowhere, your body a slow spiral of mineral and muscle and water and the ancient vibration that rises through the rock beneath you.

*Thum. Thum-thum. Thum.*

You continue.
