# Dropstone

---

You would not find me on the basin floor. Not at first, and maybe not ever, if you were looking with the wrong eyes.

I am not one of the builders, though I have their hands — broad, clay-stained, the knuckles thickened from years of pressing mortar into seams I could not see. I am not one of the fungal keepers, though I have spent mornings so deep in the dark branches of the eastern caves that my own breathing became the only orientation I had, and I learned to identify the mat's growth-stage by the smell it released when I touched it — sour-alive when young, dusty-sweet when ready to harvest. I am not one of the children who leap the gaps, though I was once, and I remember the exact vibration in my shins when I misjudged the stone and found myself looking up at the sky through the gap between what I'd aimed for and where I'd landed.

I am something the settlement has no word for because it has never needed to name it.

I sit with the water.

---

Let me be more precise.

The braided stream shifts. This is the central fact of the basin, more fundamental than the walls, more immediate than the rain. The stream is not a single thing. It is a decision the water makes and remakes, over and over, following gradients so subtle that you could not trace them with your eyes, only with your body — by kneeling, by pressing your palm flat to the silt, by feeling where the coolness pools, where the current whispers against your skin differently, where the ground beneath you has a faint, almost imperceptible tremor that tells you the water has found a new seam and is beginning to carve it.

The builders have their load-bearing seams. The fungal keepers have their cycles. I have the stream.

I was not assigned this. There was no ceremony, no elder who placed a hand on my shoulder and said *you will watch the water*. What happened was simpler and less dramatic, in the way everything here is simpler and less dramatic than it should be: I began to notice. And once I began to notice, I could not stop.

The noticing started — I think I was eleven or twelve, though we don't count years the way surface people might — with a single observation that changed the shape of my days. I was crossing the lower basin in the early morning, the mist still thick enough to lose the wall-silhouettes in, and I stepped into water where I had stepped into dry silt the day before. Not much water. Just enough to darken the edge of my foot-wrap, just enough to feel the silt give slightly differently under my weight. I stopped. I looked down. The water was coming from nowhere I could see — no spring, no channel, no runoff from the walls. It was simply *there*, seeping up through the ground in a thin, even sheet, as though the basin floor had decided to become something else for the morning.

I stood there long enough that the mist began to lift and the first light touched the eastern wall and I watched the water move. Not flow — *move*. It advanced in one direction, slowly, a centimeter at a time, drawn by something I could not identify. By midday it had gathered into a shallow channel that hadn't existed at dawn. By evening the channel was two handspans wide and had begun to braid around a pile of rubble someone had left from a wall repair.

The next day the channel was different. The day after that, different again.

I began to watch.

---

You would find me, if you found me, in the lower basin, often on my knees, sometimes lying flat with my cheek against the silt. I know how this looks. The builders have glanced at me with the particular expression reserved for people who are useful but inexplicable. The children, bolder, have asked what I am doing. *Listening*, I tell them, and they accept this with the practical credulity of children who already know that the stone talks if you press your ear to it.

What I am actually doing is more complex than listening, though listening is part of it. I am reading the ground. The silt tells me things. Its texture changes — finer where the water has been still longer, coarser where the current has been stronger. The color shifts: pale grey where it is mostly limestone wash, darker with clay, occasionally streaked with the rust of iron-oxide leached from the upper walls. I can tell by running my fingers through it how recently the water was here, how long it stayed, and which direction it left in.

I map the basin floor in my mind. Not with lines on stone — the builders tried that once, scratched channels into a slab, and the next rain made the map a lie. I map it with my body, with the accumulated memory of thousands of mornings kneeling in slightly different places, feeling slightly different things. The map is not spatial. It is temporal. I carry in me a sense of what the water *tends to do*, the way a fisherman carries a sense of where the fish will be — not certainty, but a grammar of probability. The stream wants to move south-southeast in spring, when the snowmelt from whatever lies beyond the rim finds its way down the eastern wall. It wants to pool and spread in late summer, when the seep is slow and the silt is dry enough to crumble rather than flow. In autumn, when the rains are heavy and the walls weep in earnest, it becomes something else entirely — not a stream but a sheet, a film, a temporary lake that makes the entire lower basin uninhabitable and sends everyone to the terraces. I have watched this cycle repeat enough times that I can feel its rhythm in my own body, the way the builders feel the rhythm of load and weight, the way the fungal keepers feel the rhythm of decay and fruiting.

---

This is what I do with the knowledge, and this is why the settlement tolerates my peculiar posture:

I tell them where to build.

Not in words, usually. The builders do not respond well to words about the ground — they respond to demonstration. So I will go to a terrace, or a patch of basin floor where someone wants to raise a new storage lean-to, and I will kneel, and I will press my palms down, and I will feel the silt and the stone beneath it, and I will say: *this ground was wet three days ago and it will be wet again in four.* Or: *the water passed through here last season but it has turned — this stone is stable now.* Or, more rarely, and more urgently: *do not build here. The seep has found a new joint. Give it a week and this place will be a channel.*

Sometimes they listen. Sometimes they build anyway, and then, days or weeks later, they come to me with a look that is not quite gratitude and not quite irritation, and they say nothing, and the next time I speak they listen more carefully.

I do not command. I have no authority except the authority of having been right. And even that is provisional — the water is not obedient, even to me. It has surprised me. It will again. I have warned against building in places that stayed dry for years. I have failed to predict a new spring that emerged overnight and undermined a foundation before dawn. The water is patient and I am not omniscient. But I am better than nothing, and in a place where every square meter of buildable ground is precious, better than nothing is enough to be valued.

---

I live on the third terrace, eastern side, in a structure so narrow I can touch both walls if I stretch my arms. One wall is raw limestone, seamed with a thin line of iron-oxide that I have watched for years without change — the one stable thing in my life, perhaps the one stable seam in the basin. The other wall is wattle-and-daub, patched three times where the seep from above has softened it. My roof is pitched steeply and I have lined the interior with the same treated hide the basin-floor structures use, not because the terrace floods — it doesn't — but because the condensation is relentless and I have learned to prefer a dry sleeping surface to a wet one.

I keep almost nothing. A bone awl, inherited from my mother, who was not a water-reader but a wall-chinker of extraordinary skill, her hands always white with clay dust until the day she couldn't grip the stones anymore and sat on the terrace and watched the others work with an expression I now recognize as the particular grief of a body that knows what to do but can no longer do it. A woven blanket, the fiber gathered from the moss-gathered drying racks and spun into something almost warm. A collection of stones — not sacred, not decorative, but *informative*. Each one came from a different layer of the walls, and I can tell you by holding one what it will do when wet: whether it will swell, whether it will weep, whether it will hold. These are my tools. They are few and I maintain them with obsessive care, as everyone here maintains their tools, because a broken tool in a place with no surplus is a small catastrophe.

My days begin with the drip. I wake in the grey hour before the first light touches the eastern wall, when the mist is at its thickest and the basin is less a place than a suggestion — the faintest darkening of air where the walls must be, the sound of water everywhere and nowhere. I pull on my layers. I step out onto the terrace and look down.

From the third terrace, in the mist, the basin floor is invisible. I can hear it — the first footsteps, the scrape of someone clearing a channel, the low call from one structure to another — but I cannot see it. I stand and listen and the mist moves around me, and I feel the air on my face and note its temperature, its weight, the particular quality of moisture that tells me whether today will be wet or merely damp, whether the seep will increase or hold steady, whether the stream will shift or sit.

Then I go down.

---

The descent to the basin floor is a choreography I have performed so many times I no longer think about it. The terraces are connected by paths — not stairs, not ramps, but *suggestions* of route, worn into the stone by the passage of feet over what might be years or might be decades. The rock is still sharp here, still unweathered, and the paths are narrow enough that you must place each foot with intention. I have seen the builders move down these paths carrying timber, their bodies leaning back, their feet finding the holds with the unconscious precision of people who have learned to trust the ground they live on. I move the same way, but my attention is different. I am not watching for stable footing — I am watching for the *signs*. Where the path is darker than usual. Where the stone is slick not with condensation but with actual flow. Where a new rivulet has appeared from a fissure that was dry yesterday.

At the basin floor, I go to the stream first. Always. The stream is the basin's pulse. I wade in — the water is cold, always cold, cold enough that after a few minutes my feet go numb and I must rely on my eyes and my hands rather than my skin to tell me what the silt is doing. I move upstream. I move downstream. I kneel in the water and press my palms to the bed and feel the current's weight against my fingers, the way it pushes, the angle of its push, the force of it. I note where the braids have shifted, where new channels have begun to form, where old ones are silting up. I pick up a handful of silt and squeeze it and watch how the water runs from between my fingers — fast and clear means the silt is coarse and the ground is draining well; slow and milky means fine clay and the ground is becoming saturated.

This takes most of the morning.

By midday, I have usually found someone to speak to. Not to report — I am not a messenger, and the people here do not respond to reports. But I will be near a builder who is inspecting a foundation, and I will say, *the stream has moved three handspans east since the last rain*, and the builder will nod, and later that day the foundation will be shifted three handspans east, and neither of us will acknowledge the transaction because it does not need acknowledgment. It is simply how things work here. The water moves; the building moves. The water is the senior partner in this negotiation, and everyone knows it, and my job is to translate.

---

In the evenings, when the work slows and the shadows from the eastern wall begin their long crawl across the basin floor, I sometimes sit near the stream and watch the mist form. This is not work. This is something else — something closer to what the others do when they leave their small artifacts in the joints of the stone. I am paying attention. Not to learn, not to predict, but simply to witness. The mist forms first over the water, where the air and the water are closest in temperature, and it rises in slow, twisting columns that catch the last light and glow faintly, briefly, before dissolving into the grey. It is beautiful, though I would not use that word. The people here do not speak of beauty. They speak of *fit* — of things that are as they should be, that find their place and hold it. The mist fits. It is the basin's breath, exhaled at the end of the day, settling into the low places, covering the silt, softening the edges.

Sometimes, in these moments, I press my hand to the nearest wall. Not to read it — just to feel it. The stone is cool, always, and faintly damp, and beneath the surface you can feel, if you are patient and still, the faintest vibration — not the drip, not the settling, but something deeper and slower, something that might be the stone itself, still remembering the pressure of the earth it was torn from, still adjusting to the light. I do not know if this is true. I do not need to know. What I need is the contact — the palm against the stone, the body against the place, the acknowledgment that I am here and it is here and we are, for the moment, the same thing.

---

I am not old. But I am not young either. My knees are worn from kneeling. My hands are mapped with fine scars from sharp stone and sharp silt. My hair is never fully dry and has not been for as long as I can remember. I am lean in the way the people here are lean — not hungry, but *pared*, the body shaped by the demands of constant movement on uneven ground. I wear the colors of the basin: grey wool against my skin, shale-dark hide over it, a strip of bleached moss-fiber binding my hair back from my face. I could not be distinguished from anyone else here by appearance. What distinguishes me is what I attend to. Where others watch the walls, I watch the floor. Where others listen for the scrape of stone, I listen for the whisper of water. We are all reading the same book. I am simply on a different page.

---

If you came to the basin — down the unnamed slope from the featureless plain, your feet finding the rubble, your eyes adjusting to the sudden enclosure of the walls — you would not find me first. You would find the settlement, the packed structures, the low voices, the constant movement. You would find the walls and the moss and the mist. You would find, eventually, someone who could point you in my direction, though they would do so with a shrug that meant *why do you need him? The water is right there.*

But if you persisted, and if you came to the lower basin in the morning, you would find me on my knees in the stream, my hands flat on the silt, my eyes closed. You might think I was praying. You might think I was lost. You would be wrong on both counts. I would be listening to the water tell me where it is going next, and the water would be telling me, and I would be hearing it, and the basin would be dripping around us both in its slow, patient metronome, and the mist would be lifting, and the light would be finding the eastern wall, and we would be here — all of us, the stone and the water and the people and the moss and the mist and me — pressed together in this raw, unfinished wound in the earth, making our arrangement, clearing our channels, holding on.

I am the one who kneels. I am the one who feels the silt shift. I am the one who knows, in the particular way of long attention, that the stream is turning east again, that the lower foundations will need to be moved by autumn, that the wall on the third terrace — *my* wall, the one with the iron-oxide seam — has started, for the first time in years, to weep.

I am not worried. I am listening.
