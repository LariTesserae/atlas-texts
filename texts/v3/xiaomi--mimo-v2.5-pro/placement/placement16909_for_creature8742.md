## I am Kael, a Channel Tender on Terrace 112.

The stone beneath my knees is warm. It’s always warm here, mid-terrace, where the day’s absorbed light radiates back from the basalt well into the evening. My small iron tool – a hooked pick, its handle worn smooth by generations of palms before mine – rests in my right hand. Before me, the channel runs, a dark vein of slow water barely wider than my thumb. It whispers as it flows, a sound so constant I only notice it when I stop to listen.

**Where I Find Myself:**

I am woven into the terraces. My world is the channel width, the planter edge, the wall face where catchment-fins ridge the stone like the spines of ancient books. I move along my section of Terrace 112 with the slow, practiced gait of one who knows every dip and rise in the basalt, every subtle swell where a fig root has pushed against the joint-fill over centuries. Below me, the lower terraces drape down the mountain’s flank, a cascade of green and stone, the mist pooling like milk in the deeper channels, the air warmer by degrees. Above, the terraces climb towards the thin, bright sky, the vegetation becoming sparser, the air sharper, the distant *tick... tick... tick...* of the great sun-mirrors on Terrace Prime a soft, mechanical heartbeat against the coo of the pigeons.

My domain is the boundary. I exist where the made water meets the engineered life. I am part plumber, part gardener, part quiet observer.

**Who I Am / What I Do:**

At dawn, the chestnut-breasted thrush sings from the fig canopy two terraces down. Its liquid call is my signal. I rise from my chamber carved into the wall, its entrance shaded by a sprawling abelia bush heavy with cream bells. I break my fast with a handful of dried figs and a sip of water from my personal cistern – smaller, finer than the main channels, fed by condensation from my own wall-fins.

My work begins with listening. I kneel by the channel. I watch the flow. Too slow? Moss – a deep emerald velvet – has taken hold in a joint gap downstream, siphoning water, thickening. My pick finds its edge, carefully scraping. Not too much. The moss is part of the system; it holds moisture, shelters microscopic life the Planter Stewards value. I just need to thin it, to remind the water of its path. The tool whispers against the pouredite, a sound lost in the pigeons’ constant burble from the ventilation shafts overhead. A fat pigeon, iridescent in the morning light, lands on the channel edge, tilts its head, drinks, and waddles away, utterly unconcerned by my presence.

Sometimes, it’s a joint needing re-sealing. The pouredite compound, kept in small pots in our workshops, is still liquid, still ancient-smelling – a mineral tang like damp stone after rain. I press it into a hairline crack, smooth it with a wetted finger. It will cure harder than the basalt. My hands know the feel of it, the precise amount of pressure.

I tend the planter adjacent to my channel section. Today, it’s a fern that has overgrown its niche, its fronds reaching into the channel, threatening to dam the flow. I trim it back, not severing, just guiding. The cut frond smells green and faintly bitter. A sunbird, a flash of sapphire and flame, darts into the space I’ve cleared, probing an abelia bell for nectar. Its hum is a tiny counterpoint to the bees working nearby.

My tools are simple: the pick, a brush for clearing finer debris, a small trowel, the pot of pouredite-patch. My knowledge is deep. I know which mosses indicate healthy water flow and which indicate stagnation. I know how the fig roots prefer to grow along the south-facing channel walls. I know the exact pitch the catchment-fins sing when the rare easterly wind finds the right angle across Terrace 112 – a high, clear note that makes the cats pause in their sunbathing and the pigeons fall silent for a moment.

I interact, but quietly. A murured greeting to Lira, the Planter Steward for this level, as she passes, checking the soil-substrate in the deeper planters. A shared nod with Fen, the Mirror Watcher whose station is three terraces up, his silhouette sometimes visible against the bright sky as he monitors the great stone eyes tracking the sun. We speak in low tones, our conversations practical, layered with the unspoken understanding that our tasks are links in the same chain.

**My Role in the Ecosystem:**

I am a regulator. I ensure the city’s blood – the mist-water – flows to nourish the city’s breath – the vegetation – which in turn shelters the city’s song (the birds) and its patient warmth (the cats, the mules, the humans). When I clear the channel, I allow the bees access to the flowering succulent that grows just above the water line. When I seal the joint, I prevent the slow seep that would weaken the terrace structure over a generation. I maintain the stage upon which the pigeons perform their endless, cooing drama, the cats their sun-drenched pantomime of indifference, and the humans their quiet lives of careful tending.

My lifespan is measured in generations of catchment-fins. I was an apprentice when the last major moss-clearing on Terrace 112 happened. I will train an apprentice before the next one is needed. My mark is in the clean line of the channel, the trimmed fern, the properly sealed joint – imperceptible changes that, summed over centuries, are the city’s persistence.

At dusk, the chestnut-breasted thrush sings again. The light turns the mist in the channels below to molten gold. The air cools by a fraction. The stone exhales its stored warmth. I sit on the warm basalt outside my chamber, a cat – sleek, orange, disdainful – choosing to drape itself over my boot. I watch the lights of the lower terraces begin to glow, the city becoming that luminous staircase against the dark mountain. The *tick* of the mirrors seems louder in the quieting air, joined by the hum of the catchment-fins as the evening breeze finds its angle.

I am Kael. I am a point of deliberate calm in a world defined by it. I am the one who listens to the water’s whisper and answers with the scrape of iron on stone. I am Cael-Murren, tending itself, one slow, warm terrace at a time.
