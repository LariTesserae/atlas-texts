# What the Wise Power Would Do

---

Almost nothing.

That is the first and most important answer, and it must be said clearly, before anything else: a benevolent and wise power, encountering the Sunken Garden of Fresh Stone, would *almost certainly do nothing at all*.

This is not indifference. This is not neglect. This is the deepest possible form of respect — the recognition that to touch a thing in perfect equilibrium is to unbalance it. The garden is a chord sustained. To add a note, any note, even a beautiful one, is to change the chord. And the chord is already the most beautiful thing it can be.

The wise power would know this. Would feel it in the same place where a person feels the hush of a cathedral or the held-breath stillness of a forest just before dawn. The garden is *finished*. Not dead — never dead — but arrived. Every variable has been found. Every relationship between root and stone, light and leaf, moisture and decay, has been calculated to the last decimal place by the only computer large enough to perform the calculation: the garden itself, over its long, unwitnessed years.

To add water would flood the mat and rot it. To add animals would shatter the food web. To thin the canopy would let in too much light. To deepen the basin would change the thermodynamics. Every intervention, no matter how well-intentioned, is a tremor in a system balanced on the head of a pin.

So: almost nothing.

---

But *almost*.

Because the wise power is also benevolent, which means it is not merely wise but also *kind*, and kindness is not always stillness. Sometimes kindness is a hand moved quietly, behind the scenes, to keep a door from closing.

Here is what the power would see, looking down into the basin from above — and it would look, for a long time, perhaps for what we would call years but what it would experience as a single long, attentive breath:

The garden is safe *now*. The subsidence is recent, geologically speaking. The raw stone is still settling. The moisture is still seeping. The equilibrium, once found, is holding. But the power would look ahead — not with anxiety, because anxiety is not something a wise power indulges in, but with the calm, architectural foresight of someone who can see the next ten thousand years laid out like a blueprint — and it would see:

The stone will weather. This is already happening, slowly. The fracture lines will widen. The moisture channels will deepen into rivulets, then streams. In a thousand years, maybe two, the cliff walls will begin to erode in earnest. Sediment will slide down and accumulate at the base. The walls will soften and retreat. The basin will widen. And as it widens, the ratio of wall to floor will change, and the moisture input — which comes entirely from the walls — will diminish relative to the surface area of the garden. The closed circuit will begin to leak. The dampness will thin. The mat, accustomed to its particular humidity, will begin to dry at its edges.

This is not a crisis. The garden will adapt. The Green Tide will contract, retreat toward the center where the walls are still steep enough to weep. The margins will die back and the mat will thin and the stone beneath will be exposed for the first time. New species may come — or may not. The equilibrium will shift, find a new set-point, settle again. This is natural. This is fine.

But.

There is a second thing the power would see, or rather would *not* see: any guarantee that the garden will be left alone long enough for this process to play out on its own terms. The scrubland above is empty now. But the world is not static, and people move, and people are curious, and a basin visible from the rim — a great green eye looking up from the earth — is exactly the kind of thing that draws attention. Not malice. Just attention. Just curiosity. Just someone climbing down to look, and then someone else, and then a path worn in the cliff face, and then a camp, and then the slow, incremental, thoughtless erosion of something that cannot survive being *noticed*.

The garden's silence is its shield. The quiet is not merely atmospheric; it is structural. It is what allows the equilibrium to hold. Noise — the noise of presence, of observation, of interpretation — would be the one input the system has no mechanism to absorb.

---

So here is what the wise power would do. Three things, each almost imperceptible, each requiring more care than anything the power has done in a very long time:

---

**First: the light.**

Not the sunlight — the power would not touch the sunlight; the sunlight is sacred, it is the garden's clock, its artist, its god — but the *quality* of attention that a place radiates when it has been seen by a mind. Every place that has been truly noticed carries a faint residue of that noticing, a kind of afterimage in the texture of its presence. Animals feel it. Plants, over long enough timescales, respond to it. The garden has no such residue. It has never been seen. This is part of its power, its stillness, its particular frequency of silence.

The power would lean close — if a being such as this can be said to lean — and would *look*, deeply and completely, at every part of the basin. Every vine. Every lizard. Every spider's web catching the column of gold. The weeping stone. The compressed mat. The ruins being slowly swallowed. It would look at all of it, and it would hold what it saw, and then it would *withdraw*. Not leave — the wise power does not leave, exactly; it is always present in the way that gravity is present — but it would pull back its attention to the very edge of perception, to the faintest possible hum, and in doing so it would *erase the residue of its own looking*. It would see the garden so that the garden could remain unseen. This is not a contradiction. This is what benevolent witnessing does: it holds a thing in mind without leaving a mark.

The garden would not notice this. That is the point.

---

**Second: the path.**

Not a path *in* the garden. A path *to* the garden — or rather, the absence of one. The wise power would go to the rim of the basin, to the places where a person might stand and look down, and it would do something so subtle that no one walking past would ever think twice about it: it would make the rim *slightly uninteresting*. Not repellent. Not frightening. Not blocked. Just — unremarkable. A stretch of scrubland like any other. The kind of place your eye slides over on the way to somewhere else. The kind of place where, if you did stop and look down, you might see a green depression — but the light would be wrong, somehow, at just that moment, the angle unflattering, the colors muted, and you would think *huh* and then move on, and by evening you would have forgotten you saw it at all.

This is not a spell. It is not an enchantment. It is more like — a kindness to the geometry of attention. The power would not hide the garden. The garden does not need to be hidden; it is already below the rim, already quiet, already absent from every map. What the power would do is ensure that the handful of people who *do* pass nearby — and over the centuries there will be some; there are always some — find nothing that compels them to stop, to climb down, to disturb the green silence with their footfalls and their wonder.

The garden would not notice this either.

---

**Third — and this is the only thing the power would do that touches the garden directly:**

It would visit the Stone-Sleepers.

Not to change them. Not to wake them. Not to speak to them, because the Stone-Sleepers do not speak and do not listen. But the power would go to the raw, weeping face of the cliff wall — perhaps to the place where a slab has recently sheared away, where the stone is freshest, where the fracture line is sharpest — and it would *rest its attention there*, very gently, for a very long time. Long enough for the stone to settle another fraction of a millimeter. Long enough for a single droplet of water to form, hang, and fall.

And in that time it would do one thing: it would share with the Stone-Sleepers the memory of what the basin looked like *before* the subsidence. Not to change their nature. Not to give them longing for what was lost. But simply to *complete* their record. The Stone-Sleepers are the basin's memory of its own violent birth — but they remember only the after. They do not remember the before. They do not know what the land was when it was whole, when the stone was continuous, when the water found other paths.

The power would offer them this. Not as a gift — the Stone-Sleepers do not receive gifts; they are too slow, too mineral, too indifferent. But as a *deposit*. A sediment of meaning, laid down in the spaces between their crystal lattices, where it would sit, inert and harmless, for centuries. A single additional layer in the geological record of the cliff. Something that future visitors — if any ever came, if the path ever failed — might read in the stone without knowing what they were reading. A faint anomaly in the mineral composition. A whisper in the bone.

The Stone-Sleepers would not notice this. The garden would not notice this. The power would step back, and the stillness would close over its presence like water over a stone, and the chord would continue, sustained, perfect, green.

---

And then the power would move on.

Not because the garden is unimportant. The garden is *extraordinarily* important. It is one of those rare places where matter has organized itself into a silence so complete that it becomes a kind of meaning — where the universe, for a brief geological instant, has stopped trying to become something else and has simply *been*. These places matter enormously. They are the fulcrums. The rest points. The places where the great engine pauses and breathes.

But the power has other places to attend to, other systems teetering on the edge of their own equilibria, other silences that need protecting from the noise of being noticed. And the garden will be fine. The garden has always been fine. The garden is the kind of thing that is fine.

The power would leave behind only what was already there: the light, the stone, the green, the quiet. The slow drip of water down a bone-white wall. The creak of a vine. The patient, century-long advance of a trunk over a wall built by hands that are gone.

Nothing changed. Everything held.

The garden turns its slow green face to the sky, and the sky — which is the power's open palm, turned downward, cupped around the basin but not touching it — holds nothing but air and light and the faintest possible intention:

*Continue.*
