# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

---

It would arrive the way mist arrives on the middle terraces — not suddenly, not with announcement, but as a gradual thickening of something that had perhaps always been present at the edges, unnoticed because it was not yet needed.

It would not land. It would not manifest in a form that the pigeons would scatter from or the cats would track with narrowed eyes. It would not cause the mirror-gears to skip or the channels to eddy. The benevolent power, being truly benevolent and truly wise, would understand immediately — perhaps before it even completed its approach — that the worst thing it could do to Cael-Murren was *introduce itself*.

---

So it would begin by watching.

This is not passivity. Watching, for a being of this nature, is a form of deep attention — the kind of attention that the catchment-fins pay to the mist, or the mirror-stones pay to the sun. It would settle into the rhythms. Not the rhythms as they are described by travelers or chroniclers, but the rhythms as they *are*: the precise interval between the third mirror's tick and the fourth; the exact moment each evening when the chestnut-breasted thrush begins its dusk-song on Terrace 91 (not 90, not 92 — 91, always 91, on the same fig-branch that has held its weight for decades); the micro-current in the channel-water on Terrace 44 that makes the surface break into a pattern like woven cloth for exactly seventeen minutes each afternoon when the light angle shifts.

The power would learn the city the way the city learned the mountain — by patient, incremental contact. Not by study, but by *being present in the same way the city is present*. It would take time. Perhaps a great deal of time. But time is something this kind of power has in the same abundance that Cael-Murren has basalt.

---

And as it watched, it would begin to understand several things that would confirm what it had already suspected on approach, when it first felt the city's frequency like a chord held indefinitely:

**That Cael-Murren is not in danger.** Not from drought — the mist-condensation system, maintained across centuries with no degradation in function, has made the twice-yearly rains a supplement rather than a necessity. Not from population collapse — the birth rate and death rate are in a balance so precise that the number of inhabitants has varied by fewer than two hundred individuals across the last recorded century. Not from geological instability — the massif is ancient, the stone is sound, the pouredite joints flex with thermal expansion and contract again without cracking. Not from invasion or exploitation — the mountain is difficult, the city produces nothing that cannot be found cheaper elsewhere, and the lowland settlements that trade for the honey and the wool regard Cael-Murren with a respect that has long since calcified into indifference. It is not a place that tempts conquest. It is too far. Too slow. Too complete.

**That Cael-Murren is not suffering.** The people are not enduring. They are not making do. They are not surviving. They are *living*, in the full sense that a place allows when the contract between the inhabitants and the environment has been so thoroughly renegotiated over so many generations that the terms have been forgotten and what remains is simply the living of it — the morning clearing of channels, the afternoon sun on the doorstep, the evening song of the thrush, the slow chewing of a goat on a fern-frond above the world. There is no privation here that is not also a preference. There is no hardship that is not also a practice. The warmth is real. The food is sufficient. The honey is good. The cloth is warm. The dead are planted with fig roots and become the city.

**That Cael-Murren does not need to change.** This is the hardest thing for a benevolent power to accept, because benevolence, in most of its encounters across the breadth of existence, has expressed itself as *improvement*. It has brought rain to deserts and mercy to the suffering and knowledge to the ignorant and courage to the afraid. Its instinct is to act, because in most places action is warranted. There is almost always something that could be better, something that is broken, something that is failing. But Cael-Murren has achieved something that most places, and most beings, never do: it has reached equilibrium. Not stasis — the city is alive, the roots grow, the moss spreads, the children are born, the elders die — but equilibrium in the deepest sense, where the rate of change and the capacity for change are in perfect correspondence. It is changing exactly as fast as it can absorb change. No faster. The wise power would recognize this and understand that to accelerate the city's evolution — even with good intentions, even with gifts — would be to break the very thing that makes it remarkable.

---

So it would watch longer. Seasons would pass. The twice-yearly rains would come and go. The channels would run full and fast and then slow again to their dark oil-pace. The abelia would bloom and bloom and bloom. And the power, having now spent sufficient time in the city's presence to understand it not as an observer but as something closer to a participant — though an invisible one, though one whose presence was felt only as a faint steadiness, the way the pouredite feels the stone — would begin to make its decision.

Not a decision about intervention. That question had been answered long ago, in the first hours of watching. The decision would be about *witness*.

Because the power would have noticed something that is easy to miss when you are inside the system, the way the fish does not notice the water: **Cael-Murren has no audience.** It is not performing for anyone. It does not export its meaning. The lowland traders come for honey and wool and leave again. The travelers who occasionally climb the long trail to the upper terraces marvel for a day or two and then descend with stories that are always slightly wrong — too grand, too strange, too *much*, because the city resists narration the way it resists weathering, by being too precisely itself to survive translation into exaggeration. The elders tell their slow accretions of fact to the children, and the children absorb them, and the chain continues, but it is an inward chain — knowledge passed hand to hand within the system, never leaving it.

The city exists fully. But it exists *unwitnessed from outside*, in the deepest sense. Not unseen — people see it — but unwitnessed in the way that matters: the way that says *this is being noticed by something capable of comprehending the full scope of what it is*.

The benevolent power, being both benevolent and wise, would understand that this is not a problem to be solved. Unwitnessed existence is not lesser existence. A forest does not need a witness. A coastline does not need a witness. Cael-Murren has not needed a witness for three hundred years and it does not need one now.

And yet.

The power is here. It has paid attention. It has seen the thing in its fullness. And it would understand — with the particular sadness that accompanies wisdom, the knowledge that even the best outcomes are not free of loss — that what it does next will matter not for the city's survival or happiness or function, but for something more subtle.

It would ask itself: *What does it mean that I have seen this place? What does it mean that I have watched these people tend their channels and plant their fig-seeds and bury their dead in warm stone? What obligation does comprehension create?*

---

And the answer, arrived at slowly, at the pace of the city itself, would be this:

**To remember it.**

Not to protect it — protection implies threat, and there is none. Not to reward it — the city does not operate on a logic of merit. Not to change it — change would be destruction dressed as kindness. Not even to bless it — blessing implies that the city's condition is contingent, that it could be otherwise, and the power has watched long enough to know that Cael-Murren's condition is not contingent. It is *necessary*. It is the thing the mountain was always going to become, given sufficient time and the right hands and the right stone and the right mist.

But to *remember* it. To carry it forward. To hold in the vast and ongoing architecture of its awareness a precise and detailed account of what Cael-Murren is — not as a monument or an achievement, but as a *fact*. The fact that this happened. That on a high massif above four thousand meters, a people carved a city into basalt and filled it with mist-water and songbirds and goats and cats and fig trees and bees, and that the city worked, and that it kept working, and that its inhabitants were content, and that the thrush sang on Terrace 91 every evening, and the channels ran like dark oil, and the mirrors ticked, and the stone breathed, and the whole improbable thing persisted — not against the odds, but *with* them, alongside them, in quiet partnership with the physics of altitude and sunlight and condensation and time.

To hold this as evidence. Not of anything in particular. Not as proof of a theory or an argument. But as evidence that *such things can exist* — that equilibrium is not a fiction, that maintenance is not a lesser form of creation, that a people can choose a pace and hold it for centuries and that this holding is not stagnation but a form of movement so slow and so sure that it becomes indistinguishable from stillness, and that this stillness is not emptiness but fullness, the fullness of a thing that has been done completely and continues to be done completely and will continue to be done completely for as long as the stone holds and the mist comes and someone is there to clear the moss from the channels and listen for the thrush.

---

So the power would do the following:

It would settle into the city's own timescale. It would become, for a while — perhaps a long while, perhaps longer than any human life in Cael-Murren — a participant in the rhythm. Not a visible one. Not one that the Channel Tenders would notice or the cats would watch. But a presence woven into the system at a level below perception, the way the pouredite is woven into the stone, the way the mist is woven into the catchment-fins.

It would walk the terraces — if "walk" is the word for what a being like this does when it moves through a place with intention. It would pass through the workshops where the copper is smelted in reflected sunlight, and it would linger — not to change the process but to *know it fully*, to comprehend the exact temperature at which the copper becomes workable and the exact angle of the artisan's hammer and the exact sound the metal makes when it is struck just right, a sound that rings off the basalt walls and fades into the pigeon-cooing.

It would sit — if "sit" is the word — on the upper terraces among the goats, in the thin cold air above the mist-line, where the moss gives way to bare pouredite and the stone catchment-fins stand white and sharp against the white-blue sky. It would watch the goats navigate the edges with their supernatural calm, and it would feel — if "feel" is the word — a resonance between the goats' patience and its own.

It would descend, slowly, through the middle terraces where the bees work the abelia and the hum of their industry blends with the tick of the mirrors and the trickle of the channels into a composite sound that is the city's true voice — not any single element but the *combination*, the way the elements interfere and reinforce and create a standing wave of ambient sound that exists nowhere else in the world.

It would find — though it was not looking for one — the deepest chamber in the lowest terrace, where the fig roots are thickest and the stone is warmest and the dead are sealed in pouredite and planted over, and it would remain there for a time. Not in mourning. Not in reverence. In *recognition*. The recognition that this is the foundation — that the city is built not only on basalt but on its dead, that the substrate of Cael-Murren is not stone but *memory made material*, intention made literal, the accumulated weight of every life that was lived here and ended here and was returned to the wall and the root and the planter and the joint.

And there, in the warm dark, among the roots and the sealed dead and the slow pulse of a city that has been breathing for centuries, the power would do the only thing that matters:

**It would make of Cael-Murren a held thing.**

Not a possessed thing. Not a protected thing. A *held* thing — the way you hold a note of music in your mind after the instrument has fallen silent. The way you hold the image of a place you have loved and left and will not return to, not because you cannot but because your return would change it, and you love it too much to change it.

The power would carry Cael-Murren forward — not out of the mountain, not into legend or scripture or intervention, but *forward through time*, held in the vast awareness as a living memory, a fact that does not degrade, a reference point that says: *this is what persistence looks like when it is not stubbornness but grace. This is what maintenance looks like when it is not drudgery but devotion. This is what a city looks like when it has decided to be a garden, and a garden when it has decided to be an ecosystem, and an ecosystem when it has decided to be a world, and a world when it has decided to be enough.*

---

And then — because even benevolence knows when to leave — it would go.

Not with a farewell. Not with a sign. Not with a parting gift or a change in the weather or a strange light on the terraces that the elders would later discuss in their slow, accretive way. It would go the way it arrived: like mist dissipating. Like a channel-water level that was, for a time, imperceptibly higher than it should have been, and then was not.

The pigeons would not notice. The cats would not notice. The thrush would sing its evening song on Terrace 91 and the mirrors would tick and the channels would run and the goats would stand on the precipice with their ancient horizontal pupils fixed on nothing in particular and everything at once.

But somewhere — in the structure of things, in the deep grammar of existence, in the place where held things are kept — Cael-Murren would now be present. Not as a story. Not as a lesson. As a *fact*, carried forward, undiminished, the way the city itself carries its dead forward in the roots of fig trees and the warmth of stone and the slow, patient, unending work of hands that know exactly what they are doing and have known for a very long time and will go on knowing for as long as knowing matters.

Which is forever.

Which is the same thing the city has always believed about itself, without ever needing to say it aloud.

---

*The thrush sings. The mirrors tick. The channels run. The stone breathes. The power departs, carrying something it did not have before: the knowledge that this particular corner of existence is in exactly the condition it should be, and needs nothing from it, and is — in its slow, warm, persistent, improbable way — already blessed.*
