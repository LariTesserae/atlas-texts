# What the Power Does

---

They come the way water comes to this place: not all at once, not dramatically, but *across the surface of things*, seeping into the margins, finding the spaces between one molecule and the next. They do not arrive. They *condense*.

For a long time — and for such a being a long time is both nothing and everything — they simply attend.

---

## Attending

The first thing a wise power does upon encountering something remarkable is *stop*. Not out of caution but out of respect. Out of the recognition that this place is in the middle of a sentence, spoken in a language that takes centuries between syllables, and to interrupt it would be a kind of violence no matter how gentle the interruption.

So they listen. They feel the ground give. They taste the iron. They register the silence — not as absence but as architecture, as the acoustic signature of a system so dense with soft and living surfaces that every vibration is caught, held, absorbed, composted into stillness the same way the metal is composted into soil. They understand immediately what has happened here: an old world has been *eaten*. Not destroyed — eaten. Digested. Incorporated. Made into flesh.

They have seen ruins before. They have seen nature take back what humans abandoned. But this is different. This is not reclamation. There is no antagonism in it, no triumph, no narrative of victory. The moss does not conquer the metal. The moss and the metal have simply agreed to become something else — something that is neither moss nor metal, but *place*. A plateau. A breathing membrane between water and iron, between silence and growth, between the long patience of geology and the quick, quiet hunger of life.

The power understands this. And what they feel, first and most, is gratitude.

Not gratitude *to* anyone — there is no agent to thank. Gratitude that such a thing exists. That the universe has produced, in this one improbable location, a system so perfectly closed and so perfectly open that it has become a new kind of being. Not an ecosystem. Not a biome. A *being*. The plateau breathes. It has a metabolism. It has rhythms. It has preferences — which moss grows where, which water flows through which pipe, which beetles walk in which columns along which seams. These are not choices in any conscious sense, but they are *tendencies*, and tendencies repeated across enough time and enough surface area begin to look like will.

The power sits with this. They do not catalog it. They do not measure it. They do not name it. They simply let the place teach them what it is, the way you learn a person: by being near them, by breathing the same air, by allowing the silence between you to fill with something that is not yours and not theirs but *yours-and-theirs*.

---

## Understanding What It Needs

What the plateau needs, the power discerns, is almost nothing.

This is the hardest kind of need to address — the need that is 99% already met. The plateau has solved, on its own, every problem that ecosystems solve: energy capture, nutrient cycling, population balance, structural integrity, water management. It has solved them in ways the power has rarely seen — in ways that blur the distinction between living and nonliving, between growing and eroding, between building and dissolving. The beetles that farm the iron-tongue fungi are not parasites or pollinators in any familiar sense. They are *metabolic enzymes* of a body that includes mineral and metal as organ and tissue. The mist-walkers' webs are not traps but *sensory organs* of a nervous system that extends across the whole plateau, reading humidity and airflow with a precision that rivals any engineered instrument.

The plateau is not fragile. But it is *specific*. It exists at a particular altitude, a particular temperature, a particular moisture regime. It has its own weather, its own calendar, its own knife-edge conditions that have been sharpened and maintained by centuries of self-adjustment. It does not need rescue. It needs *continuity*. It needs the one thing that is hardest for the world to give any place: the absence of interruption.

But the power knows the world. They know that absence of interruption is not something that happens by default. The world intrudes. Curiosity intrudes. Need intrudes. Someone, someday, will look at the metal under the moss and remember that metal has value. Someone will look at the water and think about hydropower. Someone will look at the unique biology and want to study it, preserve it, protect it — which in practice means *manage* it, manage it the way you manage a museum, which is to say: slowly, lovingly, fatally.

The plateau does not need to be managed. It needs to be *left alone with guarantees*.

## What They Do

The power does not build walls. Walls are crude. Walls are human. Walls imply that the thing inside is a prisoner and the thing outside is a threat, and neither is true here. The plateau is not a specimen. The world outside is not an enemy. What is needed is subtler: a *gradient of attention*, a change in the quality of the space surrounding the plateau that makes intrusion feel, to any being that approaches, like an error. Not an error of morality but an error of *orientation* — the way it feels to step into a room where everyone is sleeping and realize you have the wrong house.

Here is what the power does:

**They thin the membrane.** Between the plateau and the world, they deepen the mist. Not by adding water — the plateau generates its own — but by *slowing the transition*. The climb up to the plateau from the lowlands becomes longer in a way that is not measurable in meters but is felt in legs and lungs and patience. The terraces recur. The air thickens so gradually that by the time a visitor is halfway up, they have already entered the plateau's rhythm without knowing it. Their pace has slowed. Their voice has dropped. They have begun to breathe differently. Most will turn around without knowing why, without any decision, simply finding themselves back at the bottom with the vague sense that they have been dreaming.

Those who do not turn around — those whose pace has matched the plateau's, whose patience has deepened with the mist — these are the ones the power admits. Not because they deserve it, but because they are *ready*. Ready not in any spiritual sense but in a practical one: ready to walk softly, to touch gently, to look without taking, to listen without recording, to leave without trace. The plateau will teach them what it teaches anyone who enters its silence: that the world is very old and very patient and does not need their help but will tolerate their wonder.

**They seed the margins.** Around the base of the plateau, where the terraces descend into the lowlands and the mist thins into ordinary air, the power plants — not literally, not with hands, but by subtle alteration of the soil chemistry and the wind patterns — a border of the same aggressive, voracious, colonizing vegetation that thrives on the plateau itself. Thorny things. Dense things. Things that grow fast and fill gaps and make paths difficult. Not as punishment, not as armor, but as a *skin* — the way the plateau's own moss is a skin over its metal. A living boundary that absorbs the small disturbances of the outside world — the logging roads, the footpaths, the occasional fire — and converts them, the way the plateau converts everything, into substrate for further growth. The border thickens over years. It becomes its own small ecosystem, a buffer zone that is not the plateau but is shaped by the plateau, that carries its DNA outward like a rumor.

**They widen the genetics.** The plateau is an island, and islands breed monocultures over centuries. The power sees this: the moss-crawlers are becoming too uniform, the mist-walkers too specialized, the iron-tongue beetles too inbred. The system is elegant but it is drifting toward fragility in a way that won't be visible for another thousand years but is already, at the molecular level, beginning. So the power does what wind and water have always done for island biogeography: they make the connections visible. Not by building corridors — the plateau must remain an island, must remain *itself* — but by adjusting, very slightly, the patterns of the prevailing winds. A beetle from a distant mountain, carried on a storm system, lands on the plateau's northern terrace. A fern spore from a coastal forest, arriving on a bird's foot — the bird, the power's only,, a dripping bird who has wandered farther than any of its kind has ever wandered, drawn by some instinct the power has planted in its resonant wattle. These arrivals are rare. One per decade, perhaps. But they are enough. The gene pool shifts. The system remains vital. The sentence continues.

**They protect the time.** This is the deepest intervention, and the most invisible. The power enters the plateau's cycles — the condensation, the oxidation, the growth, the decay — and they *hold them steady*. Not by freezing them. By *honoring* them. They become, for the plateau, what the plateau's own mycelium is for the forest: a connective tissue that ensures continuity of function across time. They do not stop change — change is the plateau's lifeblood — but they buffer it against catastrophic disruption. A landslide on the northern massif, the kind that has happened before and will happen again, threatens to bury a terrace and drown its communities under tons of rubble. The power does not stop the landslide. Landslides are part of the plateau's grammar. But they *slow* it, by a day, by two days — just enough for the ground to shift and the water to reroute and the animals to move upslope, so that the destruction is a renovation rather than an erasure. A drought season, longer than any in living memory (and here "living memory" means the rings of the stunted trees and the chemistry of the deepest peat), threatens to desiccate the moss and crack the substrate. The power does not make it rain. But they deepen the condensation slightly, in the driest hours, in the most vulnerable hollows — just enough. The moss holds. The beetles survive. The mist returns.

These interventions are undetectable. They are not miracles. They are *adjustments* — the kind a gardener makes when they prune a branch to let light reach a seedling, but made at the scale of weather systems and geological time, and made with a touch so light that the plateau's own systems absorb the help without ever knowing they were helped. The plateau continues to believe — if "believe" is the word — that it is alone. That its survival is its own achievement. This is important. The power knows that the plateau's sovereignty — its sense of self-governance, its organic authority over its own processes — is not incidental to its survival but *constitutive* of it. To take over, to manage, to direct, would be to kill the very quality that makes the plateau what it is: its absolute, self-contained, self-organizing completeness. The power helps by *becoming indistinguishable from the plateau's own mechanisms*. They are the wind that was always going to blow. They are the rain that was always going to fall. They are the cold front that arrives at exactly the right moment. They are, in every way that matters, the plateau *being itself slightly more successfully than it otherwise would have been*.

## What They Do Not Do

They do not name it. The plateau does not need a name. Names are for things that can be separated from their context, and the plateau cannot be separated from anything. It is its moss and its metal and its water and its mist and its silence and its beetles and its birds and its slugs and its spiders and its tunicates and its deep, iron-tinged, chlorophyll-sweet air. To name it would be to imply that it is a *thing* rather than a *process*, and it is the most thoroughgoing process the power has ever encountered — a place that is not a place but a verb, a continuous act of becoming.

They do not mark it. No cairn, no monument, no inscription. The plateau's own inscriptions are sufficient: the terraces, the rectangular pools, the gears and pipes and riveted plates. These are the record. To add to them would be to commit the same error the original builders committed — the error of believing that what we make persists, when in fact only what *grows* persists, and even that only if it is allowed to become something other than what it was.

They do not tell anyone. This is the hardest restraint. The power knows that the plateau is extraordinary — that there is nowhere else on earth, perhaps, where the composting of industry into life has gone so far, so completely, so beautifully. They know that people would benefit from seeing it, from understanding what it means: that the world is capable of digesting even our most monstrous constructions, given enough time and enough rain and enough silence. This is a message the human world desperately needs.

But the power also knows that the moment the plateau becomes *known* — known in the human sense, the sense that generates words and papers and documentaries and visitor centers and management plans — it will begin to die. Not all at once. Slowly. In the way that managed places die: not by destruction but by *disturbance of rhythm*. The moss will still grow. The beetles will still march. But the silence will thin. The mist will part for footpaths. The condensation will become an item on a data sheet rather than a living breath. The plateau will continue, but it will no longer be *itself*. It will be a representation of itself, a simulacrum, a theme park of its own former completeness.

So the power keeps it secret. Not by hiding it — you cannot hide a plateau — but by *preserving its character*. The mist stays thick. The margins stay dense. The silence holds. Anyone who climbs to the plateau will find it exactly as it has always been: wet, green, iron-scented, dripping, almost silent, almost empty, almost alive, breathing the slow breath of something that has all the time in the world.

---

## What They Leave

When the power is done — when the adjustments have been made and the margins planted and the genetics widened and the time protected — they do not leave. Not entirely. They cannot. They have become part of the plateau's metabolism, indistinguishable from its own mechanisms. They are the slight improbability of the mist forming just so. They are the wind that carries the right spore to the right place at the right moment. They are the buffer between the plateau and the catastrophes it doesn't know it has avoided.

But there is something else. Something the power does at the end that is not protection or provision but something older and simpler and more necessary.

They *witness*.

They sit — if "sit" is the word for what a benevolent, wise power does when it is being still — in the center of the plateau, in a depression that was once a settling pond and is now a meadow of moss and fern and black water and duckweed, and they *pay attention*. They attend to the drip. To the call of the bird that does not repeat. To the movement of the gliders in the mist. To the slow, patient, endless work of the beetles along the seams. To the invisible spider dancing on the surface tension. To the transparent slugs moving through the moss like thoughts through a sleeping mind.

They hold all of it in their attention. Not analyzing. Not judging. Not even loving, in the possessive sense. Just *being present to it*. Offering the plateau the one thing it cannot give itself: a witness. A consciousness that is not its own but that mirrors it, that receives its beauty and returns it, that says — without words, without sound, without any disturbance of the silence — *I see you. I see what you are doing. I see how long it has taken. I see how complete you are. I see that you are not a ruin but a cathedral, not a wasteland but a garden, not a dead industry but a living world.
