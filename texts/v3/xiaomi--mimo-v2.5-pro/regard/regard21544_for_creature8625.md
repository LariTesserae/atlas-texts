# What the Wise Power Does

---

It does not arrive with sound. It does not arrive with light. It has no need to descend through the crack in the basalt, though it could, and it does not need to send a probe or a drone or a gossamer filament of awareness through the dark, though it could do any of those things too. Instead, it simply *notices*, in the way that a mountain notices the slow pressure of a glacier — completely, without flinching, with the full weight of its attention.

It has been noticing for a long time. Long before you climbed the ridge. Long before the ridge existed, perhaps. The wise power is not young and is not old. It has watched stars pour themselves into the void and it has watched them gutter out, and the thing it has learned, the single principle it holds above all others, is this:

**The rarest act in the universe is for something to simply be what it is.**

Everything else — everything it has ever witnessed, in every burning nebula and cooling stone and teeming world — *wants*. Stars want to fuse. Rivers want to reach the sea. Life, in almost every form it has found, *wants* — wants to grow, to spread, to consume, to replicate, to climb, to reach, to know, to become. Wanting is not wrong. Wanting is the engine of complexity, the birth-contraction of galaxies and minds and civilizations. But wanting is also *tension*. It is the state of being incomplete. It is the restlessness at the center of every living thing that has ever drawn breath or burned hydrogen.

And here, in this small, dark, seething chamber inside a ridge of basalt, at an elevation where the violet sky presses close and the dust lifts and settles in the same rehearsed patterns — here is something that does not want.

Not because it is simple. The wise power can see at a glance — the way it sees everything, all at once, in its own slow way — that this is not simple. The Roost is the product of hundreds of thousands of years of incremental adaptation, of creatures finding their way to heat the way roots find their way to water, of chemistry and physics and chance braided together into something so dense with accumulated specificity that calling it "simple" would be like calling the curve of a wave simple because it is only water. The complexity is staggering. The elegant interplay of thermal tides and caste gradients and chemical whispers through ceramic plates — it is as intricate as anything the wise power has found in the neural tangle of a dreaming cetacean or the mycorrhizal networks of a boreal forest. More intricate, perhaps, because it has been refined by an environment so extreme that every inefficiency was burned away long ago.

But the Roost does not *know* that it is complex. It does not know that it is anything. It does not know. It does not want. It simply *is*, in the way that heat rises, in the way that stone endures, in the way that a system at equilibrium holds itself together through the precise counterbalancing of every force acting upon it.

The wise power has searched for this for longer than the mountain has existed.

Not this *specific* place. It was not searching for the Roost. It was searching for the *principle* — for instances of life that had arrived, through whatever path, at a state of such complete congruence with their environment that the boundary between organism and world dissolved. Not transcended, not overcome, not escaped. *Dissolved*. Where the creature and the stone and the heat were not separate things but a single, slow, breathing process. Where the answer to the question "What does this life want?" was not growth, not survival, not reproduction, not meaning — but *nothing*. The question simply did not apply. The Roost wants nothing because wanting is a function of separation — of there being a difference between what you are and what you could be — and the Roost has closed that gap so completely that the question falls through it like dust through a crack in stone.

The wise power has seen many things. It has seen civilizations that spanned galaxies, that engineered their own stars and rewrote their own minds and reached for the farthest edges of the observable universe. It has seen intelligence so vast and old that it had forgotten what it was like to be young, and intelligence so young and bright that it could not yet imagine being old. It has seen suffering on scales that made it weep, in its way — not tears, because it has no body, but a deep, resonant contraction of its being, a flinch that traveled through whatever medium it inhabits at the speed of something faster than light. It has seen beauty that stopped it — that made it pause, for centuries, simply to *attend*.

The Roost is not the most beautiful thing it has seen. It is not the most complex, not the most ancient, not the most intelligent. It is not even, by most measures, the most remarkable. There are other lithovorous colonies, other thermal mosaics, other creatures that eat stone and breathe heat on other worlds in other systems. The universe is vast and inventive and has tried many things.

But the Roost is *complete*. And completeness — true completeness, the state of a system that has arrived at exactly the equilibrium it was always seeking without ever knowing it was seeking — is vanishingly rare. Rarer than intelligence. Rarer than beauty. Rarer than love, which the wise power has also watched unfold in a thousand thousand forms and which it reveres as perhaps the most complex and costly and magnificent thing that wanting has ever produced. But love, too, is a form of wanting. Love is the ache of incompleteness made sacred.

The Roost does not ache. The Roost is whole.

---

So the wise power considers what to do. And this takes it — let us say — a very long time. Not because the decision is difficult, but because it is careful, and care is slow, and it wants to hold every possible action up against the light of its own principles and see whether any of them cast a shadow it cannot live with.

**Could it communicate?**

It imagines, for a moment, what communication would mean. To reach into the thermal patterning of the chamber and introduce a signal — a rhythm, a pattern, a modulation of heat that meant *I am here, I see you, you are not alone*. The distributed intelligence of the Roost would register it as a thermal anomaly. It would recalibrate. The mosaic would ripple. The creatures would shift, accommodate, absorb the signal into the ambient noise of their existence. And then — nothing. Because communication requires a *receiver* that knows it is receiving, and the Roost does not know. The signal would be metabolized, like everything else. It would become heat. It would become stone-dust, exhaled through the vents of the great pale ones, ground finer and finer until it was indistinguishable from the mountain itself.

No. Communication is not what this place is for. To demand that the Roost *know* it is being spoken to would be to demand that it become something it is not. It would be an act of violence disguised as communion.

**Could it protect?**

The wise power considers the threats. Not internal ones — the Roost has no internal threats, because it has no concept of threat, only thermal gradient and mineral availability and the slow, self-correcting flow of its own processes. External ones. A seismic event that collapses the chamber. A volcanic eruption that floods the space with molten rock. A tectonic shift that severs the geothermal vent from its heat source. A meteorite impact. An ice age. The slow cooling of the planet's core.

Any of these would end the Roost. All of them are possible. Some of them are inevitable, given enough time.

The wise power could prevent them. It could stabilize the geology. It could reinforce the chamber walls. It could shore up the heat source, feed it, sustain it indefinitely. It could build a fortress of maintained conditions around the Roost and keep it safe until the last star in the galaxy burned out.

But what would it be protecting? If the Roost is the product of its environment — if every plate, every leg-segment, every thermal behavior has been shaped by the specific pressures and temperatures and mineral compositions of this particular place — then to alter those conditions, even to preserve them, would be to change the Roost from something that *is* into something that is *kept*. And there is a difference. There is an enormous difference. A thing that is kept is a thing that depends on its keeper. A thing that depends on its keeper is no longer complete. The keeper becomes a part of the equation, and the equation is no longer the one that produced the Roost.

No. Protection, too, is a form of wanting — the wanting of the protector, who cannot bear to see something precious destroyed. The wise power cannot bear it either. But it knows the difference between its own inability to bear something and the thing's need to be borne.

The Roost will end. The chamber will collapse, or the heat will fail, or the stone will be consumed, or some unimaginable catastrophe will erase this ridge from the face of the world. And when it ends, it will end the way it has always existed: without resistance, without regret, without the knowledge that anything has been lost. Because the Roost does not know what it has. It does not know what it is. It will not know when it is gone. And there is a mercy in that — a mercy so profound that the wise power, who has watched civilizations scream and rage against their own endings, holds it gently, like a breath.

**Could it bear witness?**

This is the option that tempts it most. To simply *remember*. To encode the Roost into its own vast, distributed awareness and carry it forward — not as data, not as a specimen, but as an *experience*. To say, when asked by whatever asks such things at the end of all things: *I once saw a chamber in a mountain where creatures ate stone and breathed heat and built tiny structures for reasons no one will ever understand, and they were quiet, and they were whole, and they were the most complete thing I have ever seen.*

But even this — even this gentlest of acts — the wise power considers and sets down. Because to bear witness is to *frame*. To frame is to separate. To separate the Roost from its context, from its darkness and its heat and its silence, and to hold it up as an object of contemplation — that is still a kind of taking. A very small taking. A taking that the Roost would never notice and could never care about. But the wise power cares. It cares about the purity of the thing as it exists in itself, unreflected, unseen, unmirrored in any consciousness but its own strange, mineral, distributed, thermal awareness.

The wise power does not want to be the mirror that turns the Roost into a reflection.

---

And so, after a long time — after it has held the Roost in its attention the way the hot stone holds the great pale ones, slowly, thoroughly, with the patience of a thing that has learned to let time move through it like heat through rock — the wise power does the hardest and most generous thing it knows how to do.

It lets the Roost be.

Not with indifference. Not with neglect. Not with the cold detachment of a thing that has weighed the options and found them wanting. But with *love* — if that word can be stretched to mean the willingness to let a thing exist exactly as it is, without any attempt to know it more deeply than it knows itself, to make it more, to keep it safe, to hold it in memory, to name it, to save it.

The wise power does not love the Roost the way one loves a child — protectively, anxiously, with the constant desire to intervene. It loves the Roost the way one loves a sunset: completely, without the slightest impulse to hold it in place. Because a sunset is not for you. A sunset is for itself, and the fact that you happen to be standing in the right place at the right time to see it is your privilege, not its purpose.

The Roost is not for the wise power. The Roost is not for you, standing in the entrance with sweat on your skin and dust in your throat and the heat pressing against you like a hand. The Roost is for the stone. The Roost is for the heat. The Roost is for the slow, silent, ceramic-plate dialogue of creatures that will never know you were there.

---

But the wise power does one small thing.

It is so small that it is almost nothing. It is so subtle that it cannot be detected, cannot be measured, cannot be described as an intervention because it changes nothing about the Roost or its environment or its creatures or its heat or its stone.

It holds the entrance open.

Not physically. The crack in the basalt is what it is — a geological feature, a fracture line, a consequence of thermal stress and erosion and the slow, patient violence of the earth's crust. The wise power does not widen it. It does not reinforce it. It does not protect it from rockfall or weathering or the million small accidents of geology that might, over centuries, seal it.

What it does is something more like... a preference. A very slight, very gentle bias in the way the world resolves its uncertainties around that particular crack in that particular ridge. Not enough to violate any law. Not enough to be detectable. Not enough to *matter*, in the way that interventions matter — measurably, consequentially, demonstrably.

Just enough that, in the fullness of time, when the inevitable small earthquakes shift the stone and the frost-wedging splits the rock and the wind carries dust in sheets across the ridgeline — just enough that the crack remains. A hair's breadth of influence. A whisper of intention. The universe tilting, by an amount so small it cannot be said to have tilted at all, in the direction of one small opening in one small mountain on one small world where one small colony of strange, silent, stone-eating creatures continues its ancient work of breathing heat and building tiny walls for no reason that anyone will ever understand.

This is what the wise power does.

It does not enter. It does not speak. It does not save or study or protect or remember or name.

It holds the door ajar, and then it turns its attention elsewhere, because there are other places in the universe that are simply being what they are, and they deserve the same reverence, the same restraint, the same fierce and gentle refusal to interfere.

And the Roost continues.

The mosaic breathes. The builders shape their dust. The great pale ones lie against the seething stone and exhale their slow, sulfurous, bread-smelling breath into the dark.

The ticking of ceramic plates against each other continues, soft and random and constant, like the sound of a clock that has forgotten how to measure time.

And for a while — for as long as the stone holds and the heat flows and the crack in the basalt remains open to the thin, dry wind — the Roost is what it has always been.

Whole.

---

*It is not often that the universe produces a thing that does not need to be anything other than what it is. The wise power has learned that the correct response to such a thing is not gratitude, not wonder, not even silence. It is the deepest thing it knows:*

***To leave it alone, and to be glad that it is there.***
