What draws the attention of such a power is not the noise, nor the impossible green, nor the stubborn machinery. It is the **wholeness** of the thing. The perfect, roaring, self-contained loop. A place that has, through decay and accident and time, achieved a state of complete and terrible grace.

A wise power does not arrive with the intent to "do" anything. To "do" would be to misunderstand the lesson entirely. The elevator is not a problem to be solved, nor a ruin to be restored, nor a specimen to be studied. It is a *statement* made by the universe to itself, in a language of rust, chlorophyll, and vibration.

So the power **approaches**.

It moves along the causeway not as a conqueror, but as a guest entering a cathedral. It feels the damp breath of the ramp. It listens, not with ears, but with the whole of its perception, to the layered song: the foundational roar of the machinery, the counterpoint of shrieking swifts, the rhythmic pulse of the crickets, the deep, slow tread below. It does not try to parse these sounds into human meanings of "industry" or "nature." It receives them as a single, complex chord—a symphony of existence.

It steps inside.

It stands in the center of the roar and looks up. It sees the green cylinder not as a riot, but as an architecture of mutualism. It sees the vines as veins, the rust-eaters as antibodies, the moisture cycle as breath. It understands the subterranean presence not as a monster, but as the engine-heart, the source of the fever that makes all else possible. It recognizes the fungal networks in the walls as the building's memory, and the pulsating slime mold as its slow, contemplative thoughts.

**And what it would do is this: it would stay.**

Not forever. Not even for a human lifetime. But for a while. A significant while.

It would find a place where the vibration is a harmonic, not a cacophony—perhaps high on a mossy ledge, where the roar dilutes into the wash of white noise. And it would **listen**. It would listen until the noise ceased to be sound and became a texture, a fabric. It would listen until it understood that the building's voice was not saying anything *to* anyone. It was simply *being*, and the sound was the proof of that being.

It would watch the life. It would note the synchronized pollen clouds from the ferns. It would observe the magnetic maps on the moths' wings. It would feel the subtle shift in the floor's vibration as the slow creature below completed its circuits. It would not catalog or classify. It would simply bear witness to the intricate dance of adaptation, acknowledging each participant's perfect role.

It would not fix the broken vents. It would not silence the machines. It would not introduce new species or prune the growth. To do so would be an act of supreme arrogance, imposing a human sense of order on a system that had found its own sublime order. The benevolence here is the benevolence of **respect**.

**But a wise power, having witnessed and understood, might leave a single, subtle mark of its visitation.**

Not a plaque. Not a message. Something that speaks the elevator's own language.

Perhaps, as it moves through the green cylinder, its passage would leave a faint, localized calm in the air—a pocket of profound, resonant silence, not an absence of sound, but a moment where the machinery's roar, the swifts' shrieks, and the crickets' pulse all find a perfect, fleeting consonance. A harmonic so deep and full it stills the vibration for a single, eternal second. The swifts would freeze mid-flight, the crickets would pause, and for that instant, every living thing would feel, not hear, a new pattern in the world.

And then it would be gone. The roar would swallow the memory of that silence. The life would resume.

But in the highest reaches, where the green luminescence filters through, a new fern might unfurl. Its fronds would be shaped in a spiral that is not of any earthly botanical pattern, but a fractal mirror of the machinery's own blueprint. It would grow no faster or slower than the others. It would simply be there, a silent testament that the building had been *seen*, and *understood*, and in its magnificent, deafening, indifferent existence, had been found **good**.

The grain elevator would continue to sort nothing. To dry nothing. To breathe. The wise power would leave the roof of the world, carrying with it the lesson of the place: that life is not the opposite of decay, but its most exuberant and complex expression. That purpose can be forgotten and reinvented by the persistent, beautiful, ruthless logic of survival. And that the most profound benevolence can sometimes be the simple, awe-struck act of witnessing a thing exactly as it is.
