# What the Wise Power Would Do

---

It would notice the Sump the way you notice a heartbeat — not by looking for it, but by feeling the silence around it change texture. A pocket of warmth beneath a city that has mostly gone hard and cold. A place where the collapse was not the end of anything but the beginning of something no one designed, no one planned, no one is in charge of — and which works anyway.

It would descend. Not through the storm drain passage, not through the switchback of tilted stone steps. It would simply *be there*, the way the heat is there, the way the damp is there — arriving not as an intrusion but as a change in pressure, a slight shift in the quality of the air, something the residents might register as a moment of unusual stillness and then forget, the way they forget the dripping after ten minutes.

It would stay for a long time. Longer than you'd expect.

---

## What it would see

It would see that the Sump is not broken. This would be its first and most important observation, and it would come as a relief — not because it expected brokenness, but because it has seen so much of the world above that is broken and does not know it. The surface city is full of systems that are failing in invisible ways: aquifers being drawn down by inches per year, trust between neighbors eroding in millimeters, soil compacting under foundations that were never meant to hold this much weight for this long. The surface is a ruin that hasn't happened yet.

The Sump has already happened. The ruin came and went, and what remained was not wreckage but substrate. The people here are not surviving a disaster — they are living on the other side of one, in a space that has already passed through its crisis and arrived at a kind of equilibrium. The equilibrium is imperfect. The northwest ceiling is still settling. The geometry is still strange. The water still rises. But these are not problems to be solved. They are conditions of existence, the way gravity is a condition, the way night is a condition. The people have adapted to them the way organisms adapt — not by conquering them but by folding themselves around them.

The wise power would recognize this and feel something like admiration.

---

## What it would understand

It would understand the etiquette — not by observing it from outside, but by feeling it the way the residents feel it: as a pressure in the chest, a modulation of breath, an instinct to lower one's voice and take only what is needed. It would understand that the Sump's governance is not absent but *distributed* — held in the water-sharing, in the silence during sleep hours, in the paths not taken, in the northwest corner left alone. The rules are not written because they do not need to be. They are the shape the space makes of the people who inhabit it, the way the limestone makes the shape of the water that passes through it.

It would understand the people. Not their histories — it would not need to know who they were before, what debts they owed or fled, what crimes or griefs or ordinary failures brought them here. It would understand them as they are now: organisms adapted to a niche. Slow-moving, soft-spoken, attentive to subtle signals. People who have learned to read the creak of settling stone and the lean of ferns. People who navigate by moss and smell. People who have given up the illusion of control and replaced it with something rarer — *participation*. They are not managing the Sump. They are living inside it, the way a hermit crab lives inside a shell, reshaping its interior and being shaped by its contours in return.

It would understand the vegetation, and here it would pause with something like wonder. Not at the plants themselves — it has seen plants colonize lava fields, split boulders, reclaim bombed cities — but at the *partnership*. The plants are not invading the human space, and the humans are not cultivating the plant space. Both are inhabiting a third space that belongs to neither. The ferns grow where they grow, and the humans walk around them. The humans knock through a wall, and the vines use the new edge as a trellis. Neither is serving the other. Both are serving the space.

This is rare. In most of the world above, the relationship between human intention and natural process is adversarial — one or the other dominates, and the result is either a city or a wilderness, never both at once. The Sump is both at once. It is a city that is becoming a wilderness, or a wilderness that is becoming a city, and the transition is so slow and so mutual that it will never arrive at either pole. It will remain in the middle, in the warm, damp, green, dripping space between.

The wise power would understand that this is the Sump's gift — not to itself, but to the idea of what a place can be.

---

## What it would do

Almost nothing.

This is the crucial point, and it would know it. The Sump is not a system in need of intervention. It is a system in process — slow, patient, self-correcting, already far more resilient than it appears. To intervene would be to introduce a variable from outside the system's own logic, and the wise power knows that systems which depend on external variables are fragile. The Sump's strength is that it depends on nothing but itself — its heat, its water, its stone, its people, its plants, its patience.

But "almost nothing" is not "nothing." A wise power is not a hands-off power. It is a *light-touch* power. It knows the difference between a system that is stable and a system that is *becoming stable*, and it knows that the second kind sometimes needs a small thing — a nudge, a seal, a removed stone — at exactly the right moment, to prevent a failure that would undo years of slow work.

So.

---

**It would look at the northwest corner.** It would listen to the groan of the limestone there — a sound the residents have learned to avoid, a sound that means *not yet, but soon* — and it would understand that the ceiling is going to settle further. Not catastrophically. Not all at once. But in the next year, or the next three, a section of limestone the size of a delivery truck will detach and fall into the space where people sometimes walk. It will not kill anyone — the residents avoid that corner — but it will change the geometry in a way that closes a drainage channel and redirects water into the machine shop on the second level, flooding the lathe, silencing the motor that hums at a frequency you feel in your molars.

The wise power would not hold the ceiling up. That would be an ongoing commitment, an external variable, a dependency. Instead, it would *ease the settling along*. It would find the hairline fracture that will eventually become the line of separation and encourage it — not to open faster, but to open *cleanly*, along a path that will direct the fallen stone into the dead-end alcove where no one walks and no drainage runs. It would do this in a single night, working with the stone's own stress patterns, and the next morning the residents would find a new section of raw limestone exposed in the northwest corner, smooth and slightly warm, and a ton of fallen rock piled neatly in the alcove like a deliberate deposit, and they would say to each other, in their soft voices: *The ceiling finally let go.* And the old man with the spoon-handle scissors would examine the new stone and note that it was good limestone, porous, warm, and that the ferns would colonize it within a month. And they would not ask why it fell so neatly, because the Sump has its own geometry, and they have long since stopped expecting it to make sense by the standards of the world above.

**It would look at the water.** The condensation system is beautiful — a network of pipes and catch-basins that distributes moisture from the cooler ceiling to the warmer floors, where it evaporates and rises again. It is a closed loop, mostly. But it is slowly mineralizing. The limestone seeps carry calcium and iron, and these are depositing inside the pipes in thin, accreting layers that will, over years, narrow the flow. In five years, the eastern cistern — the one that supplies the tannery and the distillery — will be running at half capacity. In eight, it will be a trickle.

The wise power would not flush the pipes. That would be a temporary fix, a surface-world solution. Instead, it would introduce a change in the microbial community of the seep water — a nudge to the existing biochemistry, not an addition from outside. There are already bacteria in the limestone water that eat mineral deposits. They are simply too few. The wise power would encourage the conditions that favor their growth: a slight shift in the water's pH at the point where it enters the eastern pipe, a micro-degree of temperature change in the seep that passes through the warmest section of stone. The bacteria would bloom — slowly, over months — and the mineral deposits would begin to soften, to flake, to be carried away as fine sediment that settles harmlessly in the catch-basins. The residents would notice, eventually, that the water in the eastern cistern tastes slightly different — faintly tangy, faintly alive — and they would shrug. Water changes. The Sump changes. That is how things are.

**It would look at the electronic workshop.** The three people who work there — the ones without names, only functions — are building something. The wise power would not look too closely at what they are building. That is their business. But it would see that they are running out of a specific component — a surface-mount capacitor, 4.7 microfarads, the kind that comes in a strip of paper and plastic — and that their supply is down to a handful, and that they cannot obtain more through the usual trading channels because the surface supplier has been arrested. It would see that without this component, their work will stall, and that the three of them will be forced to make a decision: abandon the project, or attempt a more dangerous form of procurement.

The wise power would not provide the capacitors. That would be too direct, too obvious, too much like a gift from outside. Instead, it would arrange for the surface trader who comes down through the storm drain passage — the one who brings batteries and medicine and fabric — to include, in his next load, a box of mixed electronic components that he bought at a surplus auction and has been unable to sell on the surface because they are an odd lot, a mix of old and new, useful and useless. He would include this box almost as an afterthought, or as a way to make weight. And in the box, buried among voltage regulators and obsolete op-amps and a tangle of ribbon cable, there would be three strips of 4.7-microfarad surface-mount capacitors, more than enough. The three in the workshop would find them and use them and say nothing, because components appear in the Sump the way ferns appear in the limestone — from somewhere, for no clear reason, and only a fool asks too many questions about sources.

**It would look at the old man with the spoon-handle scissors.** This one is simpler. The old man is dying. Not dramatically, not soon — but his lungs are beginning to struggle with the constant damp, and there is a tightness in his chest that he has not told anyone about, because telling anyone would mean going to the surface, and the surface is not his world anymore. He trims the ferns with increasing difficulty, his hands slower, his breath shallower, and he has started sitting on the overturned bucket near the upper-level philodendrons instead of kneeling, which is new.

The wise power would not heal him. That would be too loud, too bright, too much like a miracle, and the Sump has no miracles. But it would make a small change to the air in the upper level — a fractional reduction in humidity, achieved by subtly altering the condensation pattern on the ceiling above the fern corridor. The water would bead and run differently, collecting in a channel that leads to the eastern cistern instead of hanging in the air as mist. The air would be fractionally drier, fractionally cooler, and the old man would notice — would sit up on his bucket one morning and take a breath and feel it reach the bottom of his lungs without the usual resistance — and he would think: *The air is different today.* And he would not know why, and he would not ask, and in the evening the flowering vines would bloom and the air would be sweet and damp again and he would sleep well, and in the morning the ferns would need trimming and he would trim them, and this would continue for years longer than it would have otherwise.

## What it would not do

It would not reveal itself. The Sump is a place where things disappear, and the wise power would honor that by disappearing too — by becoming part of the ambient strangeness, part of the geometry that doesn't quite add up, part of the feeling that the shadows lean the wrong way. If any resident suspected that something was watching over them, the spell would break. Not because the watching is malevolent, but because the Sump's ecology depends on the absence of a master. There is no one in charge. There are only participants. The wise power would participate, briefly and lightly, and then it would leave, and the Sump would continue as if it had never been there, because in a meaningful sense it hadn't — it had only been one more subtle variable in a system that runs on subtle variables.

**It would not fix the geometry.** The Long Kitchen would remain long. The Stair That Remembers would remain confusing. The shadows would continue to lean. These are not flaws. They are the Sump's way of slowing people down, of forcing attention, of making navigation an act of presence rather than habit. The wise power would recognize that the disorientation is functional — that it is the spatial equivalent of the etiquette, the silence, the whispered voices. It makes the residents pay attention. And attention is the Sump's most precious resource, more valuable than water or electricity or distilled spirits. A place where everyone is paying attention is a place where almost anything can survive.

**It would not stop the subsidence.** The limestone is still settling. The cavity is still warm. The stone is still weeping. In another decade, the south wall will shift a few inches, and the thermoelectric generator will need to be repositioned, and the machine shop will need to move down one level, and the micro-forest in the V between buildings will gain six inches of headroom and send up new tree ferns to fill it. This is the Sump becoming more itself. To prevent it would be to freeze the place in amber, and the wise power knows that frozen things are dead things, and the Sump is the opposite of dead.

**It would not tell anyone about the three in the workshop.** What they are building is their business. The wise power can see that it is not a weapon, not a threat, not something that will destabilize the surface world or the Sump itself. It is something more like a — and here the wise power would search for the right word and settle on *a listening*. They are building a way to hear something that the surface world has forgotten how to hear. The Sump hears it already, in the creak of growing ferns and the tick of cooling pipes and the slow, wet breath of limestone, but the three want to hear it *precisely*, to measure it, to name its frequencies. This is their project and their purpose, and it is as legitimate as the old man's ferns or the woman's generator or the tannery's mineral-dark hides. The wise power would leave them to it. If the listening works — if the device finds what it is listening for — the wise power might lean in, just slightly, to make sure the signal is clear. But it would not announce its presence even then. It would let the three believe they found it on their own, because belief in one's own agency is part of what makes the Sump work. The people here are not waiting to be saved. They are building, growing, tending, listening. The wise power would add its silence to theirs and call it good.

---

## The smallest thing

If the wise power did one thing — one single thing that could be called an act rather than an observation — it would be this:

It would go to the limestone wall where the bioluminescent organisms glow in their slow, pulsing, greenish light, and it would place its attention — not its hand, not its power, just its *attention* — on the place where the glow is dimmest. The junction where three seep channels meet, where the organisms are spread thin, where the light falters and almost fails. And it would simply *be interested* in that spot, the way the old man is interested in the ferns, the way the woman is interested in the generator, the way the three are interested in their capacitors and their listening.

And in the weeks that followed, the organisms in that junction would grow — slowly, imperceptibly, at the pace of bacterial division and algal patience — and the glow would strengthen, and the light would reach a few inches further into a corridor that is currently navigable only by touch, and someone — perhaps the old man, perhaps the woman, perhaps one of the three — would walk through that corridor one evening and notice that they can see the moss on the wall, faintly, greenly, and they would stop and stand in the glow and breathe the warm air and listen to the drip and the tick and the slow creak of ferns, and they would feel, without knowing why, that the Sump is well.

That is what the wise power would do. It would add its attention to the attention of the people who already live there, and the Sump would absorb it the way it absorbs everything — slowly, completely, with no trace of where the new thing ends and the old thing begins — and then it would breathe, and grow, and continue.
