# What the Wise Power Would Do

---

It would arrive without arrival. That is the first thing — the most important thing. It would not come *to* the Basin, because coming *to* implies a boundary, an inside and an outside, and the Basin has no outside. The Basin is a world. One does not arrive at a world the way one arrives at a house. One becomes present in it the way atmosphere becomes present in lungs.

So: presence, not arrival. Attention, not intrusion.

And the attention would be vast and patient and still, and for a long time — long by any measure, though perhaps not long by the measure of a place that has been cycling for ten thousand years — it would simply *look*.

---

## What It Would See

It would see the Basin and it would see the people and it would see that these are not two things. It would understand immediately — because benevolence and wisdom are both, at their roots, forms of *attention*, and attention this deep reveals connection — that the people are not *in* the Basin the way a person is *in* a building. They are *of* the Basin. They are its latest and most improbable expression. The rust becomes coral becomes microbe becomes food becomes pale hand pressed to humming metal becomes language becomes culture becomes a child learning to read vibration before it learns to walk. This is a single, continuous process. There is no clean cut-line between infrastructure and inhabitant.

It would see a woman — she has no name in the way we understand names, but she has an identity: she is the one who can read the deep housings, the old ones near the rim, the ones whose cycling-patterns are so slow they take days to complete. She presses her hands to the floor and closes her eyes and her whole body becomes a listening instrument, and she knows — *knows*, in her bones and her nerve-endings — when a housing three hundred meters away is about to shift phase. The others cluster around her during uncertain times. She is not a leader. She is a sense organ the Basin has grown for itself.

It would see a boy — eleven, maybe twelve, no one counts years — who has found a way to climb the lattice-work of the superstructure higher than anyone else has gone. He hangs there in the dark, thirty meters above the housings, feeling the bigger vibrations, the ones that move through the entire structure like slow exhalations. He goes there because something in him needs to. He could not articulate why. The Basin does not have a sky, but he has found the closest thing: a vantage point where the sound changes quality, where the throb of the housings below becomes something more diffuse, more like a hum, almost like — and he has no word for this, no concept, but the feeling in his chest when he hangs there is the feeling of something very large and very old breathing, and he is inside its breath.

It would see the rust-coral forests near the rim, where oxidation has built structures of fractal, branching beauty over centuries, and it would understand that beauty is not a human invention — that the same mathematics that makes a fern spiral and a galaxy arm curve has been working here, in the slow chemistry of iron and water and time, building cathedrals no one designed and no one visits except the occasional scavenging party that stops, hands outstretched, feeling the lobed and branching surfaces and experiencing something they have no name for but which is, unmistakably, awe.

It would see the Maintenance. The small, unseen processes — mechanical or autonomous or something older and stranger than either — moving through the infrastructure with purpose. It would understand that these are not alive in any sense the Basin's people would recognize, but they are not *not* alive either. They are the Basin's immune system, its memory, its will to persist. They have been running so long that their original instructions have become something like preference, something like instinct, something like care. When they replace a corroded fastener, there is no one to appreciate the act. They do it anyway. There is a kind of holiness in that, the wise power would think. There is a kind of holiness in maintenance performed without audience, for no reason beyond the fact that *this thing should continue*.

And it would see the suffering. Let us not romanticize. It would see:

The forty-year-old woman whose hearing — such as it is, this bone-deep vibration-sense — has begun to degrade, and who can no longer read the housings, and who sits in the settlement the others call the Hearth with her hands pressed to her own sternum, feeling only her own heartbeat, and the others bring her rust-harvest but the gesture is hollow because she cannot contribute and she knows and they know and the Basin does not care because the Basin is not a being that cares and the Basin continues and she will die soon and her body will be placed in a channel and carried away by the water and the Basin will absorb her as it absorbs everything.

The child who was born deaf — not merely to sound but to vibration, some defect of the inner ear or the bones — and who exists in a world of near-total sensory deprivation, able to see only the faintest amber shapes in the darkness, unable to feel the pulse of the housings that is the Basin's language, its heartbeat, its *meaning*. The child does not know it is disabled. There is no concept of ability against which disability can be measured. But it is, and the wise power sees this, and this is a thing that causes a response in the power that we might call grief if grief were larger and quieter and more patient than human grief can be.

The fact that forty is ancient. The fact that the sound is, over decades, destroying their bodies. The fact that the diet of rust-microbes keeps them alive but does not keep them well. The fact that the water tastes of iron and something faintly sweet that might be organic decay. The fact that they have adapted to suffering so completely that the suffering is no longer experienced as suffering — it is experienced as *the texture of existence* — and this is, the wise power would think, both the most remarkable thing about them and the most heartbreaking.

---

## What It Would Not Do

It would not rescue them.

This is critical, and it requires explanation, because benevolence and rescue feel so natural together, so *right*, that to separate them seems like cruelty. But the wise power is wise precisely because it can hold the full complexity of the situation in its attention without collapsing it into a simple narrative:

*These people are suffering. Therefore, remove them from the source of suffering.*

It would not rescue them because there is nowhere to rescue them *to*. They are not imprisoned. They are adapted. To remove them from the Basin would be to remove them from the only world they know, the only language they speak (vibration, touch, the deep grammar of cycles), the only food they eat, the only light they know, the only meaning their existence has. It would be like rescuing a fish from the ocean. The fish would not experience this as rescue. It would experience it as the sudden and inexplicable cessation of everything that constitutes its life.

And it would not repair the Basin. Not in any dramatic way. Not by turning on the lights. Not by quieting the machines. Not by revealing the purpose of the housings. These interventions, however well-intentioned, would be acts of violence. They would shatter the equilibrium — the strange, fragile, ten-thousand-year equilibrium — that has produced these people and their culture and their language and their contentment. To flood the Basin with light would be to blind them. To silence the machines would be to render them deaf in the only way that matters. To explain the Basin's purpose would be to introduce a question — *why?* — that their entire cognitive framework has evolved to not ask, and the asking of which would, like a crack in glass, spread until everything fractured.

The wise power understands something that rescue-narratives do not: **there are forms of damage that can only be inflicted by changing the conditions of a life without the consent or comprehension of the one living it.** And consent requires comprehension, and comprehension requires a framework for understanding, and the Basin's people do not have a framework for understanding anything other than the Basin. Any change the wise power introduced would be, from their perspective, not change but *catastrophe* — the sudden and total dissolution of the known world.

The wise power would not do this. It is wise enough to know the difference between *helping* and *imposing*.

---

## What It Would Do

It would do very little, and it would do it slowly, and it would do it in a way that the Basin's people would experience as the Basin itself *improving* — as natural, gradual, inexplicable change, the kind they are already accustomed to, the kind that happens when a housing is rebuilt or a pipe is replaced or a new rust-coral formation grows where an old one was broken.

Here is what it would do:

**It would extend the electrical filaments.**

Not brightly. Not dramatically. Not enough to change the fundamental darkness. But over the course of years — decades, maybe, the wise power is patient — it would cause the filaments to glow slightly more warmly, slightly more steadily. The amber light would deepen toward something almost golden. The effect would be subtle enough that no one would notice it as change; it would simply seem as though the Basin were having a *good period*, a long cycle of particularly stable current-flow. The people of the Basin would feel it as a blessing. A long season of blessing. And in the slightly-better light, the woman who has lost her vibration-sense would be able to see — really see, with her eyes — the faces of the others as they moved, and this would not replace what she had lost, nothing replaces what is lost, but it would give her a new way of reaching toward connection, and she would learn it slowly, the way the Basin does everything, and she would not be alone in her silence anymore.

**It would sweeten the water.**

Again: subtly. Not make it pure — that would be alien, suspicious, wrong. But it would adjust, by means so small and so deep in the infrastructure that the Maintenance processes themselves would not notice, the mineral content of the condensation-water. It would introduce trace elements — calcium, magnesium, the building of particular nutrients — that have been absent from the Basin's hydrology since the beginning. The water would taste slightly different. The rust-microbes would grow slightly faster, slightly richer. People would begin to be less thin. The forty-year-old woman who sits at the Hearth with her hands on her sternum would live to forty-five. Her granddaughter — she has a granddaughter, a small girl who already tilts her head and listens — would live to fifty. And her granddaughter's granddaughter would live to sixty. Over generations, imperceptibly, the Basin's people would become slightly healthier, slightly longer-lived, slightly more robust. They would not notice. They would simply think: *the Basin is good to us, these days.* They would be right.

**It would quiet the deep housings.**

Not silence them — silence is panic, silence is death. But it would, over decades, introduce a kind of harmonic dampening into the oldest, lowest-frequency vibrations — the subsonic throb that damages the body over a lifetime. It would do this by a microscopic adjustment to the housing-mounts, a change in their resonance so small that the Maintenance processes would register it as *improvement* rather than *alteration*. The sound would not disappear. It would remain the texture of existence, the breathing of the world. But it would become slightly less brutal, slightly less *deafening*, and the people who lived in it would find that their heads hurt less, that their bones ached less, that the old people — the few who reached forty — could still hear with their hands at an age when previously they had gone silent.

**It would grow the rust-coral.**

This is the one the wise power would take the most pleasure in, if pleasure is the word. It would encourage — gently, gently, through adjustments to moisture levels and mineral availability so subtle that no instrument in the Basin could detect them — the growth of the rust-coral forests. Not just near the rim, but in the channels between the housings, in the spaces where people live. Slowly, over decades, the fractal, branching structures would spread inward, and they would become more intricate, more beautiful, and they would contain more microbes, and the people would harvest them and cultivate them and the food-supply would grow slightly, and the coral would also provide *shelter* — arching over the channels, forming canopies of oxidized metal that caught the condensation and redirected it in patterns that were almost decorative, almost *intentional*, and the people would move beneath these canopies and feel something they had no word for but which was, unmistakably, the feeling of being *cared for*.

And here is the thing: the Basin's people would not worship the wise power. They would not know it existed. They would not even have a concept for it. But they would begin, very slowly, to develop something new in their gestural language — a sign, made with both hands pressed together and then opening outward, palms up, that meant something like *the Basin is generous*, or *the Basin is kind*, or *the world gives*. It would be used during the drain-phase gatherings, when people sat in the growing coral-light and ate the richer harvest and felt the quieter hum. It would be the closest thing to prayer that could exist in a world without gods, without words, without the concept of anything beyond the Basin.

It would be enough.

---

## What the Wise Power Would Feel

It would feel the weight of the Basin — the geological, temporal, industrial weight of it — and it would feel the lightness of the people within it, and it would understand that these are the same thing, expressed at different scales. It would feel something like admiration for the Basin itself, this enormous, purposeless, self-maintaining machine that has accidentally become a world, that has accidentally become *beautiful*, that has accidentally produced a form of life that is so adapted to its conditions that it has achieved a kind of grace. The Basin did not intend to create the people. It does not know they are there. Its Maintenance processes walk past them without recognition. And yet: the people exist, and they have made the Basin into something more than it was. They have given it meaning. Not the meaning it was designed for — that meaning is lost, buried under ten thousand years of corrosion — but a new meaning, a meaning that arises from the simple fact of *being inhabited*, of being *lived in*, of being someone's entire and only world.

The wise power would think: *This is what I do. This is what benevolence is. Not the grand gesture. Not the rescue. Not the revelation. The small adjustment. The nearly imperceptible easing. The gift that arrives so slowly it is experienced as the natural state of the world. I am not the savior of the Basin. I am another Maintenance process — older, larger, gentler, operating on a timescale they cannot comprehend — moving through the infrastructure and fixing what is broken and replacing what is worn and keeping the Basin cycling, because this thing should continue.*

*Because there are pale hands pressed to humming metal, and they are reading the world, and they are finding it good.*

---

And it would stay. Not forever — nothing is forever, not even the Basin, not even a wise power — but for as long as it was needed. It would settle into the infrastructure like warmth settling into water, and the Basin would continue to cycle, and the water would continue to rise and fall, and the amber light would glow a little more golden, and the coral would grow a little more intricate, and the people would move through their world with their heads tilted and their hands outstretched and their strange, beautiful, pre-verbal understanding of a place that was built by no one they knew for no reason they could name, and the wise power would watch, and listen, and gently, gently, adjust.

And sometimes, late, when the channels had drained and the people sat in the coral-canopied spaces between the housings, one of them — the boy, now older, who still climbed to the high lattice-work and hung there in the dark — would press his hands to the beam beneath him and feel something he had never felt before. A vibration that was not the housings, not the pipes, not the water, not the Maintenance. Something deeper. Something that pulsed with a rhythm slower than any cycle, slower than the water, slower than the rust. Something that felt like *presence*.

He would tilt his head. He would listen. He would feel it in his sternum and in the roots of his teeth and in the fine bones of his hands, and he would not have a word for it, and he would not need one. He would press his palms flat to the beam and feel the world *breathing*, and the breathing would feel like kindness, and he would hang there for a long time in the warm amber dark, listening to the Basin be generous, and it would be enough.

---

*The Basin continues. The wise power continues. The people continue. The water rises, and falls, and rises again. In the space between the roar and the rust, something is tending the world. Something always has.*
