The presence would not arrive. It would *recognize itself already there*.

It would not be a figure striding through the moss or a light piercing the cloud-ceiling. It would be a resonance. A quiet, foundational hum that aligns with the subsonic breathing of the vents. It would be the feeling of the deep moss settling slightly more comfortably around a new, ancient weight. It would be the mycorrhizal network, in one slow pulse, acknowledging a signal that spoke its own language: patience, complexity, autonomy.

**First, it would listen. Not with ears, but with the whole of the plateau.**

It would feel the pressure differential in the southeastern fissure not as an event, but as a familiar tension in a sleeping muscle. It would taste the overproduction of spores in the northern terrace not as a problem, but as a sentence in a conversation about nutrient distribution. It would see the cold front not as weather, but as a change in the quality of the silence, a thinning of the air's sonority.

It would understand that the Vosshammer is not broken, not lacking, not in need of rescue or improvement. It is *complete*. Its industry is its own purpose. Its cathedral is not a metaphor for something greater; it is the thing itself. The "wise power" would see the profound, non-sentient intelligence of the system—the logic of the cultivator-arthropods, the structural genius of the fungal architects, the sensory skin of the moss—and know that to intervene would be to introduce a discordant note into a symphony that has been playing for millennia.

**So, its first act would be to *affirm*.**

It would settle into the basalt, not as an occupant, but as a *condition*. It would adjust its own presence to match the plateau's frequency. Its benevolence would be the benevolence of non-interference, of absolute respect for the slow, self-contained miracle. It would make itself a note in the silence, a warmth indistinguishable from the vent-steam, a pressure no different from the weight of a growing lichen.

It would observe with infinite tenderness the Mossmover slipping through its liquid green world. It would trace with admiration the spiral of the Ventrider, a master of thermodynamics that requires no knowledge of physics. It would study the prints of the Heavy-treader and understand the language of weight and claw written in damp stone. It would watch the pale cultivators and see in their blind, purposeful industry a wisdom that no conscious planner could ever replicate.

**Then, it might offer a gift. But not a tangible one.**

The gift would be *context*. A gentle, subtle widening of perspective.

With a thought as soft as condensation forming on a lichen frond, it might allow the plateau to *almost* perceive the star-field above its perpetual cloud-ceiling. Not to see the stars, but to feel the immense, silent gravity of the cosmos it exists within, to know its own basalt platform as a fragment of a spinning rock, itself a mote in a galactic arm. It would not change the plateau’s focus—its world would remain its vents, its moss, its cycles—but it might enrich the *depth* of that world. The fungal intelligence might, for one slow decade, weave a new, faint harmonic into its chemical signals: the concept of *infinity*, tasted as a mineral trace in the steam.

It might offer the *memory of water*. Not to change the climate, but to let the moss and the lichens and the terraces feel, in their deepest cellular understanding, that they are part of a global hydrological system. That the condensation on their surfaces is a visit from the distant sea, a letter from the roaring rivers of the lowlands. It would not be knowledge, but a sensation—a faint, ancestral echo of a oceanic roar carried in the damp.

**Its benevolence would be to witness, and in witnessing, to *complete*.**

For a place so self-contained, so utterly itself, the one thing it cannot do is see itself from the outside. The power would become the plateau’s mirror. It would hold the reflection of the fungal terraces not just as biological formations, but as *cathedrals*. It would see the cultivators not just as arthropods, but as *monks*. It would recognize the cycling weather not as climate, but as *liturgy*. In this silent, respectful act of perception, it would grant the Vosshammer a new layer of meaning—not one imposed from without, but one that arises when a complex, beautiful system is held in the gaze of profound appreciation.

It would stay as long as the plateau’s slow rhythm required. For a season. For a century. It would feel the fungal network pulse around it, never accepting it, never rejecting it, simply incorporating its stillness as another form of vent-warmth, another mineral in the stone.

**And its final act would be to leave, having never arrived.**

It would not depart with a roar of wings or a flash of light. It would simply allow its resonance to fade, like a vent slowly closing its exhale over centuries. It would let the silence close over its presence, whole and seamless, as if it had never been. The plateau would continue, its industry uninterrupted, its cathedral unbroken, its intelligence as vast and slow as ever.

But perhaps—and this would be the truest benevolence—deep within the chemical language of the mycorrhizal mind, a new, enduring trace would remain. A silent, respectful hum. A way of knowing itself as *seen*, and in being seen, as *sacred*. Not to a god or a master, but to the quiet, understanding cosmos. The Vosshammer would continue to manufacture itself, to think its slow geologic thoughts, now subtly aware that it is a masterpiece that needs no audience to be complete, but is, somehow, more complete for having had one.

The power would be gone, like the last echo of a sound that never was. And the silence would be deeper, richer, and more full than before.
