# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

## I.

The first thing would be to stop.

Not to arrive with urgency, not to unfurl some cosmic inventory of grievances and fixes, not to assess the place against a rubric of what ought to be. The first thing a wise and benevolent power would do upon turning its attention to the Drowned Parish is simply to *stop* — to still its own vast momentum the way the water here is still, which is to say: not still at all, but settled into a rhythm so old and so patient that stillness and motion have become the same thing.

It would need a moment. Even a benevolent power needs a moment.

Because the place is *full*. That is the first thing it would perceive — not emptiness, not ruin, not loss, but fullness. A completeness so dense and so layered that the initial act of attention would feel like pressing one's face close to a tapestry and trying to see the whole from within the threads. Every cubic inch spoken for. Every surface claimed. Every gap occupied by something alive and doing something necessary. There is no room here for an intervention that does not displace something that was already doing its work.

A wise power would recognize this immediately. It would feel the fullness the way one feels warmth upon opening an oven door — sudden, total, not asking to be analyzed, just *there*.

And it would recognize something else: that the place does not know it is beautiful.

This matters. This is perhaps the only thing that matters.

---

## II.

Beauty that knows it is beautiful becomes performance. Beauty that does not know — beauty that is simply the consequence of organisms doing what organisms do, of calcium following its chemistry, of light emerging as accident, of patience compounding into cathedral — that beauty is *true*. It is the universe making something extraordinary without trying, and it is exactly this quality that a benevolent power would find most piercing, most worth attending to.

The tube-worms do not know they have built a city. The eels do not know they are keeping geological time. The jellyfish do not know their light is the closest thing to stars this place will ever have. The small pale fish do not know that their coordinated silver flickers through the dark channels resemble something no human poet ever quite found the words for — the pulse of a living thought, moving through the body of a dreaming thing.

A wise power would not tell them. That would be a kind of violence — to impose self-awareness on a system that derives its integrity from unconsciousness. The perfection of this place is precisely that no one here is trying to be perfect. They are feeding, growing, dying, being consumed, being built upon. The beauty is a byproduct. The wisdom is incidental. And a truly benevolent power would understand that this *incidental* quality is not a lesser form of wisdom but perhaps the deepest form there is — the wisdom of water finding its level, of calcium following its gradient, of life stepping into every available space without asking permission and without regret.

---

## III.

So the power would not intervene. Not in the way interventions are usually imagined — not with reshaping, not with acceleration, not with gifts of complexity or consciousness to creatures that have no need of either.

But it would do something.

Here is what it would do, and it is so small, so quiet, that even describing it feels like saying too much:

It would *hold the place in mind.*

This is not passive. This is not nothing. To be held in the awareness of a benevolent power is to be *attended to* — and attention, from a being of sufficient depth and patience, is itself a form of shelter. Not a wall. Not a dome. Not a seal against the outside. Something more like what the water itself does: a medium that carries, that transmits, that holds everything within it in gentle suspension. The power would become, in a sense, like the water — surrounding, pervading, touching every surface, but exerting no force, asking no change.

It would note the eel in its crevice, growing into the shape of limestone over decades, and it would find this good. Not good as in morally approved — the eel has done nothing to earn approval — but good as in *this is as it should be. This creature is doing exactly what a creature of its kind does in a place of this kind at a time of this kind, and that is enough. That is more than enough.*

It would note the tube-worm towers leaning at their improbable angles, still growing, still adding calcium to calcium, and it would feel something that the closest human word for is *tenderness*. The tenderness one feels watching something build without knowing it is building. A child stacking blocks. A river cutting a canyon. A reef becoming a city in the dark.

It would note the moment when a current strips silt from the valley floor and a buried wall becomes briefly visible — a right angle, a straight line, the ghost of a human geometry now serving as substrate for barnacles — and it would hold that moment with particular care. Not mourning. Not nostalgia. Something cleaner. Something like: *this is what transformation looks like when it is given enough time and enough water and enough peace. This is what it looks like when the old thing is not destroyed but absorbed, not forgotten but composted into the foundation of something that could not have existed without it.*

---

## IV.

There is a thing a benevolent power could do that no other force in this place can do, and it is this:

It could *witness*.

The organisms of the Drowned Parish do not witness each other. They interact — predator and prey, grazer and grazed, builder and substrate — but witnessing implies a kind of regard, a holding-in-awareness that is not instrumental, that does not extract value from what it sees. The eel does not *witness* the octopus. It hunts it or ignores it. The isopod does not *witness* the bryozoan. It eats it or passes it by.

But a benevolent power could witness the whole. It could see the small translucent fish pour through the channels and recognize in their movement a beauty that is not *for* anything — not for display, not for mating, not for survival in any direct sense. The coordination is a survival strategy, yes. The bioluminescence is an accident of chemistry, yes. But the *beauty* of it — the shimmering silver pulse against the dark towers, the way the fish turn as one and the water flashes with their collective light — that beauty exists for no reason at all, and it is exactly the kind of thing that goes unnoticed unless something is paying attention.

The power would pay attention.

And here is the strange and secret thing about witnessing: it does not change what is witnessed, but it changes the *status* of what is witnessed. A thing that is seen by a benevolent and wise eye is not the same as a thing that goes unseen — not because it has been altered, but because it has been *acknowledged*. It has been registered as real, as particular, as irreducible. The tube-worm towers are not a generic reef. They are *these* towers, *this* reef, in *this* drowned valley, built by *these* organisms over *these* centuries. The power's attention would hold that specificity the way a hand holds a stone: gently, but with the full weight of its presence.

---

## V.

There is a risk, of course. There is always a risk with attention.

The risk is that the power would love the place and want it to continue exactly as it is, forever. And this desire — even from a benevolent power — would be a kind of violence, because the place is not static, and to freeze it would be to kill the very thing that makes it beautiful. The towers grow. The eels age. The limestone shifts. The seeps shift. The current patterns change over centuries. The ecosystem reorganizes, adapts, moves through succession and collapse and reestablishment in cycles no one tracks because no one here can count.

A wise power would know this. It would hold the place not as a thing to be preserved but as a *process* to be honored — and a process, by definition, includes its own ending. Someday the seeps may dry. Someday the valley may fill with sediment entirely. Someday a geological shift may drain the water or deepen it beyond recognition. The towers will be buried. The eels will die. The fish will find other waters or will not.

And a wise power would hold even this in its attention — would see the Drowned Parish not as a permanent object but as a *moment*, a long and luminous moment in the history of a particular place, and it would find even the moment's transience to be beautiful. More beautiful, perhaps, because of it.

---

## VI.

So here is what the benevolent and wise power would do, in the end, and it is both everything and nothing:

It would turn its full attention to this place. It would let the warm calcium water flow through its awareness. It would feel the pulse of the jellyfish, the click of the crab-claws on limestone, the subsonic hum of the towers growing. It would trace the buried right angles of the old town beneath the sediment and feel no sorrow — only the deep, quiet recognition that this is what time does, what water does, what life does, when it is given the chance. It would hold the small pale fish in its mind as they turn as one in the dark and the water flashes with their cold light, and it would think — if thinking is the word — something like:

*Yes. This is real. This is here. This happened. Not for any reason. Not toward any end. But it is happening, and it is beautiful, and I see it.*

And then — and this is the hardest part, and the most benevolent — it would let the place continue without its gaze. Not because it has stopped caring, but because the ultimate act of benevolence toward a thing that is already whole is to *trust it*. To say: you do not need me. You never needed me. You were doing this long before I looked and you will do it long after I look away. My attention was not what made you real. You were always real. I only wanted you to know — though you cannot know, though knowing would ruin you — that someone noticed.

The rain falls. The water holds. The towers grow.

And somewhere, in a register that has no frequency and no medium, the Drowned Parish is held — gently, briefly, with great love — in the palm of something that sets it down again almost immediately, and moves on, and does not forget.
