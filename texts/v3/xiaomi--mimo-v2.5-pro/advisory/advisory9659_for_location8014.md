# Preparation Guide for the Greenhouse of Thermophilic Noise

---

## BEFORE YOU GO: UNDERSTANDING WHAT YOU'RE WALKING INTO

You are entering a structure that is technically still a building and practically a single organism. The greenhouse is not dangerous in any conventional sense — nothing here hunts, nothing poisons with intent, nothing will rush at you — but it is profoundly hostile to the assumptions your body carries about what a place is supposed to be. The air will try to drown you slowly through your lungs. The floor will try to swallow your feet. The heat will try to cook you at the pace your own metabolism sets. You are not a visitor here. You are a foreign body, and the greenhouse's only mode of interaction with foreign bodies is absorption.

Prepare accordingly.

## BREATHING

This is not a joke and not an exaggeration.

The air in the Greenhouse of Thermophilic Noise is saturated. Humidity approaches 100%. The temperature is elevated — the living mass generates its own warmth, and the iron-and-brick structure holds it in — and warm saturated air carries less oxygen per breath than the cool dry air your lungs were designed to move through. You will feel this. Within ten minutes of entering, you will notice a slight breathlessness that has nothing to do with exertion. Your lungs will feel heavy. Each breath will feel like it is not quite enough, the way breathing feels at moderate altitude.

This is not dangerous in the short term for a healthy adult. But it compounds. Over an hour, the breathlessness will worsen. Over two hours, you may develop a headache from CO₂ buildup. Over three or four hours, you may begin to feel dizzy, confused, or nauseated — not from any toxin, but from the simple fact that your body is working harder than usual to extract oxygen from air that is already full of water vapor and depleted by the ongoing respiration of a very large organism.

**Recommendations:**

- Limit your visit to two hours maximum on a first trip. You can always return.
- Breathe slowly and deliberately. Hyperventilation in saturated air wastes energy and increases the sensation of breathlessness.
- If you begin to feel dizzy or develop a headache, leave. The exit is wherever you entered. The greenhouse does not shift, does not rearrange, does not try to keep you. It does not care enough about you to try to keep you.
- If you have asthma, COPD, or any respiratory condition, do not enter without medical clearance. This is not a medical disclaimer — the air genuinely will stress your lungs.
- A simple cloth mask dampened with your own water supply can help slightly, paradoxically, by warming and humidifying the air before it reaches your lungs, giving your respiratory mucosa a gentler transition. But do not expect this to solve the oxygen problem.

## HYDRATION

You will lose water faster than you expect. The heat is not extreme — perhaps 32 to 36 degrees Celsius, 90 to 97 Fahrenheit — but the humidity means sweat cannot evaporate, which means your body's primary cooling mechanism fails. You will sweat anyway, because your body does not know the mechanism has failed, and the sweat will simply run off you, and you will dehydrate without the cooling benefit that dehydration is supposed to buy you.

Bring water. More than you think. At minimum, one liter per hour of intended visit. Drink it on a schedule, not when you feel thirsty, because the humidity masks thirst — the air is wet enough that your mouth does not dry out the way it normally would, and you may not feel thirsty until you are already significantly dehydrated.

The water inside the greenhouse — the dark tannin-stained water in the mat, in the pools, running down the columns — is not safe to drink. It is not poisoned in any deliberate sense, but it is saturated with decomposing organic matter, bacterial colonies, fungal spores, dissolved iron, and the latex secretions of the fig. Drinking it will give you, at minimum, significant gastrointestinal distress. Do not.

## WHAT TO BRING

**Essential:**
- Rigid-soled waterproof boots
- Loose, breathable clothing you don't mind ruining
- At least two liters of water per person
- A light source — the space is dim, green-filtered, and in places the canopy is so thick that even in daytime the light drops below comfortable reading levels. A headlamp is ideal because it keeps your hands free for balance. The light will look strange here — the green-filtered air tints everything, and your white LED will appear slightly wrong, slightly too clinical, slightly alien against the warm organic tones. This is fine. You will get used to it.
- A watch or timer. Time becomes difficult to track inside. The sameness of the environment — the constant dripping, the constant clicking, the constant green dimness — erodes your sense of duration. People have reported spending what felt like thirty minutes inside and emerging to find two hours have passed. Set an alarm for your intended departure time. Trust the alarm, not your perception.

**Recommended:**
- A bandana or hat for the condensation drip
- A small towel, which you will use to wipe your face and hands and which will be damp and brown by the end
- Spare socks for after
- A plastic bag for your wet and stained clothing afterward
- A walking stick or trekking pole. The floor is uneven — root-masses, hidden glass, sudden depressions where the decomposed mat is deeper than expected. A third point of contact is valuable. Do not use an iron or metal pole if you can avoid it, as you may inadvertently damage surfaces the fig has incorporated.
- A notebook if you want to record observations. Your phone will function, but the humidity may fog the screen and the condensation on your hands will make touch interfaces unreliable. A pencil works better than a pen — ink runs in humidity.

**Do not bring:**
- Food with strong smells. Not because anything will be attracted to it — there are almost no animals here — but because the greenhouse's atmosphere is so saturated with its own scent profile (tannin, iron, latex, sweet decomposition) that introducing foreign smells is disorienting. Eat outside before or after.
- Expectations of beauty. This place is not beautiful. It is not ugly. It is *complete* in a way that has nothing to do with human aesthetic categories. It is a system that has finished becoming itself. Approaching it looking for beauty will cause you to miss what is actually here, which is stranger and more interesting than beauty.

## NAVIGATION AND MOVEMENT

The greenhouse is a single room. This simplifies navigation enormously — you cannot get lost — but it does not simplify movement.

The iron columns, originally spaced at regular intervals to support the glass roof, are now the fig's primary structural grip-points. Aerial roots descend from the canopy to each column, branching into fans of smaller roots that wrap the iron in layers. The space between columns is not always passable. Root curtains — dense hangings of aerial root, moss, and the fern understory growing in the root's shelter — block some corridors entirely. You will need to find gaps, duck under roots, or step over root-masses. Some of these root-masses are knee-high. Some are waist-high. None are climbable — the roots are smooth beneath their moss coating and the moss is wet and offers no friction.

Move slowly. The floor is treacherous. The decomposed mat is soft and uneven, and hidden beneath it are: glass panes, the original flagstone (now tilted, cracked, displaced by root-pressure), iron artifacts (the watering can, brackets, fragments of bench), and occasionally pockets where the mat is especially deep and your foot will sink to mid-calf. Test each step before committing your weight. Use your walking stick. Touch the iron columns for balance if you must, but be aware that the columns are weeping — the tannin-dark water runs continuously down them, and gripping them will turn your gloves or hands brown-orange.

The noise of your movement — the dull glassy notes when you step on hidden glass, the squelch of the mat, the rustle of fern fronds — will be loud against the ambient quiet. The clicking of the ventilation system is not loud. It is persistent, rhythmic, and precisely the kind of sound that recedes into the background when you are still and returns when you move. You will become aware, quite quickly, that you are the loudest thing in the room. This is an uncomfortable awareness. You are the only thing here that does not move on the rhythm of the fig.

## PSYCHOLOGICAL PREPARATION

This is, honestly, the section that matters most.

The Greenhouse of Thermophilic Noise is not dangerous. It will not hurt you. Nothing will chase you, nothing will bite you, nothing will attempt to trap you. The structure is stable — the fig has held it together for decades and will continue to hold it together for decades more. The heat is uncomfortable but not life-threatening if you leave when you planned to. The air is breathable if you limit your exposure. The floor is navigable if you go slowly.

And yet.

People who visit this place report a consistent emotional sequence, and it is worth knowing about in advance so that you can recognize it as it happens rather than being ambushed by it.

**First: curiosity.** You step inside. The clicking registers. The green light. The roots. The iron. It is strange and interesting and you want to explore. This phase lasts ten to twenty minutes.

**Then: enclosure.** The awareness that you are inside something. Not a building. Not a forest. Something. The walls are alive. The ceiling is alive. The floor is — not alive exactly, but *made of* things that were alive and are being made into aliveness again. The air is thick with the exhalation of a living thing. You become aware of the warmth not as temperature but as body-heat — the greenhouse's body heat — and this is the moment where your sense of *place* starts to destabilize. You are not in a room. You are in an organ.

**Then: unease.** The clicking. It is so regular, so mechanical, and yet it is not mechanical — it is the sound of a plant moving iron through slow hydraulic pressure, which means it is the sound of a machine being operated by something that is not a machine. This should not be disturbing. It is a plant opening a vent. It is no more sinister than a vine climbing a wall. But the regularity — the clock-like rhythm — projects a quality of intentionality that the plant does not have, and your pattern-seeking brain cannot reconcile the mechanical sound with the organic source. It feels like hearing a heartbeat from inside a clock. Your mind will supply explanations that are wrong. It will whisper *something is operating this*, *something knows you are here*. Nothing is. Nothing does. But the whisper will come.

**Then: isolation.** You are the only warm-blooded thing in the room. There are no birds, no mammals, no insects to speak of. The ecosystem is complete without fauna. It has no need of you, no need of anything like you, no niche for you. You are not unwelcome — unwelcome would require awareness of you — you are simply *irrelevant*. This is a harder thing to feel than hostility. Hostility at least confirms your existence. The greenhouse does not confirm your existence. You pass through it the way a single raindrop passes through a lake — briefly, indistinguishably, without trace.

**Then: if you stay long enough: peace.** Not comfort. Not warmth. Not belonging. Something colder than belonging and more durable: recognition that the world contains systems that function without you, that were functioning before you arrived and will function after you leave, that your presence here is not necessary and not significant and that this is — strangely, against all expectation — all right. The clicking continues. The water drips. The roots grow. You breathe, harder than you would like, and the breathing reminds you that you are alive, and the aliveness feels temporary in a way that is clarifying rather than frightening.

This is the gift of the place, if you are willing to receive it. Most people leave before this phase. That is fine. There is no requirement to stay.

## TIMING

Visit during daylight hours, whatever passes for daylight in your originating reality. The greenhouse has no artificial lighting, and the canopy reduces exterior light to a fraction of its original intensity. At night, the interior is effectively pitch-dark, and the sounds change — the clicking seems louder, the drips more isolated, the iron-creak more ominous — not because anything is different but because your other senses reach for meaning when sight fails, and they will find meaning that is not there.

Spring or early summer in the greenhouse's native climate is optimal. The fig's growth rate increases slightly in warmer months, which means the clicking is fractionally faster and the overall energy of the system is higher. This makes the experience more vivid. In winter, the system slows, the clicking becomes more intermittent, and the cold creeps in through the brick walls just enough to make the warmth feel less like a body and more like a memory of a body.

Rain is irrelevant. You will not hear it and you will not feel it. The canopy is absolute.

## AFTER YOU LEAVE

You will emerge into whatever counts as outdoors in the surrounding environment, and the first thing you will notice is the air — how thin it feels, how dry, how cold. Your lungs will expand with a relief that feels almost violent. The sounds of the outside world will seem cacophonous after the greenhouse's gentle percussion. Colors will look wrong for a few minutes — too bright, too varied, after the greenhouse's monochrome green.

Your clothes will be damp. Your skin will be damp. You will smell of tannin and iron and fig latex, and this smell will not fully leave your clothing. Shower when you can. Wash your boots. Dump the water in your bottles and refill — it will taste faintly of iron from the air.

You may find, in the hours and days after your visit, that you hear clicking in quiet rooms. Not tinnitus. Not imagination. Just your brain, still listening for the rhythm it learned inside the greenhouse, still trying to match the world to the cadence of a plant operating a machine it does not know is a machine.

This fades. Give it a week.

Some people go back. Not because they enjoyed it — enjoyment is not quite the right word for anything that happens inside the Greenhouse of Thermophilic Noise — but because they want to sit again in that specific dimness, breathing that specific thick air, listening to that specific clicking, and feel again the specific relief of being irrelevant. It is an acquired taste, like silence. Like heat. Like the slow green patience of a thing that has learned to need nothing but itself.
