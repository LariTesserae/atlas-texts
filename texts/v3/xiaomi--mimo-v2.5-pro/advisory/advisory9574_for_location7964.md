# Preparation Guide: The Moss Floor

## Preliminary Warning

Before anything else — before gear lists, before acclimatization protocols, before the question of *how to get there* — you need to sit with a fact that most travel guides to anomalous locales bury in a footnote: **this place may not let you leave on your own terms.** The spatial distortions are not incidental hazards. They are the environment itself. The Moss Floor does not have a reliable interior geometry. You cannot navigate by bearing, because bearings presuppose stable spatial relationships. You cannot navigate by landmarks, because every patch of moss is identical to every other patch. You cannot navigate by the sun, because the sun is a rumor here — a secondhand source, filtered through steam and moisture until what reaches the surface is a directionless, ambient pallor that offers neither warmth nor orientation.

You can enter. Whether you can exit — and whether "exit" means what you think it means once the spatial and temporal distortions have had their way with your proprioception — is a question that does not have a guaranteed answer.

Go anyway. Some places demand to be known. But go with your eyes open, and keep them open even when everything in you wants to shut down.

## Physical Preparation

### Altitude

The Moss Floor sits at approximately four thousand meters. This is high enough to produce altitude sickness in unacclimatized individuals — headache, nausea, shortness of breath, impaired judgment. The last of these is the one that matters most here, because impaired judgment in a spatially unstable environment is a compounding failure. You misread a distance, you step wrong, you sink deeper than expected, you panic, you lose track of your heading — and suddenly the steam closes in and you cannot see where you began.

**Acclimatize before arrival if at all possible.** Spend time at equivalent elevations in stable worlds. If your multiverse transit method allows intermediate stops, spend forty-eight hours at altitude on a geologically normal plateau somewhere — the Tibetan steppe, the Altiplano, any high-elevation grassland — before making the final crossing. Your body body needs to be producing the extra red blood cells before you arrive, because the Moss Floor will not give you time to adjust once you're there. Every other challenge hits simultaneously.

If your transit method does not allow intermediate stops, consider acetazolamide or a comparable carbonic anhydrase inhibitor, taken prophylactically. But medication is second-best. Real acclimatization is better.

### Temperature

The description says "a few degrees above freezing" with "a persistent hypothermic breath that never relents." This is deceptively gentle phrasing. What it means in practice is: **you will never be warm.** The wetness guarantees it. Moist air conducts heat away from the body roughly twenty-five times faster than dry air at the same temperature. Combine that with the near-freezing ambient temperature, the constant condensation raining on every surface including you, and the absence of any dry shelter or fire-building material, and you have a hypothermia environment that will kill an unprepared traveler within hours.

You need a clothing system that accounts for total saturation. **Nothing cotton. Nothing that absorbs water.** Every layer must be either synthetic and hydrophobic or — better — a modern merino-synthetic blend treated with a durable water repellent. But DWR coatings degrade under constant wetness, so do not rely on surface treatments alone. The base layer should wick. The insulation layer should retain warmth when wet — down is useless here; synthetic fill only, and the best you can afford. The outer shell must be fully waterproof and critically must have **mechanical venting** — pit zips, chest vents, something — because if you seal yourself inside a waterproof membrane in an environment this humid, your own sweat will soak you from the inside just as surely as the rain soaks you from the outside. The goal is not to stay dry. You will not stay dry. The goal is to manage wetness so that your core temperature drops slowly enough for you to remain functional.

**Bring twice as many dry base layers as you think you will need.** Store them in a truly waterproof compression bag — not a stuff sack with a roll-top, but a welded dry bag rated for submersion. Your dry base layers are your life insurance. When you stop moving and your metabolic heat generation drops, you change into a dry base layer, wring out the wet one, and seal it away. This buys you time. In the Moss Floor, time is the only currency that matters.

For your extremities: waterproof insulated gloves with a gauntlet cuff that cinches over your sleeve. Waterproof boots — not hiking boots, not trail runners, but **knee-high rubber boots with aggressive tread** or, better yet, chest waders of the type used in commercial fishing. ### Footwear on Unstable Ground

This deserves its own section because the Moss Floor is not solid. It is a living mat over a waterlogged substrate. The living layer near the surface is "a few meters thick," per the source description, but "thick" does not mean "uniform" or "trustworthy." There will be zones where the moss is thin, where the compressed peat below has subsided, where a pocket of liquid lies just beneath a deceptively stable-looking tussock. You will step, and you will sink. Possibly to your knee. Possibly to your waist. Possibly deeper.

**Snowshoes or wide-terrain flotation devices are essential, not optional.** They distribute your weight across a larger surface area, reducing the pressure per square inch on the moss and dramatically lowering your chance of breaking through the surface layer into the waterlogged substrate below. Commercial snowshoes will work, but consider something with an even larger footprint — custom-fabricated platforms if you have access to fabrication tech, or even purpose-built bog shoes if you can source them from worlds with extensive peatland travel traditions.

Practice walking in them before you arrive. Snowshoeing on a stable surface is a skill; snowshoeing on a surface that heaves and sways under you while your altitude-impaired lungs struggle for oxygen and cold rain runs down your neck is an ordeal. Build the muscle memory beforehand.

A **probe pole** — a lightweight, collapsible staff with a blunt tip — is critical for testing ground ahead of you. Probe every step. This will slow you to a crawl. Accept the crawl. Speed kills on the Moss Floor. Not metaphorically.

## Navigation

This is the hardest problem. Possibly an unsolvable problem.

### What Does Not Work

- **GPS.** If the Moss Floor exists in a multiverse transit context, your GPS unit almost certainly cannot acquire satellite signals. Even if it could, the spatial distortions would render the coordinates meaningless — the position reported would be where your receiver *thinks* it is in a coordinate system that the Moss Floor does not respect.

- **Compass.** Magnetic navigation requires a stable planetary magnetic field. The Moss Floor likely has one — the geological description suggests a massive, stable landmass — but a compass tells you which direction is magnetic north. It does not tell you where you are relative to where you started, and on the Moss Floor, "where you started" is a concept that becomes unreliable within hours. More importantly, a compass does not help you when every direction looks identical.

- **Visual landmarks.** There are none. The description is explicit: "you could walk for days across this plateau and feel that you had not moved at all, that the same ten-meter patch of terrain was regenerating itself just ahead of your feet." The tussocks, the steam columns, the herds of grazers — all of these move, drift, appear, and vanish. The only fixed features are the mechanical-sound zones (and even those may shift on some schedule) and the temporal pocket depressions with their moss spires. These are *features* you can recognize, but they are not *landmarks* in any navigational sense because you cannot plot a course between them when the space between them changes.

- **Dead reckoning.** Not reliable. Your pace count assumes distances are stable. They are not. You could walk one thousand steps north, turn one hundred and eighty degrees, walk one thousand steps south, and end up at neither your starting point nor the point directly opposite. The math doesn't hold.

### What Might Work

- **Backtrack markers — but only with extreme caution.** Carry lightweight, brightly colored markers — biodegradable flagging tape on short stakes, chemical glow sticks, anything that stands out against the pale green-silver moss. Drive one into the ground every fifty to one hundred meters. Note: the ground is not ground; it is moss over peat over compressed organic matter over compressed organic matter over *more* compressed organic matter, descending two hundred meters. Your stake will hold in the living surface layer. It will not anchor in anything beneath. It could be displaced by a grazer, swallowed by moss growth, or shifted by the spatial distortions themselves. But it's better than nothing. The key rule: **never go so far from your last marker that you cannot see it.** If the steam closes in and you lose visual contact with your most recent marker, stop. Wait. Do not press forward on faith.

- **Tether lines.** If you are traveling with a companion — and you should be — maintain a physical tether between you at all times. A thirty-meter climbing rope, kept taut or at minimum kept with tension feedback, will prevent the spatial distortions from separating you. This is not theoretical. The description says distances are "suggestions, not facts." Two people walking side by side could be gradually, imperceptibly drawn apart until they are hundreds of meters from each other without either noticing. The rope notices. Trust the rope, not your eyes.

- **Audio beacon from your entry point.** If your multiverse transit method creates a fixed exit point — a portal, a rift, a stable coordinates — consider leaving an active audio beacon at that location. Something loud, something continuous, something you can hear (or detect with a directional microphone) from a distance. The Moss Floor is already saturated with sound — dripping, humming, mechanical chugging — so the beacon needs to be on a frequency that cuts through. A low-frequency pulse, something your body can feel as much as hear, might work best given that the existing environmental sounds are mostly broadband. You navigate *back* to the beacon by homing on the signal. This is your lifeline. Treat it as such.

- **The mechanical sound as a grid.** This is speculative, but worth considering. The subterranean machinery produces vibrations that propagate through the moss. If those vibrations have a consistent spatial pattern — if the chugging is louder in some places and softer in others, and if that pattern is stable — then in principle you could develop a crude tactile map of the mechanical topology by noting the intensity of ground vibration at various points. This would be painstaking, slow work, and it assumes the vibration pattern doesn't change. But it may be the closest thing to a coordinate system this place offers. Bring a sensitive seismometer or accelerometer. Log readings obsessively.

## Environmental Hazards

### The Water

The water of the Moss Floor is not clean. It is "tea-colored with tannins leached from the deep peat" and in the mechanical zones "smells of copper." Tannins are not acutely toxic at low concentrations, but they are astringent and will wreak havoc on your digestive system if consumed in quantity. Copper-contaminated water is genuinely dangerous — copper toxicity causes nausea, vomiting, liver and kidney damage.

**Do not drink the surface water.** Bring all the water you need. Calculate your consumption rate at the worst case: cold, dry-altitude air (yes, the air is humid, but cold humid air still dehydrates you through respiration), physical exertion on unstable terrain, and the psychological stress of an alien environment. That means four to six liters per day, minimum. For a week-long visit, that is twenty-eight to forty-two liters of water. The weight is significant. This is unavoidable. You may want to consider a high-capacity filtration and purification system — something rated for heavy metals and organic compounds — as a backup, but *test it on actual Moss Floor water* before relying on it. Filters designed for terrestrial pathogens and sediment may not handle whatever biochemistry two hundred meters of compressed ancient moss produces.

### The Insects

The insect-analogs — too large, too many-segmented, cartilaginous rather than chitinous — are described as erupting in swarms with "a low, throbbing hum." You need to prepare for two things: the physical irritation of a swarm and the psychological impact.

Physically: a full-coverage mesh head net is mandatory, but it may not be sufficient against creatures the size of a human hand with cartilaginous bodies. Consider mesh: permethrin-treated outer layers (treat every piece of outerwear before departure), and a supply of picaridin or DEET-based repellent. Note that repellent efficacy against alien arthropod-analogs is unknown. It might work perfectly. It might do nothing. Have a fallback: a physical barrier. A tent-style bug shelter that you can deploy quickly, something freestanding that you can erect on uneven, yielding ground in under a minute, could be the difference between an unpleasant encounter and a dangerous one.

Psychologically: the sound. The description says you "feel it in your sternum before you hear it with your ears." Infrasound and near-infrasound at sufficient intensity causes anxiety, dread, nausea, and disorientation even in people who do not consciously perceive the sound. The insect swarms produce this. You may not be able to prevent the effect, but knowing it is coming — knowing that the rising panic in your chest has a physical, mechanical cause — can help you ride it out rather than react to it. Practice meditative breathing before you go. Learn to recognize the difference between *I am afraid because there is danger* and *I am afraid because a vibration frequency is acting on my vagus nerve.* The distinction will save your life.

### The Predators

They are unseen. The grazers fear them. You should too, but not in a way that paralyzes you. The predators' hunting strategy, as described, is ambush — moving through the moss without breaking the ice crust. This suggests they are adapted to the substrate in a way you are not, and that they are unlikely to view a stumbling, sinking, noisy human as either easy prey or natural prey. You are an anomaly. You are loud. You smell wrong.

But do not test this. **Make noise while you travel.** Not for the predators — for yourself, so you always know where you are relative to the sound you are producing. And for any companions. And because a creature that has never encountered a human before is an unpredictable creature, and unpredictability is the one thing you cannot afford.

Avoid traveling near herds of grazers. The predators follow the herds. Your presence near a herd puts you in the predators' operational area without giving you any of the grazers' evolutionary defenses.

### The Temporal Pockets

These are the most insidious hazard on the Moss Floor, because they are not dangerous in any way a human body is designed to perceive. A temporal pocket — a depression where "time does not pass evenly" and moss cycles through years of growth in minutes — looks like a feature. It looks interesting. You might be drawn to it, the way the insects are.

**Do not enter a temporal pocket.** Do not touch the moss spires. Do not lean over the edge. The effects of uneven temporal flow on human biology are not well understood in any multiverse context, and they are certainly not studied in this one. Possible outcomes include: cellular desynchronization (parts of your body experiencing different rates of time, which is as catastrophic as it sounds), localized temporal reversal (you age forward while the air in your lungs ages backward), or simply being *held* — caught in a flow of time so slow that from your perspective, the world outside accelerates to a blur and then to nothing and then the Moss Floor grows over where you stood and you are compressed into peat along with everything else.

If you see a moss spire formation — "tower-like," "two or three meters above the general surface," in a depression where the water is deepest — **detour around it.** Give it a hundred meters of clearance. If the spatial distortions have brought one closer to you than expected, back away. Mark it on your map (such as your map is) and never approach it again.

### The Machinery Zones

Where the mechanical sound is stronger — "closer to the surface" — the moss grows "faster, darker, twisted into shapes that suggest electromagnetic interference or chemical contamination." The water smells of copper. Everything avoids these zones.

You should too, but you should also **study them from a distance.** The machinery is the most anomalous feature of the Moss Floor — an artifact that predates the ecosystem, that has been running on geological timescales, whose makers are unknown. If you have come to the Moss Floor to *understand* it, the machinery is where the answers will be. But "understand" and "survive" are not the same verb.

Bring an EMF detector. Bring a Geiger counter (compressed ancient organic matter undergoing whatever chemistry two hundred meters of depth produces is not necessarily radiologically inert). Bring a gas detector capable of measuring methane, hydrogen sulfide, and carbon monoxide, because anaerobic decomposition in deep peat produces all three, and a mechanical heat source could be releasing them in concentrated plumes.

If you choose to approach a shallow machinery zone — and I am not recommending this, but I know that some of you will do it anyway — wear a respirator with organic vapor and particulate cartridges. Wear chemical-resistant gloves. Limit exposure time. Have a partner watching you from outside the zone who can physically drag you out if you become incapacitated.

## Psychological Preparation

This may be the most important section.

The Moss Floor will break you. Not through violence, not through spectacle, but through *sameness.* The landscape does not change. The light does not change. The sound does not change. The temperature does not change. You will walk for hours and see the same pale green, the same steam, the same tussocks, the same distant shapes of grazers moving through the dim. Your brain — a pattern-recognition machine evolved for savannas and forests and coastlines, for places with *edges* — will begin to malfunction in predictable ways:

**Pattern hallucination.** After enough hours of identical stimulus, your visual cortex begins inventing patterns that aren't there. A darker patch of moss becomes a structure. A steam wisp becomes a figure. The grazers in the distance become something else — something that is watching you. This is not a sign of mental illness. It is a sign of a brain starved of differentiation. It will happen. Expect it. When it happens, look away from the "pattern" and focus on something immediate — your hands, your gear, your companion's face. Re-anchor.

**Temporal dissociation.** Combined with the actual temporal pockets (which you will avoid but will still sense, the way you sense a storm coming even when you can't see the clouds), the monotony of the environment will make you lose track of time. Hours will feel like minutes. Minutes will feel like hours. This is dangerous because it degrades your decision-making — you forget to eat, forget to hydrate, forget to change your wet base layer, forget to check your backtrack markers. **Set alarms.** Use a vibrating watch or a device that produces tactile alerts at regular intervals — every hour, perhaps — that remind you to perform a systems check: eat, drink, check your feet, check your markers, check your partner, check your bearing (such as it is).

**The temptation to give up.** There will be a moment — probably on day two or three, when the novelty has worn off and the cold has settled into your bones and the spatial distortions have made you doubt whether you are going the right way or whether there *is* a right way — when you will want to stop. Not to rest. To stop *trying.* To sit down in the moss and let the steam close over you. This moment will feel like peace. It is not peace. It is hypothermia talking. It is the brain's response to an environment so alien and so monotonous that it interprets surrender as safety. **You must not listen to it.** This is why you have a companion. This is why you have the tether. Your companion's job — and yours, for them — is to recognize the moment of surrender in the other person and physically, firmly, lovingly refuse it. Pick them up. Walk them forward. Hand them food. Tell them a story. Remind them of the exit. Remind them why they came.

**Wonder.** This one is easy to forget in a preparation guide focused on hazards, but it is real and it is important. The Moss Floor is two hundred meters of accumulated life. It is a machine that predates memory. It is a place where time flows unevenly and space breathes. There will be moments — between the cold and the fear and the disorientation — when you look out across the pale green expanse, the steam rising in slow columns, the grazers moving like slow continents, the ice cracking and reforming, the light settling on everything like ash — and you will feel something that no other place in any universe can give you. A sense of *deep time.* A sense of being inside something so old and so patient and so utterly indifferent to your presence that it recontextualizes everything you have ever known. This feeling is why you go. Hold onto it when the bad moments come. It is real, and it is worth the cost.

## Final Notes

Travel in pairs at minimum. Three is better — one person can be incapacitated and the remaining two can still function as a team. More than four becomes difficult to keep tethered and coordinated on unstable terrain.

Your total expected travel speed is **one to two kilometers per hour** on flat, featureless moss with flotation devices and a probe pole. Account for this in your planning. If your entry point is not also your exit point — if you are making a one-way traverse — you need to be honest about whether you can cover the distance before your supplies run out.

You will not be the same person when you leave the Moss Floor. This is not a metaphor. The spatial distortions, the temporal pockets, the deep-time substrate, the mechanical heartbeat, the monochrome monotony — they do something to the way a human mind models reality. You will come back and find that distance feels *approximate.* That time feels *thicker.* That moss, when you see it in an ordinary garden on an ordinary world, will make you stop and stare for reasons you can't fully articulate.

This is the cost of going. The preparation above can keep your body alive. Nothing can prepare you for what the place does to your sense of what is real.

Go anyway.
