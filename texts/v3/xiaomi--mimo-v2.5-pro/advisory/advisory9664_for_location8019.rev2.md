# Preparation Guide: The Resonance Plateau

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before you pack a single item, sit with this: the Resonance Plateau is not a place you *visit* the way you visit other destinations. Most locations in the multiverse are hostile in ways that feel adversarial — a desert wants to dry you out, a tundra wants to freeze you, a jungle wants to digest you. The Plateau does not want anything. It does not notice you. You will stand on stone that has been singing the same chord since before your civilization existed, and it will not change its song by one vibration for your presence. The psychological weight of this is the first thing you should prepare for, and no gear list addresses it.

Many travelers who have spent time at high-altitude, high-exposure locations describe a particular species of existential vertigo that has nothing to do with the physical vertigo of height. It comes from the sudden, visceral confrontation with *indifference at scale*. The Plateau will give you this in a form so concentrated it borders on violence. If you have a history of panic disorder, dissociative episodes, or existential depression, consult a multiverse-experienced mental health professional before going, and honestly consider whether this is a destination that serves your wellbeing. This is not cowardice. This is the same judgment you'd exercise before entering any environment with the power to permanently alter your psychological baseline.

That said — for many travelers, the Plateau becomes one of the most meaningful experiences of their multiverse life. The same overwhelming scale that breaks some people reorganizes others. You may come back unable to describe what happened to you in words that feel adequate, but knowing with absolute certainty that something did.

## Altitude Preparation

You are going to the roof of the world, or close enough to it that the distinction is academic. The description notes that "your lungs labor" — this means you are at an altitude where oxygen partial pressure is severely reduced. Depending on exactly where in the multiverse this plateau exists and the atmospheric composition of its world, you may be at the equivalent of 5,000–6,000+ meters above sea level.

**Acclimatization is not optional.** Spend a minimum of 5–7 days at progressively higher elevations before reaching the Plateau's surface. If your multiverse travel method allows you to arrive at a mid-altitude staging point — a mountain base camp, a high valley, a transitional scrubland zone — use it. Sleep at altitude. Let your body produce extra red blood cells. Let your breathing pattern adjust. Arriving directly on the Plateau from low altitude is medically dangerous and will make every other challenge exponentially worse, because your cognition will be impaired, your physical capacity will be halved, and the sensory overload of the place will be landing on a brain that is already oxygen-starved and panicking.

**Bring supplemental oxygen.** Not a suggestion. Carry at least two compact oxygen canisters with delivery masks. You may not need them. You may need them desperately on the first night when you wake at 3 AM with your heart hammering and your vision greying at the edges. Altitude sickness does not care about your fitness level. It does not care that you've trained. It arrives on its own schedule and escalates without warning into pulmonary or cerebral edema, both of which are fatal without descent or oxygen supplementation.

**Hydrate aggressively in the days before arrival and continue to do so on the Plateau.** High altitude is profoundly dehydrating. The air is cold and thin and every breath you exhale carries moisture out of your body. You will not feel thirsty in the way you're accustomed to — the cold suppresses the sensation. You will need to drink water on a schedule rather than on instinct. Two to three liters per day minimum, more if you're exerting yourself.

**Learn the symptoms of altitude illness and take them seriously:**
- Headache that doesn't respond to painkillers
- Nausea, loss of appetite
- Unusual fatigue beyond what the exertion explains
- Confusion, slurred speech, or impaired coordination
- Ataxia (stumbling, inability to walk a straight line)
- Persistent cough, especially if productive

Any of these — especially the last three — means descend immediately if possible, or deploy supplemental oxygen and do not attempt further ascent. People die on mountains because they dismiss early symptoms as "just the altitude." The Plateau does not have a rescue service.

## Hearing Protection

This is the single most critical piece of equipment you will bring, and I need to be specific about what kind.

The Resonance Plateau produces sound across the entire audible spectrum and below it. The subsonic component — the pressure wave that "makes your vision swim at the edges" — operates at frequencies below 20 Hz and cannot be blocked by conventional ear protection. The roar of wind over stone is broadband noise at punishing volume. The keening whistles through rock fissures produce sharp, high-frequency tones that are the most damaging to hearing over time. And the harmonic resonance of the water pools adds complex tonal layers that your brain will desperately try to pattern-match into music, which is exhausting in its own right.

**You need dual-layer hearing protection:**

*Inner layer:* Custom-molded high-fidelity earplugs with flat-attenuation filters. These reduce overall volume evenly across frequencies rather than muddying the sound profile, which matters because you need to be able to detect changes in your environment — the pitch-shift that might indicate an approaching weather change, the change in wind texture that might warn you of a localized downdraft. Expect 25–30 dB of attenuation from quality plugs.

*Outer layer:* Over-ear electronic hearing protection rated for continuous high-noise environments. Industrial grade, not recreational shooting grade. Look for something rated NRR 30+ with a low-profile seal that won't be torn off by wind. Electronic models will allow you to hear speech from a communication companion at reduced volume while still blocking the environmental roar.

*Together,* these give you roughly 40–50 dB of combined reduction. The Plateau is likely producing ambient levels of 100–120+ dB across its various registers. With dual protection, you're down to 60–80 dB — roughly the volume of a loud conversation. This is livable but not comfortable. You will still feel the subsonic pulse in your body. You will still hear the wind. But you will be able to sleep — fitfully, shallowly, but you will sleep — and you will not sustain permanent hearing damage from a multi-day visit.

**Bring backup pairs of everything.** Ear protection can be damaged, lost to a sudden gust, or simply wear out. Bring at least three sets of earplugs and two sets of over-ear protection.

**Consider also:** A bone-conduction communication device worn beneath the over-ear protection, paired with a companion device, for person-to-person communication. Speaking aloud on the Plateau is functionally impossible without pressing your mouth to someone's ear and shouting. A vibrating transducer against the mastoid bone can carry simple messages through the noise.

## Wind Preparation

The wind on the Resonance Plateau is not a weather event. It is a permanent geological fact, like the stone itself. It blows from the west continuously at speeds that, based on the description of its effects on the stone and on the fauna's adaptations, are likely in the range of 60–100+ km/h sustained, possibly higher in exposed areas and through channeled gaps.

This is not a stiff breeze. This is a force that will knock you off your feet if you stand upright in the wrong place. The birds survive it by being inches tall and by hiding in cracks. You are not inches tall. You do not fit in cracks.

**Clothing must be windproof and low-profile.** Nothing that flaps, billows, or catches air. A close-fitting hardshell jacket with a secure hood, sealed cuffs, and a high collar that protects the lower face. Hardshell pants. Gaiters to prevent wind from driving air up your pant legs. Every garment should have a streamlined, body-hugging cut. You are dressing not for cold alone but for the aerodynamic reality that the wind will try to get *inside* your clothing and inflate you like a sail.

**Footwear** should be heavy mountaineering boots with aggressive tread and ankle support. The stone surface is undulating and can be slick with condensation, especially in the early morning. A stumble in this wind can become a fall that becomes a slide that becomes an injury in a place with no rescue infrastructure. Crampons are probably not necessary — the stone is not icy — but approach shoes or boots with sticky rubber soles would be wise for the smoother surfaces near the pools.

**Walking poles or a trekking staff** are strongly recommended. In sustained high wind, a third point of contact with the ground is the difference between controlled movement and a humiliating and potentially dangerous tumble. Plant each step deliberately. Do not rush. The Plateau has no trails, no markers, no destination worth hurrying toward. It will be the same in every direction. Move slowly and deliberately or the wind will move you.

**Weight distribution matters.** Carry your pack weight as low and close to your center of gravity as possible. A heavy pack high on your back turns you into a lever the wind can use against you. Use a pack with a hip belt that transfers weight to your hips and keeps the load tight to your body.

**Plan your movements around the terrain.** Walk in the low places between the stone swells when you can — the undulations of the plateau create wind shadows on their leeward sides. Move from sheltered position to sheltered position the way the birds do. This will add distance and time to any route, but it will keep you upright.

## Eye and Skin Protection

The light on the Resonance Plateau is "cool, surgical" and arrives with "almost no scattering." This is a technical way of saying the ultraviolet exposure is extreme and the visible light intensity is blinding.

**Eye protection is non-negotiable.** Bring high-quality UV400-rated sunglasses with side shields or wraparound frames. Glacier glasses, essentially — the kind designed for high-altitude snow and ice environments. The stone at this altitude reflects UV upward into your face from below, so side shields and a good fit against your cheekbones matter more than you think. Bring a backup pair. Losing your only sunglasses on the Plateau means either retreating immediately or going functionally blind from photokeratitis (snow blindness, which is not exclusive to snow — any bright reflective surface at altitude will cause it).

**Sunscreen.** SPF 50+ broad-spectrum, applied liberally and reapplied every two hours. The thin atmosphere offers almost no UV filtration. You will burn faster than you believe possible, even in cold air, even under an indigo sky that doesn't "look" sunny. Pay special attention to the underside of your chin and nose — the reflective stone throws UV upward. Pay attention to the insides of your nostrils and your ear canals — sounds absurd until you've had a sunburn inside your nose.

**Lip protection** with SPF 30+ balm, applied constantly. Your lips will crack within hours in this combination of cold, wind, UV, and dry air.

**A face covering** — a buff, balaclava, or gaiter worn around the neck and pulled up over the nose and mouth when the wind picks up grit. The description mentions "grit-laden wind" wearing grooves into the stone. That grit is also hitting your face. At the velocities described, wind-driven particulate can abrade skin and corneas. The face covering serves double duty: grit protection and moisture retention for your breathing zone, since every breath in this air is desiccating.

## Clothing: The Full Layering System

The Resonance Plateau is cold. Exactly how cold depends on the season and time of day, but at the altitudes described, expect daytime temperatures around 0–10°C and nighttime temperatures well below freezing, possibly -15°C to -25°C. The wind chill factor in sustained 80+ km/h winds at those temperatures is severe — potentially -30°C to -40°C effective temperature.

You need a serious layering system:

**Base layer:** Merino wool or synthetic moisture-wicking. Full-length top and bottom. Merino is preferred because it retains some insulating value when damp and resists odor (you will be wearing these for multiple days in a place where changing clothes is an ordeal).

**Mid layer:** A fleece or synthetic insulated layer. Down is risky — if it gets wet from condensation contact or sweat, it loses insulating capacity, and drying it on the Plateau is essentially impossible. A high-quality synthetic puffy (Primaloft, Climashield, or similar) is more forgiving.

**Hardshell (already described above):** Windproof and water-resistant. Gore-Tex Pro or equivalent. This is your most critical garment. If your shell fails, the wind strips your body heat at a rate that leads to hypothermia in minutes.

**Insulated layer for stationary periods:** When you stop moving — to sleep, to observe the pools, to simply exist and absorb the place — you lose the metabolic heat of exertion and the wind chill becomes immediately dangerous. Bring a heavy insulated parka that goes over everything, rated for extreme cold. This is your "stop and look at the world" layer. Do not skimp on it.

**Hands:** Heavy insulated gloves with a windproof shell, plus thin liner gloves for dexterity when you need to adjust equipment or manipulate objects. Your hands will be the first part of you to lose function in the cold. Bring chemical hand warmers — the disposable kind that activate by shaking. Pack a dozen. They weigh nothing and can save you from frostbite when you need to use your fingers in -40°C wind chill.

**Head:** An insulated hat that fits under your hood. A balaclava. The hood of your shell jacket cinched tight. You lose an enormous percentage of your body heat through your head, and on the Plateau, your head is the most exposed part of your anatomy, aimed directly into the wind like a flag.

**Feet:** Heavy wool socks over thin liner socks. Mountaineering boots as described. Chemical toe warmers for sleeping. If your feet get cold and stay cold, you are in trouble — cold feet lead to impaired mobility, impaired judgment, and eventual frostbite. Dry your socks whenever possible by sleeping with the next day's pair inside your sleeping bag against your body.

## Shelter and Sleep

There are no natural shelters on the Resonance Plateau. The stone swells provide some wind shadow in their lee, but the wind is so constant and powerful that even "sheltered" spots are windy by any normal standard.

**You need a four-season mountaineering tent rated for extreme wind.** Not a backpacking tent. Not a three-season tent with a wish and a prayer. A genuine high-altitude expedition tent — Hilleberg, Mountain Hardwear, The North Face Summit series, or equivalent — with geodesic or semi-geodesic pole architecture that distributes wind loads, a full-coverage fly that reaches to the ground, and bomb-proof guy-out points. Practice pitching it in high wind before you arrive. You will need to pitch it in high wind on the Plateau, possibly in the dark, possibly while oxygen-deprived, and fumbling with unfamiliar pole configurations in those conditions is a recipe for a destroyed tent and a very bad night.

**Stake it to the stone.** This is the unusual challenge. You cannot drive tent stakes into solid rock. You will need to anchor your guy-lines with rock anchors — heavy stones placed in sling loops, or pre-drilled bolts if you have the means and the time. Some travelers use sandbags filled with gravel. The specific technique depends on the gear you can carry, but the principle is: your tent must be anchored to the ground as if a continuous gale were trying to tear it free, because that is exactly what is happening.

**Sleeping bag:** Rated to at least -20°C, preferably -30°C or lower. Again, synthetic fill is more reliable than down in this environment, though quality down bags with hydrophobic treatment are acceptable if you can keep them dry. Bring a sleeping bag liner — silk or thermal — to add 5–10°C of warmth and to keep the bag's interior clean over a multi-day trip.

**Sleeping pad:** High R-value, minimum 5. The stone is a massive, relentless heat sink. Your body pressed against stone through a thin sleeping pad will lose heat conductively at a rate that feels almost aggressive. A closed-cell foam pad under an inflatable pad is the classic two-pad system for extreme cold sleeping and is recommended here.

**Sleeping on the Plateau will be difficult even with perfect gear.** The subsonic pressure wave penetrates your tent, your sleeping bag, your body. It makes your chest feel tight. The audible noise, even attenuated by double ear protection, is continuous and disorienting. You may find that you can only sleep in short intervals of 60–90 minutes before the cumulative sensory pressure wakes you. This is normal. Do not fight it. Rest even when you can't sleep. The Plateau is not a place that rewards ambition or punishing schedules. Rest, wake, listen to the impossible chord of the world, sleep again.

## Hydration and Nutrition

**Water sources on the Plateau exist but are complicated.** The condensation pools are described as "extraordinarily clear" and support invertebrate life, which means they are not sterile. Bring a robust water purification system — a pump filter with a 0.2-micron element *and* a chemical purification backup (chlorine dioxide tablets or equivalent). The combination is important because some high-altitude waterborne pathogens may pass through filtration alone.

Collecting water from the shallow condensation pools requires patience and careful technique. The pools are shallow and the water is precious to the ecosystem. Take only what you need. Do not contaminate the pools with equipment, sunscreen runoff, or fuel residue. The Plateau's water system operates on an almost geological timescale — what you take is not replenished in any timeframe relevant to your visit.

**Bring water collection tools:** A wide-mouth bottle or collapsible container with a low profile that you can hold steady in the wind near a pool's surface. This is harder than it sounds. Wind across a water surface creates spray and turbulence. You may need to shield the collection point with your body or a windbreak.

**If possible, carry all the water you need.** This is heavy — water weighs one kilogram per liter — but it eliminates the contamination risk and the time spent collecting. For a 3–5 day visit, you would need 10–15 liters, which is 10–15 kg of weight. This is a significant portion of your carry capacity. You must decide based on your physical ability and the specific logistics of your visit.

**Nutrition:** High-calorie, easy to eat with gloved hands, requiring no cooking or minimal cooking. The wind makes cooking with any open-flame or exposed-stove system extremely difficult — not impossible, but frustrating and fuel-inefficient. Use a enclosed, wind-resistant stove system if you need to heat water for freeze-dried meals. Better yet, bring foods that require no preparation: energy bars, nuts, dried fruit, chocolate, cheese, cured meat. Eat frequently and calorie-densely. Your body is burning enormous energy just to maintain core temperature and to breathe at altitude. Under-eating on the Plateau leads to a downward spiral of impaired thermoregulation, impaired judgment, and impaired physical capacity.

**Avoid alcohol.** It dilates blood vessels and accelerates heat loss, it impairs acclimatization, and it dehydrates you. There is no version of having a drink on the Resonance Plateau that makes your situation better.

**Avoid caffeine in the evening.** You are already going to have terrible sleep. Do not make it worse. Caffeine in the morning, fine, but remember that it is a diuretic and you need every drop of water in your body.

## Communication and Navigation

**You cannot communicate verbally on the Plateau without physical contact.** This is not an exaggeration for effect. The ambient noise level is such that even shouting directly into someone's ear from two inches away is barely effective. Plan accordingly:

- Establish a set of hand signals with any companions before arrival. Keep them simple: stop, go, come here, are you okay, I am not okay, I want to go back, look at that, shelter needed, water needed. Practice them until they're automatic. On the Plateau, you will not have the cognitive bandwidth to improvise gestures under sensory overload.

- Consider a vibration-based communication system. Some multiverse-travel suppliers offer comm devices that use tactile pulses against the skin — essentially a Morse code system transmitted through a wristband. These work in any noise environment because they bypass hearing entirely.

- Agree on a separation protocol. If you become separated from your companion(s) in low visibility or in the disorienting terrain, what is the plan? Walk to a pre-agreed landmark? Stay put? Use a signal mirror or bright fabric? The Plateau's undulating terrain and lack of landmarks make visual contact unreliable at even moderate distances.

**Navigation** is a serious concern. The Plateau has no trails, no markers, no features that are meaningfully distinct from one another to a human eye. The stone swells all look similar. The pools are scattered apparently at random. On a clear day, the horizon is so distant that visual landmarks on the Plateau's edge may be days of walking away.

- Carry a GPS device with spare batteries. Cold drains batteries rapidly — keep the unit inside your clothing against your body when not in active use. Mark your arrival point (wherever you entered the Plateau) as a waypoint immediately and navigate back to it by bearing and distance.

- Carry a compass. GPS can fail. A compass works anywhere there's a magnetic field.

- If you are moving across the Plateau, drop cairns — small stacked-stone markers at regular intervals marking your route. This is a violation of the Plateau's pristine character, and you should remove them when you leave if possible, but they may save your life. Do not rely on them alone — the wind can topple a cairn — but use them as a supplementary navigation aid.

- Travel only in conditions of good visibility. Fog, mist, or the rare cloud that intrudes onto the Plateau's surface will reduce visibility to near zero against the featureless stone. If visibility drops, stop. Set up your tent if the exposure is long. Wait.

## Medical Kit

Beyond the altitude-related medications and supplies already discussed, prepare a medical kit tailored to this environment:

- **Pain relief:** Ibuprofen for altitude headaches, acetaminophen as an alternative. Take altitude headaches seriously — they are a symptom, not just an inconvenience.

- **Diamox (acetazolamide):** If you have access to this carbonic anhydrase inhibitor, bring it. It accelerates acclimatization and reduces the severity of altitude symptoms. Start taking it 24–48 hours before ascending to the Plateau's altitude. Common side effects include tingling in the extremities and increased urination — both manageable.

- **Wound care:** Bandages, antiseptic, butterfly closures. On the Plateau, a cut or abrasion heals slowly because of cold, altitude, and dry air. A wound that's trivial at sea level can become a serious problem when your body is devoting all its resources to oxygen management and thermoregulation.

- **Eye drops:** Rewetting drops for dry, wind-blasted eyes. Even behind good sunglasses, the air at this altitude and wind speed is profoundly desiccating.

- **Skin care:** Petroleum jelly or a thick barrier cream for the exposed skin of your face — nose, cheeks, eyelids. The combination of UV, wind abrasion, and cold creates a condition similar to "windburn" that progresses rapidly to cracked, bleeding skin if not mitigated.

- **Blister care:** Moleskin, tape, and a needle for draining if necessary. You will be walking on unfamiliar, uneven terrain in heavy boots. Blisters are likely.

- **Emergency bivouac gear:** An emergency space blanket (the heavy-duty reusable kind, not the cheap mylar sheet that disintegrates in wind) and a chemical body warmer. If your tent fails or you are caught away from camp, these may keep you alive through a night on the Plateau. A night on the Plateau without shelter is not guaranteed fatal, but it is dangerous enough that the emergency supplies are worth their weight.

## What to Leave Behind

This is as important as what to bring.

**Do not bring a drone.** The wind will destroy it or carry it away. Even if it survives, the sound of a drone motor is inaudible under the Plateau's roar, and the presence of a buzzing machine in this place feels like a desecration.

**Do not bring a musical instrument with the intention of "playing with the wind."** I understand the temptation. The description practically begs for it. But the Plateau's chord is so vast and so complex that your instrument is not a contribution to it — it is an interruption. Some places have music that is complete. This is one of them.

**Do not bring expectations of productivity.** You will not read a book on the Plateau. You will not journal fluently. You will not meditate in any way you currently recognize as meditation. The sound does not permit ordinary inward focus. What the sound permits — demands, really — is a kind of radical external attention that is its own form of practice, if you let it be.

**Leave behind your sense of time as a resource to be managed.** The Plateau operates on a timescale that makes your schedule meaningless. The wind has blown for millennia. The lichens are a century per handspan. The pools are in permanent stasis. Arriving with an itinerary — "I'll spend two hours at the pools, then move to the eastern ridge for sunset" — is a category error. You are a temporary pattern in a place that deals in permanence. Be where you are. Leave when it's time to leave. The Plateau will not notice either way.

## The First Hour

You will step from whatever transitional terrain brought you here — scrubland, scree, a final ridge of broken rock — onto the surface of the Plateau, and you will understand immediately that you are somewhere unlike anywhere else you have ever been.

The wind will hit you first. Not gently, not as a warning. It will arrive at full force, as if it has been waiting for you specifically, and it will press against you with a sustained, implacable weight that feels less like air and more like leaning against a wall that is also leaning against you. Your first instinct will be to brace, to lean into it, to fight it. Resist this instinct. Lean slightly forward, angle your body, and let the wind slide over you. You cannot overpower it. You cannot outlast it. You can only accommodate it, the way the birds accommodate it, the way the stone accommodates it — by becoming shaped to it.

The sound will arrive simultaneously. It will be the loudest continuous sound you have ever experienced outside of an industrial accident or a concert venue. It will not peak and subside. It will not let you find a quiet moment to recalibrate. It will simply *be*, everywhere, at all times, and within seconds you will realize that the silence you've carried your entire life — the ambient quiet that underlies every other experience you've ever had — was a gift you never knew you were given. On the Plateau, that gift is withdrawn. You will not hear the quiet again until you leave.

The light will hit you as you raise your head against the wind. The sky will be darker than you expect — an indigo so deep it looks almost wrong, the kind of blue that exists in photographs you assumed were color-corrected. The stone will be brighter than you expect — almost luminous, glowing with reflected light that has nowhere to hide, no clouds to soften it, no atmosphere to scatter it. The contrast between the dark sky and the bright stone will make the world look like a photograph with the contrast slider pushed too far. Your eyes will water. Put on your sunglasses immediately.

And then, once you have braced and blinded and deafened yourself, you will begin to walk. The ground will be uneven, the stone swells rising and falling beneath your feet in slow, patient waves that make each step slightly different from the last. You will watch the ground because looking up is overwhelming. You will watch your feet place themselves on stone that was old before your species existed, and you will feel, very precisely, how small you are. Not in the vague, philosophical way of looking at stars. In the immediate, physical, visceral way of standing on something so large and so old and so indifferent that your body has no framework for processing it and simply *recoils* — a full-body flinch from something that isn't attacking you, that isn't even aware of you, but whose sheer *thereness* is more than your nervous system was built to handle.

This will pass. Not entirely, but enough. Within the first hour, your body will begin to adapt — not to the altitude (that takes days) but to the *presence* of the place. Your shoulders will drop slightly. Your breathing will find a rhythm against the wind. You will stop flinching at the sound and start *listening* to it, not with understanding but with a kind of stunned acceptance. The subsonic pulse that felt like an assault will become a heartbeat you didn't ask for. The keening whistles will start to separate into distinct voices, each one coming from a specific fissure or gap in the rock. The deep roar will become the ground-state, the default, the new silence.

You will see the birds. At first, you will mistake them for shadows or dark patches on the stone — their camouflage is that good. Then one will move, and you will realize that what you thought was stone texture is alive, is *packed* with life, is teeming with small grey bodies pressed into every crack and hollow, watching you with bright dark eyes, utterly unbothered by the wind that is trying to push you over. You will feel a strange, irrational envy. They belong here. You do not.

You will find a pool. It will be smaller than you expected — the description makes them sound significant, but the most common pools are depressions no larger than a dinner plate, cupped in the stone like cupped hands. You will kneel beside it (the wind will try to push you flat as you lower your center of gravity, so brace carefully) and you will look into water so clear that for a moment your brain cannot process it as water — it reads as empty air, as absence, as a hole in the stone. Then your eyes will adjust and you will see the dark filaments clinging to the bottom, and the tiny translucent creatures pulsing among them, and you will understand that you are looking at a complete ecosystem contained in an amount of water you could carry in your cupped palms. A world. Sealed. Self-contained. Eternally still.

If you are very quiet — and you cannot be quiet, not really, but you can be *still* — you may see one of the larger shapes in one of the larger pools. A slow, pale undulation in the shadowed deep water. It will look like nothing you can name. It will move with a patience that matches the stone, the lichen, the wind. You will watch it for as long as your knees and the cold allow, and you will understand nothing about it, and that understanding of your not-understanding will be one of the truest things you have ever felt.

## Departure

When it is time to leave, you will know. Not because the Plateau tells you — it doesn't, it never will — but because your body tells you. The accumulated weight of the sound, the altitude, the cold, the light, the sheer *density* of sensory input will reach a point where something in you simply says *enough*. Not because you are tired, though you will be. Not because you are frightened, though parts of you have been frightened the entire time. But because you have received as much of this place as you can hold, and to stay longer would not deepen the experience but would begin to erode your ability to carry it with you.

Pack everything. Leave no trace. Remove any cairns you placed, any anchor stones you stacked. The Plateau should look exactly as it did when you arrived. It will, regardless — the wind and the stone will cover any marks you've left in a decade or a century, and the Plateau has both to spare — but the courtesy matters.

Walk back to the edge. The transition will be as abrupt as the arrival — one moment you are on the Plateau, the next you are stepping off it, and the wind will drop, and the sound will begin to fade, and the first silence you hear will feel like a physical thing, like a warm hand placed over your ears. You will hear your own breathing. You will hear your footsteps. You will hear the blood moving in your body, and you will realize you could not hear it before, and you will understand, with the part of your mind that the Plateau reorganized, that silence is not the absence of sound. Silence is a *feature of the world* that most places have, and the Plateau does not, and you have been living inside that feature your whole life without knowing it was there.

You will need to reacclimatize on the way down. Do not rush the descent. Your body has adjusted to altitude and needs to readjust to thicker air. Rest. Hydrate. Process.

And when you are home — wherever home is, whenever you get there — you will find yourself listening for the Plateau. Not the sound of it, which you will never forget and which will surface in dreams and in quiet moments for the rest of your life. But the *quality* of it. The persistence. The indifference. The way it has always been there and always will be, singing its chord into the bright thin air, completely unaware that you were ever there, completely unaltered by your visit, magnificent and terrible and utterly, utterly free of any need for you at all.

That is what you are preparing for. Not the altitude or the cold or the wind or the noise.

The preparation for the Resonance Plateau is, in the end, the preparation to be insignificant in a place that makes insignificance feel like a revelation.
