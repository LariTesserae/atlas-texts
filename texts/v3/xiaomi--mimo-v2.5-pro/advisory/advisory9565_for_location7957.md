# Preparation Guide for the Forge-Continental

---

## Before Anything Else: Accept What You Are Walking Into

You are going underwater. Not into a pool, not into a flooded building, not into a shipwreck with an air pocket. You are going into a body of water that has no surface, no land, no sky, and no end in any direction except where structure stops it. The water is 3.2 degrees Celsius. It is dark beyond the reach of industrial floodlights. It is loud in a way your body is not built to process. And there is a lot of it — continental amounts of it, all around you, above you, below you, pressing against you from every direction with the quiet, impersonal patience of something that does not know you exist.

Prepare yourself mentally for this before you prepare anything else. The single most common failure mode for travelers entering high-pressure or submerged environments is not equipment malfunction — it is psychological. The panic response to being enclosed in cold water with no visible exit, no sky, and no ground you recognize as ground. You need to sit with the idea that you will be breathing manufactured air from a machine on your back, swimming or being carried through corridors where the far wall is kilometers away and the ceiling is a suggestion, and that the water touching your face is the same water touching everything, everywhere, forever. If you cannot make peace with that in abstract, do not go.

## Life Support: The Non-Negotiable Core

### Breathing

You need a closed-circuit rebreather rated for indefinite submersion at depth. Open-circuit scuba will not work here — the structure is too large, the logistics of cylinder resupply too complex, and the bubble noise will mark you as foreign and attract attention you may not want. A closed-circuit rebreather recycles your exhaled gas, scrubs carbon dioxide, and adds oxygen as needed. It is what the inhabitants use. It is what you should use.

Get trained on rebreather systems before you arrive. Not a weekend certification — real training, with failure drills, with practice hours in cold water, with time spent breathing from a loop while doing physical work. The rebreather becomes your lung. If it fails and you don't know how to fix it, you drown in a place where no one may hear you and no one may find you for days.

Carry a bailout system. A small open-circuit stage bottle, enough to get you to the nearest pressurized maintenance station if your rebreather dies completely. Know where the maintenance stations are. The inhabitants know. Ask them before you wander.

The water itself is breathable with the right apparatus. That is the good news. The structure is not sealed against you. It was not built for your species, but it tolerates you the way it tolerates the fish — as something that got in and now lives here.

### Thermal Protection

3.2°C water will kill you in under thirty minutes without protection. With a standard wetsuit, you might last an hour before your hands stop working and your core temperature drops below functional. You need a drysuit — a full, sealed, thermally insulated suit that keeps the water off your skin entirely — combined with active heating.

The inhabitants wear thick thermal suits. Some of them are powered — small heating elements woven into the inner lining, drawing from a battery pack at the hip. These are not luxury items. They are survival equipment. Get one, or get a drysuit rated for the temperature and layer aggressively underneath. Merino base layers, insulating mid-layers, and then the drysuit shell. Your extremities are the weak points. Your fingers will lose dexterity first. Bring heated gloves or accept that you will not be able to operate small tools, manipulate fasteners, or write with a pen.

Your face will be exposed if you use a standard diving mask. There are full-face masks with integrated rebreather interfaces that solve this, and the inhabitants use them. Get one fitted before you arrive. The cold water on even a small patch of bare skin is numbing and disorienting, and it creates a constant low-grade stress that wears on you over hours in ways you don't notice until you suddenly notice.

### Pressure

The structure is at depth. The outer hull endures roughly four hundred atmospheres. The interior is managed — the inhabitants maintain pressure differentials between sectors for hydraulic cycling — but you will be breathing gas at elevated pressure, and you need to understand what that means for your body.

Nitrogen narcosis is a risk at depth. The inhabitants have gas mixtures that mitigate this — trimix, heliox, or proprietary blends depending on the sector and the depth of the work being done. Get your rebreather loaded with an appropriate mix for whatever depth you'll be operating at. Don't assume the mix that works at one depth works at another. The Forge has vertical variation. A two-hundred-meter drop inside Chamber 14-East means the pressure at the bottom is meaningfully different from the pressure at the top. If you're going to traverse vertically — and you will — plan your gas accordingly.

Decompression is a fact of life here. The inhabitants rotate in and out on schedules dictated by it. They spend defined periods at depth and then move to pressurized transition chambers to offgas before ascending further. If you are staying for more than a day, you need to integrate into a decompression schedule. Going from deep interior to shallow interior to surface-equivalent in too short a time will give you decompression sickness — the bends — and in a place this far from conventional medical infrastructure, that can be fatal. Talk to the inhabitants. They have protocols. Follow them exactly.

## Navigation and Orientation

### You Will Get Lost

The Forge-Continental is the size of a tectonic plate. It has multiple numbering systems, none of them comprehensive. Corridors look the same. Chambers look the same. The scale defeats your spatial memory — your brain simply cannot hold a map of a place this large with this few landmarks. This is not a personal failing. The inhabitants get lost too, less often, because they have lived here long enough to develop intuition for the structure's logic. You do not have that intuition. You will not develop it in a visit.

Bring an acoustic positioning system. The structure is full of transponder beacons — the inhabitants use them for navigation and communication. A handheld receiver can triangulate your position from three or more beacons and give you a rough location. Rough is the operative word. Beacon coverage is dense near the core and sparse in the periphery. In the deep old chambers near the original construction core, coverage may be nonexistent. Do not go to those areas without a guide.

### Mapping

Get the most current maps you can from whatever inhabitants you can contact. These maps will be partial, contradictory, and outdated in places. That is normal. The structure changes — it is growing, being reconfigured, undergoing repair. A corridor that existed last month may be sealed for construction this month. A chamber that was open may now be partitioned. Accept the maps as suggestions, not ground truth.

Carry a physical compass. It will not work perfectly — the steel structure creates local magnetic anomalies — but it will work well enough to give you a general sense of direction if your electronic systems fail. And your electronic systems will, eventually, if you stay long enough. The electromagnetic environment inside the Forge is noisy: generators, motors, welding rigs, all of it broadcasting interference. Low-power electronics glitch. GPS does not function here — there is no sky for the signal to come from. Everything you use for navigation must be self-contained.

### Wayfinding Habits

Mark your path. The inhabitants do — small tags of adhesive tape in color-coded patterns, scratches on paint, chalk marks that slowly dissolve in the current. You should do the same. Bring supplies for marking: waterproof markers, adhesive tape in bright colors, a scoring tool for marking metal. Note the direction you came from at every junction. Write it down if you have to. The structure is disorienting in a way that feels almost deliberate — not because it is designed to confuse, but because it is designed at a scale where your sense of direction is simply irrelevant.

## Communication

### With Inhabitants

The inhabitants communicate by short-range acoustic transponder. This is a system that encodes voice or data into modulated sound pulses and transmits them through the water. Effective range is a few hundred meters in open water, less in corridors with complex geometry. You need one. It is the only reliable way to talk to anyone here.

The alternative — shouting through the water — works poorly. Sound travels far and fast in water, but it also distorts, echoes, and bleeds into the ambient industrial noise. You can yell at someone ten meters away and they may hear you. At fifty meters, they will hear noise. At a hundred, nothing but the hum of the generators.

Bring a transponder. Learn to use it before you arrive. The inhabitants' communication culture is blunt and functional — short messages, coordinate references, status updates. They do not waste bandwidth. When you transmit, be concise. Identify yourself. State your position. State your need. Listen. The inhabitants are not hostile, but they are busy, and they have been doing this longer than you have, and they will not enjoy holding your hand through a conversation that should take thirty seconds.

Some of them will enjoy it, though. The population is sparse and the work is routine and contact with outsiders is rare. You will find people who want to talk, who want to show you things, who are curious about where you came from. Accept their help when it is offered. You need it more than your pride may want to admit.

### With Your Travel Companions

If you are traveling with others, establish a communication protocol before you enter. Acoustic transponders work, but in a structure with this much ambient noise, messages can be missed, garbled, or delayed. Set check-in times. Set a rendezvous point if you become separated. Agree on a distress signal. Know that if someone goes silent, it could mean anything from a dead battery to a malfunctioning transponder to a genuine emergency, and you need a plan for each possibility.

## Equipment Beyond Life Support

### Light

The structure has lighting. Much of it works. Some of it doesn't. In the periphery, in older sections, in the deep construction core, you may find yourself in darkness absolute enough that it has texture. Bring your own primary light — a powerful, rechargeable dive light with a burn time measured in hours, not minutes. Bring a backup. Bring a backup for your backup. Darkness inside the Forge is not like darkness on land. On land, there is always some ambient light, some hint of horizon, some difference between closed eyes and open. Inside the Forge, the water swallows light completely beyond a certain range, and the structure has no horizon, and if your light dies you are in a blackness that is total and immediate and close.

Signal lights are also useful — small, bright, attachable to your suit. They help others see you, which matters when cargo submersibles are running automated routes through corridors you happen to be crossing. Those submersibles are blunt, windowless, and heavy. They do not stop quickly. They may not detect you at all if you are not illuminated. Wear a light. Wear several.

### Tools

Bring a basic tool kit: a multi-tool, a pipe wrench, a pry bar, and an underwater-rated cutting tool. Not for self-defense — though the thought will cross your mind — but because this is a structure made of bolted and welded metal, and you may need to open a hatch, remove a panel, pry apart a sealed junction box, or cut through a corroded fastener to access a passage or a utility space. The inhabitants carry tools as a matter of course. They will expect you to be similarly self-sufficient, or at least not to ask them for things you should have brought.

### Cutting and prying tools also matter for your relationship with the eels.

The eels in Sector 9 — and elsewhere, increasingly — chew through cable sheaths. They also chew on other things that look like cable sheaths, which includes anything rubber, plastic, or flexible on your suit. A rebreather hose. A drysuit seal. A light cord. The eels are not aggressive in the way you might fear. They are curious, and they explore with their mouths, and their mouths contain teeth. If one latches onto your equipment, you need to be able to dislodge it without panicking and without damaging critical gear. A pry bar or a flat blade works. Practice the motion before you need it.

### Tow and Propulsion

The structure is too large to traverse entirely by swimming. You will need mechanical propulsion or you will exhaust yourself and your thermal reserves long before you reach where you want to go. The inhabitants use small personal propulsion units — handheld or back-mounted, battery-powered, with enough thrust to move a suited diver through the water at a comfortable pace. These are not optional. They are how you move.

Alternatively, you can hitch rides on cargo submersibles. This is common practice. The automated routes pass through the major corridors on regular schedules. You attach yourself to a handhold on the exterior and ride. It is not comfortable — the submersibles are loud, their exhaust is unpleasant, and the vibration will numb your hands — but it works and it gets you between major sectors in hours instead of days.

Bring extra batteries. For everything. The cold saps battery life. The inhabitants know this and carry spares. You should carry spares for your spares.

### Recording Equipment

You will want to document this place. Bring a camera rated for the depth and temperature — housings exist, and the inhabitants use them for inspection work. A standard waterproof camera will function, but the image quality underwater in industrial lighting is challenging. Bring lights for your camera. Accept that many of your photographs will look the same — a corridor, a chamber, a void-space lit in industrial yellow, your own bubbles obscuring the frame. The Forge does not photograph well. It is too large, too uniform, too indifferent to the frame. Some things are better experienced than captured.

An audio recorder is more useful than you might expect. The soundscape is the most distinctive feature of the Forge — the layered industrial hum, the hydraulic rush of current, the clatter of metal, the distant thunder of pressurization. Recording it gives you something to study later, something to show others, something that carries more of the experience than a photograph can.

## Social and Cultural Preparation

### The Inhabitants Are Not Your Guides

They are workers. They have jobs — maintaining generators, fabricating plating, tending algae farms, running inspection routes, managing pressure differentials. They do this work on rotation schedules that take them into and out of the structure on decompression cycles measured in days or weeks. When they are inside, they are working. When they are resting, they are in habitation modules — small, pressurized, dry spaces tucked into the structure's margins — and they are tired and they want to be left alone.

Some of them will be curious about you. Some of them will offer to show you things. Accept gracefully and reciprocate with whatever you can offer — information from outside, novelty, the simple human pleasure of a conversation with someone new. But do not assume that anyone owes you their time. Do not expect a tour. The Forge is their workplace and their home, and they navigate it with the quiet competence of people who have been doing this for years, and they will find it strange that you want to see it, the way you would find it strange if someone wanted a guided tour of your city's sewer system. Interesting, maybe. Flattering, possibly. But also a little baffling.

### Communication Style

Be direct. Be brief. The acoustic transponder channels are shared, and bandwidth is limited, and the inhabitants have developed a communication style that reflects this. Short sentences. Coordinate references. Status updates. They are not rude — they are efficient, and efficiency is a survival trait in a place where a missed message can mean a flooded compartment or a delayed repair. When you talk to them, match their cadence. Ask specific questions. Give specific answers. Save the storytelling for the habitation modules, where you can sit face to face and talk in the dry, and even then be prepared for an audience that is practical and skeptical and more interested in what you know than in what you felt.

### The Culture of Normalcy

The inhabitants do not think the Forge is strange. This is important to understand. They will not react to your wonder the way you expect. If you say, "This chamber is enormous," they will agree, the way you would agree if someone told you the sky is large. If you say, "It's incredible that people live here," they will look at you the way you would look at someone who told you it's incredible that people live in cities. The Forge is where they live. They were born here, or they came here for work and stayed, or they arrived from somewhere else and adapted. The cold water, the industrial hum, the fish, the darkness — these are not remarkable to them. They are the texture of a Tuesday.

This does not mean they are unfeeling. It means they have normalized their environment in the way that all humans normalize their environments. They notice beauty when it appears — the mackerel turning in the light, the particular way a hydraulic current bends around a structural column, the brief warm zone near a thermal outflow — and they notice danger, and they notice boredom, and they notice the small frustrations of maintenance work in cold water. They have a rich interior life that happens to take place inside a continental submarine factory. Respect that. Don't romanticize it. Don't pity it. Don't treat them as curiosities. They are people who live in water, and they have opinions about the water the way you have opinions about weather.

## Psychological Preparation

### The Scale Problem

Your brain is built to process environments at human scale — rooms, streets, fields, hills. The Forge operates at a scale that exceeds your perceptual apparatus. Corridors that go on for kilometers. Chambers so large that the far wall is not visible. Vertical drops of hundreds of meters into water that looks the same as the water you are standing in. Your brain will try to process this and fail, and the failure will manifest as disorientation, vertigo, anxiety, or a flat affect that you may not notice until you are already compromised.

Prepare for this. Practice mindfulness techniques that anchor you to your immediate surroundings — your hands, your equipment, the nearest wall, the sound of your own breathing in the rebreather loop. When the scale overwhelms you — and it will — stop, breathe, touch something solid, and focus on a small detail. A bolt head. A pipe junction. The pattern of biofilm on a wall panel. The Forge is too large to comprehend, but the part of it you are standing in is not, and that is enough.

### The Sound Problem

The industrial hum is constant. It never stops. It enters your body through the water — through your suit, through your skull, through the bones of your jaw — and it vibrates at frequencies that your ears cannot consciously register but your nervous system can. After hours of exposure, you may experience headaches, jaw tension, irritability, difficulty concentrating, or a persistent feeling of unease that has no identifiable source.

This is normal. It is the structure. It is the generators and the pumps and the turbines and the hydraulic currents and the metal walls resonating. Bring earplugs — good ones, rated for industrial noise, designed for use in water if possible. Your acoustic transponder will still work with them in, or use a bone-conduction transducer that bypasses the ear canal entirely. The inhabitants wear hearing protection. Do the same. The cost of not doing it is permanent hearing damage, and the cost of permanent hearing damage in a place where communication happens through modulated sound in water is, effectively, exile.

### The Emptiness Problem

Most of the Forge is empty. Not abandoned — empty by design. You will walk corridors for kilometers and see no one. You will enter chambers the size of stadiums and find nothing but water and light and the distant hum of machinery. You will look up and see a ceiling that is really the underside of another chamber's floor, and it will be so far above you that it reads as a sky, except it is not a sky, it is more structure, and beyond it is more water, and beyond that is more structure, and so on.

Some people find this peaceful. Most find it oppressive, eventually. The emptiness is not silence — it is full of sound and movement and the slow drift of particulate in the current — but it is empty of other people, and empty of the small social signals that your brain uses to feel safe. No voices. No footsteps. No faces. Just the structure and the water and the fish.

If you are prone to agoraphobia, this will be difficult. If you are prone to claustrophobia, this will also be difficult, because despite the vast open spaces, you are enclosed — always enclosed, always inside, always in water, with no sky and no ground and no exit that leads to anything except more of the same. The structure has no outside that you can reach. The outer hull is hundreds of kilometers away through water that would crush you. You are inside, and you will stay inside, and the inside goes on forever.

Bring something to ground yourself. A personal item. A recording of birdsong. A photograph of a landscape with a horizon. Something that reminds you that there are other kinds of space and that you will return to them. The inhabitants have personal items in their habitation modules — small things, sentimental things, things that have nothing to do with the Forge. They understand the need. They will not judge you for it.

## Health and Medical Considerations

### Cold Exposure

Even with a drysuit and active heating, prolonged exposure to 3.2°C water carries risks. Your body will be working constantly to maintain core temperature, and that work has a cost — caloric, metabolic, cognitive. You will burn more calories than you expect. You will tire faster than you expect. Your decision-making will degrade before you notice it. Set strict time limits for excursions outside heated habitation areas, and honor them even when you feel fine. Hypothermia onset is insidious. By the time you feel cold — truly cold, shivering cold — you are already in trouble.

Eat more than you think you need. High-calorie, high-fat foods. The inhabitants have access to the algal protein blocks produced by the algae farms, and they supplement with imported rations. The protein blocks are nutritionally complete and taste like nothing you have ever eaten — not bad, not good, just nothing, a dense green-gray mass that fills your stomach and keeps your furnace running. Bring your own food as well, for the psychological benefit of familiar flavors if nothing else.

### Ear and Sinus Pressure

You are at depth. Your ears will equalize, or they will hurt, or they will rupture. Learn to equalize before you arrive — the Valsalva maneuver, the Frenzel maneuver, whatever works for your anatomy. Practice in a pool, at depth if you can. If you have chronic sinus problems, address them before you travel. A sinus squeeze at depth is agonizing and can incapacitate you.

### Wound Care

The water is not sterile. The biofilm on every surface — that thin rust-colored bacterial film — is not sterile. The industrial particulate in the water is not sterile. Any break in your suit's integrity that lets water reach your skin creates an infection risk. Carry a first-aid kit with wound-closure supplies, antiseptic, and antibiotics if you can get them. The inhabitants have medical facilities in the habitation modules — small, functional, well-stocked — but they are not hospitals, and they are not always nearby.

The eels make this worse. A bite from an eel — even a small one, even a curious exploratory nip — is a wound that is immediately contaminated with whatever is in that eel's mouth and whatever is in the surrounding water. Treat it immediately. Do not wait. Do not tough it out. Clean the wound, close the wound, dress the wound, and monitor it. The inhabitants have stories about infections that started small and became serious. They are not exaggerating.

## Specific Hazards

### Hydraulic Surges

Pressure management failures in a sector can cause water to rush from high-pressure zones to low, creating surges that move through corridors like flash floods. These are dangerous. They can pin you against walls, drag you into pipes, slam you into structural elements, or simply carry you kilometers from where you intended to be. The warning signs are a change in current direction, a drop or rise in ambient sound, and — if you are near a junction — the sight of water moving with purpose where it was previously still.

If you feel a surge beginning, get to the nearest wall and hold on. Grab a structural element — a beam, a pipe, a handhold — and lock your grip. Do not try to swim against the current. Wait for it to pass. The pumping systems will equalize the pressure eventually, and the surge will subside. This may take minutes or hours, depending on the scale of the failure.

### Cargo Submersibles

They are automated. They follow routes. They do not deviate for obstacles that their sensors do not classify as structural. If you are in a corridor and a submersible is approaching, move to the wall. The corridors are wide enough that this is usually not a problem, but in narrower passages, in utility tunnels, in the tighter spaces between chambers, the margin is slim. The submersibles are heavy and their stopping distance is long. Wear lights. Be visible. Stay out of the marked transit lanes — they are painted on the floor in faded but legible stripes.

### Electrical Systems

The grid is maintained but imperfect. Brownouts in the periphery mean that sections of the grid can go dead without warning — and come back on without warning. Exposed wiring exists. Damaged conduits exist. Water and electricity coexist here because the system is designed for it, but "designed for it" does not mean "safe for you to touch." Treat every wire, conduit, and junction box as potentially live. Do not open electrical panels unless you know what you are doing and have confirmed the circuit is de-energized. The inhabitants use insulated tools and rubber-soled boots. You should too.

### The Deep Old Chambers

The ones near the original construction core. The ones that are mostly decommissioned and poorly lit. The ones where the acoustic profiles show signatures that don't match any catalogued species.

The description says this is not a weird place. The description says the stories are just stories. Large fish in dark water. That is all.

Carry this knowledge with you: in the deep old chambers, something lives that the inhabitants have not identified and do not fully understand. It may be a large fish. It may be several large fish. It may be something else. The inhabitants do not go to these chambers. The lighting is failing. The transponder coverage is thin. The corridors are older, narrower, built to specifications that have since been revised and expanded, and they contain machinery that has been offline for years or decades and may not respond predictably if power is restored.

If you go to the deep old chambers, go with a local who knows them. If no local will go with you, consider carefully why you want to go at all.

## What to Bring: A Summary List

**Essential:**
- Closed-circuit rebreather with appropriate gas mix for target depth
- Bailout open-circuit stage bottle
- Full-face mask with integrated rebreather interface
- Drysuit rated for prolonged immersion at 3.2°C
- Active heating system (powered thermal suit or heated underlayers)
- Heated gloves
- Personal propulsion unit with spare batteries
- Primary dive light, two backup lights, signal lights
- Acoustic transponder (and bone-conduction transducer if possible)
- Hearing protection rated for industrial noise
- Acoustic positioning receiver with current beacon maps
- Multi-tool, pipe wrench, pry bar, underwater-rated cutting tool
- Waterproof markers, adhesive tape, scoring tool for wayfinding marking
- Physical compass

**Strongly Recommended:**
- Camera with underwater housing and supplemental lights
- Audio recorder
- First-aid kit with wound closure, antiseptic, antibiotics
- High-calorie food rations
- Spare batteries for all powered equipment (at least double what you think you need)
- Personal grounding items (familiar objects, photographs, recordings of surface-world sounds)
- Small dry-bag for keeping personal items functional in the wet

**Nice to Have:**
- Dive computer with decompression tracking
- Backup acoustic transponder
- Waterproof journal and pencil (pens do not work reliably at depth — the pressure differential affects ink flow)
- Compact filtration mask for breathing ambient air in pressurized but poorly ventilated maintenance stations (the air can carry fine industrial particulate)

## Final Advice

Go in with humility. The Forge is not your world. It was not built for you, it does not need you, and it will not accommodate you beyond what you bring with you. The inhabitants have made it their home through years of adaptation, practice, and the accumulated knowledge of a community that has learned to live in water the way other communities have learned to live in deserts or tundra or mountains. They know things you do not. They know which corridors flood during surges. They know which chambers have warm currents and which ones have eels. They know the sound the generators make when something is wrong. They know when to stay and when to leave and when to hold very, very still.

Listen to them. Learn from them. And when they say *watch out for the eels in Sector 9*, watch out for the eels in Sector 9.
