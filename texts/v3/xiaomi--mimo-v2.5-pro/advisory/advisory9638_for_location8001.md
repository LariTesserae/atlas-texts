# Preparation Guide for Visiting The Delta Works

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before you pack a single item, sit with this: you are going to a place where the land and the water are the same thing, where the economy runs on animals, and where the air is so wet it has weight. This is not a ruin. This is not a curiosity. This is a functioning city of people who have made a life in conditions that would break most visitors in a week. You are a guest in a place that does not need you, and the environment will not accommodate you. Adjust your mindset accordingly. Humility is not optional here — it's survival.

---

## Climate and Environment: What Your Body Will Experience

### The Heat

The description says "hot" but that word fails to carry the particular cruelty of saturated heat. You need to understand the physics: when the air is already holding as much moisture as it can, your sweat does not evaporate. Evaporation is the primary mechanism by which the human body sheds heat. Without it, your core temperature rises no matter how much you sweat, and you will sweat profusely because your body doesn't know what else to do. You will feel like you are wrapped in a warm, wet towel that someone keeps re-wetting. This does not go away at night. The standing water, the mud, the metal surfaces — they are all warm, and they all radiate that warmth back at you, and the air does not move to carry any of it away.

**What to bring:**

- **Electrolyte packets — lots of them.** Not the flavored sports drink kind. Get the unflavored medical oral rehydration salts. You will sweat out sodium, potassium, and magnesium at a rate you've never experienced. Plain water will not be enough. If you drink only water, you will develop hyponatremia — dangerously low blood sodium — and the symptoms (confusion, nausea, headache) will be nearly indistinguishable from simple heat exhaustion, which means you might not realize what's happening until it's serious. Dissolve a packet in every liter you drink. Carry at least six packets per day of intended stay.

- **A cooling towel.** The kind you soak in water and drape around your neck. This sounds absurd in a place where everything is already wet, but the point is to direct evaporative cooling to the areas where your major blood vessels run close to the skin — neck, wrists, temples. You'll need to re-wet it constantly, which won't be a problem.

- **Light, loose, full-coverage clothing.** Exposing skin does not help in saturated heat — it just gives the humidity more surface area to cling to. Long sleeves, long pants, in a moisture-wicking synthetic or a very light linen. Cotton is a mistake: it will absorb water and hang on you like a second skin, heavy and chafing. You want fabrics that move moisture along their fibers rather than absorbing it. Light colors — not for reflectivity (the sun is a white disk behind haze; there's no direct solar radiation to reflect) but because they show less staining from the mineral-rich water and mud.

- **A wide-brimmed hat.** Not for sun protection specifically, but to keep the perpetual condensation and drip off your face and neck. In a place where water forms on every horizontal surface, you'll be constantly rained on from above — from eaves, from pipe joints, from the undersides of structures you walk beneath. A hat gives you a dry zone for your face.

### The Water

This is perhaps the single most important thing to internalize: **the water in this place is not safe for human contact in prolonged or repeated exposure, and it is absolutely not safe to drink.** The description specifies iron-heavy, sulfurous, alkaline, and mineral-laden. This means the water will irritate and eventually damage skin with repeated contact. The sulfur compounds will be harsh on mucous membranes — eyes, nose, mouth, any open cuts. The alkalinity will disrupt your skin's acid mantle, the protective pH barrier that keeps your skin from cracking and your microbiome from being invaded by pathogens.

**What to bring:**

- **Waterproof boots — serious ones.** Not hiking boots with "water-resistant" uppers. You need vulcanized rubber boots that reach at least mid-calf, ideally knee-high. The description says ankle-deep mud in some places, knee-deep in others. You will be walking on iron grating, through puddles, across mud flats, and through standing water that may be warm, mineral-rich, and home to things that bite. Your boots should be the kind you'd wear for industrial chemical work — chemical-resistant rubber with a steel toe. Break them in before you arrive, because you'll be living in them.

- **Nitrile gloves — a box of them.** For any situation where you need to handle anything that's been in contact with local water or mud, including your own wet clothing, equipment, or food containers. Keep a few pairs in every bag you carry.

- **Barrier cream.** Before you go out each day, apply a dimethicone-based barrier cream to your hands, feet, and any skin that might be exposed. This creates a thin hydrophobic layer between your skin and the environment. Reapply every few hours, especially after washing.

- **A sealed, personal water supply.** Bring your own water from outside the multiverse-location. Carry it in a rigid, sealed container — not a soft bladder that can puncture. You need enough for drinking, cooking, and basic hygiene (brushing teeth, rinsing eyes if they get splashed). Minimum three liters per day, more if you're moving around. Do not, under any circumstances, allow local water to enter your mouth. Do not brush your teeth with it. Do not rinse produce with it. Do not assume that boiling makes it safe — heavy metals and dissolved minerals do not boil off; they concentrate.

- **Saline eyewash.** Small, sealed vials. When mineral-laden mist or splash gets in your eyes — and it will — rinse immediately. Do not rub. The irritation from sulfurous water in the eyes can escalate to chemical conjunctivitis if the compounds aren't flushed out.

- **A microfiber towel or two.** They'll never be fully dry, but they're better than cotton for wiping down surfaces before you sit, handle equipment, or eat.

### The Air Quality

The description mentions thin brown smoke from processing plants, steam from hot springs, and perpetual evaporation. This cocktail means the air contains suspended particulates — industrial soot, mineral dust from evaporated water, sulfur compounds in gaseous form, and whatever comes off the animal processing. For a healthy adult with no respiratory conditions, short-term exposure may produce mild throat irritation and a persistent feeling of not being able to take a full, clean breath. For anyone with asthma, COPD, or sensitivity to sulfur compounds, this environment could be genuinely dangerous.

**What to bring:**

- **A half-face respirator with P100 particulate filters and an organic vapor/acid gas cartridge.** Not a dust mask. Not a cloth face covering. A proper elastomeric respirator that seals against your face. You may not need it at all times, but you should have it accessible and be prepared to put it on when you're downwind of the processing plants, near the hot springs, or when the mist thickens. Practice wearing it before you arrive — the sensation of breathing through a respirator can cause anxiety in people who aren't accustomed to it, and you don't want your first experience with it to be in a panic.

- **If you have any respiratory condition, reconsider this trip.** Seriously. There is no clean air anywhere in this city. The humidity alone will fill your lungs with warm moisture that doesn't clear easily. Add the industrial emissions and the mineral particulates, and you have an environment that is actively hostile to human respiratory tissue over extended exposure.

## Fauna: Living With the Animals

This is where most visitors are caught off guard, and it's the area where preparation matters most for your psychological wellbeing and your physical safety.

### Crabs

They are in the walls. They are on the floors. They are in the drainage systems. At certain hours, they carpet the open ground. Thousands of species, ranging from thumb-sized to dinner-plate-sized. The big ones can pinch hard enough to break skin, and their claws are strong enough to damage soft equipment — bags, clothing, the seals on your water containers.

**What to know:**

- They are not aggressive in the way you might fear. They are territorial and defensive. If you step near one, it will raise its claws. If you step on one, it will grab whatever it can. Walk deliberately, watch the ground, and stomp gently as you go — the vibration will warn them to move before you get there. At night, when they move across factory floors, carry a light and sweep it ahead of you.

- They will try to get into your living space. This is not a malfunction of the building — it is the normal state of affairs. Residents deal with it routinely. If your accommodation has gaps in the walls, door frames, or floor edges — and it likely will, because the city is not hermetically sealed — crabs will find their way in. Bring a stiff brush to sweep them out. Do not try to seal every gap; you will fail, and you'll damage someone's home.

- Crab oil and chitin dust are part of the industrial output. If you visit the processing areas, you'll encounter these in aerosolized form. This is part of why the respirator matters.

### Eels and Eel-like Fish

They're in every channel, drainage ditch, culvert, and standing pool beneath buildings. They are likely not dangerous to an adult human in the way a shark or an electric eel would be, but the description gives us "eel-like" which suggests elongated, slippery, and probably bitey. They live in warm, dark water — exactly the kind of water you'll be wading through.

**What to know:**

- Do not put your hands into standing water you can't see into. This seems obvious until you're reaching for something that fell in a puddle and a hand-length eel clamps onto your finger.

- The boots are critical here. Knee-high rubber boots protect the most vulnerable areas — ankles and calves — where an eel in murky water might strike.

### Insects

"In staggering variety." In a hot, wet, plant-free environment with enormous animal biomass, the insects will be omnipresent. Expect heavy-bodied flying things that drone through the mist (the description says this explicitly), crawling things in the mud, and burrowing things in the foundations. In a place with no birds and no bats (or if there are, they aren't mentioned), the insect population may have no significant predators beyond the amphibians.

**What to bring:**

- **Permethrin-treated clothing.** Treat your outerwear, boots, hat, and bag with permethrin before you arrive. This is an insecticide that bonds to fabric and repels/kills insects on contact. It's standard preparation for tropical environments and will serve you well here.

- **A head net.** Lightweight, packable, goes over your hat. You will look absurd. You will not care when the alternative is breathing in heavy-bodied flies.

- **Insect repellent with DEET or picaridin.** Apply to any exposed skin — face, hands — before going out. Reapply frequently, because the humidity will dilute and disperse it faster than in dry climates.

- **Expect them in your food.** Keep food sealed. Eat inside when possible. Any exposed meal will attract insects within minutes.

### Amphibians

Sleek, pale, enormous-eyed. In every shadow, pipe junction, and hollow. These are probably the least threatening fauna you'll encounter — they're described as huddling, seeking cool spots, and they're likely more afraid of you than you are of them. But they are part of the sensory landscape: you will step around them constantly, you will find them in unexpected places, and they may be vocal at certain hours (though the description doesn't mention this).

**What to know:**

- They are part of the economy — the description mentions "glandular secretions of the larger amphibians" with pharmaceutical use. Don't touch them without permission from locals. They may be someone's livelihood or a regulated resource.

- Their skin secretions could be irritants. Many amphibians in extreme environments produce defensive compounds. Don't rub your eyes after handling one.

### Snails

Fist-sized. On the walls. Leaving silver trails. They climb vertical surfaces and reach rooftops, which means their grip strength is considerable and their shell mass is significant. A fist-sized snail shell falling from a rooftop onto your head is a genuine minor hazard.

**What to know:**

- Look up periodically. In a city where snails colonize roofs, the space directly above you is not always safe.

- The trails will be on every surface. They're not dangerous, but they are slick. Watch your footing on stairs and ramps where trails cross your path.

### Colony Organisms

"Things like segmented worms, like mats of interlocking bodies." No one has fully classified them. Everyone knows to step around them. They're in the warm pools.

**What to know:**

- Step around them. That's the local wisdom, and it's sufficient. Don't touch them, don't prod them, don't try to take a sample unless you have a genuine scientific purpose and proper containment equipment. You don't know what they are. Nobody does.

## Health and Medical Preparation

### Heat-Related Illness

You are going to be operating in conditions that significantly increase your risk of heat exhaustion and heat stroke. The saturated air means your cooling mechanisms are impaired. The constant low-grade exertion of moving through mud, climbing over industrial structures, and navigating uneven terrain will generate metabolic heat that your body cannot shed.

**Signs to watch for in yourself:**

- Heavy sweating that suddenly stops (this is a medical emergency — it means your body has run out of fluid and can no longer cool itself)
- Confusion, irritability, difficulty forming sentences
- Nausea, vomiting
- Headache that doesn't respond to hydration
- Rapid heartbeat at rest

**What to do:**

- Take breaks inside, in whatever shade or ventilation exists. Even a few minutes of reduced exposure helps.
- Know the location of the nearest industrial cooling area — factories that process animals will have cold-storage rooms, and in a city this hot, these may be the only genuinely cool spaces available.
- Carry a personal battery-powered fan. It won't cool you much in saturated air, but the air movement against your skin provides some convective cooling and, more importantly, a psychological sense of relief that can keep you functioning.

### Skin Conditions

Prolonged contact with mineral-rich, alkaline water will cause:

- **Contact dermatitis** — red, itchy, cracking skin, especially on feet, hands, and anywhere your clothing stays wet against your body
- **Fungal infections** — the warm, wet environment is ideal for dermatophytes. Feet, groin, armpits, anywhere skin folds and stays moist.
- **Wound infections** — any cut, scrape, or blister that is exposed to the local water will almost certainly become infected. The water is non-sterile, mineral-rich, and teeming with microorganisms.

**What to bring:**

- **Antifungal powder or cream** (clotrimazole, miconazole). Apply preventatively to feet and skin folds daily.
- **Broad-spectrum antibiotic ointment** (mupirocin or bacitracin). Apply to any break in the skin immediately, and cover with a waterproof bandage.
- **Waterproof bandages and medical tape.** Lots of them. Assume every scratch will need covering.
- **Hydrocortisone cream** for dermatitis flare-ups.
- **Foot powder.** Your feet will be wet for the entire visit. Powder them at every opportunity. Change socks twice a day minimum, and make sure your socks are synthetic, moisture-wicking, and not cotton.

### Gastrointestinal

If you accidentally ingest local water — and in a place where condensation drips from every surface and mist hangs in the air, accidental ingestion is more likely than you'd think — you may experience gastrointestinal distress. The mineral content alone can act as a laxative. The bacterial content is unknown.

**What to bring:**

- **Loperamide** (Imodium) for diarrhea management
- **Bismuth subsalicylate** (Pepto-Bismol) for general GI upset
- **Oral rehydration salts** (already mentioned, but worth repeating — GI illness plus profuse sweating is a fast track to dangerous dehydration)

## Psychological Preparation

This is the part that most preparation guides skip, and it may be the most important section here.

### Sensory Overload and Sensory Deprivation Simultaneously

The environment is overwhelming in some ways — the heat, the damp, the omnipresent animal life, the drip-drip-drip of water on every surface — and profoundly understimulating in others. The light is flat and gray. There are no sharp shadows, no bright colors, no vegetation to break the visual monotony. The buildings are industrial and utilitarian. The open ground between structures is mud and standing water in every direction. The sky is a uniform gray-white.

This combination can produce a specific kind of psychological distress: you feel trapped and claustrophobic because the heat and humidity compress your sense of space, while simultaneously feeling exposed and agoraphobic because the flat landscape stretches in every direction under a featureless sky. The constant damp makes everything feel close and intimate even when you're standing in an open field.

**Prepare for this.** Know that it's coming. Bring:

- **Something to anchor your sense of time.** In a place where the sky is always gray-white and the streetlamps are always on, you will lose track of day and night. Wear a watch. Set alarms. Maintain your normal sleep-wake cycle as strictly as possible. Disrupted circadian rhythm in an already stressful environment can accelerate psychological deterioration.

- **Something to anchor your sense of self.** A personal item — a photo, a book, a small object from home. Something that reminds you that you come from somewhere else and that this visit has an end point. This sounds trivial until you've been in the gray damp for four days and the hum of the engines has become the background frequency of your thoughts and the crabs in the walls have become normal and you realize you can't remember what dry air feels like. You need a tether.

- **Music or audio, with earbuds.** The ambient soundscape — drip, hum, hiss, the occasional clatter of crab or drone of insect — is not loud, but it is relentless and it never changes. After enough hours, it begins to feel like a pressure. Earbuds with your own audio give you a way to step out of it periodically.

### The Animal Ubiquity

For most people, the single hardest thing about this city will not be the heat or the damp or the industrial landscape. It will be the animals. They are *everywhere.* There is no space free of them. They nest in the walls, breed in the drainage, carpet the ground, climb the walls, crawl through the foundations. You will not eat a meal without insects investigating it. You will not walk across a room without stepping over or around something alive. You will hear them in the walls at night. You will find them in your belongings.

This is normal here. It is not normal for most human visitors. It may trigger disgust responses, anxiety, or even a phobic reaction in people who didn't expect the intensity of it.

**Prepare for this:**

- Spend time before your visit looking at images and videos of dense animal populations — wetland ecosystems, insect swarms, crab migrations, coral reef life. Desensitize yourself to the visual and auditory reality of being surrounded by animals that are not domesticated, not cute, and not interested in you.

- Practice the mantra: *they are not targeting me.* The crabs in the walls are not trying to get to you. The insects are not attracted to you specifically. The eels in the water are not hunting you. You are one more warm body in a landscape of warm bodies. They have their lives; you are passing through theirs.

- Have a plan for nighttime. When you're lying in the dark and you hear something moving in the wall beside your head, or you feel something brush your ankle under the blanket, or you hear the click-clack of crab legs on the floor, you need to be able to manage your startle response. A small nightlight can help — being able to see what's around you reduces the anxiety of imagining what's around you.

## Practical Logistics

### Accommodation

Housing blocks exist. Expect them to be utilitarian, damp, and minimally maintained. The concrete has cracked. Water seeps through the foundation. Condensation runs down interior walls. There may be a dehumidifier (ask for one if there isn't — they're an industrial city; they have dehumidifiers), but it will struggle against the ambient saturation.

Bring:

- **A sleeping bag liner.** Something you can sleep inside of that is yours, that you can wash, and that creates a barrier between you and whatever bedding is provided. Silk or synthetic. Treat it with permethrin before you arrive.
- **A travel sheet or light blanket.** The temperature will not drop much at night. You won't need insulation. You may want a barrier between yourself and the air while you sleep.
- **Earplugs.** For sleeping. The drip and hum are constant, and even if they're quiet, they're never absent. Your brain may fixate on them when you're trying to sleep.

### Food and Water

- Bring all your own water. I cannot stress this enough. It is the single most critical logistical item.
- For food, bring sealed, shelf-stable options for the duration of your stay, plus extra. Ration bars, freeze-dried meals, sealed nuts and dried fruit, vacuum-packed protein. The locals eat the animals they process, and if you are offered local food, use your judgment — the protein is probably safe once cooked, but the water used in cooking, the surfaces it was prepared on, and the possibility of mineral contamination make it a risk assessment you'll need to make in the moment.
- **A portable water purification system is not sufficient.** Standard filters remove particulates and biological contaminants but not dissolved minerals, heavy metals, or sulfur compounds. You would need a reverse osmosis system, which is not something you can easily travel with. Bring your own supply.

### Navigation and Movement

- **The city is sprawling and sparse.** Distances between structures are large. Moving between locations means crossing open ground — mud flats, standing water, puddles, iron grating. Plan your routes in advance. Ask locals which paths are passable and which are knee-deep mud.
- **Carry a walking stick or staff.** Useful for testing the depth of mud and water before you step in, for balancing on slippery surfaces, for gently moving animals out of your path, and for probing standing water to check for submerged obstacles or eels.
- **A headlamp.** Not a flashlight — a headlamp. You need both hands free at all times in this environment. The light is perpetually dim, and inside buildings it may be genuinely dark. A headlamp with a red-light mode is ideal for nighttime navigation without disturbing the animal life as much (the insects are attracted to white light; the crabs may be less reactive to red).

### Communication

- Learn at least basic phrases in whatever language the locals speak. The city has no universally agreed-upon name, which suggests it may be somewhat insular or that outsiders don't visit often. Learn the words for: water, danger, help, sick, hot, cold, food, direction words, and the names for the major animal species. Pointing and miming only go so far.
- If you have a translation device, bring it and keep it sealed in a waterproof pouch when not in use.

## What to Expect That You Can't Prepare For

There is a quality to this place that no checklist will address. The description says the land has found its level, that everything is settled, that nothing is being carved anymore. This is true in a way that seeps into you over hours and days. The city is *still.* Not peaceful — still. The mud doesn't change. The water doesn't clear. The sky doesn't open. The engines hum the same hum they've hummed for decades. The animals go about their business with the indifference of things that have always been here and always will.

You will begin, probably within the first day, to feel like the land is not aware of you. Not hostile — not that. Indifferent. You are a warm body among many warm bodies, and the mud does not care whether you are a person or a crab, and the water rises around your boots with the same slow patience it has always had.

This is not a comfortable feeling. It's not dangerous, exactly, but it erodes something — your sense of being a protagonist, of being the one things happen to. In this place, things simply happen, and you are present for them. The drip continues. The hum continues. The eels breed in the south canal. The snails reach the roof.

Come home when you're done. Have something waiting for you — a meal from your own kitchen, a shower with clean water, a room with dry walls. You will need to reacquaint yourself with the feeling of air that doesn't cling. Give yourself a day or two of readjustment. Your skin will take longer to recover. Your lungs may feel tight for a week. Your dreams may feature the sound of dripping water for longer than you'd like.

It's a real place, with real people living real lives. Respect that. Don't romanticize it. Don't pity it. Don't try to fix it. Just visit, observe, stay hydrated, keep your boots sealed, and watch where you step.
