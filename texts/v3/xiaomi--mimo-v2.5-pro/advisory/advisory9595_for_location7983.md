# Preparation Guide for The Resonant Plateau

## A Practical Field Advisory for the Ordinary Human Multiverse Traveler

---

## I. Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before you pack a single piece of gear, you need to understand something fundamental about the Resonant Plateau: this is not a place that was designed with human visitors in mind. It was not designed at all. It *evolved* — or rather, it *resonated* — into its current form over timescales so vast that calling them "geological" is almost reductive. The world's entire ecosystem runs on tectonic pressure and mineral cracking. There is no water cycle. There is no photosynthesis in any form you'd recognize. The air is thin, dry, metallic, and constantly moving. The ground beneath your feet is alive or was alive or is becoming alive again. The sound never, ever stops.

You are visiting a world that operates on principles fundamentally alien to every environment your species evolved to survive in. Every system your body relies on — hydration, respiration, thermoregulation, spatial orientation, even the baseline cognitive function you take for granted — will be under assault from the moment you arrive. Not from hostility. The world doesn't care about you enough to be hostile. It simply isn't built for you. It's built for itself.

Prepare accordingly.

## II. Atmospheric and Respiratory Preparation

### Air Composition and Altitude

The Resonant Plateau sits at significant elevation on a world with a thin atmosphere. The air is breathable in the barest biochemical sense — you will not suffocate the moment you arrive — but "breathable" and "adequate" are very different things. The oxygen partial pressure will be low, comparable to what a human would experience at extreme terrestrial altitudes. The air carries a persistent metallic-ite taste that visitors describe variously as iron filings, copper sulfate, or the smell of a battery being slowly crushed. This taste is not incidental. It reflects the actual chemical composition of the atmosphere, which is rich in mineral particulates and trace metallic compounds liberated by the piezoelectric metabolism of the planetary root-network.

**Required equipment:**

- **Supplemental oxygen system.** Do not attempt to acclimatize naturally. You are not climbing Everest over two weeks; you are stepping onto a world where the baseline oxygen availability will give you altitude sickness within hours. A portable oxygen concentrator with a rebreather loop is ideal. Carry backup compressed-oxygen canisters — at least 72 hours' worth beyond your planned visit duration. If your visit extends unexpectedly (and on the Plateau, time has a way of becoming unmoored), you do not want to be rationing breaths.

- **Particulate filtration mask.** Not a cloth mask. Not a surgical mask. A properly sealed half-face or full-face respirator with P100 particulate filters and an activated-carbon secondary stage for metallic vapor compounds. The mineral dust on the Plateau is extremely fine — calcium-white andite particulate that will infiltrate standard filter media within hours. Bring replacement filters. Bring more than you think you need. The air carries a constant low load of silicified cellulose microfibers shed by the plant-mineral structures, and chronic inhalation of these fibers is not something you want to discover the consequences of firsthand.

- **Pulse oximeter.** Wear it constantly. The insidious thing about the Plateau's thin air is that you can feel fine — especially given the ambient adrenaline of being in an alien world — while your blood oxygen saturation quietly drops to dangerous levels. The cognitive effects of mild hypoxia will blend seamlessly with the cognitive effects of the ambient sound (discussed below), and you will not be able to distinguish the two. Check your oximeter. Trust the number, not your feelings.

### The Dryness

This cannot be overstated: there is **no water on this world.** None. Not as ice, vapor, dew, fog, condensation, or chemical hydrate. The atmospheric humidity is effectively zero. The air has never carried water. Your respiratory mucosa, your eyes, your skin, and your lips will begin to desiccate the moment you step outside your shelter.

**Additional respiratory preparations:**

- **Humidified breathing attachment** for your respirator. A small inline humidifier cartridge that adds moisture to each breath. Replace the water reservoir frequently. This is not a luxury — this is the difference between functional airways and cracked, bleeding nasal passages within the first day.

- **Nasal saline spray.** Use it before and after every outing. The ite particulates in the air will cake inside your nasal passages and sinuses, and without moisture to help clear them, they will form mineral accretions that are exactly as unpleasant as they sound.

## III. Hydration and Nutrition

### Water

You must bring every drop of water you will consume. There is no source on the Plateau. There is no possibility of finding one. The inhabitants do not drink water — their biology has moved past it entirely — and they will not understand the urgency of your need if you run out. Plan for a minimum of **4 liters per day** under light activity, more if you intend to walk any significant distance across the open plateau. The extreme aridity will pull moisture from your body through respiration, through your skin, through your eyes, through membranes you didn't know you had.

**Critical planning notes:**

- Bring water in **sealed, opaque containers.** The mineral dust will infiltrate anything left open, and the constant sub-bass vibration has an unsettling tendency to work particulate into screw-threads and gasket seals. Double-seal everything. Consider vacuum-insulated containers not for temperature but for particulate integrity.

- Bring **oral rehydration salts or electrolyte supplements.** You will lose minerals through accelerated perspiration (the body sweats even in cool conditions when the air is this dry) and through the constant mucosal turnover that the hostile atmosphere demands. Plain water alone will not sustain you past the second or third day.

- **Water discipline** must be strict and pre-planned. Do not rely on thirst as a cue. By the time you feel thirsty on the Plateau, you are already significantly dehydrated. Set timers if you must. Drink on schedule, not on sensation.

### Food

Pack calorie-dense, low-moisture foods that are individually sealed. The Plateau's environment will desiccate anything exposed within hours — bread becomes croutons, fruit becomes leather, cooked meals form mineral-ite crusts on their surfaces. Consider:

- Vacuum-sealed protein bars and energy-dense trail foods
- Freeze-dried meals with a self-heating system (the ambient temperature is cool and you will not want to be cooking over open flame in a world where the atmosphere carries metallic vapor)
- High-fat foods: nuts sealed in foil, nut butters in squeeze tubes, chocolate in wax-coated bars
- Avoid anything crumbly or powdered — the ambient vibration and wind will scatter it, and the substrate will absorb it with alarming speed. The ground on the Plateau metabolizes organic matter quickly. Spilled food does not sit there waiting to be cleaned up. It *sinks.*

Your caloric needs will be higher than normal. The thin air means your body works harder on every breath, the constant sound puts your nervous system under baseline stress, and the unfamiliarity of the environment (discussed in the Psychological Preparation section) will burn cognitive calories at an elevated rate.

## IV. Hearing Protection — The Single Most Important Piece of Equipment

If you bring nothing else to the Resonant Plateau, bring hearing protection. If you can only afford one piece of specialized gear, make it this one.

The Plateau is **loud.** Not city-loud. Not concert-loud. Loud in a way that terrestrial experience cannot prepare you for. The sound is not a single noise — it is a **world-spanning chord** generated by kilometers of hollow, silicified plant-mineral structures acting as wind instruments in a constant, shifting breeze. The volume varies by location and by wind conditions, but measured in terrestrial terms, you should expect ambient sound levels in open areas of **90 to 120+ decibels**, with peak transients from the smaller spiral-growths potentially reaching **130+ decibels** during wind gusts. Below this, constantly, is a **sub-bass pulse** at frequencies between roughly 5 and 20 Hz — the tectonic heartbeat of the piezoelectric root-network — that you will feel in your bones, your teeth, your sternum, and your viscera before you hear it with your ears.

This is not merely uncomfortable. This is medically dangerous.

### Hearing Damage

Permanent sensorineural hearing loss will begin within minutes of unprotected exposure to the open Plateau during moderate wind conditions. The higher-frequency harmonics from the spiral-growths are particularly damaging because they are sharp, glassy, and directional — they hit your cochlea like needles. You will not realize the damage is happening because the pain response will be buried under the overwhelming sensory load. By the time you notice the ringing, the damage is done.

**Required equipment:**

- **Custom-molded musician's earplugs** with flat-attenuation filters, rated for continuous protection at 115+ dB. These will reduce volume evenly across frequencies without distorting the sound, which matters because you need to be able to *hear* — you need to be able to detect fauna movement, to listen for the resonant tones that open settlement doors, and to perceive the warning signs of structural shifts in the plant-mineral formations around you.

- **Electronic over-ear hearing protection** layered on top of the earplugs for open-plateau transit. These should have active noise cancellation tuned specifically for sustained high-volume environments (not the consumer-grade ANC designed for airplane cabins). Look for industrial or military-grade active hearing protection with adjustable attenuation curves. The ability to **dial in your own level of sound admission** is critical — you want to hear enough to navigate safely, but not so much that you sustain injury.

- **Backup earplugs.** Carry them in a sealed container on your person at all times. If your primary protection fails or is dislodged by wind, you need immediate replacement. Going even ten minutes without protection during active wind conditions is unacceptable.

### Sub-Bass Exposure

The sub-bass component is the trickier problem. Conventional earplugs and hearing protectors are designed to attenuate frequencies in the audible range. The tectonic pulse on the Plateau operates partly *below* the threshold of hearing and partly at its lowest boundary, and it is transmitted not just through the air but through the ground, through your skeleton, through direct mechanical coupling with your body.

Infrasound at the intensities present on the Plateau can cause:

- **Nausea and disorientation** (vestibular disruption)
- **Anxiety, dread, and a pervasive sense of unease** (well-documented effects of infrasound exposure on the human nervous system)
- **Visual disturbances** — the resonant frequency of the human eyeball is roughly 18-19 Hz, and prolonged exposure at or near this frequency can cause blurred vision, peripheral visual anomalies, and the unsettling sensation of seeing movement that isn't there
- **Chest tightness and respiratory difficulty** as the resonant frequencies interact with the thoracic cavity
- **Fatigue and cognitive impairment** that compounds over hours and days

**Partial mitigation strategies:**

- **Anti-vibration insoles** in your boots. These will not eliminate ground-transmitted infrasound but will reduce the mechanical coupling between the earth and your skeleton. Industrial anti-fatigue insoles made for workers standing on vibrating factory floors are a reasonable starting point.

- **A padded vest or body-harness with vibration-dampening panels.** This sounds excessive. It is not. The tectonic pulse on the Plateau is continuous, and your body absorbs it continuously. Think of it as standing next to a running diesel engine for days on end — because that is the approximate mechanical energy input your ribcage will be dealing with.

- **Planned rest periods inside tuned structures.** The inhabitants' buildings are designed to dampen the external chord into near-silence. This is not merely comfortable — it is *medically necessary* for anyone exposed to the open Plateau for extended periods. Plan your days around regular intervals of rest inside a building. If the inhabitants will allow you shelter (see Cultural Preparation below), use it.

- **Acceptance.** Some degree of sub-bass exposure is unavoidable. You will feel the world humming in your bones for the entirety of your visit. You will dream in resonance. Your sternum will ache. This is the baseline cost of being a soft-bodied organism on a world that vibrates. Prepare mentally for it (see below).

## V. Visual and Lighting Preparation

### Ambient Light Conditions

The Resonant Plateau exists in a state of **perpetual amber dusk.** The star is a late-sequence orange dwarf — dim, cool, and red-shifted compared to Earth's sun. Even at midday, the light reaching the ground is filtered through the translucent silica-stem structures and refracted into diffuse, wandering halos. Visibility is adequate for navigation but permanently muted, as if the world exists inside a sepia photograph.

**Preparation:**

- **Adapt your expectations.** You will not experience "bright daylight" on the Plateau. Ever. Your eyes will adjust to the amber dimness over the first day, and after that adaptation, you will find that you can see quite well — the bioluminescent glow of the structures provides supplemental illumination, and the pale mineralized ground reflects what little light exists with surprising efficiency. But you will be operating at lower light levels than you are accustomed to at all times.

- **Bring a headlamp or wearable light source** with an adjustable beam, but use it sparingly. Bright artificial light is disorienting on the Plateau — it washes out the ambient amber tones that your eyes use for spatial reference and makes the translucent structures flash and scatter in unpredictable ways. Use your light for reading instruments, checking maps, or inspecting close objects. For general navigation, trust your dark-adapted eyes. They will serve you better than an artificial beam.

- **Blue-light filtering eyewear** is worth considering. The dominant ambient light is amber-orange, and your circadian rhythm will struggle with the lack of blue-spectrum light. If your visit extends beyond a few days, the absence of blue wavelengths will disrupt your melatonin cycle and sleep architecture. Wear blue-enriching lenses during your "daytime" periods to give your suprachiasmatic nucleus something to work with, or bring a small blue-spectrum light therapy panel for morning use inside your shelter.

### Night Conditions

At night, the Plateau **glows.** The bioluminescent chemistry in the great structures intensifies as temperatures drop, producing enough light to read by — a soft, amber-gold radiance that fills the spaces between the cathedral-vaults with a luminous haze. This is beautiful. This is also potentially disorienting, because the light sources are everywhere, directionless, and shift slowly as the chemical processes that produce them pulse in their slow, not-quite-rhythmic way.

**Preparation:**

- Do not plan for nighttime navigation by artificial light. The bioluminescence is sufficient, and your eyes will adapt to it. Let them.
- Be aware that the fauna is most active at night, and many of the creatures are pale, translucent, and nearly invisible against the glowing landscape. Watch your step. Listen for the echolocation clicks of the elongated mammals — they will alert you to movement around you even when you cannot see its source.

## VI. Clothing and Personal Protection

### The Wind

The wind on the Plateau is **constant.** Not gusting — *constant.* It shifts direction and speed over the course of the day in patterns driven by thermal differentials between sun-warmed growth-matter and shadowed mineral plains, but it never stops. At its mildest, it is a steady, penetrating breeze. At its most intense, it is a sustained force that will lean you sideways and drive particulate into every seam and gap in your clothing.

**Clothing requirements:**

- **Windproof outer layer.** A full-body wind shell — jacket and pants — with sealed seams. The mineral dust on the Plateau is so fine that it infiltrates standard outdoor clothing with contemptuous ease. Look for garments with **taped seams, storm flaps over all zippers, and integrated gaiters at the cuffs.** You want a garment that makes you mildly annoyed to put on because of how thoroughly it seals. That is the right level of protection.

- **Face and neck coverage.** Even with a respirator, your face will be exposed. A full-coverage balaclava or neck gaiter pulled up to the seal of your respirator will protect your skin from the desiccating wind and the mineral particulate abrasion. Exposed skin on the Plateau will crack and chap within hours, and the silicified cellulose microfibers in the air will work into the cracks and cause a mineral dermatitis that is as unpleasant and slow-healing as it sounds.

- **Eye protection.** Sealed **ski goggles or industrial safety goggles** with anti-fog coating for open-plateau transit. The mineral dust will abrade contact lenses (do not wear contacts on the Plateau — wear prescription goggles or have corrective lenses ground into your goggle inserts). The wind will dry your corneas. The sub-bass vibration may cause visual disturbances that are exacerbated by an unprotected surface area. Goggles will not solve the infrasound problem, but they will keep the dust out of your eyes and the wind off your corneas, and that alone is worth the investment.

- **Layered insulation.** The Plateau is cool — not freezing, but cool — and the wind chill factor is significant. The living walls of the inhabitants' buildings generate metabolic heat, making interiors comfortable, but the open plateau between structures will chill you. Dress in synthetic or growth-fiber base layers (if you can acquire them locally — the inhabitants' fiber is excellent), insulating mid-layers, and the windproof shell described above. **Avoid cotton entirely.** It will absorb your sweat, hold it against your skin, and chill you. Synthetic wicking materials only.

- **Sturdy, sealed footwear.** The ground crunches. It is mineralized plant-matter that has the texture and structural integrity of weak sandstone — firm enough to walk on, but uneven, and punctuated by the bases of structures and by occasional subsurface voids where older growth-layers have been reabsorbed more aggressively by the substrate. Wear **stiff-soled hiking boots with ankle support,** sealed against particulate infiltration. Consider boots with anti-vibration midsoles to complement the insoless mentioned above. And be aware that the substrate can soften around organic matter — if you sit or lie down in one place for too long on the younger growth-matter, the ground will begin, very slowly, to draw moisture from you. Not quickly enough to be immediately dangerous, but enough to leave damp patches on your clothing and a disquieting impression of *suction* beneath you. Do not sleep on the open ground.

## VII. Navigation and Orientation

### The Disorientation Problem

The Plateau is flat. The structures are spaced kilometers apart. The light is dim, directionless, and amber. The color palette is bone-white, dove-grey, amber, and cobalt-blue bioluminescence — beautiful and monotonous. There are no bodies of water to navigate by, no vegetation changes to mark transitions between biomes, no dramatic geological features because the geology was planed flat before your species existed. The horizon is a soft undulation that looks the same in every direction.

You will get lost. You will get lost *easily,* especially if you are distracted by the sound, the beauty, the overwhelming strangeness of the place. The sensory environment on the Plateau conspires against spatial orientation.

**Required equipment:**

- **GPS or equivalent multiverse-positioning device.** If your world-hopping equipment includes any form of position tracking, use it. Calibrate it the moment you arrive. Set waypoints at your entry point, at any settlement you visit, and at every landmark structure you pass.

- **A physical compass** as backup. The Plateau's magnetic field is present but may differ from what you're accustomed to. Verify your compass is functional and note the local magnetic declination before relying on it.

- **Mark your path.** If you venture away from a settlement into the open plateau, leave physical markers — cairns of loose mineralized matter, reflective tape attached to structure bases. The structures themselves can serve as landmarks, but they are more similar to one another than you expect, and in the low light, a distant spiral-growth that you were using as a reference can blend into its neighbors with disorienting ease.

- **Never leave line-of-sight of at least one structure** unless you have active navigation technology functioning and verified. The open plateau between structures is a featureless expanse of pale compressed growth-matter, and in the amber dimness, with the wind-organ howling and the sub-bass thrumming, you can walk in circles for hours without realizing it.

### Settlement Navigation

The inhabitants' settlements are identifiable from a distance by the **regularity** of their structures — the spirals are more deliberate, the spacing more intentional, and at night, the bioluminescence is warmer, steadier, and tuned to specific wavelengths. Approach settlements from downwind if possible (which, given the constant wind, means approach from the direction the wind is blowing *toward* the settlement rather than *from* it). The inhabitants are attuned to the sound signatures of their environment, and an unfamiliar body moving through the wind-pattern will register in their awareness before you see them.

## VIII. Medical Preparedness

### First Aid Kit — Modified for the Plateau

Your standard multiverse travel first aid kit needs significant augmentation:

- **Eye wash and lubricating eye drops** (preservative-free, sealed single-use vials). The mineral dust will get in your eyes despite goggles — when you remove your protection, when a seal shifts, when you blink at the wrong moment. Have sterile saline to flush particulate and lubricating drops to combat the desiccation.

- **Wound care supplies** augmented for the environment. Cuts and abrasions on the Plateau will be contaminated with mineral particulate and will dry out rapidly in the arid air. Carry **hydrogel wound dressings** that maintain a moist healing environment, and antiseptic irrigation solution for thorough wound cleansing. The silicified cellulose microfibers in the environment can embed in open wounds and cause granulomatous inflammation if not removed. Tweezers. Fine-point tweezers. Good ones.

- **Nasal and sinus care:** saline spray, a small nasal irrigation kit (neti pot equivalent), petroleum-based nasal moisturizer for overnight use. Sinus infections from mineral dust accumulation are a predictable and preventable problem.

- **Lip balm with high wax content.** Petroleum jelly works. Your lips will crack within hours of arrival without protection, and cracked lips in a mineral-dust environment become infected lips with unpleasant speed.

- **Skin barrier cream** — a thick, occlusive moisturizer for hands, face, and any exposed skin. Apply it before you go outside and reapply when you return. This is maintenance, not vanity.

- **Electrolyte supplements and oral rehydration salts,** mentioned above but repeated here because dehydration is the most insidious and pervasive medical risk on the Plateau.

- **Broad-spectrum anti-inflammatory medication** (ibuprofen or equivalent) for the bone-ache and muscle soreness that will result from prolonged sub-bass vibration exposure.

- **Anti-nausea medication** (ondansetron or meclizine) for the vestibular disruption caused by infrasound exposure during the acclimatization period.

- **Sleep aids.** You will have difficulty sleeping on the Plateau. The sub-bass pulses through the ground even inside tuned structures (though attenuated). Your nervous system will be in a state of chronic mild activation from the sound environment. The lack of blue-spectrum light will disrupt your circadian rhythm. Bring melatonin at minimum. Consider a mild sedative if your visit will last more than a few days and you have no contraindications.

### Psychological and Cognitive Monitoring

This deserves its own section because it is the most underappreciated risk of visiting the Plateau.

**The sound will change how you think.** This is not metaphorical. The combination of constant high-volume ambient noise, sub-bass infrasound exposure, and the cognitive load of navigating an utterly alien sensory environment will alter your mental state in ways that are difficult to self-assess. Expect:

- **Difficulty concentrating.** Sustained focus on any task that requires verbal reasoning or sequential logic will be markedly impaired. You will lose your train of thought mid-sentence. You will forget why you walked into a room. This is normal. It is the sound.

- **Emotional volatility.** Infrasound exposure is associated with anxiety, irritability, and a nonspecific sense of dread. You may find yourself frightened or angry without identifiable cause. Recognize this as a physiological response, not an accurate emotional assessment of your situation.

- **Altered perception of time.** The Plateau operates on geological time. The sounds change slowly. The growth is imperceptible. The fauna moves with a slowness that feels deliberate. Your own internal clock, already disrupted by the unfamiliar light cycle and the constant sensory input, will drift. You may experience periods of apparent timelessness — stretches where you lose track of minutes or hours — interspersed with moments of startling temporal clarity. Do not trust your time perception. Use a watch. Check it often.

- **A creeping sense of belonging.** This is the subtlest and most dangerous psychological effect of extended Plateau exposure. The world is so total, so immersive, so *present* in every sensory channel, that after enough time, your nervous system begins to accommodate it — to treat the sound as normal, to find the amber light comfortable, to feel the sub-bass as a heartbeat rather than an assault. This is not your body adapting. This is your body *surrendering.* The moment you feel at home on the Plateau, the moment the sound stops bothering you, the moment you think *I could stay here* — that is the moment to check your oxygen, check your water, check your timeline, and consider whether you are making decisions with a sound mind or a resonant one.

**Buddy system.** If at all possible, do not visit the Plateau alone. Bring a companion. Agree in advance that either of you can call for withdrawal at any time, no questions asked, no arguments. Monitor each other for signs of cognitive impairment, dehydration, hypoxia, and the slow psychological erosion described above. You will not be able to monitor yourself reliably. This is what companions are for.

## IX. Cultural and Social Preparation

### The Inhabitants

The people of the Resonant Plateau are ancient. Not merely old — *ancient* in the way that the landscape is ancient, in the way that the root-network is ancient. The oldest among them show physical signs of integration with the mineral substrate: silicified fingertips, translucent patches at the temples where the bone has been infiltrated by piezoelectric chemistry. They do not die in the way you understand death. They *tend toward the substrate.* This is, to them, as natural and unremarkable as the graying of hair is to you.

They are **not hostile.** They are also not particularly interested in you. The Plateau's inhabitants have the temperament of people who have been listening for something very important for a very long time and who do not appreciate being interrupted. They will tolerate your presence. They may, if you approach with appropriate respect, allow you shelter in their tuned structures. They may, if you demonstrate patience and genuine curiosity, share something of their knowledge. But they will not perform hospitality for you. They will not rush to accommodate your needs. Their pace is the pace of the world, and the world does not hurry.

### Communication

The inhabitants speak a **tonal language** in which melodic contour carries semantic weight — not just emotional nuance, as in some terrestrial tonal languages, but actual grammatical and referential meaning. Their speech sounds, to an unaccustomed ear, like singing — or like chanting, or like the resonant structures themselves when the wind catches them right. This is not coincidence. The language co-evolved with the structures. Certain tonal patterns in the language are understood by the plant-mineral formations themselves — they respond to specific pitches by flexing, opening, closing, growing. The inhabitants speak to their buildings and their buildings listen.

**Practical implications:**

- You will not be able to speak their language on any useful timescale. The tonal precision required is beyond what a visiting human can achieve without years of practice, and the semantic structures are so deeply entangled with the acoustic environment that textbook phrase-learning will not suffice.

- **Learn to listen.** Before you try to communicate anything, spend time simply hearing the language spoken around you. The inhabitants will notice if you are paying attention to their speech — not to its content, which you will not understand, but to its *texture, its rhythm, its relationship to the ambient sound.* They respect attentive listening. On a world where listening is the foundational act of existence, it is the highest compliment you can pay.

- **Learn the settlement-entry tones.** If you intend to enter any of the tuned structures, you will need to produce a specific resonant frequency — by singing, by striking a resonator, by whatever means you can manage — that causes the opening to flex and part. The inhabitants will show you the tone if you ask patiently. Practice it. Get it right. A poorly pitched entry attempt will not anger the structures, but it will earn you the quiet, weary contempt of anyone watching — the same contempt a musician feels watching someone sit down at a piano and slap the keys at random.

- **Bring a small, tunable resonator** if you can acquire one. The inhabitants use instruments of polished mineral-glass to interact with their environment — to open doors, to signal greetings, to guide growth. A small glass or crystal rod, struck or bowed, can produce tones that the structures respond to. Learning even a few basic interactions — a greeting tone, a request-for-attention tone, a tone that means *I am a stranger and I mean no harm* — will communicate more than any amount of gesturing or attempted verbal communication.

### Etiquette

- **Do not touch the structures without permission.** They are alive. They are also someone's home, someone's tool, someone's ancestor (in some cases literally — some of the oldest inhabitants are integrated deeply enough with specific structures that the distinction between person and building has become philosophically fraught). Touching a structure without understanding its resonant state is like grabbing a musical instrument out of someone's hands. It is rude at best and physically disruptive at worst — your touch introduces new frequencies into the structure's resonance, and the inhabitants will feel the disruption.

- **Do not make unnecessary noise.** You are already an intrusion into the world's sound-scape. Your footsteps on the mineralized ground, your breathing through a respirator, the click and hum of your equipment — all of these are alien frequencies that the root-network can detect and the inhabitants can perceive. Move quietly. Speak softly. When you are inside a tuned building and the silence is deep and perfect, do not break it with idle chatter. The inhabitants value silence not as the absence of sound but as a *specific resonant state* — a tuning — and your idle noise is, to them, like a wrong note in a meditation.

- **Do not rush.** This is the hardest rule for a human to follow, and the most important. The inhabitants do not hurry. They do not respond to urgency. If you approach them with the frantic energy of a human on a schedule, they will simply... wait. They will watch you with patient, faintly luminous eyes, and they will wait for you to slow down, and they will not find your urgency interesting or compelling. The Plateau has been here for longer than you can comprehend. The chord it is listening for has been resolving for eons. Your timeline is, to them, less than a single vibration. Adjust your expectations. Slow down. Breathe. Listen.

## X. What to Expect — The Experience

### Sensory Overload and Adaptation

Your first hours on the Plateau will be overwhelming. The sound, the light, the dryness, the strangeness — everything will compete for your attention simultaneously, and your nervous system will respond with a cascade of stress hormones that will leave you jittery, irritable, and profoundly fatigued within a few hours. This is normal. This is expected. Do not make important decisions during your first twelve hours. Do not wander far from your entry point. Set up your shelter (see below), secure your water supply, seal your equipment against the dust, and then sit down and *acclimate.*

The acclimatization period varies, but most visitors report that the sound shifts from unbearable to merely oppressive within 24 to 48 hours, and from oppressive to *background* — a constant presence that you think around rather than through — within a week. This is the point at which you can begin to actually perceive the Plateau rather than simply enduring it. The structures begin to look different when you are not flinching from them. The fauna becomes visible. The amber light starts to feel not dim but *suffused.* The chord begins, very slowly, to make a kind of sense.

If you stay long enough — and "long enough" varies by individual, but typically two to three weeks — you will enter a phase that previous visitors have described as *resonant perception.* The distinction between hearing the world and seeing it begins to blur. You start to feel the structures with your skin, to taste the wind-direction, to perceive the sub-bass pulse as a spatial orientation tool rather than a physical assault. Your body begins, in small ways, to *accommodate* the Plateau. This is beautiful. This is also the beginning of the psychological accommodation described above — the creeping sense of belonging, the temptation to stay. Enjoy the perception. Monitor the attachment.

### Shelter

You have two options:

1. **Bring a self-contained shelter** — a sealed, insulated, sound-dampened tent or portable habitat module. This is the safer option, because it gives you full control over your immediate environment: filtered air, controlled humidity, darkness for sleep, and a sound barrier against the worst of the external chord. The downsides are weight, bulk, and the fact
