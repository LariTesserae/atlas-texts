# Preparation Guide: Visiting the Peneplain of Canopy

---

## I. Understand What You're Walking Into

Before you pack a single item, sit with this: you are planning to enter a place that has been absorbing things for longer than your species has existed. This is not hostility — the Peneplain doesn't have enemies — it is a process, and you are not exempt from it. The moment you set foot on the substrate, the mosses begin. This is not metaphor. The forest will try to incorporate you the way it incorporates everything: gently, slowly, and with the quiet inevitability of water finding a groove.

This is not a place that will kill you dramatically. There are no apex predators waiting to charge, no sudden cliffs, no volcanic vents. The dangers here are cumulative, ambient, and easy to overlook until they are no longer overlookable. Everything about this environment is subtle, and the human mind — calibrated for threat in sharp edges and loud sounds — is poorly equipped for a threat that is soft, silent, and everywhere at once.

Prepare yourself psychologically for the dissolution of boundaries. You will lose track of where the forest ends and you begin. This is not a hallucination. It is a real perceptual consequence of a place with no shadows, no horizon, no clear foreground or background, and a constant warm humidity that blurs the edges of everything. Many visitors describe it as a kind of waking sleep — a trance state that sets in within hours and deepens over days. It is not dangerous in itself, but it will make you careless, and carelessness here means wandering in circles, missing the signs of substrate collapse beneath your feet, or failing to notice that a root has begun to coil around your ankle. Yes, that is something that happens.

---

## II. Getting There

The Peneplain is bordered by cliff-lines — the eroded remnants of the plateau's ancient margins. These are the only reliable access points because the vegetation is otherwise impassable. You cannot walk horizontally through the canopy-membrane; it is ten to twenty meters of fused, living, decaying wood and fiber, and it has no paths. The cliff-lines, however, have water — the tannin-dark, warm, slow sheets of it that eventually find the plateau's edge and cascade down into the lowlands. Where water falls, it has carved gullies, and where gullies exist, there are thin corridors of slightly less impenetrable growth. These are your entry points.

**Approach from the lowlands.** Find the base of a cascade line and follow it upward. Expect to be wetter than you have ever been within minutes. The spray from the cascades is perpetual and warm and smells of iron and vegetation. The rock near the cliff-lines is exposed in places — bare, slick, stained black and orange with mineral deposits — and this is the last time you will see stone until you find the ruins, if you find them.

**Budget three to five days** for the ascent from the lowlands to the plateau surface, depending on the height of the cliff-line (which varies; some are hundreds of meters, others thousands). The climb is not technically difficult in a mountaineering sense, but it is relentless, wet, and disorienting. There are no switchbacks, no trails, no cairns. You are following water, and water takes the most direct path, which is often nearly vertical.

## III. Air and Altitude

The peneplain sits at significant elevation — high enough that the air is thinner than sea level, though not so high that you will experience acute mountain sickness under normal circumstances. The key word is *normal circumstances*. The air is also warm, saturated, and almost perfectly still. The humidity hovers near one hundred percent at all times. This creates a paradox: the air feels thick, heavy, almost drinkable, but it is actually oxygen-poor relative to what your lungs expect. You will feel as though you are breathing deeply and well. You are not. The oxygen partial pressure is reduced, and the humidity means your body cannot cool itself through evaporation as efficiently as it normally does.

**Acclimatize if possible.** Spend at least two to three days at intermediate elevations before reaching the plateau surface. If you are coming from sea level, this is not optional. The symptoms you are trying to avoid are subtle in this environment: not the classic headache and nausea of altitude sickness, but a creeping fatigue, a heaviness in the limbs, a slowness of thought that you will attribute to the humidity or the light or the monotony of the place. It is partially those things. It is also hypoxia.

**Monitor your breathing rate.** In the perpetual warmth and humidity, you will not feel short of breath in the way you expect. Instead, you will simply stop moving as quickly, stop thinking as sharply, stop noticing as much. This is the environment normalizing your decline. If you have a pulse oximeter, use it. If you don't, count your breaths. If you are breathing more than twenty times per minute at rest, descend or rest until the rate drops.

**The air smells of volatile organic compounds** — a sweet, faintly chemical scent that the plants produce continuously. These compounds are not acutely toxic in the concentrations you will encounter at the plateau surface, but prolonged exposure may cause mild headaches, lightheadedness, or a strange sense of euphoria that feels like well-being but is actually neurochemical interference. You cannot filter these compounds with a standard respirator. A carbon-activated filter will reduce exposure. Use one if you plan to stay more than a few days.

## IV. Water

You are surrounded by water. It is on every surface, in every breath, dripping from every leaf, pooling in every depression. You cannot escape it. You also cannot safely drink it.

The water of the Peneplain is **dark with tannin, warm, faintly acidic, and biologically active**. It is not water in the way a river or a lake is water. It is a solution — a slow, warm, tannin-rich broth of dissolved organic compounds, fungal metabolites, bacterial byproducts, and the leachate of a thirty-meter-thick mat of decaying vegetation. It smells of wet bark and fermentation and something faintly sweet that you will eventually stop noticing. It tastes, if you are foolish enough to taste it, of iron and bitterness and the ghost of something that was once living.

**Do not drink it untreated.** Boiling will kill the microorganisms but will not remove the dissolved organic compounds, many of which are hepatotoxic in chronic exposure. A activated charcoal filter is essential — not a simple particulate filter, but one rated for chemical adsorption. Even then, limit your intake and rotate your filtration method. The water will clog filters faster than almost any terrestrial source because of its particulate density. Bring redundant filtration. Bring chemical treatment as backup.

**Collect condensation from canopy surfaces** if possible. The water that beads on the upper canopy — the thin, bright layer just below the cloud-layer — is the closest thing to potable water the Peneplain produces. It is still warm and slightly acidic, but it has not yet passed through the organic matrix below. It is your best source.

**Dehydration is a real risk despite the humidity.** Your body will sweat continuously in the warm, saturated air, but because the sweat cannot evaporate, you will not feel thirsty in proportion to your fluid loss. Drink on a schedule, not by thirst. Two to three liters per day minimum, more if you are exerting yourself. Electrolyte supplementation is critical — the warm humidity and constant low-grade sweating will leach sodium and potassium faster than you expect.

## V. Light and Vision

There is no direct sunlight on the plateau surface. The cloud-layer is permanent, generated by the vegetation below, and it diffuses all light into a uniform amber glow that comes from every direction at once. There are no shadows. None. This will affect your vision and your spatial reasoning in ways that are difficult to prepare for but important to anticipate.

**Depth perception will fail.** In a shadowless environment, the human visual system loses one of its primary cues for judging distance. Things that are ten meters away will look as though they are five. Things that are fifty meters away will look as though they are fifteen. You will reach for things that are farther than you think. You will walk into things that are closer than you think. This effect worsens over time as the visual system adapts to the ambient light and begins to accept the new normal.

**Bring a light source but use it sparingly.** A headlamp or flashlight will cut through the twilight and restore shadows, but it will also attract attention. The fauna of the Peneplain is quiet and hidden, but it is not indifferent to light. In the understory, where the light is lowest, a bright beam will cause a brief, localized eruption of movement — small animals fleeing, insects scattering, the papery flutter of reluctant flyers launching from their perches. This is not dangerous in itself, but it is disorienting, and in the dense understory, a sudden explosion of movement around you can cause you to stumble, panic, or lose your bearing. Use a red-filtered light if you must navigate in the darkest zones. Red penetrates the tannin-stained atmosphere more effectively and is less disruptive to the fauna.

**The light never changes.** There is no day-night cycle. The amber twilight is constant. You will lose your circadian rhythm within forty-eight hours if you do not impose one artificially. Bring a watch. Set alarms. Create a schedule and force yourself to follow it. The most dangerous thing about this place is not any physical threat — it is the way it dissolves your sense of time, and with it, your sense of self-preservation.

## VI. Clothing and Body Protection

**Expect to be wet for the entire duration of your visit.** Not damp. Not moist. Wet. Water will bead on your skin, soak through your clothing, pool in your boots, and film your face. You will never dry off. There is no dry surface anywhere on the plateau. The substrate is saturated. The air is saturated. The plants drip constantly. The only question is how you manage the wetness, not whether you can avoid it.

**Wear synthetic, quick-drying layers** that wick moisture away from the skin. Cotton will become a cold, heavy second skin within hours — not that cold is a significant risk here, since the air is warm, but the weight and friction of sodden cotton against your body will cause chafing and skin breakdown within days. Merino wool is acceptable but will eventually become waterlogged. Synthetics are best.

**Waterproof outer layers are useless and counterproductive.** In an environment of near-100% humidity, a waterproof shell traps your sweat against your skin and creates a microclimate of superheated, saturated air that is worse than simply being wet. You will be wet regardless. Accept it. Wear breathable layers that manage moisture rather than blocking it.

**Footwear is critical.** The substrate is thirty meters of accumulated organic matter — soft, spongy, riddled with voids where decomposition has created pockets of air or water beneath a surface that looks solid but may not be. You will sink. Regularly. To your ankles, your shins, occasionally your knees. Boots should be tall — above the ankle at minimum, mid-calf preferred — with aggressive tread for gripping the slick, fibrous surface of root-mats and decayed wood. Gaiters are essential to keep the substrate out of your boots, which it will try to enter constantly.

**Exposed skin is an invitation.** Not to predators in the conventional sense, but to the Peneplain itself. The plants grow toward warmth and moisture, and your body is a beacon of both. In the understory, where some roots have evolved to grow toward movement, exposed skin is a surface for colonization. This does not mean the plants will attack you — they are not capable of rapid movement — but roots will orient toward you over hours and days, and if you are stationary for long enough, they will make contact and begin to adhere. Long sleeves, long pants, gloves, and a hat are mandatory. Tuck your pants into your boots. Tuck your sleeves into your gloves. Check your clothing for root incursion regularly — at least twice a day. A fine-toothed comb can help you find and remove the hair-thin exploratory roots that will begin to thread through the weave of your fabric.

**Skin care is important.** The perpetual moisture and warmth create ideal conditions for fungal and bacterial skin infections. Apply antifungal powder to your feet, groin, and armpits daily. Treat any cuts or abrasions immediately and aggressively — the biological load in the environment is immense, and infections here are fast and difficult to control. An open wound is an invitation for the Peneplain's fungal networks to begin the work of incorporation. This is not a metaphor. Wound care should be your most rigorous daily discipline.

## VII. Navigation

**You will get lost.** Not because the Peneplain is complex in the way a city or a mountain range is complex, but because it is uniform in a way that the human spatial mind was never designed to process. The canopy is continuous. The understory is continuous. The twilight is continuous. There are no landmarks — or rather, everything is a landmark, which means nothing is. The rolling swells of the peneplain rise and fall over kilometers with such gentleness that you cannot feel the gradient underfoot. You could walk in a perfect circle and believe you were walking in a straight line.

**GPS is your primary navigation tool, but it may be unreliable.** The thick canopy, the saturated atmosphere, and the high elevation can all degrade GPS signal. Bring a unit that supports multiple satellite constellations (GPS, GLONASS, Galileo, BeiDou) and check your position frequently. Mark your entry point — the gully line where you ascended from the lowlands — and take a bearing from it. If you are unable to navigate back to your entry point, your only reliable fallback is the direction of water flow. Water on the plateau moves laterally toward the cliff-edges. If you can find a channel or flow-line and follow it downhill (which is imperceptibly gradual), you will eventually reach the margin.

**Leave markers.** Tie bright-colored tape to branches at eye height every hundred meters. Drive stakes into the substrate at intervals. The vegetation will obscure your markers within days — the mosses grow that fast — but you can use this as a clock: if a marker you placed yesterday is already hidden, you have been stationary too long or your sense of time is slipping.

**A compass is unreliable here.** Not because of magnetic interference — there is none — but because the shadowless environment removes the primary visual cue your brain uses to orient the compass reading. You will look at the compass needle, see it pointing north, and then look up and have no idea which direction that corresponds to in the landscape. Practice using a compass in conjunction with a map before you arrive. Mark bearings on your map and follow them mechanically, not intuitively.

**Sound navigation is not possible.** The ambient soundscape is too uniform — the drip, the creak, the subsonic hum — to serve as a directional reference. The canopy absorbs and scatters sound in every direction. A noise that seems to come from your left may originate from your right or from above or from beneath you.

## VIII. Shelter and Camp

There is no dry ground. There are no clearings. The canopy is continuous and lightless from below. You cannot build a fire — the substrate is too wet, too organic, and too flammable in the way that wet peat is flammable: it will smolder rather than burn, producing dense, acrid smoke that is toxic and that will permeate the canopy and potentially trigger a reaction from the fungal network, which communicates chemical signals across kilometers.

**Sleep elevated.** The substrate is saturated and unstable, and at night — or what passes for night, if you are maintaining a sleep schedule — the activity level of the understory changes. The predatory roots are more exploratory in darkness, when the chemical gradients that guide them are unopposed by the faint light. Hammocks suspended between the trunks of the midstory are your best option. The trunks are soft and will accept anchor points easily, but you must check the wood for structural integrity before committing your weight. A trunk that looks solid may have a core of advanced decay. Test by pressing your fist into the surface at multiple points. If it yields more than a few centimeters, choose another trunk.

**A bivy sack or enclosed hammock with a waterproof floor and mesh canopy is essential** — not for rain protection, but for drip protection and to create a barrier between you and the exploratory roots and the small, quiet fauna that will investigate you while you sleep. Insects will find you. Small canopy-dwelling animals will investigate you. You will wake to find things on you or near you that were not there when you fell asleep. This is not dangerous — nothing on the plateau is large enough to threaten a human — but it is psychologically wearing, and after several days, the constant low-level intrusion into your personal space will begin to erode your composure. The bivy is your only private space. Guard it.

**Do not stay in one camp for more than two or three days.** The Peneplain begins to incorporate stationary objects quickly. After forty-eight hours, moss will be visibly growing on any surface you have left in contact with the substrate. After seventy-two hours, root tendrils will have found the edges of your equipment. After a week, you will need to physically cut your gear free from the substrate. Move regularly, even if you are not going far.

## IX. The Psychological Dimension

This is the section that matters most and that you are least likely to take seriously.

**The Peneplain is monotonous.** Not in the way a desert is monotonous — a desert has vistas, horizons, a sense of scale that reminds you of your smallness. The Peneplain has none of that. It has no horizons because the canopy blocks them. It has no vistas because there is nothing to see beyond the next few meters of trunks and hanging roots and ferns and moss. It has no sense of scale because every direction looks the same. After a few hours, your visual system will stop trying to extract information from the environment and will simply accept the input as background noise. You will stop looking. You will stop seeing. And when you stop seeing, you will start to drift.

**The trance state is real and it is the primary psychological risk.** Visitors describe it variously as a dreamlike dissociation, a meditative calm, or a gentle dissolution of the boundary between self and environment. It feels pleasant. It feels like the place is welcoming you. It is not. The Peneplain has no intentions. But the combination of uniform light, uniform temperature, uniform humidity, uniform sound, and the subtle neurological effects of the volatile organic compounds in the air creates a state of consciousness in which you stop monitoring your own welfare. You forget to eat. You forget to check your position. You forget to watch for substrate collapse. You forget that you are a separate thing from the forest.

**Bring a companion or maintain regular contact with someone outside the Peneplain.** A radio works if you can reach a relay above the canopy, but the canopy absorbs and scatters radio waves as effectively as it absorbs light. Satellite communication is more reliable but may require you to climb to the upper canopy — a difficult and dangerous ascent through the thick, soft, decay-riddled trunks of the midstory — to get a signal through. Schedule regular check-ins. If you miss a check-in, your contact should assume you are in the trance state and should initiate recovery protocols.

**Carry tactile anchors.** Small objects with strong textures or temperatures that can break the trance: a piece of rough metal, a smooth stone from outside the Peneplain, a packet of something very cold or very warm. When you notice the dissociation setting in — and you will notice, at least at first — grip the object tightly and focus on the sensation. This will not prevent the trance indefinitely, but it will buy you time.

**Monitor your own speech.** If you are with a companion, listen to how you are talking. If your sentences become shorter, slower, less specific — if you begin to describe things in terms of feeling rather than fact — if you catch yourself saying things like "it's fine" or "it doesn't matter" or "I'm fine" without being able to articulate why — you are entering the trance state. Act immediately. Eat something with strong flavors. Splash cold water on your face (the water is warm, so you will need to have cooled some in advance). Engage in a task that requires focus: navigating, repairing equipment, recording observations. Do not rest until the dissociation passes.

## X. The Plants

**You are inside a single organism.** This is not a metaphor. The Peneplain's vegetation has fused into a continuous, interconnected network — root, trunk, canopy, fungal hyphae — that shares nutrients, water, and chemical signals across the entire plateau. You are standing on its body. You are breathing its exhalations. You are, to a first approximation, inside it.

**Treat the vegetation as you would treat the body of a sleeping animal.** Move carefully. Do not make sudden, large disturbances. If you cut through a trunk or a root, you are wounding the organism, and it will respond — not with aggression, but with the same slow, chemical response that any living system mounts to injury. The area around the wound will increase its metabolic activity. The fungal network will send signals. Nearby roots will grow toward the wound site to seal it. This takes hours to days, but if you are camped near a wound you have created, you will find the vegetation actively growing into your space. Better to go around than through.

**The predatory roots in the understory are real.** They do not move quickly — their growth rate is measured in millimeters per hour — but they are persistent, and they are guided by chemical and thermal gradients. Your body is a source of both. If you are stationary for more than a few hours in the understory, roots will begin to orient toward you. They will not ensnare you in minutes. They will not drag you down. But they will make contact with your clothing or skin, and once contact is made, they will adhere and begin to grow into the fabric or, if bare skin, into the outer layers of epidermis. This is not painful — the roots are thinner than threads and the penetration is superficial — but it is insidious, and you may not notice it happening until you try to move and feel the faint tug of attachment. Check yourself. Check your clothing. Pull roots away gently before they establish. If a root has begun to grow into your skin — you will feel a faint itch and see a dark line beneath the surface — remove it with tweezers and treat the wound as you would any puncture: clean, antiseptic, monitor for infection.

**The smell.** The understory smells of decay and growth simultaneously — the sharp, almost alcoholic scent of fermentation alongside a green brightness that has a faintly metallic edge. This is the smell of the Peneplain's metabolism. It is not toxic in acute exposure, but it is the olfactory signature of the volatile organic compounds that will affect your neurology over time. If the smell begins to seem pleasant or comforting, that is a warning sign. It means your nervous system is adapting to the compounds, and the trance state is not far behind.

## XI. The Fauna

**Nothing on the Peneplain is large enough to kill you.** The largest land animals are the size of a large dog, and they are neither aggressive nor interested in you. They will flee before you see them. The insects are vast in number but not venomous in any way that threatens a human. The amphibians live in the cups of epiphytic plants and will never interact with you.

**The fauna is more important as a psychological factor than a physical one.** The sounds — the rasping call from the midstory, the scrabbling in the leaf-litter, the papery flutter of small bodies — will be your constant companions, and in the uniform twilight and the trance-prone environment, they will begin to take on significance that they do not deserve. You will start to feel watched. You will start to feel that the sounds are directed at you. They are not. The animals are going about their lives, which happen to be taking place in the same environment as yours. But the human mind is a meaning-generating machine, and in the absence of clear sensory data, it will generate meaning from whatever is available. The sounds of the fauna are what is available. Remind yourself, regularly, that the animals are indifferent to you.

**The birds that fly above the canopy are the only creatures that see the sun.** You will not see them from below, but you may hear their calls — faint, high, distant — filtering down through the membrane. If you are in the upper canopy, you may see them dropping back through it: sudden, bright shapes that flash through the green and then disappear into the twilight. This is the closest thing to a horizon that the Peneplain offers. It may be the most beautiful thing you see here. It may also be the most disorienting, because it reminds you that there is a world above this one — a world with light and sky and distance — and you are not in it.

## XII. The Ruins

**You may not find them.** The plateau is continental in extent and the ruins are scattered, low, and half-swallowed by vegetation. They are not marked on any map that exists in your world. You could walk for weeks in any direction and never see them.

**If you do find them, be careful.** The stone is ancient and the vegetation has spent millennia infiltrating every joint and fissure. The structures are unstable — not in the way of crumbling masonry, but in the way of things that have been slowly, imperceptibly moved by forces that do not stop. A wall that looks solid may have been lifted from its foundation by root pressure and may shift if you lean on it. The floor of a structure may be a shell of compacted organic matter over a void where the original floor has been consumed.

**The patina on the stone is biological.** It is a film of organisms — bacteria, algae, fungi — that have been growing on the surface for millennia. Do not touch it with bare skin. The organisms in the film are adapted to the Peneplain's environment, and while they are not pathogenic to humans in the conventional sense, they are aggressively colonizing, and the boundary between "on the surface" and "in the surface" is very thin when the surface is skin.

**The hum.** Some of the ruins hum. It is faint, low-frequency, and persistent. It might be wind moving through gaps in the masonry. It might be the vibration of the fungal network transmitted through the root-mat into the stone foundations. It might be something else. If you hear it, note the frequency and location. If you can, record it. But do not spend too long listening. The hum has a hypnotic quality that compounds the trance state, and visitors who have lingered near the humming stones report a deep reluctance to leave — not because they want to stay, exactly, but because leaving begins to feel unnecessary. The Peneplain is patient, and patience is its most persuasive argument.

## XIII. The Question of Exit

**Leave before you want to.** This is the single most important piece of advice I can offer. The Peneplain does not trap you. It does not restrain you. It does not pursue you when you leave. But it changes you in ways that make leaving feel less and less necessary, and by the time you realize you should have left days ago, the trance state has progressed to a point where the effort of leaving seems disproportionate to the benefit. You will tell yourself that you are fine, that another day won't matter, that the place is beautiful and peaceful and you are learning so much. You are not fine. Another day does matter. And what you are learning is how to stop being a separate thing from the forest.

**Set a hard exit date before you enter and do not extend it under any circumstances.** Tell your external contact. Write it on your skin with permanent marker if you have to. When the date comes, leave, even if you feel fine, especially if you feel fine. The Peneplain's most effective trap is the feeling that there is no trap.

**On the way out, you will notice things you didn't notice on the way in.** The moss on your equipment. The dark lines in your skin where exploratory roots found purchase. The way your boots have begun to grow a fine fuzz of hyphae along the seams. The way your watch has stopped, even though it was working yesterday. The way the sound of dripping follows you down the cliff-line and into the lowlands and does not stop until you are a day's walk from the cascade, and even then, in the silence of the outside world, you will hear it — drip, long pause, drip — for weeks. It fades. It always fades. But it fades slowly, and you will never be entirely sure whether it was real or whether the Peneplain left a part of itself in your ear, listening.

## XIV. Summary Checklist

- **Altitude acclimatization:** 2-3 days at intermediate elevation
- **Filtration:** Carbon-activated water filters (redundant), chemical treatment backup
- **Hydration discipline:** Minimum 2-3 liters/day, scheduled, with electrolytes
- **Clothing:** Synthetic layers, no cotton, no waterproof shells, tall boots with gaiters, full coverage (long sleeves, long pants, gloves, hat)
- **Skin care:** Antifungal powder, wound care kit, daily skin inspections
- **Navigation:** Multi-constellation GPS, compass, map, bright trail markers, stakes
- **Shelter:** Enclosed hammock or bivy with waterproof floor and mesh canopy
- **Light:** Headlamp with red filter, spare batteries
- **Respiratory protection:** Carbon-activated filter mask for extended stays
- **Psychological tools:** Tactile anchors, external communication device, hard exit date
- **Companion:** Strongly recommended; if solo, satellite communicator with scheduled check-ins
- **Root removal:** Fine-toothed comb, tweezers, antiseptic
- **Patience:** Bring more than you think you need. The Peneplain has more than you do.
