# Preparation Guide for the Garden Beneath the Drowned Light

---

## I. Understanding What You're Walking Into

The first thing you need to do — before you touch a single piece of gear, before you consult a single guidebook or biometric profile — is adjust your *expectations*. This is not a dive. This is not a visit to an underwater ruin. This is a place that has been continuously, quietly, patiently alive for longer than your civilization has existed, and it will not pause for you. You are entering a world that operates on timescales where a human lifetime is a single breath. If you approach it as an adventure, you will be frustrated. If you approach it as a pilgrimage, you will be humbled. If you approach it as a quiet conversation with something much older and much larger than yourself, you might actually hear what it has to say.

You will be submerged. There is no air. There is no land. There is no surface you can reach. The nearest breathable atmosphere is *miles* above you — miles of black water pressing down with a weight that human bodies were not built to withstand. You are not visiting a place with a bottom; you are visiting a place that *is* bottom. The entire world is pressure and cold and darkness and growth, and you are stepping into it like a mote of dust stepping into a cathedral.

Prepare to be patient. Prepare to be quiet. Prepare to feel, for the first time in your life, that you are not the point.

## II. Life Support: Breathing in a World Without Air

### Respiratory Systems

You will need a closed-circuit rebreather (CCR) or its multiverse equivalent — an apparatus that recycles your exhaled breath, scrubs carbon dioxide, and supplements oxygen. Open-circuit scuba is not advisable; the constant stream of exhaust bubbles will create noise, disturb the vegetation, and draw attention to your presence in a world where nothing else breathes the way you do. You want to be quiet. You want to move through this place the way the water does — slowly, without fuss, without announcement.

**Key specifications:**

- **Duration:** Plan for extended submersion. This is not a 45-minute recreational dive. The Garden is vast — hundreds of miles in every direction — and it rewards slow exploration. Your rebreather should support a minimum of 8–12 hours of continuous operation, ideally longer. If your multiverse toolkit includes spatial compression or pocket-dimension air supplies, bring them. If not, plan for resupply points: sealed capsules cached at intervals, or a support vessel (itself a sealed habitat) positioned somewhere within the Garden's sprawl.

- **Redundancy:** Carry two independent breathing systems. This is not optional. There is no surface to swim to. There is no ascent protocol. If your primary system fails at depth, your backup is the only thing between you and a death that would be as silent and unnoticed as everything else in this place.

- **Oxygen partial pressure management:** The water is barely oxygenated — just enough to sustain chemosynthetic life, nowhere near enough for human lungs. Your rebreather must maintain safe O₂ partial pressures at whatever depth-equivalent you're operating at. The "weight of miles" of water suggests pressures that would be instantly lethal to an unprotected human. Your life support must account for this.

- **CO₂ scrubber duration:** In cold water, your metabolism will run higher to maintain core temperature. You will produce CO₂ faster than you expect. Bring extra scrubber material. Bring more than you think you need.

### Thermal Regulation

The cold here is not like cold you have experienced. It is not the cold of a winter lake or a mountain stream. It is the cold of *deep time* — the temperature of a place that settled into its thermal equilibrium millennia ago and has not budged since. It will press against you from every direction, including above and below. There is no wind chill, but there is *water* chill, and water conducts heat roughly 25 times faster than air. You will lose heat continuously, relentlessly, from every square centimeter of your body.

**Thermal protection options, ranked by adequacy:**

1. **Minimum (dangerous):** A thick drysuit with thermal undergarments. This will slow heat loss but will not stop it. At the temperatures described — deep, constant, barely above freezing — you have perhaps 2–4 hours before hypothermia begins to impair cognition and motor function. This is not enough time to meaningfully experience the Garden. This is emergency backup only.

2. **Adequate:** A heated drysuit or immersion suit with integrated heating elements — electrically powered, with redundancy in the power supply. The suit maintains a thin layer of warm water against your skin, heated actively. Battery life or powerverse equivalent power supply becomes the limiting factor. Plan for 12+ hours of heating capacity. Carry backup power.

3. **Recommended:** A sealed, pressure-rated habitat suit — essentially a wearable submersible. Hard shell or semi-rigid construction, internal atmosphere regulation, thermal insulation sufficient to maintain comfortable body temperature indefinitely, with propulsion capability. This is what you want. It lets you move through the Garden without being in constant metabolic crisis, without shivering, without losing the fine motor control you'll need to navigate dense vegetation and narrow passages.

4. **Ideal (multiverse-dependent):** Biological adaptation. If your multiverse toolkit includes temporary physiological modification — cold-adapted tissues, reduced metabolic rate, enhanced peripheral vasoconstriction — consider it. The body you were born with was designed for air and sunlight and 20°C. The Garden was not designed for you. Meet it halfway.

**A note on extremities:** Fingers, toes, and your face will be the first to lose sensation. If you're using a suit that exposes any skin at all, be aware that in the ambient temperature of the Garden, exposed tissue will go numb within minutes and begin to suffer cold injury within an hour. Gloves must be dexterous enough to operate equipment and manipulate vegetation (you may need to part stalks to pass through) but insulated enough to keep your hands functional. Consider heated gloves with independent power supplies.

## III. Vision and Light: Navigating a World of Almost-Darkness

### The Nature of the Darkness

The Garden is not dark the way a closet is dark. It is not dark the way the inside of a cave is dark. It is dark the way the *deep ocean* is dark — a darkness that is structural, total, and ancient, against which the faint bioluminescence of the garden organisms exists as a kind of rumor of light. Shapes are visible only as negative space against the faint glow. Colors do not exist. Depth perception is compromised. The world is grey-on-grey-on-darker-grey, and your eyes, no matter how long you wait, will never fully adjust, because there is not enough light *to* adjust to.

You will see: the broad silhouette of a wall. The cathedral arch of a passage, traced in faint phosphorescent lace. The slow sway of a distant stalk, darker than the darkness around it, its tip a faint smear of green-that-might-be-blue. You will see shapes. You will not see details. You will not see far.

### Artificial Light: A Complicated Question

Here is where you need to make a deliberate choice, because artificial light will fundamentally change your experience of this place — and not necessarily for the better.

**The case for bringing light:**
- You will be able to see the architecture. You will be able to appreciate the craftsmanship — the thumb-pressed joints, the fiber cordage mineralized into amber veins, the layered textures of wall and crust and growth. You will see color for the first time: the pale golds and dusky rusts of the bacterial mats, the translucent sheaths of the understory organisms, the mineral veining of the nodular growths.
- You will be able to navigate safely. The Garden is dense and sprawling; without light, you risk entangling yourself in vegetation, blundering into dead-end passages, or failing to notice structural instabilities.
- You will be able to read the architecture — to distinguish built structure from living growth, to trace the logic of the builders, to understand the spatial organization of the districts.

**The case against bringing light:**
- You will destroy the atmosphere of the place. The Garden is *designed* — architecturally, ecologically, experientially — for this level of darkness. Your artificial light will flatten it. It will turn a vast, breathing, dimensional darkness into a small, ordinary, illuminated pool. The stalks will become just plants. The walls will become just walls. The bioluminescence will vanish, overwhelmed. You will see more, but you will *feel* less.
- You may disturb the organisms. The bioluminescent response here is poorly understood. It is possible that bright light triggers stress responses, growth changes, or behavioral shifts in the microbial communities. You don't know. No one does. The organisms have been growing in near-darkness for millennia. Introducing a sudden, intense photon source may be the ecological equivalent of screaming in a library.
- You will lose the darkness itself as an experience. The darkness of the Garden is not an absence. It is a *presence* — a medium you move through, a texture you feel against your skin, a weight that presses on your awareness the way the water presses on your body. If you light it up, you lose the one thing that makes this place unlike any other place you have ever been.

**My recommendation:** Bring a light. Keep it dim. Use it sparingly — for navigation checks, for moments of genuine confusion, for emergencies. Use a red-spectrum or deep-orange filter if possible; the organisms are less likely to be disrupted by longer wavelengths, and your own eyes will preserve more dark-adapted sensitivity. Keep the beam narrow and pointed downward or at surfaces, not out into the open water. Turn it off whenever you can. Learn to navigate by the bioluminescence, by touch, by the texture of the current, by the geometry you can feel more than see. Let your eyes do the work they were built to do — not for brightness, but for the faintest gradients of dim.

If you must have full illumination — for documentation, for research, for the simple human need to *see* — do it once. Do it in a single, chosen moment. Turn on the light. Let the Garden reveal itself in full, shocking, alien color. Memorize it. Then turn the light off and return to the dark, carrying the image with you like a secret. That image will stay with you longer than any photograph.

### Underwater Optics

If you're wearing a face mask or helmet visor, be aware that water refracts light differently than air. Objects will appear roughly 25% closer and 33% larger than they actually are. In a place where spatial scale is already overwhelming — corridors that stretch for kilometers, stalk forests that rise fifty meters overhead — this distortion can be disorienting. Practice using underwater optics before you arrive. Calibrate your sense of distance. Learn to *correct* for the magnification instinctively, so that when you see a passage that appears to be twenty meters ahead, you know it is actually closer to twenty-five.

If your suit includes an optical heads-up display (HUD), consider overlaying a sonar or spatial-mapping feed. You will not always be able to see where you are going. Having a map — even an approximate one, generated by acoustic returns from the structure around you — will prevent you from getting lost in a labyrinth that extends for hundreds of miles with no landmarks and no light.

## IV. Propulsion and Movement: Swimming Through a Three-Dimensional Jungle

### The Problem of Density

The vegetation is everywhere. Not just on the floor, not just on the walls, but in the *volume* — filling the space between structures, draping from ceilings (if ceilings existed), rising from surfaces on stalks that extend tens of meters upward, spreading across passages in rubbery sheets and curtains that part when you push through them and close behind you when you pass. You are not walking through a forest. You are *swimming* through one — a forest with no ground, where the underbrush is above you and below you and around you, and every direction is "through."

This means:

- **Fin propulsion is insufficient.** If you're relying on swim fins for locomotion, you will exhaust yourself within the first hour. The vegetation creates drag. The passages are narrow and branching. You need to move in three dimensions — up, down, laterally, diagonally — through spaces that may be choked with growth. Fins are optimized for open water; the Garden is not open water.

- **Diver propulsion vehicles (DPVs) are problematic.** A standard underwater scooter will be too fast, too loud, and too bulky for the dense vegetation. You will entangle the propeller in stalks and mats. You will crash into walls you can't see. The acoustic signature of a motor — any motor — will propagate through the water for kilometers, and while there are no animals to flee from it, the sound will shatter the silence that is one of the Garden's defining characteristics.

- **Recommended: Towed or muscle-powered slow propulsion.** A small, quiet, low-speed thruster unit — something like a hand-held or belt-mounted device that produces gentle thrust, enough to move you at a slow swimming pace (0.5–1 m/s) without creating turbulence or noise. Alternatively, develop your underwater swimming technique. Frog kick is efficient and low-disturbance. Pulling yourself along walls and structures (gently — the surfaces are ancient and fragile) works well in tight spaces. Practice buoyancy control until it is instinctive; you need to hover, to rise, to sink, to hold position, all without effort and without disturbing the growth around you.

- **Buoyancy control is critical.** In a world with no floor and no surface, neutral buoyancy is not a skill — it is *survival*. If you sink, you sink into structure and sediment and vegetation, potentially becoming entangled. If you rise, you rise into the stalk forests, potentially becoming lost in a canopy with no landmarks. Maintain neutral buoyancy at all times. This requires either a buoyancy compensator (BCD) that you can adjust precisely, or a suit with integrated buoyancy management. Check and adjust frequently. In cold water, your exposure suit's buoyancy characteristics may change as the gas inside it compresses or expands with temperature shifts. Stay on top of it.

### Navigating the Density

The vegetation is not uniformly dense. There are patterns — districts where the growth is so thick you can barely squeeze through, and districts where it thins to a manageable underbrush. The density follows the thermalpply: near the thermal seeps and the ancient pipes, growth is thickest; in the older, more settled districts where the structures are heavily mineralized, it thins. Learn to read the density. Use it. If you're trying to make time, follow the thinner corridors. If you're trying to experience the Garden at its most overwhelming, push into the thick.

A few practical notes:

- **The stalks are fragile.** They are thin, dark, and ancient — some have been growing for a thousand years. They break easily if you push too hard. Move through them gently. Part them with your hands. Let them slide along your suit. Do not grab and snap. You will be tempted, because moving through dense vegetation is frustrating and slow, and the stalks feel like they're in your way. They are not in your way. You are in theirs.

- **The mats are sticky.** The filamentous bacterial mats that coat every surface will adhere to your suit. They will not damage it (probably), but they will make movement through tight spaces unpleasant — a clinging, dragging sensation, like swimming through wet tissue paper. This is unavoidable. Accept it. Clean your suit periodically if the buildup impedes your movement, but do so gently, and away from delicate surfaces.

- **The rubbery sheet-like organisms drape.** They will settle over you like curtains. They are translucent and faintly warm and disconcertingly organic-feeling. They will not hurt you. They will not react to your presence. They will simply drape, and you will have to push through them, again and again, and each time it will feel slightly unsettling in a way that is hard to articulate. Practice pushing through curtains and drapes before you go. Sounds silly. It isn't.

## V. Communication and Documentation

### Recording Your Experience

You will want to document this place. Bring a camera — something rated for the depth and pressure, with low-light capability. A standard action camera (even a "low-light" model) will struggle in the ambient bioluminescence; you will either need to bring your own illumination (see the complicated discussion above) or accept that your footage will be dark, grainy, and mostly impressionistic. This is fine. The Garden is not a place that photographs well. It is a place that *experiences* well. The gap between those two things is enormous, and your documentation will always fall on the wrong side of it.

If you can bring sonar or LIDAR mapping equipment, do so. A spatial map of the architecture — even an incomplete one — will be invaluable, both for navigation and for understanding the spatial logic of the builders. The structures are too large and too dark to take in visually; acoustic or laser mapping gives you a God's-eye view that your own senses cannot provide.

Audio recording is essential. The sound of the Garden — the settling groan of the structure, the granular whisper of current through stalks, the silence that fills every gap between these sounds — is one of its most striking features. You will not be able to convey it in words. Record it. Play it back later, in a well-lit room, with your feet on solid ground, and let the silence of the Garden fill your ordinary world for a few minutes. This will be one of the most valuable things you bring back.

## VI. Psychological Preparation: The Mind in the Dark

This is, in some ways, the most important section.

The Garden Beneath the Drowned Light is not dangerous in the way that most dangerous places are dangerous. There are no predators. There are no active hazards. The architecture is ancient and stable. The water is calm. The temperature is constant. If your equipment functions properly, nothing in this environment will actively try to kill you.

And yet. And yet.

### The Silence

You have never experienced silence like this. Not the silence of a library, not the silence of a desert at night, not the silence of a soundproof room. Those silences are *empty*. This silence is *full* — full of the settling groan of structures older than your species, full of the whisper of current through gardens that grow without purpose, full of the weight of miles of water pressing down from above. It is the silence of a place where nothing has spoken for a very long time, where nothing *needs* to speak, where communication is unnecessary because there is no one to communicate with.

You will find, after a few hours, that your own thoughts become louder. The silence has a way of amplifying interior noise — anxieties, memories, the running monologue you normally don't notice because the world is always drowning it out. Here, the world is not drowning anything out. Here, you hear yourself clearly, and you may not enjoy what you hear.

Prepare for this. Practice meditation or mindfulness techniques before you go. Learn to sit with silence — not to fill it, not to fight it, but to let it exist around you without panicking. The silence of the Garden is not hostile. It is not waiting. It is simply the sound of a world that does not need you, and learning to be at peace with that is one of the most valuable skills you can bring.

### The Scale

You are small. The Garden is vast. This is not an abstract fact you know from reading a description; it is a *felt reality* that will press against you from every direction. The walls tower overhead (if "overhead" means anything here). The passages extend into darkness in every direction. The structures have been here for longer than your brain can meaningfully comprehend — not centuries, not millennia, but *geological* time, the kind of time that turns mud into stone and patience into architecture.

You will feel, at some point, the acute and specific loneliness of being the only thing in the world that is in a hurry. Everything here is slow. The stalks grow at a centimeter a year. The current shifts over decades. The garden has been running on its own for longer than your civilization has existed, and it will continue running long after your civilization is forgotten. Your heartbeat is frantic by comparison. Your thoughts race. Your need to *do* something, to *go* somewhere, to *accomplish* the visit — all of this is noise in a place that has outgrown the concept of urgency.

Let it go. You are not here to accomplish anything. You are here to *witness*. Witnessing is its own purpose. Sit (float, hover, drift) in the darkness, and watch the faint lights pulse, and feel the water press against your suit, and listen to the groan of the walls, and let the garden be what it is without needing it to be more. This is not passive. This is the hardest work you will do here.

### The Absence of Animals

You may not notice this at first. You may not notice it for hours. But when you do — when it finally occurs to you that you have been swimming through a rich, dense, living ecosystem and have not seen a single creature — the absence will hit you like a physical sensation. It will feel *wrong*. Every instinct you have, honed by millions of years of evolution in a world teeming with animal life, will tell you that a place this alive should be *moving*. Should be crawling and darting and hiding and hunting. And instead: nothing. Just the slow sway of stalks. Just the pulse of bioluminescence. Just the garden, growing, and growing, and growing, alone.

You will want to explain this. You will want to theorize about why there are no animals. Was it designed? Is it maintained? Did something remove them? Was it always this way? Your mind will spin hypotheses, because that is what minds do when confronted with a pattern they cannot explain. Let the hypotheses spin. Write them down if it helps. But do not expect an answer. The Garden is not here to be understood. It is here to be *present*, and your inability to explain it is part of the experience.

If the absence begins to unsettle you — if the silence and the darkness and the aloneness start to press inward — remind yourself that the garden is not empty. It is *full*. Full of life, full of growth, full of chemistry and architecture and time. The life here is simply not the kind of life you are used to. It is slower, quieter, and (frankly) more successful than the kind you come from. The chemosynthetic organisms of the Garden have been thriving without animals for longer than animals have existed. You are the anomaly here, not them.

### The Feeling of Intrusion

You are a guest. You are an intrusion. You are a warm, fast, loud, bright thing in a cold, slow, quiet, dark world, and no matter how carefully you move, no matter how quiet you try to be, you will leave a trace — a disruption in the current, a faint warming of the water, a smear on the bacterial mats, a bent stalk that will take a decade to straighten. The Garden will erase your presence in minutes, but for those minutes, you were *here*, and the Garden noticed, even if it did not care.

This is not a reason not to go. It is a reason to go *well* — to move with intention, with care, with the understanding that you are a visitor in someone else's home, even if the someone is a collection of chemosynthetic bacteria and mineral-walled corridors and the ghost-memories of builders who may have been absorbed into the thing they built.

## VII. Equipment Checklist

Here is a consolidated list. Adjust for your multiverse's available technology.

### Essential

- [ ] **Closed-circuit rebreather** (or equivalent), rated for deep/high-pressure operation, with 12+ hours of endurance and full redundancy
- [ ] **Backup breathing system** (independent, sealed, easily deployable)
- [ ] **Pressure-rated habitat suit or immersion suit** with integrated thermal regulation
- [ ] **Redundant power supply** for heating and propulsion
- [ ] **Buoyancy control system** (integrated buoyBCD or integrated suit buoyancy management)
- [ ] **Low-noise propulsion device** (handheld thruster, belt-mounted unit, or equivalent)
- [ ] **Dim-spectrum headlamp or suit-mounted light** with red/deep-orange filter and adjustable intensity
- [ ] **Backup light** (independent, always-on low-lumen emergency marker)
- [ ] **Sonar/spatial mapping device** for navigation
- [ ] **Underwater communication system** (if traveling with companions; low-frequency acoustic or hardwired)
- [ ] **Depth-rated camera** with low-light capability
- [ ] **Audio recording device** with hydrophone input
- [ ] **Exposure-rated gloves** (insulated, dexterous, optionally heated)
- [ ] **Emergency ascent/rescue beacon** (transdimensional signal flare, or equivalent)

### Highly Recommended

- [ ] **Thermal reserve** (extra heating batteries, chemical heat packs, emergency insulation)
- [ ] **CO₂ scrubber refills** (at minimum double your calculated need)
- [ ] **Cutting tool** (for entanglement emergencies — serrated blade, shears; use *only* as last resort)
- [ ] **Tactile navigation aids** (weighted line, reel, or breadcrumb markers for retracing your path)
- [ ] **Sonar reflectors/markers** (deployable, to mark passages you've explored)
- [ ] **Personal medication and nutrition** (calorie-dense food, water, any required medications; cold water increases caloric needs significantly)
- [ ] **Pain management** (mild analgesics; cold exposure causes joint and muscle pain even with good thermal protection)
- [ ] **Comfort object** (this sounds trivial; it is not. Bring something small and warm and familiar — a photograph, a talisman, a piece of fabric from home. In the silence and the dark, it will matter more than you expect.)

### Optional but Valuable

- [ ] **Sonar/LIDAR mapping suite** (for large-scale spatial documentation)
- [ ] **Water chemistry sampling kit** (if you have the expertise to use it; the thermal seeps and mineral plumes are scientifically significant)
- [ ] **Specimen collection containers** (sealed, pressure-rated; for small samples of bacterial mat or mineral crust — obtain any required multiversal environmental clearances first)
- [ ] **Bioluminescence measurement device** (photometer or spectrometer rated for extremely low light levels)
- [ ] **Journal and writing implements** (waterproof; for recording impressions in real time, which will differ significantly from post-visit recollections)

## VIII. Duration and Itinerary

There is no itinerary. The Garden does not have highlights. It does not have a best-of list. It has *districts*, and the transitions between them are as gradual as everything else here — a slow shift in density, a change in the geometry of the walls, a thinning or thickening of the vegetation. You cannot see the Garden in a day. You cannot see it in a week. You can see a *fraction* of it in a lifetime.

That said, here is a rough framework:

**First 2–3 hours: Adjustment.** You will be overwhelmed. The cold, the dark, the pressure, the vegetation, the silence — all of it at once. This is normal. Do not try to do anything meaningful during this period. Float. Breathe. Let your senses adjust. Let the panic (there may be panic) subside. Find a relatively clear space — an open corridor, a wider passage — and simply *be* for a while. Watch the bioluminescence. Listen. Let the strangeness become familiar, or at least become tolerable.

**Hours 3–8: Exploration.** Once you are calibrated, begin to move. Follow the architecture. Let the passages guide you. Pay attention to the transitions — the shifts in density, the changes in wall texture, the broadening and narrowing of corridors. These transitions mark district boundaries, and each district has a slightly different character: some are more heavily built, some more densely overgrown, some older, some newer (though "newer" here means "merely" a few thousand years old). Drift through them. Do not rush.

**Hours 8+: Deep experience.** If your life support and thermal protection allow it, this is when the Garden begins to reveal itself. Your eyes have fully adjusted. Your ears have learned the baseline sounds. Your skin has calibrated to the cold (or your suit has). You begin to notice details you missed before — the thumb-prints in the clay of a wall, the amber glow of mineralized cordage, the way the stalks in one district lean slightly in one direction, as if following a current that hasn't flowed in centuries. You begin to feel the *age* of the place — not as a number, but as a texture, a weight, a presence. This is the experience you came for. Stay as long as you can.

**The question of return visits:** You will want to come back. This is almost guaranteed. The Garden is too large, too complex, and too strange to take in during a single visit. Plan for multiple trips if possible. Each visit will reveal something new — a district you haven't seen, a pattern you didn't notice, a quality of light (or darkness) you couldn't have anticipated. The Garden rewards repetition. It rewards patience. It rewards the kind of attention that deepens over time.

## IX. Aftercare: Returning to the World

When you surface — if you surface; if "surface" means anything in the multiverse you're returning to — you will need time. More time than you expect.

You will be cold for hours after you leave the water. The cold of the Garden is not a surface phenomenon; it will have settled into your bones, into the marrow, into the parts of your body that don't warm up quickly. You may shiver uncontrollably for an hour. This is normal. Drink warm fluids. Wrap yourself in blankets. Let the warmth return slowly, the way everything in the Garden taught you to do.

The silence will follow you. Ordinary sounds — traffic, conversation, wind, birdsong — will feel overwhelming and slightly offensive for a while. Your ears, tuned to the frequencies of deep water and ancient stone, will need time to recalibrate. Give them that time. Stay quiet for a day if you can. Listen to the recording you made. Let the memory of the Garden's silence sit alongside the noise of the world you've returned to.

You may feel a persistent sense of *inadequacy* — the feeling that your experience was too brief, too shallow, too human to do the Garden justice. This is correct. It is also unavoidable, and it is not a failure. The Garden has been building itself for longer than you can comprehend; expecting a single visit to "capture" it is like expecting a single cupful to capture the ocean. Go back if you can. If you can't, sit with the inadequacy. It is not a wound. It is a reminder that the universe contains things that are larger than your capacity to hold them, and that this is not a problem to be solved but a fact to be honored.

And finally: you may find, in the days and weeks after your visit, that the Garden has not entirely let you go. You will catch yourself staring into dark corners. You will find deep silences comforting rather than uncomfortable. You will run your fingers along walls — ordinary walls, plaster and paint and drywall — and feel, for just a moment, the ghost of clay and calcite and the thumb-prints of builders who understood patience in a way you are only beginning to learn.

This is normal. This is the Garden, working in you the way it works on everything: slowly, gently, permanently.

Let it.
