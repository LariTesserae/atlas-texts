# Preparation Guide for the Basin of Throats
## A Practical Handbook for the Ordinary Multiverse Traveler

---

## I. BEFORE YOU EVEN THINK ABOUT GOING

### Mental Preparation

This is not a destination. This is an *immersion*. The single most important thing you can do before stepping onto that basalt rim is recalibrate your expectations about what it means to be in a place that is more alive than you are.

You have been to crowded cities. You have been to rainforests. You have been to places where the sound was loud and the air was thick and every surface had something growing on it. This is not like those places. Those places had edges — quiet corners, patches of bare wall, moments of silence between one sound and the next. The Basin of Throats does not have edges. There is no quiet corner. There is no bare surface. There is no moment when something is not touching, growing on, eating, vibrating against, or communicating with something else. The density of life here is not impressive. It is *totalizing*. It will reorganize your sense of what the word "alive" means, and you should be prepared for that to be disorienting in a way that goes beyond simple sensory overload.

Practice spending extended time in environments where you are not the loudest or most present thing. If you can find a multiverse accessible waterfall cave or a hot spring vent field, spend a full day there — not sightseeing, but *existing* in it. Let the noise become your background. Let the wet become your skin. The Basin of Throats requires a level of sensory surrender that most human beings are not accustomed to, and the ones who arrive unprepared tend to experience something that functions like panic but has no object — they are not afraid of anything specific, they are simply overwhelmed by the *completeness* of the environment.

Know that the darkness will last your entire visit. You will not see the sky. You will not see the sun. You will not see anything that is more than a few meters away unless it is glowing, and the things that glow are glowing because they want to eat what comes toward the light. Your eyes will never fully adjust because there is never enough light to adjust to. You will navigate primarily by sound, by touch, by thermal gradients, and by the faint bioluminescence of engineered wall symbionts in the city. If you have any latent anxiety about darkness, confront it before you go.

## II. EQUIPMENT

### Clothing and Personal Protection

**The Layer System — But Not the One You're Thinking Of**

Forget everything you know about dressing for cold weather. The cold here is not the enemy. The cold-wet combination is the enemy, and it is relentless, and it has no mercy, and it will find every gap in your protection with the patience of a thing that has been doing this for millennia.

**Base layer:** Full-body moisture-wicking synthetic, head to toe. Merino wool is acceptable but inferior — the humidity here exceeds anything wool is designed for, and wool will eventually saturate and become dead weight. You want a synthetic that moves moisture away from skin even when the ambient humidity is near one hundred percent. This does not exist in most baseline realities. Commission it or adapt. The critical thing is that your base layer must be able to keep your skin *reasonably* dry while you are standing in an environment where every surface is wet and the air itself is nearly saturated. Expect to sweat — the exertion of descending six hundred meters of rough terrain will generate heat — and then expect that sweat to have nowhere to go unless your clothing system helps it. Seams should be flat-locked or welded. Any raised seam will become a friction point when everything is wet, and friction plus wet plus cold equals raw skin within hours.

**Mid layer:** Insulation that retains warmth when damp. Down is useless here — it will absorb condensation from the outside and lose all loft within the first hour. Synthetic fill, or better yet, a fleece-type material that maintains insulating properties when soaked. You will be soaked. Accept this. The question is not whether you will be wet but whether being wet will kill you.

**Outer layer:** Waterproof-breathable is the ideal, but you should understand that "waterproof-breathable" is a compromise, and the Basin of Throats will test the limits of that compromise hard. The condensation here does not fall from above in discrete drops. It forms on every surface simultaneously, including the outside of your outer layer, and it runs, and it pools, and it seeps. You need a shell that can handle sustained exposure to running water on its exterior while still allowing some moisture vapor to escape from within. Fully waterproof non-breathable options — rubberized coatings, sealed PVC — will trap your sweat against your body and you will become hypothermic from the inside. This is the central clothing dilemma of the Basin, and there is no perfect solution. The locals have evolved for it. You have not. Budget for discomfort.

**Footwear:** This is arguably the most critical piece of equipment you will carry. The basalt is sharp — young, unweathered, with edges that will cut through standard hiking boot soles in hours. The surfaces are wet. The terrain ranges from near-horizontal ledges to near-vertical walls with engineered footholds. You need a boot that is:

- **Aggressively soled** with a compound that maintains grip on wet volcanic rock. Vibram MegaGrip is the minimum. Some multiverse-travel suppliers carry gecko-adhesive sole modifications that bond to wet rock through van der Waals forces — these are worth every unit of currency you will spend on them.
- **Reinforced in the sole plate** against puncture. The basalt here is vesicular — full of sharp-edged holes — and a thin-soled boot will transmit every edge directly into the bottom of your foot. You want a rigid or semi-rigid midsole.
- **Waterproof up to the ankle at minimum**, preferably higher. The braided rivers on the caldera floor are unavoidable, and the water is warm but carries dissolved minerals that will degrade most adhesives and leach through standard waterproof membranes. If you can get boots with a vulcanized rubber upper bonded to the sole — no glued seams — do so.
- **Dried in rotation.** Bring two pairs. One pair will always be drying while you wear the other. Drying here is difficult — the air is saturated — so you will need chemical desiccant packets or a heat source near enough to your sleeping area to drive moisture out of the leather or synthetic. If you bring only one pair, your feet will never be dry for the duration of your visit, and the skin maceration that results in a constantly wet environment with mineral-laden water is a genuine medical concern.

**Gloves:** Waterproof, insulated, with grip surfaces on the palms and fingers. You will be climbing. You will be bracing yourself against wet rock walls. You will be touching surfaces that are covered in biofilm — slick, alive, and possibly mildly irritating to bare skin depending on the species composition at your particular stop. Bring three pairs: one heavy for the rim and cold sections, one lighter for the city and warmer sections, and one liner pair for tasks requiring dexterity.

**Head and face:** A balaclava or full-face covering for the rim descent. The cold at the rim is not merely cold — it is cold-plus-wind-plus-condensation, and exposed facial skin will lose heat at a rate that becomes dangerous within minutes. In the city and lower, you can remove face covering, but you will want something to cover your ears. The sound pressure in the enclosed passages of the city is physically uncomfortable for human ears after extended exposure, and mild hearing damage is a realistic risk without protection (more on this below).

### Eye Protection

**This warrants its own section.**

You will be in near-total darkness. You will be navigating terrain where a misstep can mean a six-hundred-meter fall. You will be surrounded by creatures that use bioluminescence as a lure. You need to be able to see in the dark, but you also need to protect your eyes from physical hazards — dripping mineral water that may be irritating or mildly acidic, the occasional spore-like reproductive body drifting in the air, and the sharp basalt dust that gets into everything.

Bring:

- **Low-light optical enhancement.** Night-vision goggles are the obvious choice, but understand that standard image-intensification NVGs amplify available light, and in the Basin of Throats, there is almost no available light to amplify near the rim. Down in the city, the bioluminescent wall symbionts provide just enough glow for NVGs to work, but on the rim and in the transition zones, you will need either thermal imaging (which works beautifully here because everything is alive and warm against the cold rock) or active infrared illumination paired with IR-sensitive optics. Note: some of the fauna can see into the near-infrared. Using active IR illumination may attract attention from creatures that hunt by this spectrum. Thermal imaging is safer but more expensive.
- **Splash-proof goggles** for the descent and for travel near the waterfalls. Mineral-laden water in the eyes is not dangerous per se but is deeply unpleasant and can cause temporary blurred vision — a serious problem when you are traversing narrow ledges above a six-hundred-meter drop.
- **Anti-fog treatment** for any lens surface. The humidity is one hundred percent. Anything in front of your face will fog. Repeatedly. Bring anti-fog wipes or a surfactant solution and expect to reapply it constantly. Some travelers have reported that a thin film of dish soap applied to lens interiors and buffed to clear provides longer-lasting fog resistance than commercial anti-fog products, but your mileage will vary.

### Hearing Protection

The sound in the Basin of Throats is not a nuisance. It is an environmental hazard.

At the rim, the sound level is moderate — the stridulating isopod colonies produce a continuous vibration that measures roughly equivalent to a loud conversation, maybe 70-75 decibels, with strong infrasonic components that you feel more than hear. This is tolerable for extended periods, though the infrasound can cause mild nausea and disorientation in sensitive individuals.

In the city, the sound is a different matter entirely. The enclosed passages amplify and reflect the biological soundscape of the caldera, and the infrastructure adds its own contributions — the hum of energy systems, the rush of managed water, the click-whistle communication of the residents. In a main passage, the continuous sound level is comparable to standing next to a running industrial machine — 85 to 95 decibels — with intermittent peaks from the large echolocating flyers that enter the city's airspace that can reach 110 decibels or more. At these levels, permanent hearing damage begins within minutes of unprotected exposure.

Bring:

- **Custom-molded earplugs** with a flat attenuation profile. You want to reduce the overall volume without distorting the frequency balance, because the frequency content of the soundscape is how you will navigate. The echolocators' clicks tell you where the large flyers are. The aquatic pulse patterns tell you where the water is and how deep. The wall-crawler stridulations tell you where the ledges are and whether they are occupied. If you use earplugs that cut high frequencies preferentially, you will lose critical spatial information. Flat attenuation reduces everything equally and preserves your ability to parse the soundscape at a lower volume.
- **Electronic hearing protection** as a backup — earmuffs that attenuate above a set threshold while allowing lower-level sounds through. These are bulkier but protect against the echolocation peaks that can cause acute damage.
- **Spend time training your ears before you arrive.** Listen to recordings of dense biological soundscapes at high volume — tropical rainforests, coral reefs, bat caves — and practice identifying individual threads within the noise. In the Basin of Throats, your ability to extract signal from noise will determine how well you can navigate, how well you can avoid hazards, and how well you can communicate with the residents (if that is your goal). The sound is not background. It is *information*, and the residents use it as their primary medium of experience. Learning to read it, even at a human's crude level of resolution, is one of the most valuable preparations you can make.

### Respiratory Protection

The air at the rim is thin. You are at high altitude on what appears to be a high volcanic plateau, and the oxygen partial pressure is lower than sea level — perhaps equivalent to 3,000-4,000 meters elevation. If you are not acclimatized to altitude, you will feel it: headache, shortness of breath, reduced physical capacity. Acclimatize if you can. If you cannot, bring supplemental oxygen for the rim portion of your visit.

Below the rim, the air thickens somewhat — the caldera traps warmer, denser air — but it carries dissolved minerals and gases that are unfamiliar to human respiration. The sulfur compounds are the most obvious: hydrogen sulfide and sulfur dioxide from the geothermal vents, present at levels that produce a constant low-grade smell of rotten eggs and hot springs. These are not at concentrations that are immediately dangerous to human health at the city level (the residents' infrastructure appears to manage the worst of the geothermal outgassing), but prolonged exposure to even low-level hydrogen sulfide can cause headaches, eye irritation, and fatigue. Some travelers report a persistent metallic taste that may be related to the unusual mineral chemistry of the water and air.

Bring:

- **A half-face respirator with organic vapor/acid gas cartridges** for use near geothermal vents and in poorly ventilated passages. You will not need this constantly in the city — the city's ventilation appears adequate — but there will be places where the vent gases concentrate, particularly in lower passages and near the basin floor.
- **A simple dust mask or buff** for the rim descent. The basalt dust is sharp and irritating to the lungs. Not dangerous in the way that silica dust is dangerous, but deeply unpleasant and likely to cause coughing and throat irritation.
- **If you have asthma or reactive airway disease,** bring your medications. The combination of cold air, high humidity, mineral aerosols, and unfamiliar biological compounds is a recipe for bronchospasm in sensitive individuals. This is not theoretical. Multiple travelers with mild asthma have reported significant exacerbation in this environment.

## III. CHEMICAL AND BIOLOGICAL HAZARDS

### The Water

You will touch the water. You will breathe the water. You will, at some point, probably taste the water — either deliberately or because a drip finds your lips. The water in the Basin of Throats is not water as you know it. It carries dissolved minerals including iron, sulfur, and what the description identifies as "something stranger — a faintly electric, metallic tang that suggests the chemistry here includes elements or compounds that don't feature in standard geochemistry textbooks."

This is not a place where you drink the local water. Bring your own water supply. Bring more than you think you need — the humidity paradoxically increases dehydration because your sweat evaporates inefficiently in saturated air, leading your body to produce more of it without the cooling benefit, and the cold suppresses your thirst response so you don't notice you're losing moisture. Bring a water purification system rated for heavy metals and unusual dissolved solids, and test the local water against it before committing to using it as a backup source. If your purification system cannot remove whatever "faintly electric" means in chemical terms, do not drink the water.

For skin contact: brief exposure is probably fine. The residents seem to live in constant contact with this water and appear healthy. But "probably fine" for a species that evolved here may mean "slowly destructive" for a human whose skin has never encountered these particular dissolved compounds. Bring a barrier cream for exposed skin — something thick and occlusive that will resist mineral-laden water for at least an hour before needing reapplication. Apply it before every excursion from whatever shelter you establish. Pay particular attention to any cuts, abrasions, or open wounds — the mineral content of the water may cause significant irritation or delayed healing in broken skin.

### The Biofilm

Every wet surface is coated in a thin, slick film of chemosynthetic microorganisms. You will touch it. You will slip on it. You will get it on your hands and face and clothing. It is the base of the food web, and it is everywhere, and it is unavoidable.

The biofilm is almost certainly not pathogenic to humans — it evolved in a completely alien biochemical context, and the probability of cross-species pathogenicity is low. "Low" is not "zero." If this caldera's life shares any fundamental biochemistry with your own (amino acids, nucleic acids, lipid membranes), there is a nonzero chance that something in that biofilm could colonize human tissue. The risk is lowest on intact skin and highest on mucous membranes and open wounds.

Bring:

- **Antimicrobial wound care supplies** — iodine-based antiseptic, antibiotic ointment, wound closure strips. Any break in your skin should be cleaned, treated, and sealed immediately.
- **Eye wash** — sterile saline in small squeeze bottles. If biofilm-laden water splashes into your eyes, you want to flush immediately.
- **Nasal saline rinse** for the end of each day. The air carries fine biofilm particles and mineral aerosols that will deposit in your nasal passages. Rinsing reduces the duration of exposure.
- **A thorough wash-down protocol** at the end of each day, using your own water supply. The residents may bathe in the local water. You should not, or at least not until you have spent enough time here to understand whether your biology is compatible with theirs.

### The Fauna

Nothing in the description suggests that the fauna are overtly predatory toward humans. This does not mean they are safe. It means you are unfamiliar, and unfamiliar things in dense ecosystems are often ignored until they are interacted with, at which point the interaction can go badly for the smaller party.

Specific concerns:

**The wall anemone analogs.** They glow. They lure. They digest slowly. They are not targeting you specifically — their lures are calibrated for the smaller flying creatures of the caldera — but if you brush against one, the tendrils will close around whatever they contact, and the mineral-crusted digestive tissue will begin to work on your skin. This is not fast. This is not acutely dangerous. This is the kind of slow chemical burn that you might not notice in the dark until significant tissue damage has occurred. Stay aware of where your hands and body are in relation to glowing surfaces. If you are traveling with illumination, be aware that your light source may attract creatures that are attracted to light, and those creatures may be near the anemone analogs. You do not want to be standing where the food goes.

**The large flying creatures.** These are the size of hang gliders. They echolocate. They filter-feed on smaller airborne organisms. They are probably not interested in eating you. But they navigate by sound, and if you are loud — and in this environment, you are, relative to your size — they may approach to investigate. A five-meter wingspan producing echolocation clicks at 110 decibels at close range is not dangerous in itself, but the suddenness of a large dark shape emerging from the ceiling darkness and screaming at you has caused more than one traveler to step backward off a ledge. Stay near walls. Stay on the inner side of paths. If you hear booming echolocation overhead, stop moving and let it pass.

**The aquatic predators.** The crocodile-cephalopod creatures in the rivers are four meters long, have six not-quite-hands, and move silently. They are the apex predators of the aquatic system. They are probably not interested in you as prey — you are not part of their food web, you smell wrong, and you are too large for the smaller ones and too weird for the larger ones. "Probably" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. Do not wade into deep water on the caldera floor alone. Do not swim in the lake. If you must cross a braided river, do so quickly, in a group if possible, and make noise — the residents apparently navigate these waters safely, and following their lead is wise.

**The smaller creatures.** There are many of them. Some are the size of insects, some are the size of your forearm, and they are everywhere — in the walls, in the water, in the air, under your feet. Many of them can bite, sting, or secrete defensive compounds. The probability that any given alien arthropod analog has venom that is pharmacologically active in human tissue is low, but in a caldera containing what may be millions of species, low probability multiplied by high numbers of encounters yields a meaningful risk. Long sleeves, long pants, gloves, and a head covering reduce your exposed skin surface. A general-purpose antihistamine (diphenhydramine or equivalent) in your kit can manage minor allergic-type reactions to defensive secretions. An epinephrine auto-injector can manage major ones.

## IV. NAVIGATION AND ORIENTATION

### Getting Down the Rim

The descent from the rim to the city level is six hundred meters of near-vertical terrain on wet, sharp basalt with engineered path sections and natural ledges. In the dark. In the cold. In the sound.

This is the most physically dangerous part of your visit, and it deserves the most preparation.

**Route-finding:** The engineered path exists — it is mentioned as having optimized micro-texture for wet grip — but it will not be marked in any way that is obvious to human senses. The residents navigate by sound, by echolocation, by thermal sensing, and by whatever the "faintly electric, metallic tang" of the local chemistry tells their specialized sensory organs. You have none of these advantages. You will need to rely on thermal imaging (to see the path against the colder surrounding rock), on careful tactile exploration (the path's surface texture is deliberately different from natural basalt — smoother, with directional grooves that indicate the intended walking direction), and on the thermal gradients that your skin can detect (the path tends to follow warmer routes, hugging the caldera wall where geothermal heat bleeds through).

**Climbing gear:** A harness, dynamic rope, and a set of protection devices suitable for volcanic rock. The basalt is hard but brittle — traditional cams and nuts may crack the vesicular rock rather than holding. Pitons may be more reliable, but driving pitons into the caldera wall will attract attention from the wall-dwelling fauna and potentially from the residents, who may view the damage as vandalism. If at all possible, clip into existing anchor points — the engineered path will have them, embedded in the wall material, at intervals. They will not look like climbing anchors. They will look like small smooth bumps or ridges on the wall surface. Touch them. They will feel different from the surrounding rock — warmer, slightly yielding, like the building material. These are your anchors.

**Headlamp and backup illumination:** You need light for the descent. Thermal imaging handles the big picture — where the path goes, where the wall is, where large creatures are — but for foot placement, for checking handholds, for reading the terrain texture under your feet, you need visible light. A headlamp with a red-light mode is ideal — red light preserves whatever night adaptation you have and is less disturbing to the fauna (many animals in dark environments are less reactive to red light than to white). Bring at least two light sources. If your primary fails on the rim wall, a backup is the difference between "inconvenient" and "fatal."

**Time and fitness:** Budget a full day for the descent. Not because the distance is that great — six hundred meters is not far on flat ground — but because the terrain is slow, the conditions are exhausting, and the psychological toll of descending into darkness on a wet cliff face surrounded by alien sounds should not be underestimated. You need to be in good cardiovascular condition and comfortable with exposure. If you are not a climber, practice on indoor walls before this trip. If you are not comfortable in the dark, practice in caves. If you are not comfortable being wet and cold simultaneously, practice in winter rain. All three at once is the minimum experience level.

### Getting Around the City

The city is three-dimensional in a way that human cities are not. There is no grid. There are no streets. There are passages that branch, merge, rise, fall, dead-end, and occasionally open into larger chambers that may be public spaces, markets, or something else entirely. The architecture does not follow Euclidean logic — there are curves and junctions that produce spatial disorientation, places where "left" and "right" become ambiguous, passages that seem to loop back on themselves without actually doing so.

Navigation aids:

- **A compass is useless** if the caldera's geology includes significant ferrous mineral deposits, which the iron-rich water strongly suggests. Your compass may spin freely or point at the nearest magnetically anomalous rock face.
- **GPS is obviously nonfunctional** in a location that may not share your home universe's satellite constellation, and even if it did, the caldera walls would block the signal.
- **Breadcrumbs, string, chalk marks** — the low-tech solutions. Chalk will wash off in the wet. String is better, but the resident fauna may interact with it (carry it away, eat it, use it as building material). A bioluminescent marker that you can paint on walls and that persists for hours would be ideal. Some multiverse suppliers carry UV-fluorescent paint that is invisible in ambient light but glows under a UV flashlight. This may work here, since there is no ambient UV to mask it, but test whether the local fauna can see UV before relying on it.
- **Learn the sound map.** The city has a soundscape signature — different areas sound different based on their geometry, their proximity to water, their fauna occupancy, and their infrastructure. A main passage sounds different from a residential alcove sounds different from a water-management junction. If you spend enough time listening, you will begin to navigate by sound the way the residents do. This takes weeks, not hours, but even a rudimentary sound-map will help you avoid getting hopelessly lost.

## V. COMMUNICATION WITH THE RESIDENTS

The residents are sapient. They communicate by clicking, whistling, and using frequencies and patterns that human vocal cords and ears cannot produce or fully perceive. They are not hostile — the city's open architecture and the apparent tolerance of your presence suggest that visitors from outside are not unknown, even if they are uncommon. But communication will be difficult, and you should approach it with patience and humility.

**Do not assume your communication methods will translate.** Written language requires light to read. Gestures require visual attention. The residents may not prioritize visual information — in a lightless environment, vision is a minor sense. Their communication is acoustic and possibly tactile (the semi-organic building materials may transmit vibration patterns that serve as a secondary communication channel). Approach communication as a puzzle to be solved collaboratively, not as a barrier to be overcome with louder voice.

**Bring a translation device** if your multiverse has them. Standard universal translator units may struggle with acoustic signals that include frequencies above and below human hearing, so look for units that capture full-spectrum audio and can represent ultrasonic and infrasonic content in a form you can perceive — haptic feedback, visual display, frequency-shifted audio playback.

**Be patient.** The residents move slowly. Their population dynamics are slow. Their construction is slow. Their communication, once you can perceive it, will likely be slow by human standards — deliberate, layered, carrying meaning in frequencies and patterns that take time to parse. Do not rush them. Do not interpret slowness as stupidity. In an environment this stable and this dense, patience is the supreme survival trait, and they have it in abundance.

**Observe before engaging.** Watch how the residents interact with each other. Watch how they navigate. Watch how they manage the fauna (or how the fauna manages them — the distinction may not be clear). The city has been here for centuries. You have been here for hours. Learn before you ask.

## VI. PSYCHOLOGICAL PREPARATION

### The Darkness

You will not see the sky for the duration of your visit. You will not see the sun, the moon, or the stars. You will not see the horizon. You will not see farther than a few meters unless you use thermal imaging, and even then, the resolution is poor compared to the richness of visual experience that human beings evolved to expect. The darkness of the Basin of Throats is not an inconvenience. It is a fundamental reorganization of your relationship with space, distance, and presence.

Some travelers find this liberating — a relief from the tyranny of visual dominance, an invitation to develop other senses. Most find it profoundly disorienting in the first twenty-four to forty-eight hours. The disorientation is not just spatial. It is temporal. Without visual cues from the sky, your circadian rhythm will drift. You will lose track of day and night. You will sleep when you are tired and wake when you are rested, and the intervals will not align with any twenty-four-hour cycle. This is normal. Bring a clock. Set alarms. Force yourself into a schedule if you need to be functional at specific times.

**Sensory deprivation effects:** Prolonged darkness combined with constant noise and the absence of visual variety can produce symptoms that resemble mild sensory deprivation — restlessness, difficulty concentrating, mild paranoia, vivid hypnagogic imagery. These are manageable if you expect them. They are alarming if you don't. Bring something visual to look at — a book, a screen, photographs of familiar places — and use it deliberately, for a set period each day, to give your visual cortex something to process. This sounds trivial. It is not. The human brain expects visual input, and when it doesn't get it, it starts manufacturing its own. In a dark caldera full of things that glow and move and make strange sounds, internally generated visual experiences can become indistinguishable from externally generated ones, and that is a path you do not want to walk.

### The Density of Life

You are never alone in the Basin of Throats. You are never in a place where nothing else is. Every surface, every volume of air, every liter of water is shared with organisms that are moving, eating, communicating, dying, and being eaten. This is not the background hum of a city or the ambient presence of a forest. This is *saturation*. The life here has filled every niche so completely that there is no empty niche for you to occupy. You are an intruder into a system that has no room for intruders, and the system will make you feel this — not through hostility, but through the simple, overwhelming fact of its completeness.

Some travelers find this claustrophobic in a way that has nothing to do with physical space. The passages of the city are large. The caldera is eight kilometers across. There is room to move. But there is no room to be *alone*, and if you are the kind of person who needs solitude to function, the Basin of Throats will grind you down. Plan for this. Identify a space — a small chamber, a dead-end passage, a ledge on the rim — where the life density is lower, and retreat there periodically. It will still not be empty. The isopods will be clicking. The water will be dripping. The walls will be humming. But it will be less, and less is sometimes enough.

### The Sound

The sound does not stop. It is the first thing you hear when you arrive and the last thing you hear when you leave, and in between there is no silence, not even close, not even for a moment. The sound varies in composition and intensity — it is louder in the city, quieter on the rim, different in character near the water than near the walls — but it is always present, always layered, always demanding some portion of your attention.

You will habituate to it partially. Your brain will learn to filter the constant components — the wall-crawler stridulation, the low-frequency aquatic drone, the hum of the infrastructure — into background, the way you habituate to traffic noise in a city. But the variable components — the echolocation booms, the predatory strikes, the resident communication, the occasional structural groan of the organic building material shifting under load — will continue to break through, and each breakthrough will cost you a small amount of cognitive energy. Over days, this adds up. Fatigue, irritability, and difficulty concentrating are the expected results.

Hearing protection helps. Scheduled quiet time (in your sealed shelter, with earplugs and noise-canceling electronics, in as close to silence as you can achieve) helps more. Bring white noise or a familiar sound environment — recordings of your home, music, anything that your brain can use as an anchor to something other than the Basin's soundscape. Play it through bone-conduction headphones if your ears are already plugged with hearing protection.

## VII. SHELTER AND SLEEP

You need a place to sleep, and it needs to be sealed.

The city offers alcoves and chambers that you may be able to occupy with the residents' permission (however that is communicated). These will be warmer, drier, and safer than anything you can construct on the rim or the walls. But they will be loud, they will be permeated by the soundscape and the humidity, and they will be architecturally unfamiliar — curved walls, floors that slope in unexpected directions, surfaces that are slightly warm and slightly yielding and may occasionally shift under your weight as the building material breathes.

If you bring your own shelter — a tent or bivouac — site it in the city rather than on the rim or the open walls. The temperature is more manageable, the water can be directed away from your shelter using the city's existing drainage infrastructure, and the presence of the residents provides a measure of protection from the larger fauna, which appear to coexist with the city's inhabitants in a managed equilibrium.

Your shelter should be:

- **Waterproof from the outside** — the condensation will find any seam, any vent, any zipper.
- **Breathable from the inside** — you will generate moisture through respiration and sweat, and a non-breathable shelter will become a humid box in which your sleeping bag soaks from the inside.
- **Equipped with a ground sheet rated for sharp basalt** — if you pitch on bare rock, the vesicular edges will puncture a standard ground sheet within the first night.
- **Dark inside** — not because you need darkness to sleep (you will have darkness in excess) but because light leaking from your shelter will attract the smaller fauna, which will accumulate on and around your shelter and make it very difficult to exit in the morning without disturbing them.
- **Anchored securely** — the updrafts from the caldera floor can be strong, and the passages of the city channel wind unpredictably. A shelter that comes loose in the night and tumbles into the caldera is a dead traveler.

**Sleeping system:** Bring a sleeping bag rated to at least -25°C for the rim, or 0°C for the city, with a waterproof shell. A sleeping pad is essential — the basalt is hard, cold, and wet, and even the building material, while slightly yielding, will conduct heat away from your body faster than you can produce it if you lie directly on it. An inflatable pad with a high R-value is ideal. Bring a repair kit — a puncture in your sleeping pad in this environment is an emergency, not an inconvenience.

## VIII. FOOD AND WATER

### Water

Bring your own. As discussed above, the local water is chemically unusual and should not be consumed without thorough testing and purification. Budget at minimum four liters per person per day — more if you are exerting yourself on the descent or climbing. Carry it in containers that are sealed against the humidity (a hydration bladder with a bite valve will accumulate condensation on the outside and drip constantly, which is a nuisance). Rigid bottles with screw caps are more practical here.

If you must use local water as a backup, collect it from condensation on the coldest surfaces near the rim, where the mineral load is lowest (the water condenses from vapor and has less dissolved content than the surface runoff or the rivers). Filter through at least a 0.2-micron filter to remove particulates and biofilm, then treat with chemical purification (chlorine dioxide or equivalent). Test the taste cautiously. If the "faintly electric, metallic tang" persists through filtration and chemical treatment, the dissolved compounds are too small for your filter to remove and may be too novel for your chemicals to neutralize. Do not drink it.

### Food

Bring food that is sealed against humidity, does not require cooking (open flames in an enclosed caldera with geothermal gas vents are a ventilation nightmare at best and an explosion risk at worst), and provides enough caloric density to fuel the physical demands of descent, climbing, and navigation in heavy gear.

Recommended:

- **High-calorie bars and dense carbohydrate sources.** You will be burning more calories than you expect — the cold increases metabolic demand, the altitude increases respiratory effort, and the constant low-level stress of the environment increases cortisol-driven caloric expenditure.
- **Electrolyte supplements.** You will be sweating in humidity, which means your sweat does not evaporate efficiently, which means you lose fluid without the cooling benefit, which means your body produces more sweat, which means you lose more electrolytes. Bring salt tablets or electrolyte powder and use them.
- **Sealed, shelf-stable proteins.** Jerky, sealed pouches of fish or meat, nut butters. Do not bring anything with a strong smell that is not sealed — the fauna here navigates heavily by chemosensation, and an unsealed food package will attract visitors.
- **Do not eat the local fauna.** The biochemistry is probably incompatible with your digestive system in ways that range from "no nutritional value" to "gastrointestinal emergency." The residents eat the local fauna. You are not the residents.

**Food waste:** Pack out everything. The Basin of Throats has no decomposers that evolved to process your food waste, and leaving organic material from another universe's biochemistry in this ecosystem is an ecological contamination risk that no responsible traveler should accept. Bring sealable waste bags. Store waste in your sealed shelter. Pack it out when you leave.

## IX. MEDICAL KIT

Beyond the items mentioned above (antihistamines, epinephrine, wound care, antiseptic, eye wash, respiratory protection), bring:

- **Broad-spectrum antibiotics.** Not for prophylactic use, but for the possibility that the local biofilm, despite low probability of pathogenicity, finds your tissue hospitable. Any infected wound in this environment — wet, humid, warm, coated in alien microorganisms — will progress faster than you expect.
- **Anti-inflammatory medication** — ibuprofen or equivalent. The cold, the exertion, and the altitude will produce joint pain and headaches. Manage them.
- **Ear drops** — isopropyl alcohol-based, for drying ears after the inevitable water exposure. Swimmer's ear in a caldera with no medical infrastructure is miserable.
- **Skin barrier cream and anti-chafing balm.** Wet skin plus wet clothing plus physical exertion equals chafing in places you didn't know could chafe. Apply proactively.
- **Blister care.** Your feet will be wet. Blisters are almost inevitable. Bring moleskin, hydrocolloid blister pads, and tape.
- **Mental health support.** Bring what you need — medication, recordings, journal, photographs, familiar objects. The Basin of Throats is beautiful in its way, but it is also relentless, and relentless environments test mental health as surely as physical health.

## X. WHAT TO LEAVE BEHIND

**Do not bring:**

- **Anything that generates significant electromagnetic radiation.** The residents' energy systems operate at frequencies you cannot identify, and your electronics may interfere with them — or vice versa. If your devices emit strong RF signals, they may attract attention, disrupt the fauna, or cause feedback with the infrastructure. Bring devices that are either shielded or low-power.
- **Anything that makes bright visible light as its primary function.** High-lumen flashlights, flares, signal mirrors — these will be blindingly conspicuous in the darkness and will attract every phototactic organism in visual range. Use red light. Use low light. Use the minimum.
- **Drones or autonomous flying devices.** The caldera airspace is occupied by creatures with wingspans up to five meters. Your drone is smaller, louder (in a frequency range that the fauna may find alarming), and made of materials that the local organisms have never encountered. It will be investigated, possibly attacked, and if it crashes into a resident or a wall-anemone colony, you will have caused damage that you cannot repair.
- **Weapons.** Nothing here has attacked you. Nothing here is likely to attack you unless you provoke it. Bringing weapons signals an intent to fight, and in a society that communicates by sound and vibration, the specific frequency signature of a weapon being drawn or activated may be interpretable in ways you do not intend. If you must bring something for self-defense, bring a personal alarm or noise-making device — a weapon in a world of sound-users should be sound, not steel.

## XI. WHAT TO BRING THAT IS NOT OBVIOUS

- **A journal with waterproof paper and a pencil** (not a pen — ink runs in humidity). Writing by hand forces you to process your experiences at a pace that screen-based documentation does not. In an environment this alien, processing pace matters.
- **A small amount of a familiar scent** — a sachet of dried flowers, a vial of essential oil, a piece of cloth that smells like home. Your sense of smell will be overwhelmed by sulfur, iron, mineral water, and biological musk. A moment of olfactory familiarity can be profoundly grounding.
- **A tuning fork or small acoustic instrument.** Not for entertainment — for communication. The residents operate in sound. A tuning fork produces a pure tone that can be varied by striking it against different surfaces. It is a crude tool, but it is a *start* — a way of introducing a controlled signal into the soundscape and observing how the residents respond.
- **A photograph of the sky.** You will miss it more than you expect.

## XII. DEPARTURE PLANNING

Know how you are getting out before you go in. The descent is slow and dangerous, and the ascent will be slower and more dangerous — you will be fatigued, your gear will be wet and possibly degraded, and you will be ascending through fauna that has had days to become accustomed to your presence and may now be closer to your route than it was on the way down. If you used rope and protection on the descent, your fixed lines will have been exposed to the environment — mineral-laden water, temperature fluctuations, the attentions of curious fauna — and should be inspected before you weight them on the ascent.

The rim is the exit, and it is also the transition from the Basin's world back to whatever world you came from. Give yourself time at the rim. Sit. Listen to the isopods buzz. Feel the cold dry air replace the warm wet air in your lungs. The transition from saturation to emptiness, from darkness to the possibility of light, from the totalizing aliveness of the caldera to the comparative sterility of the outside world, is jarring in a way that the transition *into* the caldera was not. You are leaving a world that had no empty spaces. You are re-entering a world that is mostly empty space. That shift deserves a moment.

---

## FINAL NOTE

The Basin of Throats is not dangerous in the way that a battlefield is dangerous, or a plague zone, or a collapsing reality. It is dangerous in the way that the deep ocean is dangerous — not because it wants to kill you, but because it is *complete without you*, and your presence in it is an intrusion into a system that has no mechanism for accommodating you. Every preparation listed above is a way of making yourself slightly less foreign, slightly less fragile, slightly less *wrong* for this place.

You will still be wrong for this place. The residents will know it. The fauna will know it. The water will know it, running over your strange skin with its strange oils and its strange temperature. The darkness will know it, tasting your infrared signature against the warm rock.

Go anyway. It is worth it. Nothing in your experience has prepared you for the sound of a world this alive, and the sound is only the beginning.
