# Preparation Guide for Visiting The Throat

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before you pack a single item, sit with this: you are preparing to enter a room that does not want to exist yet. It is a cavity inside a mountain that is still being born — still pushing upward through the crust, still cooling from its own molten origin. The rock has barely decided to be rock. You will be standing inside raw geology, inside a process, inside a place where the walls are still sweating out the chemistry of their own formation and the water arrives not as weather but as *force*.

This is not a destination. It is an event you happen to be inside of.

You will be wet. You will be deafened. You will be compressed. You may not be able to breathe. And you will be standing in something so new that the universe hasn't finished making it yet.

## THE SENSORY REALITY — What Your Body Will Experience

### Sound: The Dominant Threat

The roar of water entering The Throat is not background noise. It is the *primary condition* of the place. Hundreds of needle-thin jets of meltwater striking a confined pool in a stone cavity, at pressures generated by an entire mountain's worth of drainage above — this produces sound at levels that are not merely loud but physically injurious.

You need to understand the scale. The description says you could not hear a detonation from three feet. That means the ambient sound level is well above 150 decibels — possibly approaching 160 or higher at peak surges. For reference, a jet engine at close range is approximately 140 dB. A gunshot is approximately 165 dB. At these levels, sound is not experienced through the ears alone. It is felt as pressure in the chest, behind the eyes, in the sinuses. It distorts vision. It makes it difficult to maintain balance. Extended exposure at even 140 dB can cause permanent hearing damage in seconds. At the levels described, your eardrums will rupture almost immediately without protection. At sustained exposure, the vibroacoustic pressure can cause organ damage — pulmonary hemorrhage, cardiac disruption.

The sound is described as "a physical compression of the skull, a pressure that lives behind the eyes." This is not metaphor. This is infrasound and extreme-volume sound acting on the fluid in your cranial cavity.

**What to bring:**

- **Electronic hearing protection rated for impulse noise above 165 dB NRR.** Not simple earplugs — you need over-ear electronic muffs with active noise cancellation layered over custom-molded earplugs. The electronic system should be able to compress sudden peaks while still allowing you to perceive some spatial awareness. Standard foam earplugs (NRR 33) combined with electronic over-ear muffs (NRR 25-30) stacked together give you approximately 40-45 dB of real-world reduction, which *may* bring the peak levels into survivable range, though still painfully loud.
- **A full-face helmet with integrated acoustic dampening** if you can obtain one. The pressure behind the eyes suggests that protecting only the ears is insufficient — you want mass and damping between the sound and your skull. A motorcycle or tactical helmet with a sealed, padded interior will help.
- **Vibration-dampening insoles and gloves.** The sound transmits through the water and through the rock. Your whole body will be a resonating surface. Anything that decouples your skeleton from the ground helps.
- **Bring backup protection.** If your primary hearing protection fails — and in this environment, with the water, the pressure cycling, and the physical impacts, mechanical failure is likely — you need a secondary system you can deploy in under five seconds. Practice putting it on blind, underwater, while being shoved.

**Mental preparation:** Even with full protection, the sound will be overwhelming. It will be the only thing in the world. You will not be able to think in words. You will not be able to plan. If you need to make decisions inside this chamber, they will need to be made through muscle memory and rehearsed instinct, not through reasoning. The sound swallows cognition. Prepare accordingly — decide everything before you enter.

### Water: Not a Flood, a Pulse

The water in The Throat does not behave like water you have encountered before. It enters the chamber as a wall of spray — hundreds of jets from the ceiling — and pools to a depth that fluctuates between knee-deep and waist-deep on a cycle of minutes, driven by pressure changes in the rock above. These cycles are not predictable. The water rises and falls fast enough that you may be standing in ankle-deep water one moment and thigh-deep the next.

When the water rises, the chamber *shrinks*. The air gap above the water compresses. The sound intensifies because the spray now has less volume to dissipate into. This is a feedback loop: more water, less air, more noise, more pressure, more disorientation.

The water itself is meltwater from cloud-ice at the summit, which means it is *extremely cold* — likely near freezing. It has also been in contact with freshly formed basalt, sulfur deposits, and mineral condensations. The chemistry is unknown but likely includes dissolved sulfur compounds (hydrogen sulfide, sulfur dioxide), elevated silica, calcium carbonate, iron in various oxidation states, and trace heavy metals leached from still-cooling rock. The pH is likely acidic, possibly severely so.

**What to bring:**

- **A full drysuit, not a wetsuit.** You need total waterproofing with thermal insulation beneath. A drysuit designed for cold-water diving or swift-water rescue, with latex or neoprene gaskets at the neck, wrists, and ankles. Underneath, wear a base layer of merino wool or synthetic thermal insulation — you will be in near-freezing water that cycles up to your waist, repeatedly, for the duration of your visit.
- **Waterproof boots with aggressive tread and ankle support.** The floor is mineral accretion — chalky, slippery when wet (which is always), layered over raw basalt that is uneven and bucking with seismic tremor. You need boots that grip wet mineral surfaces and protect your ankles from rolling on unseen irregularities. Consider boots with a steel or composite toe — falling rock is a constant possibility.
- **Thermal protection for your hands.** Waterproof insulated gloves, but thin enough that you can still manipulate small objects (you may want to examine the cairn, the mineral deposits, the microorganism rosettes). Bring two pairs: one for warmth, one for dexterity, and be prepared to swap.
- **A wetsuit hood or balaclava** under your helmet. Cold water on the scalp accelerates hypothermia dramatically, and the spray from the ceiling will hit your head continuously.
- **Chemical splash protection for your face and eyes.** The water is mineral-laden and likely acidic. Even with a helmet, you will get wet. Sealed goggles — not glasses, goggles with a silicone gasket — rated for chemical splash. Anti-fog treatment is essential because the air will be saturated and the temperature differential between the water and your face will cause constant fogging.

**Mental preparation:** The water's behavior will feel hostile. Not because it is — it is simply doing what water does in a newly forming cavity — but because the unpredictability of the surges will keep your body in a constant state of startle. You will brace, and the water will drop. You will relax, and it will slam into your thighs. This cycle will not stop. It will erode your composure the way it is eroding the floor. Accept it before you enter: *you will not be able to predict the water, and you will be standing in it the entire time.*

### Altitude and Air

The Throat is described as being high on a peak that is still rising, at an altitude where the air "is barely there at all." This suggests extreme elevation — possibly above 7,000 meters, where the partial pressure of oxygen is less than half that at sea level. At these altitudes, without acclimatization, you will experience:

- Rapid onset hypoxia (confusion, impaired judgment, loss of coordination within minutes)
- Altitude headache
- Possible pulmonary or cerebral edema (fluid in the lungs or brain — potentially fatal)
- Extreme exertion even from simple movements due to oxygen debt

Additionally, the chamber contains sulfur mineral condensation on the walls. At these temperatures and in this still-forming volcanic environment, the off-gassing likely includes hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) and sulfur dioxide (SO₂). H₂S is lethal at surprisingly low concentrations — above 100 ppm it causes rapid unconsciousness, and above 500 ppm it kills within minutes. In a confined stone cavity with poor ventilation, these gases can pool, especially in the lower portions of the chamber when the water level drops and creates a low-lying air pocket.

**What to bring:**

- **A supplemental oxygen system with a full-face mask or sealed respirator.** Not a simple cannula — you need a system that delivers oxygen-enriched air at a rate sufficient to compensate for altitude hypoxia and physical exertion (you will be fighting the water, maintaining balance, and managing panic). A lightweight oxygen concentrator or compressed oxygen cylinder with a demand valve, connected to a sealed full-face mask.
- **A gas monitor — specifically calibrated for H₂S and SO₂.** Clip it to your chest where you can see it. If H₂S readings rise above 10 ppm, leave. Immediately. Do not investigate. Do not take one more look at the cairn. Hydrogen sulfide deadens your sense of smell at dangerous concentrations — you will stop being able to smell it right when it becomes lethal. Trust the monitor, not your nose.
- **Acclimatization.** If your multiverse transit allows it, spend at least 48-72 hours at progressively higher altitudes before entering The Throat. Rapid ascent to extreme altitude in a state of physical stress (cold, wet, deafened, terrified) is a recipe for acute mountain sickness progressing to HACE (high-altitude cerebral edema) — your brain swelling inside your skull while you are standing in a roaring flood inside a mountain. This will kill you in a way that is silent, insidious, and disguised as confusion.

**Mental preparation:** Your thinking will be impaired. At extreme altitude, with depleted oxygen, your brain will not work the way it usually does. Complex decisions will become difficult. Spatial reasoning will degrade. You may not realize this is happening. Build redundancy into every plan. Assume you will be operating at reduced cognitive capacity and design your actions accordingly — simple, rehearsed, sequential.

### Heat and Cold: Simultaneously

This is one of the counterintuitive aspects of The Throat. The basalt is still hot from its formation — "still holds heat in its deeper seams." The rock surfaces may be warm or actively hot to the touch, depending on how close you are to a thermally active seam. Simultaneously, the meltwater is near-freezing. The air temperature at extreme altitude is far below zero.

You will be experiencing cold water hitting hot rock. Steam may form in patches. The thermal interface between your body, the water, and the rock will be chaotic.

**What to bring:**

- **A non-contact infrared thermometer.** Point it at the rock before touching anything. Some surfaces may be hot enough to burn through your gloves. Some seams may be cool. The variation will be unpredictable.
- **Thermal layering that handles both extremes.** Your drysuit provides waterproofing and wind protection. Underneath, use a layered system: moisture-wicking base layer, insulating mid-layer (fleece or down — synthetic is better here because it insulates even when damp), and the drysuit as shell. This manages the cold water but also provides some buffer if you brush against heated rock.
- **Heat-resistant gloves for rock contact.** If you intend to examine the walls, the microorganism rosettes, or the cairn, you need gloves rated for contact with hot surfaces. Some aramid-fiber (Kevlar) work gloves under your waterproof layer will provide this.

## NAVIGATION AND ACCESS

### Getting There

"The nearest other sign of habitation is kilometers below the cloud line." This means you are not walking to The Throat from a parking lot. You are approaching an unclimbed or barely-explored peak at extreme altitude, through a cloud-ice zone, to find a pocket cave in the raw flank of a mountain that is still geologically forming.

The mountain is rising. It is volcanically active. The terrain is loose basalt — sharp, fractured, unstable — overlaid with glassy veins where fractures cooled too fast for crystal structure. This rock cuts. It does not erode into rounded footholds; it stays jagged and blade-edged.

**What to bring:**

- **Full alpine climbing gear:** ice axes, crampons (for the cloud-ice zone), helmet (which you're already bringing for acoustic protection), harness, dynamic rope, and protection for placing in volcanic rock — cams and nuts designed for irregular, non-uniform fissures. The rock here is new and unnamed; no one has rated its climbing quality. Assume it is unreliable. Test every hold.
- **Route-finding equipment.** The Throat is described as a cavity "scooped into the raw flank of a peak." Finding a single bedroom-sized cavity in the side of a mountain in a cloud-ice zone with near-zero visibility will be extremely difficult. Ground-penetrating radar, thermal imaging (the chamber may be slightly warmer than surrounding rock due to the hot basalt and water), or acoustic detection (the sound of the water jets may be transmitted through the rock as faint vibration) may be necessary. If you have access to magical or technological means of detection, bring them.
- **Approach time.** Plan for a multi-day approach through hostile terrain. Bivouac gear rated for extreme altitude, high winds, and temperatures well below -30°C. High-calorie food that does not freeze solid. Water purification (melt snow, but be aware the cloud-ice may already contain mineral contamination from the volcanic peak).
- **A partner.** Do not go alone. The conditions described — deafening noise, blind spray, surging water, unstable geology, toxic gas risk, altitude impairment — mean that a solo traveler who suffers any single point of failure (a turned ankle, a moment of disorientation, a gas alarm they can't hear) will die. Bring someone. Agree on hand signals in advance, because you will not hear each other's voices. Practice those signals until they are reflex.

## WHAT YOU WILL EXPERIENCE

### The First Thirty Seconds

You enter through what the text describes as a passage that requires you to have found it from the outside — a crack or fissure in the mountain's flank. The moment you cross the threshold, three things happen simultaneously:

1. **The sound hits you.** Even with double hearing protection, the roar is a physical wall. Your chest compresses. Your vision may narrow or blur. The spray from the ceiling jets is not rain — it is a hundred high-pressure streams hitting a pool and atomizing into a mist so dense it functions as a fluid. You cannot see clearly past a few meters. The air is more water than air.

2. **The water hits you.** If the cycle is at peak, you are immediately waist-deep in frigid, mineral-laden water with enough force to stagger you. Even at trough, you are ankle-deep in a current that is moving unpredictably as it drains through floor fissures.

3. **The light surrounds you.** Despite being inside a stone cavity at extreme altitude, it is not dark. The light has no source — it is diffracted, scattered, reflected by the spray and the glistening walls until the entire chamber glows with a diffuse, soft luminance. It takes a moment to understand this, because your brain expects darkness inside rock and instead finds something that resembles overcast daylight without a sky.

Your instinct will be to freeze. This is partially correct — you need to orient yourself before moving. But do not freeze for long. The water is cold and your thermal clock has started. You have a limited window of functional time before hypothermia, even through the drysuit, begins to degrade your coordination.

### The Cairn

The three stones stacked on the narrow ledge are the reason you are here. They are approximately one meter above the fluctuating waterline, on a shelf of less-eroded rock. To reach them, you will need to move across the chamber — through the surging water, on the slippery accreting floor, in the deafening noise, through the spray — and either climb or reach up to the ledge.

The stones themselves are not local basalt. They are darker, denser, carried here from elsewhere. There are no markings, no inscriptions. Just three stones, balanced on each other, in a chamber that should be unreachable, inside a mountain that barely exists.

Take your time looking at them. The text suggests they are the fossil of a decision — someone came here and stopped, and placed a marker. They have not fallen despite the perpetual tremor of the chamber. This is either extraordinarily careful placement or something about this location stabilizes them in a way that defies the surrounding chaos.

You may want to touch them. If you do, wear your heat-resistant gloves — the stones may have a different thermal signature than the local rock. You may want to photograph them — bring a waterproof camera rated for extreme conditions, and be prepared for the lens to be continuously coated in mineral spray. Wipe it with a microfiber cloth stored inside your drysuit.

**Do not move the stones.** You do not know what they are. They may be a survey marker, a spiritual artifact, a navigational beacon, or a geological anchor point for someone's future plans. They belong to someone who walked here before you and chose to leave something behind. Leave them as you found them.

### The Microorganisms

The dark rosettes on the wetter walls are extremophile microorganisms — something that predates or exists outside the plant-animal divide. They photosynthesize using the sourceless, diffracted light. They migrate upslope as the water level dictates, which means they are responding to environmental conditions in real time.

If you have any background in biology, you will want to sample these. Bring sterile collection vials sealed in a waterproof case. Use a sterile tool — not your glove — to scrape a small sample from the wall. Document the location, the wall texture, the water exposure pattern, the light conditions. These organisms may represent a biosphere that is entirely independent of whatever evolutionary tree you are from. They are possibly the most scientifically valuable thing in this chamber.

They will not care about you. They will not react to your presence. They have been here since before the mountain had a name, and they will be here after the chamber seals itself shut and becomes a dry geode. Your visit means nothing to them.

### The Translucent Creature

"Eyeless, boneless, barely more than a nerve-sheath with a propulsion cilia." It enters through a fissure too small to see, feeds for less than a minute, and leaves. It visits. It does not stay.

If you see it — and you may not; the description says "once, perhaps twice, in a full cycle of observation" — do not attempt to capture it. Do not touch it. Observe it. It is navigating a flooded, chaotic, chemically hostile environment with apparent intelligence, entering and exiting through a passage you cannot even perceive. It knows something about this place you do not. Watch how it moves. Watch where it feeds. Watch which fissure it exits through. That fissure may connect to larger passages in the mountain that are not flooded, and knowing where they are could save your life if you need to evacuate by a route other than the one you entered through.

## PSYCHOLOGICAL PREPARATION

This is the section most travelers will underestimate. Do not.

### The Sound Will Break Your Sense of Self

The roar in The Throat is described as "absolute" and "the only thing in the world." At the levels implied, sustained noise does not merely damage hearing — it dissolves the boundary between your body and the environment. You stop being a person standing in a loud room and become *a surface that the sound is moving through*. This is not poetic language. At extreme volumes, proprioception, spatial awareness, and the sense of having a discrete body all begin to degrade. You may feel as though you are the chamber itself, or that you have no edges, or that the water and the sound and the light are all the same thing and you are part of it.

This experience is not dangerous in itself, but it is profoundly disorienting, and disorientation in a chamber with surging water, unstable footing, toxic gas potential, and limited air is dangerous. Anchor yourself. Touch the wall. Feel the rock beneath your feet. Remind yourself — physically, not intellectually — that you have a body with edges, and that those edges are inside a drysuit, and that the drysuit is on a human being who needs to leave this place alive.

### The Place Will Feel Like It Is Being Born Around You

Because it is. The mineral accretion building on the walls, the erosion cutting the floor, the water arriving and departing on its cycle — this is a place in the act of *becoming*. You are standing inside a geological verb. This can produce a feeling that is difficult to describe but is commonly reported by people who witness large-scale geological processes in real time: a sense of radical insignificance combined with an almost ecstatic intimacy with the process itself. You are watching the earth make something. You are inside the making.

This feeling is beautiful and it will try to keep you here. The water surges and your mind says *just a little longer*. The light shifts and you want to see it again. The rosettes on the wall move and you want to follow their migration. The creature enters and you want to see it leave.

You cannot stay. The cold, the altitude, the gas risk, the sound — they are all counting down. Set a hard time limit before you enter. Stick to it. The Throat will still be here tomorrow. It will not be the same — it will never be the same, because it is becoming something new with every surge — but it will be here. You may not be, if you overstay.

### The Cairn Will Haunt You

Someone came here. Someone with hands and intent and no visible technology beyond carrying rocks and balancing them. They climbed a still-rising mountain through cloud-ice, found a cavity that should not have been findable, entered a chamber of annihilating sound and surging water, and placed three stones on a ledge.

You will want to know why. You will not find out. The stones do not tell you. The chamber does not tell you. The description offers only possibilities: boundary marker, warning, greeting, the fossil of a decision to stop.

Sit with the not-knowing. It is part of the place. The cairn is an artifact of a consciousness that was here before you and made a choice you cannot interpret, and the stones remain, balanced in the roar, unchanged by the water that rises and falls around them.

You will carry this question out of the mountain with you. There is no answer attached. Bring it anyway.

## TIME MANAGEMENT

### Maximum Recommended Duration of Stay

**Forty-five minutes.** No more.

This is not an arbitrary limit. It accounts for:

- Progressive hypothermia despite drysuit insulation (near-freezing water cycling to waist depth, repeatedly)
- Cumulative hearing damage even with double protection
- Oxygen supply if using supplemental systems
- Gas accumulation risk (monitor readings may trend upward as the chamber's atmosphere shifts)
- Cognitive degradation from altitude, cold stress, sensory overload, and oxygen debt
- Physical exhaustion from maintaining balance in surging water on a slippery, bucking floor

If any of the following occur before the 45-minute mark, leave immediately:

- H₂S reading above 10 ppm
- Hearing protection failure
- Drysuit breach (water intrusion)
- Signs of hypothermia you can self-identify: uncontrollable shivering, confusion, slurred speech, loss of coordination in the hands
- Signs of altitude sickness: severe headache, nausea, vision disturbance, loss of balance not attributable to the water
- Any injury that limits mobility — a twisted ankle in this chamber is a death sentence if the water surges to chest height and you cannot stay upright

## PACKING LIST (Summary)

### On Your Body
- Merino wool base layer (top and bottom)
- Fleece or synthetic insulation mid-layer
- Full drysuit with sealed gaskets
- Waterproof insulated boots with aggressive tread and composite toe
- Chemical-splash sealed goggles with anti-fog treatment
- Heat-resistant aramid gloves (inner)
- Waterproof insulated gloves (outer)
- Wetsuit hood or balaclava
- Full-face helmet with integrated acoustic dampening

### Hearing Protection (Worn)
- Custom-molded earplugs (NRR 33+)
- Electronic over-ear muffs with active noise compression (NRR 25-30)
- Practice putting both on in under five seconds, blind, before entering

### Breathing and Air Quality
- Supplemental oxygen system with sealed full-face mask or demand-valve respirator
- H₂S/SO₂ gas monitor, chest-mounted, with audible AND visual alarm (you will not hear the audible alarm — rely on the visual; tape a small LED to your goggles if necessary)

### Tools and Documentation
- Waterproof non-contact infrared thermometer
- Waterproof camera rated for extreme conditions
- Microfiber lens cloth stored in drysuit pocket
- Sterile sample collection vials in waterproof case
- Sterile scraping tool for biological samples
- Headlamp (backup to the sourceless light — waterproof, helmet-mounted)
- Route-marking tape or chalk (to mark your entry path for egress in spray and disorientation)

### Emergency
- Personal locator beacon or distress transponder
- Chemical glow sticks (if the sourceless light fails for any reason, you need to be able to see the water level)
- Emergency bivvy sack (if you are injured and cannot immediately evacuate, you need to survive long enough for extraction)
- Whistle — yes, even though you cannot hear it. Your partner might, if they are outside the chamber in the entry fissure.

### For the Approach
- Full alpine climbing kit (ice axes, crampons, harness, dynamic rope, cams/nuts for volcanic rock)
- Multi-day bivouac gear for extreme altitude and cold
- High-calorie, non-freezing food
- Water purification
- Altitude medication (acetazolamide / dexamethasone — consult a physician before departure)

## FINAL NOTE

You are preparing to visit a place that does not know you are coming and would not change its behavior if it did. The Throat is not hostile — hostility requires awareness. It is simply *happening*: water is falling, rock is forming, light is scattering, organisms are migrating, a creature is visiting, three stones are balanced on a ledge. All of this will continue whether you are there or not.

The greatest danger of The Throat is not the sound, or the water, or the altitude, or the gas. It is the temptation to believe that witnessing something extraordinary gives you a right to more of it. It does not. You are a visitor inside a process that predates your arrival and will outlast your presence by geological time. Come. Look. Listen — with what remains of your hearing. Touch what is offered. Sample what you can carry. And then leave, carrying nothing but the memory of standing inside a mountain that had not yet finished becoming itself, watching three stones that did not move.
