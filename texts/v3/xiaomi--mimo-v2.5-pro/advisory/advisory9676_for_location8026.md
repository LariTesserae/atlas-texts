# Preparing for The Weeping Highlands: A Traveler's Guide

---

## Before You Go: Understanding What You're Walking Into

The first thing to internalize is that nothing in your prior experience of darkness will prepare you for this. You have been in caves. You have been in windowless rooms with the lights off. You have perhaps been in the deep ocean or underground passages where headlamps fail. None of this is adequate preparation. The Weeping Highlands have no sky. There is no "above" that resolves into anything familiar — no stars, no clouds, no gradient of grey that tells your brain where the horizon is. The darkness extends infinitely in every direction, including upward, and it is not the passive absence of light you're used to. It is thick. It is warm. It presses against your skin with a humidity so intense that your first several minutes will feel like stepping into a wet, hot towel that wraps around your entire body and doesn't stop.

You will not be able to see your hand in front of your face. You will not be able to see *anything*, ever, for the entire duration of your visit. This is non-negotiable. There is no flashlight bright enough, no torch sustainable enough, no biological adaptation quick enough. The ecosystem here evolved without light. Your eyes are useless appendages that will, if anything, generate stress and phantom images as your brain desperately tries to interpret the total absence of visual input. Plan accordingly. Plan to be blind. Then plan beyond that.

## Gear and Equipment

### Navigation and Orientation

**Primary: Vibrotactile mapping device.** The single most important piece of equipment you can carry. The Weeping Highlands are a world of sound and vibration, and you need to become part of that world quickly. A vibrotactile mapper — worn as a harness or integrated into a suit — uses a network of small transducers against your skin to convert sonar-ping returns into tactile patterns. You send out a pulse (acoustic, subsonic ideally so as not to disturb the clickers more than necessary), and the returning echoes are translated into pressure patterns across your torso: a ridge feels like a flat band of pressure on your chest, a canyon drop-off feels like a cold absence on your left side, a wall feels like a hard push on your sternum. These devices exist in various multiverse catalogs. Get one with a range of at least 200 meters and configurable sensitivity, because the acoustic environment here is *dense* — echoes come back from every direction simultaneously, filtered through dripping water, growing organisms, moving animals, and the grinding of ancient machines. You need software that can discriminate between geological returns and biological noise, and you need it tuned before you arrive.

**Secondary: Thermal imaging overlay (worn as a visor or implant feed).** While nothing emits visible light, everything here emits *heat*. The thermal differentials are rich with information: the warm plumes of geothermal vents, the cooler (relatively) rock of exposed ridgeline faces, the body heat of threshers moving through a filament forest, the radiating warmth of a machinery cluster. A good thermal visor will not give you the kind of vision you're used to — there are no edges, no colors, no shadows — but it will give you gradients. Warm blobs against slightly-less-warm backgrounds. Hot lines where pipes run along canyon walls. The distinctive infrared signature of a threadling's muscular body coiled in a filament curtain, nearly ambient-temperature except for its feeding organs, which run a few degrees hotter. You will learn to read these gradients the way you learned to read faces: slowly, then suddenly, then fluently.

**Tertiary: A trained companion-clicker or equivalent echolocation guide.** The human inhabitants of the highlands use companion-clickers — small arthropod-analogues trained to produce specific click-patterns in response to environmental features. The clicker rides on your shoulder or in a harness on your back, and it clicks constantly, and you learn to interpret the changes in its clicking rhythm the way a person in a lit world reads the body language of a dog. A sudden acceleration in click-rate means proximity to a drop-off. A change in pitch means a material change — metal instead of stone, water instead of rock. A cessation of clicking entirely means *predator nearby, freeze*. These creatures are not pets. They are instruments, and the relationship is professional. The human inhabitants will sell or loan them to visitors they trust. Earning that trust is a separate preparation entirely (see: Social Preparation, below).

**Do not bring light sources.** This sounds counterintuitive, possibly insane. But light in the Weeping Highlands does something worse than being useless — it *panics* the ecosystem. The clickers interpret sudden photon emissions (not that there are normally any to interpret, so the sensory organs that detect electromagnetic disturbance are vestigial but still wired to threat-response pathways) as a catastrophic anomaly. A large colony's clicking pattern will destabilize instantly. The threshers will stampede. The threadlings will strike at anything nearby, including you, because a destabilized click-map means their ambush patterns fail and they go into a frenzied, indiscriminate hunting mode. Light has been tested by previous visitors. It always ends badly. The human inhabitants regard anyone who brings a torch the way a surgeon would regard someone who brought a loaded shotgun into an operating theater.

### Breathing and Air Quality

**Essential: Mineral respirator with adjustable filtration.** The air in the Weeping Highlands is breathable in the technical sense — it contains sufficient oxygen for human survival — but it is saturated with mineral particulates, sulfur compounds, iron vapor, and complex chemical byproducts of the geothermal-biological cycle. In normal conditions, this means every breath tastes of metal and warm earth, and prolonged exposure irritates the mucous membranes, dries out the throat (paradoxically, in a place this wet), and can cause mineral deposits to form in the bronchial passages over extended stays. During density storms — periodic events where the air becomes supersaturated with moisture and mineral particles — breathing becomes genuinely labored without filtration. The particles are fine enough to penetrate deep into the lungs, and the sulfur compounds at peak concentration can cause chemical burns to the respiratory tract.

A good respirator should have adjustable filtration levels. You don't want maximum filtration at all times, because the air's chemical composition carries *information* — the sulfur gradient tells you how close you are to a geothermal vent, the iron tang tells you when you're near a corroding machine, the sweet fermentation smell warns you of deep filament-forest mats where the organic decay produces gases that aren't toxic exactly but will make you lightheaded. Learn to breathe through the filter with your mouth when you need protection and through your nose (lightly filtered) when you need data.

**Bring a personal atmospheric monitor.** Carbon dioxide, hydrogen sulfide, and methane concentrations can spike in enclosed spaces near geothermal vents or within the old machinery. The machines themselves sometimes release processed gases that have unpredictable chemical profiles. Your monitor should alert you before you walk into a pocket of air that will knock you unconscious.

### Clothing and Physical Protection

**A full-body moisture suit is mandatory.** Not waterproof — nothing here is waterproof because the water comes from every direction, including up — but moisture-managing. The suit should wick moisture away from your skin while allowing your body to thermoregulate, because the ambient temperature ranges from warm (35–40°C) to dangerously hot (60°C+ near active vents). The suit material should be resistant to mineral deposition, because the water here leaves behind calcium, sulfur, and iron deposits on every surface it touches, and over the course of a day your clothing will stiffen and crust if it's not treated with an anti-mineral-adhesion coating.

The suit should also be acoustically dampened. This is critical. The clickers and threadlings navigate by sound. The threshers detect threats by vibration through their feet. You are, to every organism in this ecosystem, a *noise* — your footsteps on stone, your breathing, the rustle of fabric, the beat of your heart. In a world without light, sound is visibility, and you are a walking shout. Acoustic dampening in your suit fabric and footwear reduces your sound signature to something closer to the ambient noise floor. It won't make you silent — nothing will, because you're a warm, breathing, metabolically active body in a world that detects warm, breathing, metabolically active bodies — but it will make you less conspicuous.

**Footwear: soft-soled, high-grip, with vibration-dampening insoles.** The terrain is wet basalt, which is slippery even when it's rough. The ridgeline paths worn by millennia of thresher traffic are polished smooth in places. The canyon-floor substrate is meters of mineral sediment over compressed organic matter — it squelches, it shifts, it can be unexpectedly deep. You need boots that grip rock, that don't sink too far into soft substrate, and that dampen the vibrations of your footsteps enough to avoid triggering the territorial responses of organisms that feel you coming through the stone.

**Gloves with textured fingertips.** You will be touching everything. In the absence of sight, your hands become your primary spatial sensors. The stone here is rich with textural information — the smooth grooves worn by water, the rough ridges of crust-community growth, the warm slickness of filament curtains, the gritty surface of corroded metal. Good gloves protect your hands from the mineral-laden water (which will irritate skin over prolonged exposure) while preserving enough tactile sensitivity to read the landscape.

### Sustenance

**Water: do not drink from local sources.** The water in the Weeping Highlands is geothermally heated, mineral-saturated, and biologically active. It contains dissolved sulfur, iron, calcium, arsenic (in some regions), and a complex soup of dissolved organic compounds from the decomposition of local organisms. It will make you sick immediately and possibly kill you within days. Bring your own water supply, sealed and separate from the ambient environment. If you must supplement, the human inhabitants have filtration systems — crude but effective, usually involving layered sediment filters and chemical precipitants extracted from the machines' processing outputs — that can render local water potable. It still tastes of iron. You will get used to it or you will be thirsty.

**Food: bring high-calorie, sealed rations.** There is local food, technically. The human inhabitants eat certain crust-community organisms (dried, ground, baked into hard biscuits), certain filament-forest mats (fermented into a paste that tastes like strong miso mixed with iron filings), and some of the marsh-floating bladder-plants (when they're young and haven't yet accumulated dangerous mineral concentrations). None of this will kill you immediately, but all of it contains chemical compounds that the local humans have developed tolerance for over generations. You have not. Eat local food in small quantities, introduced gradually, and expect gastrointestinal distress. Better to bring your own food and supplement locally only when you must.

**Caloric needs are elevated.** The heat, the humidity, the constant low-level stress of navigating in total darkness, the effort of processing sensory information through unfamiliar channels — all of this burns energy faster than you expect. Eat more than you think you need.

### Health and Medical Preparation

**Heat management is your primary health concern.** The ambient temperature combined with near-100% humidity means your body's cooling mechanisms (primarily sweating) are severely compromised. You will overheat. A personal cooling system — chemical ice packs, a cooled-liquid circulation vest, or a compact evaporative cooling unit — is not optional. Without one, heat stroke becomes a realistic danger within hours of sustained physical activity. The human inhabitants manage this through a combination of behavioral adaptation (they move slowly, rest frequently, and have built their settlements in locations where air circulation is slightly better) and physiological acclimatization over generations. You do not have their advantages.

**Skin care.** The mineral-laden water is a persistent irritant. After 24 hours of continuous exposure, you will develop a red, itchy rash on any exposed skin — contact dermatitis from the sulfur and iron compounds. After 48 hours, the skin will begin to crack. Barrier cream, applied thickly and reapplied regularly, is essential. Pay special attention to the face, neck, and hands — the areas most exposed to the weeping water that drips from above and splashes from below.

**Ear care.** The noise environment is intense and constant. The clickers alone produce sound pressure levels that, over extended exposure, can cause hearing damage. The machines add low-frequency rumble that is felt more than heard but that fatigues the auditory system. The density storms produce sharp, unpredictable acoustic events — static discharges, sudden silence, sudden roar — that can startle and disorient. Bring high-fidelity ear protection that attenuates damaging frequencies while preserving your ability to hear navigational sounds. Custom-molded earplugs with tuned acoustic filters are ideal. Foam plugs are inadequate because they block too much information.

**Mental health.** This is not optional preparation; this is survival preparation. Total darkness for extended periods causes documented psychological effects: disorientation, anxiety, depression, hallucination, time-perception distortion. The Weeping Highlands add to this the constant sensory bombardment — the noise, the wetness, the heat, the smell, the feeling of being *watched* (which is not paranoia here; you are being watched, or rather sensed, by every organism within detection range). The human inhabitants have cultural practices that help — rhythmic chanting, storytelling traditions, communal sleeping arrangements, structured daily routines — and you should learn and adopt as many of these as you can. Do not attempt a solo visit of more than a few days. Have a companion or, failing that, regular communication with an outside party who can check on your psychological state.

## Communication

**Do not plan to use standard radio communication.** The mineral content of the air and water, the density of the atmosphere, and the constant acoustic noise floor make conventional radio unreliable. Short-range communication works — within a few hundred meters, if both parties are stationary — but the signal degrades rapidly with distance and is useless during density storms.

The human inhabitants communicate over distance by tapping into the old machinery's pipe network. The pipes run for kilometers, they're made of metal, and they carry vibrations beautifully. A code of rhythmic taps — not unlike Morse code, but adapted for a medium that transmits percussion more reliably than tonal signal — allows messages to travel the full length of a pipe system in minutes. Learn this code before you arrive. It is the only reliable long-distance communication method in the highlands, and if you become separated from your group or injured in a canyon, it may be the only way to call for help.

For real-time communication with the outside multiverse, you will need to reach one of the few locations where a signal-boosting relay has been installed — usually at or near one of the larger human settlements, built around one of the more intact machine-complexes. These relays are cobbled together from scavenged components and are temperamental. Do not depend on them.

## Social Preparation

The human inhabitants of the Weeping Highlands are few, scattered, and self-selected for extreme resilience and independence. They did not end up here by accident — or if they did, they chose to stay, which tells you something about their psychology. They are wary of outsiders, not because they are hostile but because outsiders tend to be *loud, bright, and disruptive* in ways that threaten the delicate acoustic equilibrium on which their survival depends.

**Before approaching any settlement:**

1. **Reduce your noise signature to minimum.** Move slowly. Speak softly. If you have a companion-clicker, keep it calm. The inhabitants will detect your approach long before you detect them — they have spent their lives learning to interpret the sound-landscape, and a new human body moving through it is immediately identifiable.

2. **Announce yourself through the pipe network.** If you can find a pipe junction (they are marked by specific tactile codes — raised bumps welded to the metal in patterns that can be read by touch), tap the traditional greeting pattern: three slow, two fast, three slow. Then wait. Someone will respond. It may take hours.

3. **Do not offer light as a gift.** Do not carry visible light sources. Do not activate any technology that emits electromagnetic radiation in the visible spectrum. This sounds absurdly specific, but it is the single most common mistake outsiders make, and it is the fastest way to be permanently unwelcome.

4. **Bring trade goods that matter.** The inhabitants have everything they need for survival from the local environment. What they lack — what they value — is *information from outside*. News of other worlds. Maps of other places. Stories. Medical knowledge (their own medicine is effective but limited). Durable components for maintaining their filtration and communication systems. Precision tools. Above all: *patience and willingness to learn*. The inhabitants will teach you to navigate if you let them. The process is slow, immersive, and involves spending long periods in silence, just listening. If you cannot do this — if you are impatient, if you need to fill silence with talk, if you are uncomfortable with darkness and dependence — do not come.

## Psychological Preparation: What It Will Actually Feel Like

No amount of gear replaces the need to prepare your mind. Here is what your first hours will be like, honestly:

You will arrive — via whatever mechanism delivers you — and immediately the darkness will close over you like water. Not gently. There is no transition period. One moment you are in a place with some kind of horizon, some sense of space defined by light; the next, there is *nothing*. Your visual cortex will rebel. You will see phosphenes — random firing of your retinal cells, producing ghost-lights, swirling colors, afterimages of nothing. This is normal. It will pass in hours. It will be terrifying.

Then the heat will hit. It is not the dry heat of a desert or the soft heat of a warm bath. It is the dense, heavy, wet heat of a place that has been warm for longer than your species has existed. It presses on you. It enters your lungs with every breath, carrying the taste of iron and sulfur and the sweet-rot smell of a billion organisms living and dying in the dark.

Then the sound. It builds slowly because at first you will not have the context to parse it. It will register as noise — formless, overwhelming, *loud*. But as your brain adjusts (and it will, faster than you think, because the brain is remarkably plastic when it has no alternative), the noise will begin to resolve into layers. The constant background of dripping water — not one drip but ten thousand, a soft unceasing applause from every direction. Beneath that, the clickers: a rainstorm of percussion, fast and complex and full of meaning you cannot yet read. Beneath that, the deep infrasonic pulse of the threshers, felt in your sternum, in your stomach, in the marrow of your bones. And beneath all of it, the machines: a slow industrial heartbeat, grinding and pumping, a rhythm that has not changed in ten thousand years.

You will stand very still. You will want to sit down, but you cannot see what you're sitting on, and the ground may be a shallow shelf above a hundred-meter canyon. So you will stand, and you will breathe, and the water will drip on your shoulders, and the air will press against your face, and the darkness will be complete, and you will feel — with absolute certainty — that you are being *watched*.

You are not being watched. Nothing here has eyes. But you are being *sensed* — by the clickers mapping you with sound, by the threadlings detecting your body heat, by the communion clusters reading your bioelectric field, by the threshers feeling your footsteps through the stone. You are not invisible. You are not unimportant. You are a new stimulus in an ancient system, and the system is *paying attention*.

This feeling will never entirely go away. The human inhabitants describe it as the presence of the darkness itself — not metaphorically, but as a literal sensory experience. The dark has weight here. It has intention, or something so close to intention that the difference is academic. Whether this is a real phenomenon (some kind of ambient electromagnetic or chemical field generated by the ecosystem's collective activity) or a psychological projection of a primate brain deprived of its primary sense, no one has determined. It doesn't matter. You will feel it. You will learn to live with it, or you will leave.

## Timeline of Adaptation

**Hours 1–6:** Disorientation, anxiety, phantom visual disturbances, acute awareness of heat and moisture, sensory overwhelm. Stay close to your landing point. Do not attempt to navigate. Breathe. Listen. Touch the ground beneath you and learn its texture. This is your anchor.

**Hours 6–24:** The visual disturbances fade. Your hearing begins to sharpen — not literally, but your brain starts to extract signal from the noise. You begin to distinguish the dripping of water from the clicking of arthropods from the grinding of machines. The heat becomes less oppressive as your body begins to thermoregulate more efficiently (or you activate your cooling system and stop suffering). You are still disoriented, but you can function. Eat. Drink. Rest.

**Days 2–5:** Acclimatization accelerates. You start to navigate by sound and touch. Your companion-clicker (if you have one) becomes your most trusted instrument — its click-patterns are your eyes. You begin to develop a mental map of your immediate area: this ledge is three paces wide, the drop-off is to the left, the filament curtain hangs from above and brushes your face at shoulder height. The human inhabitants, if you have made contact, begin to teach you. You begin to listen for the things they listen for — the change in dripping pattern that indicates a change in rock type, the shift in clicker-song that means a thresher herd is approaching, the deepening of the machine-rumble that means you're near an engine cluster.

**Days 5–14:** Functional adaptation. You can navigate known routes. You can interpret thermal-sense data with reasonable accuracy. You can read the pipe-code at basic proficiency. You know the smell of a geothermal vent (sharp sulfur, wet heat) from a filament-forest (sweet fermentation, warm damp) from a machine-cluster (hot metal, chemical solvents). You sleep in short cycles, because the darkness never changes and your circadian rhythm, deprived of light cues, fragments into polyphasic patterns. The human inhabitants sleep in groups, in shifts, and you join their rhythm.

**Days 14+:** You begin to forget what sight was like. Not literally, but experientially — your brain starts to prioritize other senses so heavily that vision becomes a secondary consideration, a memory rather than a mode. You dream in sound and touch and smell. You navigate by echolocation clicks and vibration and the taste of the air. You have become, functionally, a creature of the Weeping Highlands.

This is when you should leave. Not because you are in danger (you are always in danger, but you have learned to manage it), but because the longer you stay, the harder it is to return. The human inhabitants know this. They have seen visitors arrive and adapt and forget. Some of those visitors are still there — inhabitants now, no longer visitors, their old lives in lit worlds as distant and theoretical as the sky.

## A Final Warning

The Weeping Highlands are beautiful. This is the most dangerous thing about them. Not the threadlings, not the heat, not the mineral water, not the density storms. The beauty. Because when your other senses fully awaken — when you hear the clicker-song as music, when you feel the thresher-bellow as a heartbeat in your chest, when you taste the iron and sulfur as the flavor of a world that has been alive and dreaming in the dark for longer than you can comprehend — you will feel something that lit-worlds rarely produce. You will feel *belonging*. You will feel that the darkness is not hostile but *indifferent*, and that indifference is, in its way, a kind of acceptance. The Weeping Highlands do not care that you are there, and that lack of caring means they will not reject you either. You are another warm body in a world of warm bodies. You are another vibration in a symphony of vibrations. You drip, as everything drips. You breathe, as everything breathes. You make noise, and the noise is absorbed into the noise, and the noise is the world, and the world is old and patient and endless and dark.

Come home when you planned to. Do not stay because it feels like you belong. You do belong — that is the trap.
