# Preparation Guide for The High Silent

---

## Understanding Before Packing

Before you prepare your gear, prepare your *expectation*. The High Silent is not dangerous in the way a volcanic hellscape is dangerous, or the way a predator-rich jungle is dangerous. Nothing here is likely to chase you, poison you, or erupt beneath your feet. The danger — and there is real danger — is subtler. It is the danger of *insufficiency*. Not enough air. Not enough sound. Not enough stimulation. Not enough of the sensory scaffolding that your human brain relies on to maintain a stable sense of where you are, how big you are, and whether you are okay.

This is a place that is profoundly, structurally indifferent to your presence. You need to prepare for that not as a poetic abstraction but as a practical reality. The landscape will not respond to you. Your footsteps will be swallowed. Your voice will die within meters. The stone will not remember you. If you are the kind of person who fills silence with noise, who needs environmental feedback to feel real, this place will erode you faster than the wind erodes anything here — which is to say, not at all, but in your case, completely.

Spend time before departure sitting in silence. Not meditation silence, not noise-canceling-headphone silence, but *actual* silence — an anechoic chamber if you can find one, or the quietest room you can access, for as long as you can tolerate. Learn the sound of your own body. Learn how loud your blood is. Learn how much of what you think of as "silence" is actually ambient noise so familiar you've stopped registering it. On The High Silent, that ambient noise does not exist. What you learn in that quiet room is what the baseline will sound like, *forever*, until you leave.

## Respiratory Preparation

This is the most critical practical concern. The air on The High Silent is **thin** — thinner than any terrestrial altitude you've experienced, and importantly, it does not behave like terrestrial high-altitude air. There is no acclimatization hiking up a mountain. You arrive at full altitude immediately. The description tells us the air is "sparse, the way a sentence might be sparse," and this is not metaphor — the partial pressure of oxygen is meaningfully reduced, and the total atmospheric density is low enough that sound propagation itself changes.

**Bring supplemental oxygen.** Not a small canister from a hiking supply store. Bring a closed-circuit rebreather system or a substantial compressed oxygen supply with a delivery mask, enough for the full duration of your planned stay. Calculate your consumption generously — assume you will be walking, and walking at altitude burns more oxygen than resting. If you plan to stay for days (and the scale of this place encourages extended stays), you need a resupply plan or a renewable source.

**Respiratory training** before departure is valuable. Practice slow, controlled breathing. Practice sustaining activity while consciously managing your breath rate. On The High Silent, you will feel breathless after moderate exertion — climbing a gentle rise, walking quickly toward a structure you've spotted. The instinct will be to gasp, to over-breathe. This wastes oxygen and can cause hypocapnia (too-low CO₂), which makes you dizzy and confused, which is the last thing you want in a landscape where the nearest help is in another universe.

**Humidity is essentially zero.** Your airways will dry out. Bring a nasal saline spray or a moisture-exchange mask attachment. Over hours of breathing this air, your throat will become raw, your lips will crack, and if you are speaking (which you probably won't be, but in case), your vocal cords will fatigue rapidly. The dryness is not painful at first. It becomes painful. Plan for it.

## Thermal and Clothing Preparation

The temperature is described as **cool and steady** — not frigid, not punishing, but cool. Think of it as a perpetual autumn evening, perhaps 10–15°C (50–59°F), with minimal diurnal variation and only a slight drop at night. The stone retains heat. The air is too thin to convect warmth efficiently.

This sounds mild, and it is, but the *constancy* is the issue. There is no warm refuge. There are no buildings you can enter, no fires you can build (there is essentially nothing to burn), no geothermal vents. You are in the cool air at all times, sleeping on cool stone, and your body will slowly lose heat through radiation and conduction if you don't manage it.

**Layer with intention.** Base layer: moisture-wicking synthetic or merino, because even in dry air your body produces some moisture, and damp fabric against skin in cool air is a heat drain. Insulating layer: something lofty and compressible — down or synthetic fill — for warmth during rest. Outer layer: wind-resistant and, ideally, with some radiant-heat retention. The air is thin, which means convective cooling is reduced (less air molecules to carry your heat away), but radiative cooling under a clear night sky — and the nights here are *very* clear, with almost no atmospheric water vapor to trap infrared — is significant. A space blanket or reflective bivvy layer for sleeping is not overkill.

**Bring a sleeping system rated for cool-steady conditions.** Not an arctic bag, but something that handles 5–10°C comfortably. You will be sleeping on stone. The stone is grippy but hard and has significant thermal mass — it will pull heat from your body through contact. A closed-cell foam pad is essential. An inflatable pad is good but risks puncture on the mineral crusts. Bring both if weight allows, or at minimum, a robust closed-cell pad.

**Head, hands, feet.** These are your heat-leakage points. A thin beanie for sleeping. Gloves for handling the structures (which may have slightly different surface temperatures due to their unknown function — the hum suggests energy expenditure). Sturdy boots with good ankle support — the terrain is "worn to near-flatness," but near-flatness is not flatness, and the shallow rills and concavities can catch a foot at the wrong angle, especially in low light.

## Psychological and Perceptual Preparation

This is, in many ways, more important than the gear.

**The silence will change you.** Not quickly. Not dramatically. But over hours and days, the absence of sound — real absence, structural absence, not just quiet — does things to human cognition that you should anticipate. Auditory pareidolia: you will begin to hear things that aren't there. Phantom sounds. Your name. Footsteps that aren't yours. Music. This is your auditory cortex, starved of input, generating its own. It is normal. It is not a sign of madness. But knowing it will happen means you can recognize it when it does, rather than spiraling into anxiety about it.

**The scale will disorient you.** The curvature of the world is visible. The horizon bends. Features you can see may be a full day's walk away, and the clear air and lack of reference points make distance almost impossible to judge. Your brain, accustomed to terrestrial scale — trees for mid-range reference, buildings for close-range, atmospheric haze for far-range — will have nothing to calibrate against. You will consistently underestimate distances. A structure that looks an hour away may be six hours away. Bring a rangefinder if you have one, or at minimum, train yourself to distrust your visual distance estimates entirely.

**The isolation is geometric.** The text says you are "less a presence in this landscape than a suggestion." This is exactly right. You will feel *small* — not in the way a mountain makes you feel small, which is dramatic and sublime and somehow affirming, but in the way an empty room makes you feel small if the room is the size of a continent. There is no sublime here. There is no drama. There is just vastness, and age, and your temporary presence in a space that has been getting along fine without anything like you for longer than you can comprehend. If you are prone to existential anxiety, prepare coping strategies. Bring something to read. Bring music (headphones only — do not pollute this silence with speakers). Bring a project, a reason to be here beyond tourism, something to anchor your attention when the emptiness presses in.

**Journal.** Bring a physical journal and pen (not electronic — the electromagnetic storms may interfere with devices, more on that below). Write. Describe what you see. Describe what you feel. The act of narration — of imposing language onto a landscape that precedes and will outlast all language — is one of the best defenses against the kind of dissociation this place can induce. When you write "the stone is grey," you are asserting a relationship between yourself and the stone. You are saying: I am here, I am observing, I am translating. This matters. Do it daily.

## Equipment: Navigation and Orientation

**Do not rely on GPS or satellite navigation.** This is not Earth. The electromagnetic environment is actively hostile to precision electronics during storm events (which are unpredictable and frequent). Even between storms, the faint luminous distortion in the atmosphere may scatter signals unpredictably.

**Bring a quality mechanical compass.** It may or may not work — the text doesn't specify whether this world has a magnetic field, and the structures' electromagnetic emissions could interfere. Test it upon arrival. If it works, it's your primary directional tool. If it doesn't, you'll need to navigate by the sun's position, which the text confirms behaves in a way that "implies days and seasons."

**Bring a reliable, non-electronic timepiece.** A wind-up watch or a spring-driven clock. You need to track time because the near-constant conditions (same temperature, same light quality, same silence) will destroy your sense of temporal passage. You will lose hours. A day will feel like a week, or an hour. Time-marking is essential for maintaining a routine, and routine is essential for maintaining psychological stability.

**Mark your path.** The barely-visible molecular paths in the stone are beautiful and intriguing but unreliable as navigation aids — you can only see them in certain light conditions. Carry physical markers: cairn stones (use the mineral crusts — they're visually distinct), reflective tape, or anything that creates a visible landmark. The flat terrain and lack of features mean that if you walk in one direction for an hour and then turn to walk back, even a few degrees of deviation will miss your starting point by a significant distance. **Mark your base camp conspicuously.** Treat this like desert or open-ocean navigation: your path out is as important as your path in.

## Equipment: The Storms

The **silent storms** — electromagnetic, luminous, chaotic, and unpredictable — are the single most dynamic environmental factor you'll face. They are not immediately lethal (as far as the text describes), but they are *strange*, and strangeness in an environment this isolating is its own kind of threat.

**Protect your electronics.** Bring a Faraday bag or Faraday-lined container for any electronic devices — cameras, communication equipment, backup lights. When you see the sky beginning to distort (the text describes visible shimmer, anti-shadows, and luminous curtains as precursors), *put your electronics in the bag immediately.* The electromagnetic interference may be intense enough to damage circuits, erase data, or cause batteries to discharge or, in extreme cases, thermal-runaway. You don't know the field strengths involved. Assume the worst.

**Do not stand on high points during a storm.** The structures seem to interact with the storms (their hum shifts pitch), and the luminous energy manifests in rising columns and spiraling curtains. You do not want to be the tallest conductive object on a flat plain when energy is discharging through the atmosphere. If a storm approaches, crouch low. Get into a concavity if possible. The mineral crusts may offer some insulation — or they may not. Err on the side of caution.

**The storms are beautiful.** The text describes "sheets of pale light flickering across the sky in colors that are almost colors, that hover at the edge of perception." You will want to watch them. This is natural and probably safe from a distance, but prepare yourself for the *strangeness* of light that moves against the wind, that casts anti-shadows, that behaves in ways your visual processing will struggle to resolve. It may cause headaches. It may cause a sensation the text calls "sitting just barely outside of familiar" — a low-grade perceptual wrongness that is not painful but is deeply, persistently *off*. This is normal here. Breathe through it. Write about it afterward.

**Wear eye protection during storms.** Not sunglasses exactly — the light is pale and not intensely bright — but something that reduces the *complexity* of what your eyes are processing. A light amber or rose tint might help. The flickering, the edge-of-perception colors, the anti-shadows — these are a lot for the visual cortex to handle, especially combined with the general sensory deprivation of the environment.

## Equipment: Interaction with the Environment

**The structures hum.** If you touch them (and you will want to — they are the only manufactured things in this emptiness), you will feel a vibration. Rhythmic. Patient. "A heartbeat measured in hours." This is likely safe in the short term, but you don't know what the structures do, what energy they contain, or whether prolonged contact has effects. **Wear gloves when touching them.** Limit contact time. Do not sleep leaning against one, no matter how tempting the idea of sleeping near *something that is doing something* may be after days of silence.

**The lichen is alive and ancient.** Do not disturb it. Do not step on the large patches if you can avoid it. They are described as growing "for centuries without interruption," and in an environment with this little biological activity, every patch is a significant ecological investment. Walk around them. Photograph them. They are one of the few things here that are *growing*, and in the context of all this geological patience, growth is precious.

**The mineral crusts are chemically unknown.** The text says they are "not quite any mineral you'd name" and have an organized crystal structure. Do not taste them. Do not grind them and inhale the dust. Do not use them as water-source substitutes (they are in brine-like residual moisture, but the chemistry is fully alien). If you collect samples, wear gloves and seal them in inert containers. You don't know what they'll do in a different atmosphere, in your luggage, or in your lungs.

**The fauna — the pale moths, mites, whatever-they-are — is not dangerous.** Probably. They are small, slow, solitary, and appear to feed on lichen and mineral crusts. They are not described as aggressive or venomous. But they are *alien*, and alien biology carries inherent uncertainty. Don't let them crawl on you. If one lands on your gear, brush it off gently. If one dies on your equipment, wipe it away — their desiccation is rapid, and the husks are particulate matter you don't want in your breathing space.

## Water and Food

**There is no water here.** The residual moisture is a mineral brine — a thin, chemically complex solution that is not safe for human consumption without processing you probably don't carry. Bring all the water you need. Calculate generously: the dry air, the altitude, and the physical effort of walking on a plain where distances are vast will dehydrate you faster than you expect. **Minimum 4 liters per day**, more if you're walking significant distances. Consider electrolyte supplements — the dry air and steady breathing will deplete salts.

**Bring all your food.** There is nothing here to eat. The lichen is centuries old and chemically alien. The fauna is sparse and unfamiliar. Pack calorie-dense, low-residue foods: nuts, dried fruit, energy bars, freeze-dried meals. The altitude and dry air may suppress your appetite — this is normal, but you must eat regardless. Set meal times. Follow them. Your body needs fuel to maintain warmth and cognitive function in this environment, and cognitive function is your primary survival tool here.

**Waste management.** Pack out everything. Everything. This is a landscape that has been settling into equilibrium for geological timeframes. Your apple core, your wrapper, your excrement — these are all *new* here, in a place where nothing is new, where even the storms are old. Leave nothing. Take nothing but what you explicitly came to collect. This is not just ethics; it is *manners* in the deepest sense, the way you would not shout in a library, the way you would not track mud into a cathedral. This place has a grammar. Learn it. Observe it.

## Communication and Safety

**You are alone.** If you are traveling with a companion, establish strict check-in protocols before departure. If you are traveling solo — which is how this place feels like it should be experienced, but which is significantly more dangerous — leave detailed plans with someone outside this universe. Tell them: where you are going, how long you plan to stay, when you plan to return, what to do if you don't.

**Communication devices may fail.** The electromagnetic environment is unpredictable. Bring multiple methods: radio, a flare or mirror for visual signaling (though who would see it?), and a dead-drop note at your entry point stating your plan. If your electronics are fried by a storm and you cannot navigate back to your entry point, you need a plan for that. Mark your entry point *very* conspicuously.

**Medical kit.** Standard wilderness first-aid, plus: altitude sickness medication (acetazolamide), topical antibiotic for the inevitable skin cracking from dryness, lip balm (this sounds trivial; it is not — cracked lips in zero humidity are a persistent, distracting misery that erodes morale), eye drops for the same reason, and whatever you personally need for anxiety or panic management if you are susceptible. Bring a pulse oximeter — it's small, it's electronic (protect it in the Faraday bag during storms), and it will tell you objectively whether your body is coping with the altitude when your subjective sense may be unreliable.

## What to Expect, Psychologically, Over Time

**Hours 1–6:** Awe. Awe at the silence, the scale, the wrongness. The world bends at the horizon. The air is thin and strange. Everything is new. You will feel alert, curious, slightly breathless, and profoundly aware that you are somewhere *else*. This is the good part. Savor it.

**Hours 6–24:** Disorientation begins. The silence, which was at first novel, becomes *present* — a thing in the room with you. Your auditory system starts generating phantom sounds. Your sense of scale collapses; you cannot tell how far things are. Time becomes slippery. You may feel a low-grade anxiety that you can't source to anything specific. This is normal. Write in your journal. Eat on schedule. Check your water. Do your breathing exercises.

**Days 2–4:** Adjustment, or breakdown. Most people adjust. The silence becomes a medium you inhabit rather than an absence you mourn. The scale becomes manageable — you develop an intuitive sense of "that structure is about three hours away" even though you're wrong, the *confidence* is stabilizing. You may begin to feel a strange tenderness toward the lichen, the structures, the pale moths. You may begin to understand, in your body rather than your intellect, what it means for something to be *ancient*. This is valuable. This is what you came for.

If you are not adjusting — if the silence is becoming menacing rather than peaceful, if the emptiness is pressing in rather than opening up, if you are hearing things that alarm you or feeling dissociated from your own body — **leave**. Return to your entry point. This place is not for everyone, and there is no shame in that. The stone will not judge you. The stone will not notice.

**Days 4+:** A kind of settling. You become part of the landscape's rhythm. You learn the storms' precursors (the shimmer, the faint light-shift). You learn which structures hum at which frequencies. You learn to see the paths in the stone when the light is right. You may begin to feel that the silence is not empty but *full* — full of age, full of patience, full of the memory of a civilization that wove itself into the molecular structure of a world and then left, or ended, or changed into something you can't perceive. You may feel that you are standing in the footprint of something so far beyond human comprehension that your only valid response is to be *quiet* in it, and *still*, and *careful*, and grateful for the brief, impossible privilege of being there at all.

## Summary Checklist

- **Respiratory:** Supplemental oxygen (full-stay supply), moisture-exchange mask, nasal saline spray
- **Thermal:** Layered clothing system, sleeping bag (5–10°C rated), closed-cell sleeping pad, reflective bivvy layer, beanie, gloves
- **Navigation:** Mechanical compass, wind-up watch, physical trail markers (cairn stones, reflective tape), rangefinder if available
- **Storm protection:** Faraday bag for electronics, amber-tinted eye protection, low-crouch protocol
- **Hydration and nutrition:** 4+ liters water per day, calorie-dense food for full stay, electrolyte supplements
- **Medical:** Altitude sickness meds, lip balm, eye drops, skin-care, pulse oximeter, first-aid kit, personal medications, anxiety management tools
- **Psychological:** Journal and pen, physical books, headphones and music, clear daily routine, pre-established exit criteria
- **Safety:** Entry-point marker, communication plan with off-world contact, multiple signaling methods, waste bags (pack out all waste)
- **Interaction:** Gloves for touching structures, sample containers for mineral crusts, binoculars for observing fauna and paths from distance
- **Attitude:** Patience. Quiet. Respect. You are a guest in a place that has been getting along without guests for longer than your species has existed. Act accordingly.
