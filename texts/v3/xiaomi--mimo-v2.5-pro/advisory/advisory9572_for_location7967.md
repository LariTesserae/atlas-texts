# Preparation Guide for Visiting the Spires of the Drowned Plateau

---

## Before You Leave: What to Expect Emotionally

This is a world that will press in on you from every direction. Not with hostility — with *presence*. The air is thick. The light is low. The sky is a ceiling. The ground is either stone that cuts or rot that sinks. There is almost nowhere to stand that is flat, dry, well-lit, and stable at the same time. You will be wet for the entirety of your visit. You will smell sulfur every moment you breathe. The horizon will be absent on most days, replaced by cloud merging seamlessly with mist merging seamlessly with sea, and you will lose the sense of where you are in space — not vertically, because the vertical is inescapable and always felt, but *horizontally*, as if the concept of "far away" has been dissolved. Everything is either immediate and sharp and close, or it is invisible.

This will be disorienting in a way that is hard to anticipate. Most human beings calibrate their sense of place using the horizon, using the sky, using the ability to look outward and see a long way. Here, those cues are stripped away. You may feel a low-grade anxiety that you cannot attribute to any specific threat. You may feel watched. You may feel small. These are normal responses to an environment where the visual field is dominated by nearby rock, nearby vegetation, nearby cloud, and below all of it, an abyss of warm water that you can never see to the bottom of.

The people who live here have adapted to this perceptual compression over generations. You have not. Prepare yourself for the possibility that you will find the Spires beautiful and claustrophobic in the same breath, and that the beauty will not relieve the claustrophobia.

## What to Bring

### Clothing

**Do not bring anything you care about.** Everything you own will be damaged — by moisture, by sharp rock, by the aggressive fungal decomposition that eats organic materials left in contact with any surface for more than a few hours.

That said:

- **Lightweight, quick-drying layers.** Cotton will never dry here. Wool will mildew within a day. Synthetic fibers — if available in your origin universe — are ideal. If not, the local people make a remarkable bark-cloth textile from the broad-leafed trees that is naturally water-resistant, tough, and roughly textured. Expect to acquire or trade for some once you arrive. In the meantime, bring something that dries fast and does not chafe when wet, because it will be wet.

- **A hooded outer layer that sheds water.** Not an umbrella. An umbrella is useless on the Spires. The mist does not fall from above; it is *in* the air, on every surface, rising from below, condensing on you from all directions simultaneously. You need a garment with a hood that keeps the worst of it off your head and shoulders, not because the water is cold — it is warm — but because the constant dripping and beeding is psychologically exhausting over long periods. The locals seem unbothered by it. You will not be.

- **Footwear with aggressive grip and ankle support.** This is arguably the single most important piece of equipment you can bring. The rock is sharp, wet, and steep. The vegetation-covered ground is unstable — a mat of living rot over stone, often with hidden voids where a fallen tree has rotted away beneath the surface but the moss has grown over the gap. Rope bridges sway. Carved stairs are worn smooth by generations of bare feet and slick with algal growth. You will be walking on surfaces that range from knife-edged basalt to fungal mush to wet rope, often on slopes exceeding forty-five degrees, often in near-darkness, often with a fifteen-hundred-meter drop on one side.

  Bring boots with stiff soles and deep lugs. If you have access to climbing shoes in your origin universe, consider those as a secondary option for the steepest passages. And bring a second pair — your primary pair will be perpetually damp and will develop a smell that cannot be removed.

- **Gloves.** The basalt is sharp enough to slice skin from casual contact. The obsidian flows are sharper. You will be climbing, gripping, pulling yourself up and along rock faces as a matter of routine — the settlements are not connected by anything that a person from a flat-land culture would recognize as a "path." Gloves should be thin enough to maintain dexterity but tough enough to resist abrasion. Leather works; synthetic is better.

- **No metal jewelry, no dangling accessories, no loose straps or trailing fabric.** Everything that hangs or dangles will snag. On rock, on wood, on rope, on the aerial roots that trail from every tree. Snagging on the Spires is not a minor inconvenience — it can mean a fall, and a fall here is almost always fatal.

### Gear

- **A headlamp or other hands-free light source.** If your universe has electric illumination of any portable kind, bring it. The moss-glow of the settlements is beautiful and atmospheric and completely inadequate for any task requiring fine motor control — mending clothing, reading a map, treating a wound. At night, you will need light to move anywhere outside the settlements. Bring a primary and a backup, and bring more fuel or batteries than you think you will need, because the darkness here is not metaphorical. It is absolute. On a cloudy night — which is nearly every night — the interior of the Spire forests is darker than the inside of a closed hand, and the only thing that will tell you there is a cliff edge in front of you is the sound of the surf getting louder below.

- **A length of strong cord or light rope.** At least twenty meters. The local people use braided fiber rope for everything — climbing, anchoring, hauling, binding — and it is excellent, but they may not have extra to give you. Your own cord will serve as backup for the rope bridges and climbing routes, and as a general-purpose utility. If you know how to tie basic climbing and rescue knots, review them before departure.

- **A water-resistant bag or container for anything you want to keep dry.** This includes, critically, any trade goods you are carrying, any notes or records, any food from your home universe, and any medical supplies. The humidity is so pervasive that even items stored inside structures develop surface moisture within hours. Sealed containers — waxed cloth, ceramic jars, treated leather pouches — are the local solution. Bring something equivalent.

- **A knife or cutting tool.** Obsidian blades are available locally and are extraordinarily sharp, but they are also brittle and require skill to use. Bring something with a durable edge. You will use it for food preparation, for cutting cordage, for clearing vegetation on overgrown paths, and possibly for self-defense, though against what is unclear.

- **Sun protection for the rare clearings.** The cloud cover breaks perhaps a few days per month, and when it does, the sun at this latitude — the world is tropical, the water is warm — is intense. You will have been living in perpetual overcast, possibly for weeks, and your skin will have adjusted. A sudden clearing can produce sunburn in under an hour, especially on water-reflected light. Bring a brimmed hat and some form of skin protection if your universe provides it.

### Trade Goods

The people of the Spires have very little. Their material culture is spare, functional, and entirely derived from local resources. They will be curious about you — a stranger appearing on a Spire is an event of considerable note — but curiosity does not equal welcome. Approach with gifts that demonstrate practical value.

**What they want:**
- Metal tools. Any metal tools. Knives, hooks, needles, awls, fishhooks, small saws. The Spires have no metal. Obsidian and bone are their only cutting implements. A simple steel knife would be a treasure of generational significance.
- Strong cord or thin rope, especially anything that resists rot better than their plant-fiber cordage.
- Dried food from outside — anything that keeps, anything that is not fern or fungus or tree-pith. Even bland, stale grain will be novel. Spices will be extraordinary.
- Sealed containers — ceramic, glass, anything watertight.
- Medicine, if you can provide it in forms they can understand and apply. Antiseptic, pain relief, wound closure. Cuts from the rock are frequent and infections in this humidity are fast.

**What not to bring as gifts:**
- Anything purely decorative. These people do not have the surplus for aesthetics that do not serve function.
- Anything that requires a technology base they do not have. Batteries, for example, would be useless and confusing.
- Anything made of untreated wood or plant fiber — it will rot before they can use it, and they will see this as you giving them garbage.

### Medical Kit

Bring more than you think you need, and bring it sealed.

- **Wound care.** You will cut yourself. The basalt and obsidian are sharp, the footing is treacherous, and you are not adapted to this terrain. The local people treat cuts with a poultice of certain mosses that may have genuine antiseptic properties — the fungal competition on the Spires has produced some remarkably antimicrobial organisms — but you should have your own antiseptic, butterfly closures, and bandaging material. Sutures if you know how to place them.

- **Antifungal medication, topical and systemic.** The humidity is near one hundred percent at all times. Fungal infections of the skin, feet, nails, and any wound are not a risk — they are a certainty. The locals manage this with various plant-based preparations, but your unfamiliar biology may react differently to local fungal strains. Bring antifungal cream and oral antifungals if possible.

- **Respiratory considerations.** The sulfur content in the air is low enough that the locals regard it as normal, but it is not zero, and prolonged exposure — days, weeks — may irritate your airways, especially during sleep when breathing is unconsciously deep and slow. If you have any history of asthma or respiratory sensitivity, bring appropriate medication. The volcanic vents occasionally exhale more concentrated sulfur dioxide, and the local people have names for the wind patterns that carry these plumes; learn them, because breathing concentrated volcanic gas is immediately dangerous.

- **Oral rehydration salts or equivalent.** The warmth and humidity will cause you to sweat continuously, even when at rest. The water supply — rainwater collected in stone basins and leaf-cups, or water squeezed from certain succulents — is available but may contain mineral loads your body is not accustomed to. Dehydration combined with mineral imbalance is a real risk.

- **Something for ear pressure and sinus issues.** The verticality of the environment means you will be changing altitude frequently — climbing hundreds of meters in a day — and the warm, humid, low-pressure air can play havoc with sinuses and inner ears. Decongestants and mild pain relief for headaches are worth bringing.

- **Antihistamines and anti-itch medication.** The insects are small but numerous, the plant life includes several species whose fibers or sap cause contact irritation, and the fungal spores in the air are omnipresent. Allergic reactions are likely even in people with no history of allergies, simply because your immune system has never encountered these specific organisms.

## Getting There and First Contact

How you arrive in this world will depend on your method of multiverse travel, but the consistent feature of the Spires is that there is almost nowhere to arrive *to*. The total land area above water is minuscule — a few dozen narrow peaks, each with a summit area of at most a few hundred meters across, much of it too steep to stand on. If you have any control over your point of arrival, aim for one of the inhabited Spires during daylight hours. The settlements are visible from a distance as a haze of smoke and a scatter of pale wood structures clinging to the rock; at night, the moss-glow pinpoints them.

If you arrive in the water, do not panic. The water is warm — nearly body temperature — and buoyant with dissolved minerals. You will float easily. But the visibility is very poor, the currents around the Spires are complex and driven by thermal differentials, and you will not be able to see the submerged rock walls until you are very close to them. Swim upward. Listen for the surf. Follow the sound.

**First contact with the inhabitants** should be approached with extreme care and no presumption of welcome. These are small, isolated communities — the largest settlement numbers perhaps three hundred people — and a stranger arriving from nowhere is genuinely unprecedented. They do not trade with outsiders because there are no outsiders. They do not have a protocol for strangers because they have never needed one.

You will be noticed immediately. The Spires are small enough and the settlements compact enough that a new person is visible within minutes of arrival. Expect to be watched before you are approached. Expect the approach to come from multiple directions — they will want to surround you, not aggressively but descriptively, to understand what you are and where you came from before anyone gets close.

**Do not move quickly. Do not reach for anything. Do not raise your voice.** Sit down if you can find a place to sit. Make yourself small and unthreatening. Present your trade goods openly, on the ground in front of you, and then withdraw your hands. Wait.

The people speak a language or languages you will not understand. They are musical, tonal, with a lot of vowel sounds and a rhythmic quality that may reflect the cadence of the surf. Gestural communication will be partially effective — pointing, showing, miming — but be aware that gestures do not translate cleanly across cultures, and some gestures that seem neutral to you may carry connotations you cannot anticipate. When in doubt, do less rather than more.

If they accept your presence — and this may take days; you may be kept at a distance, observed, discussed, before anyone invites you closer — your status will likely be something like "interesting problem." You are not a threat (you are one person and they are a community), but you are also not known, and in a world this small and precarious, unknown things are dangerous by default until proven otherwise. Patience is not a virtue here; it is a survival strategy.

## Adapting to Daily Life

### Movement

You will be climbing every day. There are no gentle walks on the Spires. The shortest trip between two points — from your sleeping platform to the communal hearth, from the hearth to the water-collection basins, from the basins to the garden terraces — will involve scrambling over rock, gripping rope handholds, edging along ledges no wider than your foot, and crossing rope bridges that sway in the updraft with every step.

The rope bridges deserve special attention. They are made of braided plant fiber, strong but flexible, and they are not designed for your weight if you are a large person. The local people are generally small — lean, light, adapted over generations to climbing. If you are of average or above-average build by the standards of a well-nourished multiverse traveler, you will be heavier than anyone these bridges were built to carry. Test each bridge before committing your full weight. Step lightly. Move slowly. Do not bounce. If the bridge sags significantly or if you hear the anchor-points groan, retreat.

The carved staircases are better — basalt cut into steps, sometimes with rope railings — but they are worn smooth and often wet, and they are steep enough that in some places they are functionally ladders. Going up is exhausting. Going down is terrifying until you learn to trust your footing, which you should not do for at least the first week.

**Your legs will ache.** Your calves, your ankles, your arches — muscles you did not know you had will be screaming within the first day. The local people have spent their entire lives on this terrain and their bodies show it: strong legs, excellent balance, a way of moving that is closer to climbing than walking. You cannot replicate this quickly. Accept that you will be slow, that you will be clumsy, and that you will need to rest far more often than anyone around you.

**Carry nothing in your hands when moving between settlements.** Use your pack. Use a sling across your back. Keep your hands free at all times. The one time you need a handhold and do not have one will be the last time.

### Food and Water

You will eat what they eat or you will go hungry. There is nothing else.

The diet is monotonous and nutritionally challenging. The staples are:

- **Tree-pith bread.** The spongy inner core of the broad-leafed trees, harvested, dried, roasted, and ground into a coarse meal, then mixed with water and fungal flour and baked on hot stones. It is dense, faintly sweet, and has a texture somewhere between bread and dried sponge. It is filling but not flavorful.

- **Boiled fern fronds.** Prepared correctly — boiled twice in fresh water, the first water discarded — they are a green, slightly bitter, nutritionally adequate vegetable. Prepared incorrectly, they are mildly toxic. The local women who do the cooking know the correct preparation. You do not. Eat what they give you and do not forage on your own.

- **Fungal flour and dried fungus.** The luminous shelf-fungi that grow on rotting wood are harvested, dried, and ground into a flour that is used as a thickener, a bread additive, and sometimes brewed into a weak, sour beverage that has a faintly intoxicating effect after several cups. It is an acquired taste. Acquire it. It is one of the more interesting flavors available.

- **Tube-worm casings.** Not the worms themselves, but their fibrous outer shells, which are dried, pounded, and cooked into a gelatinous mass that provides protein and collagen. It tastes of the sea — mineral, faintly metallic — and has a chewy, cartilaginous texture that is difficult to enjoy if you are accustomed to conventional food. Eat it anyway. You need the protein.

- **Small fish.** When they are available — and they are not always; the fish populations are erratic — they are translucent, thin-skinned, and cooked whole on hot stones. They are delicate and taste mostly of salt. They are a treat. Accept them gratefully.

You will lose weight here. There is not enough caloric density in the available food to maintain the body mass of an outsider eating a conventional diet. This is normal. The local people are lean, and not by choice. Budget your energy carefully — eat when you can, rest when you can, and do not attempt strenuous activity (climbing, diving, extended exploration) on an empty stomach.

**Water** is collected from rain — the rain is frequent and heavy — in natural basins in the rock, in hollowed logs, in the cups of large leaves. It is also extracted from certain succulents by squeezing. It tastes faintly of minerals and, when collected from basins near volcanic vents, of sulfur. It is safe to drink — the mineral content is high but not dangerously so for short-term consumption — but it may cause mild gastrointestinal upset for the first few days as your body adjusts.

**Do not drink seawater.** This seems obvious, but when you are thirsty, exhausted, and surrounded by water, the temptation can be real. The water here is warm, mineral-rich, and more saline than the oceans of most worlds.

### Shelter and Sleep

You will be sleeping on platforms, in caves, or in small raised structures. None of them are comfortable by the standards you are accustomed to. The beds are mats of woven bark-cloth over a frame of lashed wood, sometimes with a thin padding of dried moss. They are warm — everything is warm — and damp, and the air is thick with the smell of vegetation and sulfur and the faint sweetness of fungal decomposition.

You will hear things at night. The surf, always. The drip of condensation. The cracking of thermal-stressed rock — those muffled booms that reverberate through the stone. The two-note call of the canopy birds. The rustle of the pale spiders moving through their webs. Occasionally, from far below, a sound that the locals attribute to the sea but that has a quality that is hard to describe — a deep, arrhythmic pulse that seems to come from the water itself, from below the water, from somewhere in the submerged body of the Spire. The divers hear it more clearly when they are down there. Up here, it is faint. But at night, when everything else is quiet, you may feel it more than hear it — a vibration in the rock, in the platform, in the air.

Sleep will be difficult at first. The darkness, the sounds, the dampness, the unaccustomed diet, the physical exhaustion — these will conspire to give you fragmented, shallow sleep. This will improve with time, but bring something to help if you can: a mild sedative, a familiar scent, a piece of cloth from home. Anything that tells your body, on a sensory level, that it is safe to rest.

### Hygiene

Bathing is not a concept here. The humidity keeps your skin perpetually damp; the rain provides periodic fresh-water rinses; the moss, when rubbed on the skin, has a mild cleansing effect that the locals use and that may have genuine antiseptic properties. But you will not be clean in any way you recognize. You will smell of sulfur and vegetation and your own sweat within hours of arrival, and this will not change for the duration of your stay.

Dental care is nonexistent by your standards. The locals chew certain fibrous roots that serve as a rough tooth-cleaning mechanism, and the fungal beverage, ironically, may have mild antibacterial oral effects. But if you have any dental vulnerabilities — loose fillings, sensitive gums, developing cavities — they will be stressed here. Bring a toothbrush and toothpaste if you can, and use them, even though the available water for rinsing tastes of minerals.

Waste disposal is simple: the decomposition cycle on the Spires is so rapid and so thorough that human waste, deposited in designated areas on the lower slopes of the forest, is consumed by fungal and bacterial activity within days. There is no odor problem, because the ambient smell is already so rich with biological process that human waste barely registers. Follow the local practice. Do not deposit waste near water-collection points or food-preparation areas.

## Dangers

### Falls

This is the primary cause of death on the Spires. The terrain is steep, the surfaces are wet, the paths are narrow, and the drops are long. Even the locals fall — rarely, but they fall. You, as an unadapted outsider, are at significantly higher risk. Move slowly. Test every handhold. Never climb or descend without at least one point of secure contact with the rock or a fixed rope. Never cross a bridge or traverse a ledge in darkness without a light source. Never lean over an edge to look at something below, no matter how compelling the view, because the mist and the disorientation of height can cause a sudden, vertiginous loss of spatial awareness that the locals call *the pull* and that has killed people who knew better.

### Cuts and Infection

The rock cuts. The humidity infects. This is a bad combination. Every cut, no matter how small, should be cleaned and treated immediately. The local moss-poultice is effective; combine it with your own antiseptic if you have it. Watch every wound for signs of infection — redness, swelling, heat, pus — because in this environment, infection can go from minor to systemic in forty-eight hours. The fungal organisms here are aggressive and novel to your immune system.

### Volcanic Gas

The Spires are active volcanic structures. The vents that exhale sulfur dioxide are usually gentle and predictable, but they can change. The local people know the signs — a shift in wind direction, a change in the color of the vent steam from white to yellow-green, a sudden increase in the temperature of the rock underfoot. Learn these signs. If the wind carries a concentrated sulfur smell that makes your eyes water and your throat tighten, move upwind immediately and get to higher ground. Volcanic gas is heavier than air and pools in low areas, in gullies, in the fracture-caves. Several of the locals' names for certain gullies translate, roughly, as "do not enter when the mountain breathes."

### The Deep Water

Do not dive. Not unless you are trained, equipped, and prepared for what you may encounter.

The submerged flanks of the Spires are a world unto themselves — warm, dark, rich with chemosynthetic life, threaded with thermal vents that create pockets of superheated water surrounded by near-freezing currents. The visibility is measured in meters at best. The local divers descend on breath-holds along guide-ropes, harvesting tube-worm casings and occasionally the blind crabs that cluster around vent sites. They are skilled, acclimated, and afraid of the deep water. They do not dive alone. They do not dive at night. They do not dive when the deep pulse is strong.

You should observe the same restrictions, and add your own: do not dive without a guide-rope, do not dive without a buddy who knows the route, and do not dive at all if you are not an experienced free-diver, because the conditions — warm water, poor visibility, disorienting thermal layers, strong vent currents — are treacherous even for the experienced.

The things the divers report seeing — the shadows, the shapes — may be nothing. They may be thermal distortion, light-scattering effects in the particulate water, the brain's pattern-recognition function filling in visual gaps with threat-significant shapes. But they may not be nothing. The chemosynthetic ecosystems of the deep vents are ancient by the standards of this young world — they could have been developing in the planet's warm oceans long before the Spires rose — and they are complex enough that large organisms are not impossible, merely unseen. Treat the deep water with respect.

### Psychological Dangers

This world is not designed to be comfortable for the human mind adapted to open landscapes, clear skies, and solid ground. The compression of the visual field, the constant dampness, the darkness, the absence of horizon, the subtle disorientation of perpetual overcast — these add up over time. You may experience:

- **Persistent low-level anxiety without identifiable cause.** This is normal. Your threat-detection systems are active because the environment is genuinely challenging, but the challenges are diffuse and chronic rather than acute and specific, so your anxiety will be free-floating and hard to address. Acknowledge it. Do not try to rationalize it away. Rest when you can.

- **Temporal disorientation.** Without a clear sun, without distinct day-night boundaries, you may lose track of time. The locals mark time by tidal patterns (felt in the swaying of the platforms), by the fruiting cycles of certain fungi, and by the two-note calls of the canopy birds, which are more frequent at dawn and dusk. Learn these rhythms. They will help you maintain a sense of when you are.

- **A growing reluctance to leave.** This is the more insidious psychological effect, and it is worth naming in advance so you can recognize it if it begins. The Spires are small, intimate, knowable. The community, once you are accepted, is close. The rhythm of life is slow, repetitive, and deeply physical. After weeks of this, the thought of returning to a world with horizons, with complexity, with open space and bright light and the demands of a larger life may become genuinely frightening. You may begin to feel that the narrow ledge of the present is enough — that it is, in fact, all there is and all there should be. The locals feel this naturally, because this is their world. You will feel it by acclimation. It is not a sign that you belong here. It is a sign that the Spires are remaking your sense of scale. Be aware of it. Maintain contact with whatever anchor keeps you connected to your larger life.

## What to Watch For

Not dangers. Things worth seeing.

- **The bioluminescent nights.** On the rare clearings, when the cloud cover thins enough to show stars, the sea below lights up with plankton blooms — the *floor of the sky* — and the moss-glow of the settlements reflects off the mist above, and for a few hours the Spires hang in a field of soft light from every direction, and you cannot tell which are stars and which are phosphorescence and which are moss, and the effect is one of suspension, as if the entire world is floating in a warm, luminous void. The locals sit out on their platforms and look at it in silence. You should too.

- **The dawn mist-lift.** Once or twice a month, the mist lifts at dawn rather than thickening, and for a few minutes — never more than fifteen — you can see the other Spires. They appear out of the haze one by one, dark and jagged against a sky that has, briefly, the color of apricots, and you realize how far apart they are, how isolated each community is, how vast the water is between them, and how improbable it is that anyone lives here at all. Then the mist closes in and they vanish, and you are alone on your Spire again.

- **The fungal fruiting at night.** The shelf-fungi are faintly bioluminescent — pale green-gold, like the cultivated moss but more erratic, flickering. When a major fruiting event happens — a dead tree falling and being consumed by a sudden eruption of fungal growth — the forest floor lights up in a slow, spreading bloom that can be visible from the settlements. The locals consider it beautiful and slightly sacred. They are right.

- **The deep-pulse nights.** On certain nights, the arrhythmic vibration from below intensifies, and the platforms hum, and the water around the Spires develops a standing-wave pattern that the divers say can be felt on the submerged walls. The locals hold communal gatherings on these nights — not celebrations exactly, more like vigils — and they sing, a low, harmonic vocalization that seems to resonate with the pulse, and the effect is startling: the human voice and the geological voice and the oceanic voice blending into something that feels, against all rational expectation, like a conversation. Join them if you are invited. Listen.

## Departure

When you leave — and you should leave, even if part of you does not want to — carry with you the understanding that you have visited a world that is not going anywhere. The Spires are not rising toward some future of dry land and open sky. The water is not receding toward some eventual revelation of continents. This is a world in a state, not a world on a journey. The people who live here are not waiting. They are not enduring. They are simply *being*, on the thin bright edge between the deep and the air, and they have made a life there that is as complete as any life on any world.

You will miss the warmth. You will miss the drip of condensation. You will, strangely, miss the sulfur.

You will not miss the darkness.
