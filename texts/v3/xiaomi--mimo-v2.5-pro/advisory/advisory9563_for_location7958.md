# Preparing for the Forgebanks of Unsettled Altitude: A Visitor's Guide

---

## Before You Leave: Understanding What You're Walking Into

The first thing to understand is that nothing in your prior experience of travel will have adequately prepared you for Tolmere. You may have visited high-altitude cities. You may have visited places with strange weather. You may have visited places where the geometry felt off, or where the local wildlife regarded you with unsettling intelligence. You may have visited places that were still, in some sense, *unfinished*. Tolmere is all of these things simultaneously, and it is none of them exactly, and it will test not just your physical resilience but your ability to tolerate a world that refuses to be fully committed to the laws you are accustomed to depending on.

The second thing to understand is that the people who live there are not insane and are not suffering. They have simply adapted to conditions that would break a person who arrived expecting the world to behave. If you arrive expecting the world to behave, you will be broken within a week. If you arrive expecting to be confused, uncomfortable, and perpetually disoriented, and you are at peace with that, you will be fine.

## Physical Preparation

### Altitude

Tolmere's inhabited zones sit between 4,200 and 5,200 meters. If you come from a world where you live near sea level, this alone can kill you. Begin acclimatizing weeks before departure if your multiverse travel allows for staged arrivals. If you can arrange to spend time at progressively higher elevations — 2,500 meters for several days, then 3,500, then 4,000 — do so. Your body needs time to increase red blood cell production, to adjust your breathing rate, to learn to operate on less oxygen. People who arrive in Tolmere directly from low elevation spend their first week with splitting headaches, nausea, a persistent feeling of being slightly drunk, and an inability to climb more than a single flight of stairs without gasping. This is not dangerous in itself — the Tolmereans are accustomed to newcomers staggering around like recovering patients — but it is miserable, and it makes everything else about the experience worse.

If your world has pharmaceutical altitude aids — acetazolamide, dexamethasone, or their equivalents — bring them. Bring more than you think you need. The altitude in Tolmere is not the worst of what the environment does to your lungs, but it is the foundation upon which everything else is stacked.

You will, even after acclimatization, move more slowly here than you are accustomed to. Accept this now. Tolmereans do not rush. The city does not reward rushing. The distances may be short today, or they may be impossibly long, and either way you will be breathing hard by the end.

### Respiratory Protection

The mineral haze is not smoke, but your lungs do not know that. The microscopic stone dust — primarily silicate particles, with traces of iron oxide and calcium carbonate — will coat the lining of your respiratory tract within hours of arrival. Short-term exposure causes irritation, a dry cough, and a sensation of grittiness in the back of the throat that never quite goes away. Long-term residents develop a characteristic wheeze that they consider perfectly normal. Newcomers find it alarming.

Bring a respirator. Not a surgical mask — that is useless against particles this fine — but a proper filtering respirator rated for mineral dust, equivalent to an N95 or better. You will need to wear it constantly during your first several days, and you should continue to wear it in the industrial quarter and near active rawite faces indefinitely. The locals do not always wear them, but the locals have lungs that have been conditioning themselves for years or decades. You do not.

You will notice, after a few days, that even with the respirator, you begin to develop a faint chalky taste in your mouth. This is the condensation — the mineral-laden water that coats every surface and that you are perpetually touching, breathing near, and inadvertently ingesting. There is no practical way to avoid this entirely. It is unpleasant but not, apparently, harmful in the short term. Long-term residents have a peculiar mineral pallor to their skin that may or may not be related.

### The Cold, the Damp, and the Warmth

Temperature at altitude is obviously lower than at sea level, and Tolmere exists in a narrow band between 2°C and 10°C for most of the year. This does not sound extreme. It is not extreme. What is extreme is the *dampness*. The constant condensation means that every surface — every handrail, every bench, every doorframe, every stone step — is slick with moisture. The air itself is saturated. Clothes do not dry. If you hang a wet garment in your quarters, it will be wetter in the morning, because the condensation will have added to it. The Tolmereans have developed various solutions — heated drying closets in every dwelling, powered by bleed-off from the geothermal lines; oiled leather overcoats that repel the worst of the moisture; a technique of layering fabrics so that the innermost layer remains tolerably dry even as the outer layers become sodden.

Bring layers. Bring wool or synthetic equivalents that retain warmth when wet. Bring nothing made of cotton — cotton in Tolmere is a hypothermia delivery system. Bring waterproof outer layers, not for rain (which is rare) but for the perpetual damp. Bring a hat — the condensation collects on hair and runs down the neck with miserable efficiency.

Here is the paradox: the rock itself is warm. The rawite faces that form the walls, the floors, the foundations of the city bleed geothermal heat at a steady, gentle rate. Press your palm against a rawite wall in any older district and you will feel a faint warmth, like touching the side of a sleeping animal. In the tunnels and the lower districts, near the active domes, the warmth is more pronounced — enough to heat water, enough to keep the air comfortable even at altitude. The city is, in a sense, heated from below and chilled from above, and the result is a perpetual gradient that you will learn to navigate: stand near the walls when you are cold, move to the center of a room when the walls are too warm, and never, ever lean your bare skin against a rawite face that hasn't been checked for temperature. Some of them are still hot enough to blister.

### Eyes and Skin

The dimness of Tolmere is not darkness, but it is not light either. Your eyes will adjust, but slowly. The daywash — that diffuse, directionless gray-white glow — is bright enough to see by but never bright enough to feel *illuminated*. You will find yourself squinting not because it is dark but because there are no shadows, no contrast, no directional cues. Depth perception becomes unreliable. Surfaces flatten. The world takes on a quality of unreality that is, at first, merely disorienting and, after several days, becomes psychologically oppressive in a way that is hard to articulate. You will begin to crave a sharp edge of shadow, a bright point of light, anything to anchor your visual field to something definite.

Bring sunglasses. This sounds absurd, given the dimness, but you will need them for the veil-shifts (discussed below). And bring, if you can, a small artificial light source — a lantern, a headlamp, something that produces directional, hard-edged light. You will not need it to see. You will need it to *feel*. The Tolmereans burn oil lamps in their homes not because they need the light but because the flame provides something the daywash cannot: a center, a point of warmth and definition in the gray. You will understand this within your first night.

The mineral haze dries the skin. Despite the constant dampness on surfaces, the air itself — paradoxically — strips moisture from exposed skin through the abrasive action of the suspended particles. Bring moisturizer. Bring it in quantity. The locals use rendered fat from veil-tree capsules mixed with palemoss extract; if you can tolerate the smell (wet stone and something faintly fungal), it works better than anything you will have brought from elsewhere.

## Psychological Preparation

### The Distances

This is the part that most visitors find hardest to adapt to, and it is the part for which there is no physical preparation — only mental.

The distance between two fixed points in Tolmere changes. Not daily, not hourly, but *continuously*, and unpredictably. The walk from your lodging to the nearest forge entrance might be 400 meters one morning and 1,200 meters the next. The path is the same. The landmarks are the same. The distance is different. Your stride does not change, your pace does not change, but the amount of ground you must cover to reach the same place *changes*.

The Tolmerean response to this is to stop measuring. They do not think in kilometers. They think in *effort* — "that is a half-hour walk" — and they accept that the same half-hour walk may cover different distances on different days. They leave early for everything. They carry supplies for journeys that may take three times as long as expected. They do not complain when they arrive early, and they do not apologize when they arrive late, because the concept of lateness assumes a fixed distance, and there are no fixed distances here.

You will want to measure. You will bring a GPS device or a rangefinder or a length of string, and you will try to quantify the changes, and you will fail, because the changes do not follow a pattern. The drift-cartographers of Tolmere have been mapping the spatial fluctuations for seventy years and have produced charts of extraordinary detail and total predictive uselessness. They can tell you what the distances were yesterday. They cannot tell you what they will be tomorrow. Accept this. Stop counting your steps. Walk until you arrive.

A practical note: the spatial instability is worse at higher elevations and near active rawite upwellings. The lower saddle-valleys, where the oldest districts sit, are relatively stable — distances there vary by no more than ten or twenty percent. If you are finding the upper districts intolerable, descend. The valleys will feel almost normal. Almost.

### The Time

Your watch will not work correctly here, or rather, it will work correctly but the information it gives you will not correspond to local experience. The time-dilation zones near the forges are well-documented and well-managed — the master-clock in the lower district is the reference, and everyone in the city knows how to convert — but the experience of living in a place where time moves at different rates in different locations is genuinely disorienting in a way that people do not warn you about adequately.

You will climb from the lower district to the forge quarter, spend what feels like four hours working or exploring, and descend to find that six hours have passed below. Or two. You will have lunch and discover that, in the neighborhood across the valley, it is still morning. You will try to arrange meetings with people in other districts and learn that "noon" means something different depending on which massif you are standing on.

The Tolmereans handle this through a system of "clock-quarters" — designated locations where the master-clock's time is publicly displayed, and everyone synchronizes to it at the start of each day. Carry a timepiece and reset it at the nearest clock-quarter every morning. Do not trust your internal sense of how long things have taken. It is wrong. The mountain is lying to your body about time, and your body is too polite to argue, and you will believe it until you check a clock and realize that three hours vanished without asking your permission.

### The Shapes in the Stone

You will see them. There is no avoiding them if you visit the active zones. You will be standing in a tunnel where the rawite is still translucent, still warm, still deciding what it wants to be, and you will look into the amber depths of the stone and you will see structures. Geometries. Spirals. Things that are not rocks and not fossils and not anything with a name.

You will want to know what they are. You will ask the miners, and they will shrug. You will ask the philosophers, and they will give you four different theories, none of which will satisfy you. You will look at them for a long time and feel, with increasing certainty, that they are looking back.

My advice: do not stare. Not because it is dangerous — there is no evidence that it is dangerous — but because the more time you spend trying to resolve what you are seeing into a familiar category, the more distressed you will become when you cannot. They are shapes in young rock. They are not alive. They are not dead. They are not waiting. They are simply *present*, the way a cloud is present, or a knot in wood is present, and they will fade as the rock matures, and the rock will be stone, and the stone will be mountain, and the mountain will be old and cold and finished and the shapes will be gone, and in a million years no one will know they were ever there.

If you find that you cannot stop thinking about them — if you find yourself returning to the same cooling face day after day, pressing your hands against the translucent surface, watching for changes — leave. Descend. Spend a week in the lower valleys where the stone is old and opaque and finished. The shapes are not calling you. The shapes are not doing anything. But this place is, as the Tolmereans say, *thin*, and the things you bring into the thinness — your curiosity, your pattern-recognition, your human need to *understand* — interact with the thinness in ways that no one fully comprehends, and it is better to err on the side of leaving too early than too late.

### The Veil-Shifts

Once or twice a year, the mineral haze lifts from a section of the city, and for hours or days, that section is exposed to unfiltered, direct sunlight. If you happen to be in the affected area, here is what will happen:

The light will hit you like a physical force. After days or weeks of daywash — that soft, gray, directionless glow — the sudden arrival of real, directional, hard-edged sunlight is *painful*. Not metaphorically. Your eyes will water and burn. Your skin, adapted to the dim, will feel the UV radiation within minutes. The palemoss on every surface around you will begin to brown and curl. The veinferns will retract, fronds folding in on themselves like hands closing. The haze-drifters overhead will rise rapidly, their translucent bodies catching the light for a moment before they flee upward into the remaining haze.

And the world will become *solid*. This is the part that visitors describe as the most disturbing. The spatial flex will stop. A walk that has been 800 meters for a week will become 800 meters, exactly, permanently, with no variation. Time will run at a single, consistent rate. The background hum of the forges will become distinct and sharp — individual sounds separated from the whole, like instruments in an orchestra suddenly playing solo. You will be able to see to the horizon, see the mountain in full, see the curve of the ridgeline and the precise angle of every building and every bolt and every anchor point, and you will feel, as many visitors have described, *claustrophobic*. As if the world, which has been vast and vague and breathing, has suddenly drawn a tight box around you. As if the merciful ambiguity that makes life here bearable has been withdrawn.

The Tolmereans close their shutters during veil-shifts. They stay inside. They wait. Some of them describe the exposed light as beautiful — a reminder of what the world is like elsewhere, a reminder that they are choosing to live in the thinness and could, if they wished, return to the clarity. Others describe it as an invasion. Both descriptions are accurate.

If you are caught outside during a veil-shift: cover your eyes. Cover your skin. Find shelter. If none is available, descend — the shifts tend to affect higher elevations first, and the lower valleys may still be shrouded. Do not try to enjoy the light. It is not the light you are accustomed to. It is the light of a world that has not yet decided what it wants to be, suddenly forced into clarity, and the clarity is violent.

When the haze returns — and it always returns — you will feel relief so profound it may bring you to tears. This is normal. This is the place working on you. This is the beginning of understanding why the people here stay.

## Gear and Supplies

In summary, and with additions:

**Essential:**
- Respirator (N95 or better, with replacement filters — the mineral dust clogs them within days)
- Layered, moisture-wicking clothing (wool, synthetics; no cotton)
- Waterproof outer shell (for condensation, not rain)
- Sturdy, waterproof boots with excellent grip (every surface is wet; every surface is slick; the terrain is uneven and may change angle without warning as the ground shifts beneath it)
- A directional light source (lantern, headlamp, lamp-oil if your light requires fuel)
- Extra food and water for journeys that may take longer than expected (beetle larvae are available locally and are, I am told, fine; *sett*, the compressed moss cake, is an acquired taste that most visitors do not acquire; the condensation water, filtered through moss-beds, is safe but flat and faintly sweet in a way that becomes cloying after a week)
- Skin moisturizer and lip balm (the mineral haze will crack your lips within hours)
- Sunglasses (for the veil-shifts)
- A timepiece that you are willing to recalibrate daily
- An attitude of patience that borders on resignation

**Recommended:**
- Earplugs (the deep geothermal thrum in the forge quarter is constant and subsonic and some visitors find it produces anxiety, headaches, or a persistent feeling of dread that is not psychological but vibrational)
- A small mirror (for checking your skin for mineral deposits, which accumulate in skin folds and can cause irritation; also useful for checking the color of your lips, which in Tolmere tend toward a grayish pallor that indicates dust accumulation in the bloodstream — if this happens, increase your respirator use and descend if possible)
- Something beautiful that is not from this place. A photograph. A flower pressed in a book. A piece of polished wood. You will need, at some point, to look at something that was made by a world that has finished becoming. It will help you remember that such worlds exist, and that you came from one, and that you can return to one. This is not a warning. This is practical advice.
- A notebook. The drift-cartographers keep meticulous records of spatial fluctuations, and you are welcome to observe and contribute, and the act of measurement — even futile measurement — is a comfort to a mind that is accustomed to a world where measurement means something.

## Cultural Preparation

The Tolmereans are hospitable but not effusive. They are accustomed to visitors — traders, scholars, the occasional thrill-seeker — and they will offer you lodging, food, and information freely, but they will not coddle you. If you are cold, they will point you to the wall. If you are lost, they will point you downhill. If you are frightened by the shapes in the stone, they will look at you with something that is not quite pity and not quite contempt and say, "Yes. They are there." And then they will wait for you to decide what to do about it, because in Tolmere everyone must decide what to do about the things that are there, and no one can decide for you.

Do not complain about the distances. Do not complain about the damp. Do not complain about the haze. These are not inconveniences in Tolmere. They are the *medium*. Complaining about them is like complaining about the existence of the mountain. The mountain exists. The damp exists. The distances change. You are in a place that is still being made, and the making is slow and wet and strange, and you are standing on the surface of a process that does not care about your schedule or your comfort or your need for the world to make sense.

The Tolmereans have a saying: *"The rock does not owe you stone."* It means: the world is not required to be finished for you. You are standing on rawite. It is becoming granite, or basalt, or something that does not yet have a name. It will get there. But not on your schedule.

Listen to the veil-trees. They sing in the wind, a low resonance that carries through the haze, and it is the most beautiful sound in this place, and possibly in any place, and it will be the thing you remember most clearly when you leave. If you are here long enough, you will begin to hear harmonics in it — patterns that may or may not be patterns, sequences that may or may not be intentional — and you will wonder if the trees are communicating, and you will never know, and this will become comfortable in a way that would have been impossible when you arrived.

## When You Leave

You will be changed by Tolmere, and the change will be subtle and persistent and you will not fully understand it for weeks after you return to a world that has finished becoming.

You will find, in the first days of your return, that you flinch at shadows. Not with fear — with *surprise*. Shadows are so sharp elsewhere. So definite. A shadow here means the light is real and directional and the object casting it is *certain of its position*, and after Tolmere, that certainty will feel aggressive. Unnatural. You will find yourself reaching out to touch walls to check for warmth, and being faintly disappointed when they are cold, and you will miss, with an ache you cannot explain, the feeling of a world that is still deciding what it wants to be.

You will miss the dripping. The constant, arrhythmic patter of condensation in the channels — the baseline sound of a world slowly, slowly arriving. You will be sitting in a dry room in a dry city on dry ground that has been solid for a million years, and you will realize that you cannot hear dripping, and the silence will feel *wrong*, as if something has stopped breathing.

You will hear the veil-trees in dreams for months. The low, haunting organ-note, rising and falling, the sound of something vast and fungal and alive and patient, singing to the haze.

And sometimes, in quiet moments, you will press your hand against a wall — any wall, in any world — and you will wait for warmth, and you will feel, for just a moment, a faint sense of betrayal that the wall is not rising.

The mountain rises. The city rises with it. You do not rise with it. You go home, and home is still, and home is finished, and home is *stone*, and you will carry the memory of rawite — warm, translucent, deciding — like a splinter in the architecture of your understanding, and it will never fully resolve, and you will learn to live with it the way the Tolmereans live with everything.

Patiently. Without expectation. With your hand against the wall, waiting for warmth that is not there, remembering the world that is still being made.
