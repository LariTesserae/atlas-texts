# Preparing for the Flooded Tarns of Veska

---

## I. Understand What You Are Walking Into (and Through, and Into Up to Your Waist)

The first thing to internalize is that there is no version of visiting Veska where you stay dry. There is no dock, no pier, no shore-based guesthouse, no dry patch of granite with a welcome mat. The settlement exists in water. The pathways between dwellings are water. The floors inside the homes are water-slick living plant over water. If you are the kind of traveler who clings to the comfort of dry land — who needs a solid floor, a place to sit that isn't damp, a wall that doesn't bead with moisture — Veska will be a profound and unending discomfort. This isn't a place you visit and then retreat to a dry camp. You will be wet from the moment you step off the granite rim until the moment you climb back out. Not submerged (not in the shallower districts, at least), but thoroughly, inescapably damp. Your clothes will be damp. Your skin will be damp. Anything you bring with you will be damp within the hour.

Accept this before you go. It is not a problem to be solved. It is the medium you will be moving through.

---

## II. Physical Preparation

**Altitude acclimatization.** 2,400 meters is high enough to affect you, especially if you're arriving from low elevation. At this altitude you may experience mild headaches, shortness of breath on exertion, disrupted sleep, and a general feeling of being slightly *off* for the first two to three days. If your multiverse transit deposits you at altitude suddenly — if you arrive from sea level to 2,400 meters in a single step — you will feel it immediately. The exertion of wading, climbing kelp-columns, pulling yourself onto platforms, and navigating the settlement will compound this. You will get winded doing things that should not wind you. Plan for it. Arrive a day before you intend to actually interact with the settlement, if possible. Rest on the granite rim above the tarn. Drink water. Let your blood thicken at its own pace. There is no rushing this.

**Cold tolerance.** The water is snowmelt-fed and never warm. Even in summer it will numb exposed skin within minutes of submersion. The air at this altitude can swing from near-freezing at night to surprisingly warm in direct sun during the day, but the water is a constant cold anchor. You will be in it and on it and surrounded by it. Your core body temperature will be under assault the entire time. This is not a place where hypothermia is dramatic — it doesn't hit you like a wall. It creeps. You'll feel fine and then you'll realize your hands don't work properly and your thinking has slowed and you're shivering and you didn't notice when it started. The Veskan people have lived with this for generations and their bodies are adapted — denser subcutaneous fat, higher baseline metabolic rate, a cultural knowledge of how to rewarm efficiently. You are not adapted. You will need to be deliberate about maintaining your core temperature.

**Core strength and grip.** The settlement is navigated by wading through waist-deep water, climbing knotted rope-ladders of braided algae, pulling yourself onto platforms, and squeezing through kelp-column doorways that were built for people who have lived in this environment their whole lives. The algae is slippery. The stonewort flooring is slippery. The rope-ladders are damp and organic and they flex and stretch in ways that rope should not. You will need to be able to hold your own weight with one hand while your feet find purchase on something that gives under you. This is not mountaineering, but it is a physical environment that demands more than casual fitness. If you have shoulder, knee, or grip issues, they will be tested severely.

**Wading endurance.** You will be on your feet in water for hours at a time. There are no chairs in the Veskan sense (there are woven platforms you can sit or crouch on, but getting to them requires navigating the water first). The water provides buoyancy, which helps, but it also provides resistance, and the uneven substrate — granite bedrock overlaid with living and dead plant matter, soft in some places, impossibly hard in others — will work your ankles and calves in ways that dry-land walking does not. If you're not accustomed to spending long stretches standing and moving in thigh-deep water, start preparing your legs and feet well before you go.

## III. Clothing and Gear

**Forget everything you know about mountain dress codes.** You are not hiking. You are not climbing. You are wading through a living aquatic settlement at altitude. The usual mountain layering system — base layer, insulating layer, shell — will fail you here because the shell layer traps moisture against your body (you will be constantly wet from the outside in, not sweating from the inside out) and the insulating layer will become waterlogged and heavy.

What works:

- **A single layer of close-fitting wool.** Merino or a similar cold-water-adapted fiber. Wool retains some insulating capacity when wet, it doesn't chafe as badly as synthetic fabrics when soaked, and it dries faster than cotton (which you should not bring in any form — cotton at this altitude, in this water, is a hypothermia accelerant). A long-sleeved wool top and close-fitting wool leggings. Nothing loose. Loose fabric will snag on the kelp-fronds, which are everywhere, and the constant catching and pulling will drive you to distraction.

- **A thin wind layer that you can remove easily.** The air temperature will vary significantly between sun and shade, wind and calm. You need something to break the wind when it comes over the ridge, but you also need to be able to strip it off quickly when the sun hits and you overheat. A simple, light shell jacket with a front closure. Nothing complicated. You'll be taking it on and off constantly.

- **Footwear is critical and difficult.** The ideal is something that protects your feet from the stonewort (which, as the description says, crunches underfoot like calcium celery — and it will cut you if you step on it barefoot), provides grip on wet living algae, and doesn't fill with silt and debris that you can't remove. Traditional Veskan footwear, if they'll make you a pair, would be ideal — woven stonewort sandals with algae-fiber bindings that grip the kelp surfaces. Failing that, thin-soled water shoes with aggressive tread. Avoid anything with a thick sole; you need to feel the substrate beneath you to navigate it safely, and thick soles will make you clumsy on the uneven surfaces. Neoprene socks can help with warmth but will make your feet sweat on the approach, creating their own problems. There is no perfect solution. Accept some discomfort.

- **Nothing you can't afford to lose to water.** Pockets are useless unless sealed. A belt pouch with a waterproof closure for small essentials — that's it. Anything dangling, anything loose, anything hanging from your body will catch on kelp, drag in water, and eventually be surrendered to the lake.

- **Head covering.** A wool beanie, close-fitting. Your head will be above water most of the time, but you will occasionally need to duck under kelp-arches, and a bare head against wet algae in cold air is a recipe for rapid heat loss. Also: the sun at 2,400 meters is more intense than you expect, even through clouds. You will sunburn. A close-fitting cap helps.

## IV. What to Bring

**Bring almost nothing.** This is the most important gear advice for Veska. The environment destroys equipment. Everything you own will be wet, pulled at by living plant, potentially stepped on by you or someone else in murky water, and subjected to a level of organic dampness that degrades most materials faster than you'd think. A leather satchel will mold within a day. A metal tool will develop surface corrosion within a week. Paper becomes paste. Fabric rots if it doesn't dry, and nothing dries quickly in Veska.

What to bring:

- **A small, hard-sided waterproof container** — bone, treated wood, or ceramic if you can get it — for anything you truly cannot lose: notes, a small trade good, a communication device, medicine. This container should be small enough to carry on your person at all times, because you will not have a secure dry storage space.

- **Trade goods, not gifts.** The Veskan economy is based on exchange, not charity. Do not arrive with trinkets intended as "gifts" — this will mark you as someone who sees them as quaint and beneath you. Bring something useful. Obsidian or flint blanks (if you can source them) are highly valued. Bone tools. Dried plant fiber cordage from a different biome, which they can study and potentially adapt. Ground calcium paste from a lower elevation (they use it in construction; it saves them a grinding step). Salt, if you can carry it — they have no local source and must trade for it, and it's valuable for food preservation. The point is: arrive as a trading partner, not a tourist. Ask what they need. Listen to the answer.

- **No food unless asked to bring it.** The Veskan diet is specific — fish, algae, newt — and it sustains them. Bringing outside food implies theirs is insufficient. If you have specific dietary needs (allergies, medical restrictions), bring what you must, but explain it as a personal limitation, not a preference. And be open to trying what they offer. The dried kelp is chewy and mineral-flavored. The fish are small and bony. The stonewort paste is calcium-sharp. It's all edible. It's all nutritious. It's an acquired taste.

- **A small, well-maintained iron blade, if you have one.** Metal is precious here. A single iron knife is a generational heirloom. Offering one as a trade good — or even lending one for the Veskan to examine and use — would be an extraordinary gesture of respect and trust. But only if the blade is good. A cheap or damaged iron tool is worse than useless; it's insulting.

## V. Sensory and Psychological Preparation

This is the part most travel guides skip, and it's the part that will matter most.

**The noise.** You are not prepared for how loud Veska is. The description tells you this, and you will read it and think you understand. You will not understand. The natural amphitheater of stone takes every sound — the waterfall, the birds, the insects, the creaking kelp — and compresses it, reflects it, layers it on itself until the acoustic environment is a constant, unbroken wall of sound. There is no quiet moment. At night, when the birds are slightly less active, the waterfall and the insects fill the silence. In the morning, the birds resume. There is no pause. If you are someone who needs silence to think, to sleep, to regulate your emotions — and most people are, whether they know it or not — Veska will tax you in ways you don't expect. It's not painful. It's not distressing in any obvious way. But it is *relentless*, and relentlessness has a psychological cost.

Prepare by spending extended periods in noisy environments where you cannot control the sound. Sit next to a waterfall for six hours. Spend a day in a crowded market. Learn to think *through* noise rather than waiting for it to stop, because it will not stop.

**The wetness.** I've said this already, but it bears repeating in the psychological context. You will never be dry. Your brain is wired to seek dry shelter — it is one of the oldest mammalian drives, and it has served you well in every environment you've ever visited. In Veska, it will misfire constantly, because there is no dry shelter to seek. The closest you will come is a platform three meters above the waterline where the air is slightly less humid and the spray from the waterfall is slightly less reaching, but even there, the platform is damp, the ropes are damp, and the kelp columns on either side of you are damp and alive and breathing moisture into the air. If you are not careful, the constant low-grade discomfort of wetness will erode your mood, your patience, and your ability to be present in the moment. You will start to fixate on the next dry place. There is no next dry place. Let go of the need. It will free you.

**The intimacy.** Veskan homes are two meters in diameter. Two meters. You will be sharing that space — damp, dark-green, faintly translucent walls around you — with the family that has invited you in. There is no privacy in the Western sense. The walls are alive and thin. Sound passes through them. Light passes through them. The Veskan people have adapted to this by developing a cultural framework where proximity does not imply intrusion — where being physically close to someone, hearing their breathing, seeing the green light move across their skin as the sun shifts, is simply *how life is*, the way a fish does not notice water. You have not adapted to this. You will feel exposed. You will feel like you are intruding. You will want to apologize for your own presence. Resist the urge. Your discomfort is yours to manage, not theirs to accommodate. Accept the closeness. Breathe through it. It will feel less strange faster than you think, because the environment is so total, so immersive, that the normal social anxieties lose their grip. There is nowhere to retreat to, so you stop retreating, and something else takes its place.

**The patience.** Nothing in Veska is fast. The algae grows slowly. The people speak slowly (or seem to — they are not slow people, but their rhythms are tuned to a place where urgency is meaningless). The fish move slowly in the cold water. Even the waterfall is a slow, constant sound rather than a dramatic roar. If you arrive from a place of speed — a city, a busy trade route, a world where things happen on schedules and deadlines — the pace of Veska will feel like wading through syrup. This is not lethargy. This is wisdom earned over centuries of living in a place where the only building material takes a decade to reach structural density and a home takes three generations to complete. The Veskan understand, in their bones, that there is no way to rush the things that matter. You will need to learn this, at least temporarily, or the visit will feel like waiting for something that never arrives. Stop waiting. The visit is the thing.

## VI. Health and Safety

**Leeches.** They exist in the tarn. The Veskan have developed cultural and botanical countermeasures — specific kelp species that repel them, sleeping platforms lined with these species, knowledge of which areas of the lake are more or less infested. Learn this knowledge immediately upon arrival. Ask. The Veskan will likely find your concern amusing (they've been dealing with leeches their entire lives), but they will also understand that you are not adapted and will help you. If you do get a leech, do not pull it off. Let the Veskan remove it their way. The mouthparts can tear and become infected, and infection in a permanently damp environment at altitude is a serious problem. Carry no modern antiseptic that you can't keep dry — but if you have a sealed, waterproof medical kit with wound care supplies, bring it. Not because the Veskan can't treat wounds — they can, with botanical knowledge refined over generations — but because you are an outsider with an outsider's immune system, and a cut that a Veskan shrugs off could become a serious problem for you.

**Waterborne illness.** The tarn is cold and mineral-rich, which helps, but it is also an enclosed alpine lake with dense organic matter at every depth. If you have a sensitive digestive system, do not drink the water unboiled. The Veskan may drink it straight — they are adapted; you are not. If you can bring a small metal vessel for boiling water, do so. This is one of the few pieces of metal equipment worth carrying.

**Sun exposure.** 2,400 meters. Thin atmosphere. Intense UV even on cloudy days. You will be in water that reflects light upward while the snowfields above reflect it downward. You are essentially in a light-box. If you burn easily, bring a physical barrier — a close-fitting hat, coverage for your neck and ears. Chemical sunscreen will wash off in minutes.

**Falls.** The kelp surfaces are slippery. The stonewort flooring is slippery. The rope-ladders are slippery. You will fall. Probably multiple times. The water is shallow enough in most of the settlement that falling means getting wetter than you already are and possibly striking a submerged granite cobble with a knee or shin. This is not life-threatening, but it is demoralizing if it happens repeatedly, and bruised shins in a damp environment heal slowly. Move slowly. Test every surface before committing your weight. Watch how the Veskan move — they have a gait adapted to this environment, a kind of slow, flat-footed shuffle with a wide base that keeps their center of gravity low. It looks ungainly. It works. Copy it.

**Infection prevention.** You will cut yourself. The environment will ensure it. Bring what you need to clean and close small wounds, and be fanatical about it. A minor scrape that you ignore in a dry environment becomes a serious liability in a place where you are never dry, where organic matter is in constant contact with your skin, and where the nearest proper medical care is a mountain descent away. The Veskan have botanical remedies that work for them — and they may work for you — but don't rely on this as your only option. Prepare for the worst case.

## VII. Cultural Preparation

**Arrive as a guest, not an observer.** The Veskan will know immediately whether you are there to see them or to be with them. The difference matters. If you arrive with visible note-taking equipment, if you stare at the kelp-columns with the wide eyes of someone cataloguing wonders, if you ask questions with the rapid-fire cadence of someone trying to capture everything before it disappears — they will be polite, but they will not let you in. Not really. You will see the surface of Veska. You will not see Veska.

Instead: arrive slowly. Sit with them. Let the first hours be silent if silence is what comes. Eat what they offer. Watch how they move through the water. Ask one question, not ten. Let the answer unfold at its own pace. The Veskan are not performers. They are people living in a place that happens to be extraordinary, and they have been doing it long enough that the extraordinariness is invisible to them. What they will share with you is not the wonder — it's the ordinariness. The way a mother checks a kelp-wall for structural weakness with the same absent competence that a carpenter checks a beam. The way children play in the channels with the casual fluency of creatures that have never known land. The way an elder traces a stalk's growth with a finger and can tell you, by texture alone, which year it thickened, which winter stressed it, which season it began to lean. This is the real Veska. It's not dramatic. It's the accumulated fluency of a people who have become native to an environment no one else would recognize as habitable.

**Do not pity them.** You will be tempted. You will see the dampness, the cold, the limited diet, the absence of metal, the cramped living spaces, and your mind will generate a narrative of hardship. This narrative will be wrong. The Veskan are not enduring their home. They are *living* in it, fully, in a way that most people in most places never manage. They have chosen this — or their ancestors chose it, and each generation has reaffirmed the choice by staying. The kelp-architecture is not a slum. It is a masterpiece of biological engineering refined over centuries. The diet is not deprivation. It is a sophisticated exploitation of an ecosystem that most people would walk past without seeing. Come with respect. Come with the humility to recognize that these people know something about living that you do not.

**Learn before you ask.** If a Veskan is pruning a stalk, watch before you ask what they're doing. If a child is weaving stonewort into a floor-panel, sit beside them before you ask to be taught. The Veskan communicate through doing. The most important conversations in Veska happen without words — a hand placed on a kelp-column to feel its internal structure, a pause at a channel junction to listen for the sound of fish, a shared silence on the upper platform at dusk when the waterfall catches the amber light. Words come later, once the doing has established whether you are worth the words.

---

## VIII. Departure

**Leave slowly.** The altitude descent will affect you — not as dramatically as the ascent, but you'll feel a kind of lightness, a looseness in your thinking, as the oxygen thickens around you. Take the descent in stages if you can.

**Carry the dampness with you.** Not literally — though your clothes will still be damp, your gear will still be damp, and you will smell like lake-water and algae for days. But the real dampness, the one that matters, is the shift in your sense of what a home is, what a life can be, what it means to live inside a living thing rather than on top of a dead one. This will stay with you. It will make your dry rooms feel strange for a while. It will make your solid walls feel oddly lifeless. This is normal. It will fade. But something of it will remain — a slight preference for the green and the wet, a faint memory of light filtering through translucent walls, the sound of water and birds and creaking plant that you will hear, unexpectedly, in quiet moments for the rest of your life.

You were a guest in a house that was building itself before you arrived and will continue after you leave. Carry that knowledge gently.

## Summary Checklist

- Acclimatize to 2,400m before entering the settlement
- Wear close-fitting merino wool; avoid cotton and loose layers
- Thin-soled water shoes with good tread, or accept Veskan-made footwear
- Bring almost nothing; seal anything essential in a hard waterproof container
- Trade goods: flint, obsidian, bone tools, cordage, salt, iron blade (if you have a worthy one)
- Small metal vessel for boiling water
- Compact waterproof medical kit (wound care, infection prevention)
- Wool beanie; UV protection for head, neck, ears
- Psychological preparation for: constant noise, constant wetness, extreme physical intimacy, glacial pace
- Move slowly, test every surface, copy the Veskan gait
- Arrive as a guest, not an observer; listen before you ask; let them teach you through doing
- Do not pity; do not rush; do not seek dry land
- Leave slowly, and carry the green light with you
