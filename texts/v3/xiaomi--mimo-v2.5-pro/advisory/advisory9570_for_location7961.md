# Preparation Guide: The Greenhouse Below

---

## UNDERSTANDING WHAT YOU'RE WALKING INTO

Before we talk gear and logistics, it's worth sitting with what this place *is*, because your body and mind need to be ready for something most surface-worlders have never experienced: a living room that belongs to the earth, not to you. You are a visitor in the most literal sense. Nothing here evolved for your presence. Nothing here needs you, notices you, or benefits from you. The chamber is warm, green, alive, and utterly indifferent. That indifference will be the hardest thing to prepare for, and it's worth starting there.

This is a geothermal cave system with a breathable atmosphere but compromised air quality, near-total darkness, high ambient heat concentrated unevenly through the space, extreme humidity, unstable geology, and no fauna whatsoever. The silence is profound. The isolation is real. You will likely be the only human-scale consciousness in that space for the duration of your visit. Plan accordingly — not just physically, but psychologically.

## DESCENT AND ACCESS

The entrance passage is described as a "throat of fractured basalt" that has been recently cleared. This tells you several critical things:

**The rock is sharp.** Basalt that has been freshly fractured — not weathered, not rounded by time or water — is essentially a corridor lined with blades. It will cut through soft fabric, skin, and even thin leather with minimal provocation. You need:

- **Heavy-duty gloves** — not gardening gloves, not nitrile-dipped work gloves. Leather or synthetic climbing-grade gloves with reinforced palms and fingers. You will be using your hands for balance in a tight, descending passage where the walls are close and the footing is uncertain. Your gloves will get shredded. Bring a backup pair.
- **Full-length, abrasion-resistant clothing** — think canvas or ripstop nylon, not cotton. Cotton will tear on basalt crystal faces and leave your skin exposed. Long sleeves, long legs, everything tucked and secured. No loose cuffs or hems that can catch on protruding shards.
- **A helmet with a chin strap.** This is non-negotiable. The passage has recently been cleared, which means it has recently *collapsed*, which means the ceiling of that throat is not stable. Small fragments will continue to dislodge. The chamber ceiling itself is described as actively shedding shards — stone that has begun its "slow separation from the roof." A falling basalt shard from even a modest height will split your skull. Wear a climbing or caving helmet with a reliable light mount. Test the chin strap before you descend.
- **Sturdy, ankle-supporting boots with aggressive tread.** The basalt will be uneven, possibly damp from condensation as you approach the chamber floor. You do not want to twist an ankle in a passage you might need to climb back out of. Break the boots in before you travel. The last thing you need is a blister forming at 40°C in 90% humidity.

The descent is described as something you go *through* — a throat, not a staircase. Expect a tight, possibly irregular passage. If you are claustrophobic, this will be a meaningful challenge. The passage narrows before the chamber opens, and the air will change — thickening, warming — while you are still in the tightest section. There is no shame in turning around. There is considerable shame in panicking in a tight space and injuring yourself where no one can reach you.

## AIR QUALITY AND RESPIRATION

The air in the chamber smells of **sulfur, wet iron, and "something older than either."** This is geothermal outgassing. The composition is likely dominated by:

- **Hydrogen sulfide (H₂S)** — the sulfur smell. At low concentrations, it's an odor nuisance and mild irritant. At moderate concentrations, it deadens your sense of smell within minutes, which means you lose your early warning system for increasing levels. At high concentrations, it's lethal.
- **Carbon dioxide (CO₂)** — geothermal vents commonly exhale CO₂ in concentrations well above the atmospheric norm. In an enclosed chamber with limited air circulation, CO₂ can pool, especially in low-lying areas. Elevated CO₂ causes headaches, confusion, drowsiness, and at sufficient levels, unconsciousness and death.
- **Carbon monoxide (CO)** — possible, given the industrial history and the iron-rich environment.
- **Water vapor** — the humidity will be near-saturated in many areas. This is uncomfortable but not dangerous in itself, unless it compromises your ability to thermoregulate (see below).

**Required preparation:**

- **A personal gas detector / multi-gas monitor** — the kind miners and rescue workers carry. It should monitor at minimum H₂S, O₂, CO₂, and CO. Set audible alarms. Check it before you descend. Carry extra batteries. This is the single most important piece of safety equipment for this location.
- **A half-face respirator with appropriate cartridges** — combination organic vapor / acid gas / P100 particulate cartridges. You may not need to wear it the entire time, but you need it accessible and you need to know how to don it quickly. If your gas monitor alarms, mask up immediately and move toward the descent passage.
- **Do not sleep in the chamber.** The abandoned shelter was occupied, and its occupant left. There may have been a reason beyond boredom. In a space where gas concentrations can shift with geological breathing rhythms, unconsciousness is a death sentence. The vents exhale "in slow, geological rhythm" — this means gas levels are not static. They pulse. You cannot predict the peaks without extended monitoring.
- **Test the air in the descent passage before committing.** Gas can pool in low passages. Descend cautiously, monitor in hand, and be prepared to turn back.

## HEAT AND THERMOREGULATION

The chamber is **hot**. The heat is geothermal, rising from below, and is described as greatest at the center — the trench area where even bacterial films have retreated. The margins are cooler but still warm. You will be sweating before you reach the chamber floor.

This creates a serious physiological challenge. In high heat with near-saturated humidity, **your body's primary cooling mechanism — evaporative sweating — is severely impaired.** You cannot cool yourself by sweating if the air is already saturated with moisture. Core body temperature rises. Cognitive function declines. Decision-making degrades. This is the insidious progression of heat exhaustion into heatstroke, and it happens faster than most people expect, especially underground where the temptation is to attribute symptoms to fatigue or stress.

**Required preparation:**

- **Hydration** — bring far more water than you think you need. A minimum of 4 liters per planned hour of exploration, and that may not be enough depending on your exertion level and the exact temperature. Bring a hydration bladder with a bite valve so you can drink continuously without stopping. Pre-hydrate for 24 hours before your visit. Avoid alcohol and caffeine for 12 hours prior.
- **Electrolyte supplementation** — you will lose sodium, potassium, and magnesium through sweat at a rate that water alone cannot replace. Bring electrolyte tablets or powder. Dissolve them in your water. Set a timer if you have to — drink on a schedule, not just when you feel thirsty, because by the time you feel thirsty in this environment you are already behind.
- **Cooling strategies** — a wet bandana around your neck is better than nothing. Phase-change cooling vests exist and are worth the investment if you plan to spend more than 30 minutes in the central area. Know the signs of heat exhaustion: heavy sweating that suddenly stops, nausea, dizziness, confusion, rapid pulse. If you experience any of these, retreat to the descent passage immediately — it will be cooler.
- **Time limits** — impose a hard time limit for your visit and stick to it, regardless of how you feel. Heat-related cognitive impairment is insidious precisely because it impairs your ability to recognize that you are impaired. Set an alarm on your watch or device. When it goes off, leave. You can always come back.
- **Clothing trade-off** — you need abrasion-resistant clothing for the descent, but you will overheat in heavy fabric once inside the chamber. Consider layering: protective outer layers for the passage, lighter base layers for the chamber itself. You can change at the transition point, but keep your protective gear accessible for the return journey.

## LIGHT AND VISION

The darkness is described as "near-total." The only sources of illumination are the faint, borrowed glow in the veins of the broad-leaved plants — a light so dim it exists only in peripheral vision and vanishes when you look at it directly. This means you cannot rely on ambient light for any practical purpose. You will be navigating entirely by your own artificial light and, eventually, by temperature gradients as your other senses compensate.

**Required preparation:**

- **Primary headlamp** — high-output, rechargeable, with a minimum burn time of 6 hours on your intended brightness setting. A caving headlamp with a wide flood beam and a focused spot option is ideal. Mount it on your helmet, not on a separate strap. Test it before you travel. Bring a backup battery.
- **Backup headlamp** — a separate, independent light source. Not a backup battery for your primary — a different headlamp entirely. If your primary fails in the center of a 80-meter chamber in near-total darkness, you need to be able to navigate back to the descent passage without fumbling with battery swaps in the dark.
- **A handheld flashlight** — third redundancy. This is also useful for looking into crevices, examining plants, and checking the ceiling for loose stone without directing your head-mounted light upward and blinding yourself with reflected glare off moisture.
- **Chemical glow sticks or LED marker lights** — if you plan to move away from the descent passage, drop small light markers at intersections or decision points on your way in. The corridors of bare stone between plant communities will look identical in darkness, and the chamber is 80 meters across — large enough to become genuinely disoriented in if you lose your bearings. Mark your path. Retrieve the markers on your way out, or don't, depending on your ethics about leaving trace.
- **Understand your dark adaptation will be useless.** In a space with essentially zero ambient light, your pupils will dilate fully but have nothing to work with. The faint plant glow is below the threshold of dark-adapted vision in most areas. You will be light-dependent for the entire visit. This means if your lights fail, you are blind. Prepare for that possibility.

## NAVIGATION AND TERRAIN

The chamber floor is fractured basalt — uneven, possibly damp, and cut by at least one artificial trench in the center (roughly 5m × 3m, 2m deep) containing warm, mineral-heavy water. The plant communities create natural obstacles, and the corridors between them may feel maze-like in low light. The margins of the chamber are cooler and drier; the center is hot and increasingly hostile.

**Required preparation:**

- **A rangefinder or measuring tape** — if you want to map the space at all, or even just understand your distance from the descent passage at any given moment. In darkness and unfamiliar terrain, distance estimation is unreliable.
- **Trekking pole or walking staff** — useful for testing footing, probing ahead in darkness, checking the depth of the trench before you approach its edge, and maintaining balance on uneven rock. A collapsible carbon fiber pole weighs almost nothing and could save you from a broken ankle.
- **Stay away from the central trench.** The water in it smells of copper, is mineral-heavy, warm-to-hot, and even the extremophile bacteria have retreated from its margins. The stone around it is likely thermally degraded and structurally unpredictable. The water itself is an unknown chemical soup. Do not touch it. Do not drink it. Do not fall into it. The trench is 2 meters deep — if you fall in and are injured, the heat alone could incapacitate you before you can climb out.
- **Watch the ceiling.** The description is explicit: stone shards are actively separating from the roof. There are no stalactites because there hasn't been time — the geology is too young, too unstable. Periodically scan the ceiling with your light. Avoid standing directly beneath large overhangs or visibly fractured sections.

## BIOLOGICAL HAZARDS

The ecology here is microbial and vegetal only — no animals, no insects, no visible fauna. This sounds safe. It is not necessarily safe.

- **Unknown microbial communities** — chemosynthetic bacteria and archaea in a geothermal environment that has likely been isolated for a significant period. The bacterial films described on wet stones are "faint, unsettling sheen" — this is a biofilm. Biofilms in geothermal environments can contain organisms not well-characterized by surface-world medicine. **Do not touch the biofilms. Do not touch the water sources. Do not touch your face, eyes, or mouth with wet gloves.** Bring a small bottle of hand sanitizer or, better, a mild biocide hand wash, and use it after any contact with surfaces in the chamber.
- **The plants are chemosynthetic, not photosynthetic.** This means their chemistry is fundamentally different from anything in your home ecosystem. Their sap, their tissues, their reproductive structures — all unknown. Some chemosynthetic organisms produce hydrogen sulfide or other toxic metabolites as byproducts. The broad-leaved plants have veins with a "faintly luminous" quality — whatever is carrying that glow is a chemical you do not know and should not absorb through your skin. **Wear long sleeves and gloves if you need to examine plants closely. Do not eat anything. Do not break open stems or leaves.** If you need to collect a sample, use sterilized tools and seal the sample in a biohazard container.
- **Spore risk** — the mosses are described as "so green they seem to vibrate." Mosses and ferns reproduce via spores. In a warm, enclosed, high-humidity space, spore dispersal will be concentrated and prolonged. If you have any respiratory sensitivity, wear at minimum a P100 particulate filter. If you are asthmatic, reconsider this visit entirely.
- **Allergic and sensitization risk** — you have no history of exposure to these organisms. You do not know if you are allergic to them. Treat every unknown plant and microbe as potentially sensitizing. If you develop any unusual respiratory symptoms, skin irritation, or eye irritation during your visit, leave immediately.

## PSYCHOLOGICAL PREPARATION

This is the section most travelers will skip, and it is arguably the most important.

**The silence will affect you.** Human beings are not adapted for true silence. Our auditory systems are designed to monitor a process, and when there is nothing to monitor, the brain begins generating its own input. After 10–15 minutes in a near-silent environment, most people begin to hear things that aren't there: distant voices, footsteps, low-frequency hums. In the Greenhouse Below, these hallucinations will be layered onto an already unfamiliar environment — darkness, heat, strange plants, strange smells. It will be easy to convince yourself that something is in the chamber with you.

Nothing is. No animal lives there. But your brain will not believe this easily, because your brain is a social organ that has evolved to detect other living things, and in the absence of real data, it will fabricate.

**Strategies:**

- **Name the sounds.** When you hear the *plip* of condensation, say it out loud: "condensation." When you hear the vent hiss, name it. Naming externalizes the sound and reduces the brain's tendency to assign threat to it.
- **Talk to yourself.** Narrate what you're doing. It will feel foolish. It will also keep your auditory cortex occupied with real input and reduce phantom sound generation.
- **Bring a timer and check it regularly.** Time perception distorts in dark, silent, hot environments. You may feel you've been in the chamber for two hours when it has been forty minutes, or vice versa. A timer grounds you in objective time and gives you a concrete reason to consider leaving.
- **Set a visit duration and commit to it before you descend.** Do not negotiate with yourself underground. Decide on the surface, when you are thinking clearly, how long you will stay. Write it on your hand if you have to.

**The feeling of being watched** is explicitly anticipated by the location's description — "something ancient and green and watchful." This is a projection, but it will feel real. The plants are described as "patient," as having "all the time in the world," as "circling" the abandoned shelter. These are metaphors, but they describe a genuine experiential quality of the space: everything there operates on a timescale that makes your presence irrelevant. You will feel, viscerally, that you are the fastest, most temporary, most fragile thing in the room. This is true. It may be distressing. Prepare for it by reminding yourself, before you descend, that you are a visitor, not a participant. You can leave. The plants cannot. Your transience is your freedom, not your weakness.

## EMERGENCY PREPAREDNESS

- **Communication** — standard communication devices will not function underground through basalt. If you have access to multiverse-level communication technology, bring it and test it before descent. If you do not, leave a written record of your planned route, expected return time, and the location of the chamber with someone on the surface who will come looking for you if you don't return.
- **First aid kit** — include: wound closure strips and antiseptic (for cuts from basalt), burn gel (for thermal contact burns from stone or water), electrolyte packets, oral thermometer (to self-check for heatstroke), ant basicphrine auto-injector if you have any history of anaphylaxis or severe allergies.
- **Extraction plan** — if you are injured in the chamber, can you get yourself out? The descent passage is tight and you will be climbing. A broken leg or dislocated shoulder in the chamber means you are trapped in heat, humidity, and darkness. Consider bringing a lightweight emergency bivouac and a personal locator beacon if such technology is available to you. At minimum, ensure someone knows where you are and when to expect you back.

**On your person:**
- Caving helmet with chin strap and light mount

- Backup headlamp (independent)
- Handheld flashlight

- Leather or synthetic heavy-duty gloves (+ backup pair)
- Abrasion-resistant long-sleeve shirt and pants

**In your pack:**
- 4+ liters of water with electrolyte supplementation
- Hand sanitizer / biocide hand wash
- First aid kit (as detailed above)
- Trekking pole
- Glow sticks or LED marker lights (10+)
- Measuring tape or rangefinder
- Biohazard sample container (if collecting)
- P100 particulate mask (backup for respirator)
- Cooling bandana or phase-change cooling vest
- Written note of your plan left with an outside party

**Mental preparation:**
- Fixed visit duration decided in advance
- Familiarity with signs of heat exhaustion and heatstroke
- Familiarity with H₂S exposure symptoms
- A strategy for managing silence-induced auditory phenomena
- Acceptance that you are a temporary guest in a space that does not need you and will not remember you

## FINAL NOTE

The Greenhouse Below is not hostile. That is important to understand. It is not trying to harm you, trap you, or drive you away. It is simply *indifferent* — a living system operating on timescales and energy sources that have nothing to do with you. The heat is not aggressive; it is geological. The darkness is not malevolent; it is a consequence of depth. The silence is not oppressive by design; it is the natural state of a place where sound has no purchase.

The danger of this place is not dramatic. It is the slow, cumulative danger of an environment that is just barely outside human tolerances — hot enough to impair, dark enough to disorient, quiet enough to unsettle, chemically complex enough to surprise. The margins are narrow. The buffer between "uncomfortable" and "dangerous" is thin, and it thins further the longer you stay.

Go with respect. Go with preparation. Go with the understanding that the chamber has been doing what it does — growing its green parishes in the patient dark, breathing its slow sulfur breath, drinking its deep water — for longer than you have been alive, and will continue long after you leave.

Visit briefly. Observe carefully. Leave no trace.

## WHAT THE VISIT WILL ACTUALLY FEEL LIKE

Because this matters as much as any gear list.

The descent will be tight, warm, and tactile. Your hands on sharp rock. Your boots finding uncertain footing. The air changing around you — thickening, sweetening with sulfur, warming degree by degree. By the time you step into the chamber proper, you will be sweating freely and slightly breathless, not from exertion but from the air itself. It will feel like stepping into a room where someone has been running a hot shower for hours, except the steam smells of earth and copper and something faintly rotten.

The moment of opening — when the passage releases you into the vaulted dark — will be disorienting even with your headlamp blazing. The space is large enough that your beam will not reach the walls. You will point your light into darkness and find only more darkness, with the occasional gleam of wet basalt or the ghostly suggestion of a plant cluster at the periphery. The scale of the space will feel wrong — too large for underground, too enclosed for open air. Your body will not have a comfortable reference frame.

The heat will press on you like a physical weight, especially as you move toward the center. It's not the dry, sharp heat of a desert — it's a wet, enveloping heat that sits on your skin and in your lungs. Within ten minutes, your shirt will be soaked. Within twenty, you will stop noticing the sweat because it will be constant, universal, as natural as breathing. You will become aware of your own heartbeat in a way you rarely are on the surface — it will feel louder in the silence, more present, more yours.

The plants will be strange in a way that is hard to articulate. They are recognizably *plants* — they have leaves, stems, roots, the structural of of green photosynthesis to is — also are fundamentally alien in their context. They exist in a world without sunlight, drawing energy from the chemistry of stone and water, glowing faintly with borrowed heat. They will look, wrong to you in a way that your conscious mind won't be able to pin down, and the longer you look, the more the wrongness will resolve into something like respect — for their patience, their adaptation, their absolute mastery of a niche you could not survive in for a week.
