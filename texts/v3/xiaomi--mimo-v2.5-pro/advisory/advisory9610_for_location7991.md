# Preparing to Visit The Nascence Pockets

## What You're Walking Into

Before anything else, you need to understand what this place *is*, because nothing in your prior experience as a traveler will have prepared you for the specific category of strangeness you'll encounter. The Nascence Pockets are not dangerous in any conventional sense. There is nothing inside that will try to kill you, nothing that will poison you, nothing that will attack you. The creatures will not bite. The stone will not collapse. The water will not drown you. And yet, this may be one of the most psychologically demanding places you ever visit, because it does something far more intimate than threaten your body — it gently, patiently, without malice, *unravels the assumptions your nervous system has been running on since birth*.

You are entering a space where things have not finished becoming real. That sounds poetic. It is also literal. The stone is still mineralizing. The creatures are still deciding their body plans. The water is maintaining itself through a law it seems to have written for itself. Causality is present but *loose*, like a threaded bolt that hasn't been tightened. You will feel all of this in your body before you understand it in your mind, and the feeling will be unlike anything you have a word for.

Think of it this way: you know the feeling of standing on ground that you trust — pavement, bedrock, the floor of your home. The Nascence Pocket is the opposite of that. Not unstable ground. *Pre-stable* ground. Ground that hasn't yet committed to the concept of stability. Your vestibular system, your proprioception, your visual processing — all of these have been calibrated for a world that has finished loading. In the pocket, the world is still loading. And you will be inside it while it does.

## Physical Preparation

### Getting In

Access is through solid-appearing tufa. You will be pressing your hand into rock and feeling it yield like *nothing* — not like water, not like mud, but a textureless softness that your fingers will not be able to categorize. This is the first test, and it is a significant one, because your body has a deep, lizard-brain-level resistance to pushing your hand into stone. Every instinct will tell you that this will hurt, that your fingers will break, that the rock is real and you are real and solid things do not yield. You will need to override that instinct with calm, deliberate pressure.

A few practical notes:

- **Remove rings, watches, and bracelets before reaching in.** The tufa seals gently when you withdraw your hand, and it will seal around any jewelry you're wearing. The text describes the stone as yielding "like nothing," which implies it conforms to whatever passes through it. A metal ring could become a problem at the wrist. Leave your valuables outside.

- **Reach in bare-handed if possible.** Gloves may work — the stone seems to respond to intention and presence rather than bare skin specifically — but the descriptions suggest the pocket is *aware* of what enters it. Bare skin is the most honest introduction you can make. You are not a tool. You are a visitor. Lead with that.

- **The transition is instantaneous.** One moment your fingers are in rock; the next they're through. There is no resistance point, no gradual yielding, no "thinnest spot" you can feel. It is binary — solid, then not. Prepare yourself for the suddenness. Your arm will go in up to the elbow, your shoulder will follow, and then you're through. It is not a tunnel. It is a threshold. Think of stepping through a curtain that has no thickness.

- **Your body will fit.** The pocket is described as root-cellar-sized, and the entrance is hand-shaped. How your entire body passes through a hand-sized opening in tufa is not explained by any physics you know. It simply happens. Don't think about it too hard while it's happening. Thinking about it is what makes it feel like being stuffed into a mailbox. Breathe out as you go through. Relax your shoulders. Let the threshold decide the geometry.

### Inside the Pocket

**Clothing.** Wear something simple and natural-fibered if you can — cotton, linen, wool. This is not for mystical reasons. The air inside is described as cool and dry, with a fine mineral mist perpetually descending. Synthetic fabrics may generate static in the dry air, and static electricity in a space where reality is already having trouble distinguishing between cause and effect is an unnecessary complication. You don't want to shock yourself and experience the shock before the spark, or feel the pain of a discharge that hasn't happened yet.

More importantly: natural fibers will *feel* more correct against your skin when your proprioception starts to go. You need every anchor to normal sensory experience you can get. Wear clothes you've worn a hundred times, clothes whose texture on your body is so familiar that your nervous system doesn't have to think about it. This is not the place for new boots or an unfamiliar jacket.

**Footwear.** This is a genuine dilemma. The floor is soft — not muddy, not wet, but soft in the way cartilage is soft, embryonic stone still deciding its own hardness. Heavy boots will leave deep impressions in a surface that may take days or centuries to fill back in, and those impressions will *disturb* the pocket's process of self-creation. Bare feet would allow you to feel the stone's texture (or lack thereof) and give you better proprioceptive information about the space.

My recommendation: soft-soled shoes, thin moccasins or socks with grip. You want to be able to feel the floor through your feet — that tactile feedback will be important when your sense of direction fails — but you also want some barrier between your skin and a surface that is still actively mineralizing. The stone is depositing calcium carbonate from mineral-laden seepage. That seepage will get on your feet. It probably won't burn you — it's gentle, embryonic, *polite* in its chemistry — but there is no reason to test whether your skin reacts to limestone that hasn't finished being limestone.

**Sensory aids.** Bring nothing that relies on consistent physics to function. A digital watch will not help you. Time in the pocket is described as a suggestion, not a law, and any device that attempts to measure it will either give you meaningless readings or — more likely — will simply not be able to maintain its internal logic in a space where causality overlaps. A mechanical watch might fare slightly better, but it will still be wrong, and its wrongness will be different each time you look at it, which will not help your sense of temporal grounding.

If you want to time your visit (and you should, because the temptation to stay longer than is wise will be significant), use your own counting. Count breaths. Count heartbeats. These are biological clocks, and biology seems to be better tolerated in the pocket than technology — the light- *are* biology, or something like it, and the pocket clearly understands biological processes even if it doesn't understand quartz oscillation. Count to six hundred breaths, which should be roughly ten minutes if your breathing is calm, and then consider leaving. You can always come back.

The air is breathable. Multiple previous visitor survived without apparent respiratoryatory distress, and the description of the air — cool, dry, smelling of limestone and ozone and something sweet — implies a complete oxygen-nit atmosphere, sustain human life. However:

- **The mineral mist is constant.** It coats every surface, It will coat your skin, It will coat your eyes. It is not't breatheable, not water droplets or fog in any traditional sense — but it is *present*, your respiratory system will be processing mineral particulates with every breath. Over a long visit, you may notice a faint chalky taste in the back of your throat, or a slight dryness in your sinuses. This is normal. It is the stone becoming stone inside you, briefly, harmlessly.

- **The brightness requires no protection.** The light in the pocket is not ultraviolet, not infrared, not any part of the electromagnetic spectrum as you understand it. The creatures "simply are light" — it is an intrinsic property, not a radiative one. You will not get sunburned. You will not need sunglasses. The brightness is *present* but not aggressive, the way a full moon is bright but doesn't hurt your eyes. However, be aware that the light changes — it shifts in color temperature and geometry as the creatures "tide" — and your pupils will be constantly adjusting. This is fine. Your eyes know how to do this. The problem is that the shifting light will interact with the scale distortion in ways that can produce visual vertigo. More on this under psychological preparation.

- **The sweetness.** The text describes a sweetness in the air — biological, suggesting dark-growing fruit. This is coming from the creatures, or the walls, or the water, or the spaces between them. It is not a toxin. It is not a narcotic. But it is *novel*, and your olfactory system will fixate on it because it cannot categorize it. Expect to spend the first minute or two inside just smelling the air, your brain repeatedly attempting to match the scent to something it knows and repeatedly failing. This is fine. Let it fail. The scent is part of the place.

## Psychological Preparation

This is the most important section. The Nascence Pocket will not hurt your body. It will, however, ask your mind to do things it was not designed to do, and the asking is gentle, patient, and relentless.

### The Direction Failure

This happens suddenly and without warning. One moment you are oriented; the next you are not. It is not dizziness. It is not vertigo. It is the complete, instantaneous erasure of your internal compass. You will be facing the far wall, and then you will be certain — with the same bone-deep certainty with which you know your own name — that the far wall is behind you. You will not have turned. Your body will not have moved. But your sense of "forward" will have simply... reorganized.

**How to prepare:** Practice losing your sense of direction *before* you enter. Go into a windowless room, spin around with your eyes closed, and try to point north. Feel what that disorientation is like. Now multiply it by a thousand, and remove the part of your brain that says "this is disorienting" — because in the pocket, the disorientation does not feel wrong. It feels *correct*, every time, from every angle. That is the dangerous part. Not that you'll be lost, but that being lost will feel exactly like being found.

**What to do when it happens:** Stop moving. Close your eyes. Breathe. The direction failure is not painful and it does not indicate that anything has gone wrong. It is simply the pocket's geometry asserting itself over your vestibular system's assumptions. When you open your eyes, pick a wall — any wall — and call it "the far wall." It doesn't matter if it's the wall you originally called the far wall. The pocket doesn't care about your labels. Give it a label anyway. You need one for your own sanity.

### The Scale Distortion

This is the hardest perceptual shift to cope with, because it involves a direct conflict between what your eyes are telling you and what your body knows to be true.

Your eyes will tell you that the creatures on the far wall are at a great distance — star-sized, vast, spanning what appears to be miles of dark space. Your eyes will simultaneously tell you that the creatures on the near wall are inches from your face, cupped-hand-small, intimate. Both of these will be true *at the same time*. The room will be both a root cellar and a cathedral. Both readings will be real. Your visual cortex will attempt to reconcile them and will fail, and the failure will manifest as a pressure behind your eyes — not painful, but insistent, like a word on the tip of your tongue that your brain keeps almost-recognizing.

**How to prepare:** Practice looking at optical illusions — Escher drawings, forced-perspective photographs, images where two contradictory spatial readings coexist. The Nascence Pocket is like this, but rendered in three dimensions and imposed on your actual visual field rather than on a flat image. The key skill is learning to hold two contradictory perceptions simultaneously without forcing your brain to choose between them. Artists and meditators tend to handle this better than most, because they have practice with non-resolve — the ability to perceive something without demanding that the perception make sense.

**What to do when it happens:** Do not try to "figure out" the real size of the room. There is no real size. The room is the size it is, and it is also the size it isn't, and both are simultaneously true. Instead, focus on a single point — one creature, one spot on the wall, the cairn in the corner — and hold your attention on it. Let the peripheral distortion happen around your focus point without trying to process it. Your central vision is more resistant to scale tricks than your peripheral vision. Use that.

If the pressure behind your eyes becomes uncomfortable, close them. The light-creatures' glow will be visible through your eyelids — the text says the light persists as a presence — but your closed eyes will not have to deal with the competing scale inputs, and the pressure will ease.

### The Causality Loops

This is the most philosophically destabilizing aspect of the pocket, and the one for which there is the least preparation.

You will watch something happen. Then you will watch it happen again. Then it will have already happened, and the memory of watching it will feel like someone else's memory, like a story you were told rather than an experience you had. A creature will detach from the ceiling, drift to the floor, and land. Then it will detach from the ceiling and drift to the floor again. Then it will be on the floor, and the detaching-and-drifting will have the quality of a dream.

This is not a hallucination. This is not your brain misfiring. This is causality in the pocket operating as a *suggestion* rather than a law, and you are watching it from inside, where the suggestion is being made. Events happen. Events happen again. Events are already complete. All three states are simultaneously true.

**How to prepare:** You cannot fully prepare for this. I want to be honest about that. There is no exercise, no meditation, no mental framework that will make the experience of watching causality *loop* feel natural. Your brain is a cause-and-effect machine. It has been running on sequential time since before you were born. The pocket will ask it to stop doing that, and it will not succeed, but it will try, and the trying is the experience.

What you *can* do is practice holding ambiguity. Spend time with the knowledge that a photon is both a wave and a particle, not as an intellectual fact but as a felt reality. Sit with the idea that two contradictory things can be true simultaneously and neither is wrong. The pocket will test this in your body, not just your mind, and the more practice you have with non-dualistic thinking, the less disorienting the causality loops will be.

**What to do when it happens:** Do not narrate it. Do not say to yourself, "I just watched that happen and now I'm watching it happen again." Narration imposes sequence, and sequence will fight the pocket's temporality, and the fight will manifest as nausea, confusion, or a creeping sense of unreality that edges toward panic. Instead, simply *watch*. Be a witness, not a narrator. Let the event happen, happen again, and be already done, without insisting that those three states resolve into a single timeline. They won't. They don't need to.

If you begin to feel panicky — and the causality loops are the most likely trigger for panic — focus on your breathing. Breathing is a process that your body manages automatically, and it is happening *now*, in whatever "now" means in the pocket. Your breath is the one sequence you can trust, because it doesn't require your conscious mind to impose order on it. Your body will breathe in, breathe out, breathe in, breathe out. Let that be your clock, your sequence, your thread through the labyrinth.

## What to Bring

**Almost nothing.** This is not a survival situation. The pocket is not hostile. You will not need supplies beyond what you'd bring for a short walk in the hills — water, a light snack, perhaps a basic first aid kit left outside the entrance with your other belongings.

What you *might* bring, specifically:

- **Three stones.** If you feel moved to add to the cairn — and the pocket may request this of you, in the same indirect way it apparently requested the original handprint — bring three stones from outside. Basalt, if you can find it, to match the existing cairn. Smooth river stones will also work. The important thing is that they come from *outside* the pocket — that they are old, finished, decided-upon stones, already committed to being stone. They will serve as anchors, gifts, offerings — call them what you will. Place them with intention. The pocket will notice.

- **A small notebook and a pencil (not a pen).** Not to write in while you're inside — the light will be sufficient, but the perceptual distortions will make fine motor tasks difficult, and anyway, the pocket seems to exist slightly outside the reality where writing makes sense. Bring it for *afterward*, because you will want to record what you experienced before it dissolves, and it will dissolve, faster than you expect. The pocket gives you an experience and then gently, persistently, takes it back. Write down what you saw while the light is still behind your sternum. A pencil will work outside in the dry scrubland heat. A pen may leak.

- **A companion, cautiously.** The pocket is small. Two people will fill it. The experience is intimate enough that having a second person present may either help — by providing a grounding point, another consciousness to anchor to — or make things worse, by doubling the perceptual input in an already overfull space. If you bring someone, choose someone you trust completely, someone whose presence you find calming rather than stimulating. Brief them thoroughly. Establish hand-squeeze signals: one squeeze for "I'm okay," two for "I need to leave," three for "something is wrong." You will not be able to speak during the direction failures and causality loops — or rather, you will speak, but the words may arrive at your companion's ears before they leave your mouth, and that is a specific kind of terrifying that no one needs to experience in a space the size of a closet.

- **Nothing alive.** Do not bring an animal. Do not bring a pet. Do not bring insects in a container, or a potted plant, or seeds. The pocket is already alive with its own nascent biology, and introducing a terrestrial organism into that space would be — I do not know what it would be. The text does not tell us. But the pocket is a place of becoming, and what a terrestrial organism might *become* inside it is not something I want you to discover through experiment. Leave all living things outside.

## What to Bring *Back*

Nothing. Bring nothing back.

Do not try to collect a piece of the embryonic stone — it will harden outside the pocket into ordinary calcium carbonate, disappointing and pointless, and you will have taken something from a place that is still becoming itself. Do not try to capture one of the light-creatures — they exist in a layer of reality adjacent to your own, and bringing one through the threshold into the ordinary world would be like pulling a fish out of water and expecting it to teach you about the ocean. Do not try to bottle the water. Do not try to chip away the handprint.

What you will bring back, inevitably, is the light behind your sternum. This is the one souvenir the pocket offers, and it does not offer it — it simply leaves it, the way the tide leaves foam, the way the seepage leaves mineral. A bright, cool, pocket-shaped presence somewhere between your ribs that takes its time dissolving. An hour, maybe more. It is not unpleasant. It is not comfortable, either. It is simply *there*, a reminder that you stood inside something that was still becoming and that some small part of it recognized you as part of its becoming and included you in the process.

Do not try to make the light linger. Do not try to make it leave faster. Let it do what it does. It will ask, gently, whether dissolving is really what it wants to do. It will eventually decide that it does. Or it won't. I have heard from travelers who say they still carry a faint warmth behind their sternum, years later, a pocket-shaped space that never quite closed. They don't seem troubled by this. They seem, if anything, slightly more present than the rest of us, as though part of them is still standing in a bright chamber of unfinished stone, watching light-creatures tide across embryonic walls, while the stone grows around their footprints and the water holds its impossible shape on the floor and the cairn stands in the corner, marking nothing, meaning everything.

## A Final Note on Respect

The Nascence Pocket is not yours. It is not anyone's. It is a place in the process of creating itself, and you are a visitor to that process — a witness, briefly, to something that does not need your witness but permits it.

The person who came before you understood this. They left three stones and a handprint. They did not leave their name. They did not leave directions for others to follow. They left the smallest possible mark — *I was here* — and then they left, and the pocket began, slowly, gently, to forget them.

Be that kind of visitor. Be small. Be quiet. Be grateful. Press your hand into the stone when you are ready to go, and let it close behind you, and walk back out into the ordinary world with its flat white sky and its dry scrubland and its solid, decided-upon stone, and carry the light for as long as it wants to stay, and when it is gone, let it be gone.

The pocket will continue becoming without you. It was becoming before you arrived. It will be becoming long after you have forgotten the way the sweetness tasted in the back of your throat, long after the pressure behind your eyes has faded, long after you have stopped trying to reconcile the intimate and the vast.

Let it become. That is all it asks. That is all it has ever asked.
