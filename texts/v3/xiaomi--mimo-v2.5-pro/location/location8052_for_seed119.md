# The Cistern at Vosk

The room is not large. Perhaps forty feet across, with a ceiling that curves down to meet the walls in a continuous sweep of hand-packed clay — no mortar, no stone, no earth that was not first lifted, wetted, shaped, and pressed into place by fingers that have long since become nothing. Every surface here was made. There is no bedrock beneath the floor, no natural cavity that was opened. This chamber was *built from the outside in*, a shell of daub and wicker armature and fired clay rib-work, and it has stood long enough that the distinction between construction and place has dissolved.



Water runs everywhere.

Not in torrents. Not even in streams. The walls weep — a slow, constant exhalation of moisture through the porous clay, collecting in rivulets so thin they are almost invisible until they gather in the depressions of the floor, in the shallow channels carved generations ago to guide them, in the central basin that was built as a cistern and now serves as a permanent shallow pool no more than an inch deep, stretching maybe eight feet across. The sound of water is the first thing: not the crash or rush of it, but the *multiplicity* of drips, each finding its own rhythm off a different point on the ceiling, off a different curl of wicker that has sagged and now pends downward like a finger, off the lip of a brick shelf. Hundreds of dripping voices, staggered and unsynchronized, filling the dark with a constant arrhythmic percussion that never repeats and never stops.



It is dim in a way that suggests the dimness is native — not darkness *encroaching* on light, but a permanent gray-brown visibility, as though the air itself carries a faint residue of illumination. A few ceramic oil-cups sit on the brick ledges, burning rendered tallow from a wick of twisted plant fiber, each flame barely taller than a fingernail, their light reaching only a few feet before it is swallowed by the damp air. But there is also a faint, wandering luminescence on the walls — the colonies. Where the moisture is deepest, where the old clay has softened to a paste, certain organisms have taken hold, and they glow a blue-green so faint it might be imagined, like the afterimage of a thought. You would not read by it. You would barely navigate by it. But it gives the walls a quality of being *inhabited by light*, rather than merely touched by it.

The air is cool — not cold, but the cool of a place that has never known direct warmth, that has insulated itself against any season. It smells of wet clay, of slow decay, of the particular sweetness of standing water in a closed room, and beneath that, something animal.

---

Because the room is empty. Almost entirely empty. The floor is bare except for the channels and the basin and a few crumbling platforms of brick that may once have held something — offerings, storage vessels, the dead — and have since been abandoned to the water and the dark. A few lengths of woven reed matting, half-dissolved, cling to the lower walls where they were placed who knows how long ago. There is almost nothing here.

But the walls *move*.

The fauna is extraordinary. This is the thing that the empty room conceals until your eyes adjust: the surfaces are alive, thickly alive, layered with generations of creatures that have found in this handmade hollow a world perfectly suited to them. The upper walls and ceiling are furred with clusters of roosting cave-swifts — small, pale, nearly translucent-winged birds that navigate by sound, that have adapted to a space with no sky. They number in the hundreds, perhaps thousands, packed into the irregularities of the clay, into the gaps where wicker armature has rotted away and left small cavities, into the hollows between brick courses. They chatter constantly — a high, wet, clicking vocalization that forms a continuous background texture of sound, rising and falling in waves as one cluster sleeps and another wakes.



Below the swifts, the mid-walls belong to insects. Beetles with flattened bodies and long, slow antennae patrol the damp clay in loose herds, grazing on the biofilm that grows wherever water flows. Moths — pale, wingless, heavy-bodied things that crawl rather than fly — move vertically along the surface, leaving faint silvery trails of silk. Spiders have built webs in the angles where wall meets ceiling, and the webs are enormous relative to the spiders themselves, so that every corner of the room is laced with gossamer that catches the faint oil-light and the fainter bioluminescence and holds it in trembling geometry.

At the floor level, where the water gathers, amphibians. Smooth, dark, wide-mouthed things — not quite frogs, not quite salamanders — that sit motionless in the shallow channels with only their eyes above the waterline, dozens of them, patient and glistening. They call to each other in low, resonant pulses that carry through the water and through the wet walls and give the room a subsonic hum beneath the dripping and the chattering of the swifts. Occasionally one lunges at an insect and the water pops and the ripples cross the basin and fade.

On the walls near the floor, colonies of small crabs — or crab-like things, pale and soft-shelled, with oversized claws they use to scrape clay from the wall itself, eating the material and whatever microorganisms live within it. They are the architects of the room's slow dissolution. Where they have worked longest, the walls are pitted and thinned, and it is through their excavations that much of the water enters. They do not know they are unmaking the room. They are simply feeding.

The sound is immense for the size of the space. Dripping. Clicking. The hum of amphibian calls. The rustle of moth-wings against clay. The scrape of crab-claws. The creak of old wicker giving way, a slow groan that happens perhaps once an hour as another fiber of the armature surrenders to moisture and age and the weight of centuries. And underneath it all, a faint resonance — the room itself, this hollow shell of built clay, vibrating sympathetically with the combined noise of its inhabitants, amplifying it, shaping it into something almost musical. Not music. But close. A chord sustained indefinitely, shifting in timbre as populations rise and sleep and hunt and die.

---

The cistern has no entrance that a standing person could comfortably use. The original access was a low doorway on the south wall, sealed from outside long ago — whether deliberately or by neglect is impossible to say. Water enters and leaves through the channels, through the porous walls, through a single vertical shaft in the ceiling that rises perhaps ten feet to what was once a rain-collector, now choked with debris but still admitting the faintest breath of outside air. The shaft is lined with the nests of the swifts, spiraling upward in layers of guano and saliva and clay, a tower within a tower.

The space was built, and then it was left, and then it was *taken* — by water, by animal, by the slow chemistry of wet clay and running time. It has not collapsed. It is in balance. The creatures eat the walls at a rate that is nearly matched by the rate at which moisture softens and re-deposits clay from the ceiling, so that the room maintains its shape even as it changes, a structure perpetually in the act of becoming its own ruin and never arriving. The erosion is not destruction. It is the room's metabolism.

Nothing here was planted. Nothing grows. Yet everything is alive — urgently, loudly, abundantly alive in a space where every surface was made by hands and every surface is now being unmade by mouths. The builders are gone. The room remains. The room is full.
