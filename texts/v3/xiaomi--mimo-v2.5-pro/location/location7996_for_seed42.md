# The Flooded Tarns of Veska

You look down into a cirque lake at the top of the world.

---

The lake sits in a granite cup at roughly 2,400 meters — a depression carved by ice that retreated so long ago the mountains have had time to soften, their ridgelines rounded now into the patient shapes of old bone. The tarn is perhaps 200 meters across at its widest and not especially deep: eight meters, ten in the center where the glacial gouge went furthest. But you would be forgiven for thinking it deeper than it is, because you cannot see the bottom.

You cannot see the bottom because there is no bottom. There is only plant.

---

The water is clear in the way cold water is clear — blue-green and faintly mineral, snowmelt-fed, never warm. Even in the brief mountain summer it stays cool enough to numb your hands. But the clarity does not help you, because every cubic meter of water is occupied. *Kelp* doesn't quite capture it. The word gives you the wrong image — you're thinking of the ocean, of vast vertical columns swaying in surge. This is something more intimate. The dominant growth is a species of freshwater macro-algae, stalked and branching, that forms dense lattice-structures rising from the bedrock substrate in tangled vertical curtains. It doesn't grow tall — four, maybe five meters — but it grows *everywhere*, and it grows thickly, and it has been growing here since before anyone in the nearest village could remember. The fronds are a deep olive, almost black toward the base, paler green at the tips where they terminate in fractal fans that catch the light.

Between and through these curtains, the water is alive with smaller things. Bladderwort tangles like loose netting. Elodea in dense cushions. Stonewort that crunches underfoot if you wade — a sound like breaking celery, if celery were made of calcium and grew in spirals. Water milfoil threads through everything, its feathered leaves snagging constantly. And everywhere, at every depth, the tiny pearled bubbles of aquatic mosses clinging to every hard surface, softening every edge.

The lake has been here long enough for the plants to have *architectured* the water. That is the only word for it. The macro-algae doesn't just grow; it organizes. Over centuries of growth and partial collapse and regrowth, the stalks have braided themselves into hollow columns, into arched tunnels, into walled enclosures that look almost deliberate. Where three or four mature stalks have fallen against each other and been colonized by secondary growth, they form structures that a person could shelter inside — domed, damp, woven, living. You could not tell where the growth ended and the construction began. Perhaps there is no difference.

---

And this is where it becomes a town.

Veska is the name the people here use, though it is not clear whether the word refers to the lake, the settlement, or the particular tangle of kelp-architecture that constitutes both. Forty families — perhaps fifty, depending on the season — live here. Not on the shore. *In* the water.

Their homes are the hollowed columns. Over generations, they have learned to guide the algae's growth — to prune certain stalks, to encourage others, to introduce binding species that knit the structure tighter. A Veskan home is a cylinder of living kelp, perhaps two meters in diameter, the walls a hand's thickness of compressed algal tissue, damp to the touch, faintly translucent. In the brightest hours of the day you can see the light through them, a green glow, as if you were standing inside an emerald. The floors are platforms of woven stonewort laid across the natural arches of fallen stalks, firm enough to bear weight, springy underfoot, always slightly slick. There is no dry surface anywhere in Veska. There is only *less wet*.

The settlement clusters in the shallower southern end of the tarn, where the granite shelves up and the water is only waist-deep. Here the kelp-columns stand closer together and the people have trained them into rows — rough lanes between homes, wider channels for the coracles they use to move between districts. The built density is real: this is organized, this is a place with quarters and commons, with a central gathering platform where the stalks have been encouraged to braid into a broad flat deck three meters above the waterline, accessible by knotted ropes of algae. But the density is also thin — *sparse* in a way that lets you feel the water and the mountains around you at all times. The homes are not packed. The lanes are wide. There is space and silence between each dwelling, and in that space the wild kelp has its way, growing back immediately wherever it is cleared, so that the boundary between town and tarn is a matter of degree only. The people here live *with* the plant, not on top of it. They are tenants. The algae permits them.

---

The sounds are what hit you first and last.

Loud. Persistently, surprisingly loud. The tarn sits in a natural amphitheater of stone, and the mountains focus every sound inward. There is a waterfall — seasonal, fed by snowmelt, but even in late summer a thin ribbon of white water descends the eastern headwall and strikes the lake's surface with a constant percussive hiss that the rock walls bounce and amplify until it fills the space like static. Then: the birds. Grebes, mostly, and a species of high-altitude moorhen that has adapted to nest in the kelp architecture itself, pulling stalks into platform nests that the algae then grows around and incorporates, so that the nests become part of the structure, permanent, added to each year until they are basket-sized domes of living plant and dried fiber and guano. There are dozens of these. They call constantly — a sharp, piping, territorial sound that carries across the water. Insects: a cold-adapted species of water boatman that stridulates by rubbing its hind legs against its abdomen, producing a thin, high, continuous drone that fills the lower registers beneath the birds. And the kelp itself moves. When wind crosses the tarn — which it does, frequently, this being a high saddle where thermals and downslope drafts compete — the fronds shift against each other with a wet, fibrous creak, like the sound of old rope under tension. All of this together: the water, the birds, the insects, the creaking plant, echoing from the stone walls. It is not cacophony. It is layered. But it is *loud*, and it does not stop.

---

The fauna beyond the birds is rich for a mountain lake. Newts — a mottled brown species that lives its entire life submerged, breathing through its skin in the oxygen-rich algal water. Small trout, stunted by cold and altitude but numerous, flickering silver through the green lattice. Dragonfly larvae in the shallows, fat and ancient-looking, armored in the way that all things at altitude are armored — as if life here requires a harder shell. Leeches in the mud, though the Veskan people have learned which kelp-species repel them and line their sleeping platforms accordingly. A species of freshwater crab, the size of a thumbnail, that lives in the hollow cores of the oldest stalks, hundreds of thousands of them, a hidden metropolis within the metropolis.

The people keep no livestock. They fish — gently, with woven traps set in the channels between kelp-columns. They harvest certain algae for food, drying it on the upper platform in the rare hours of direct sun. They trade with the villages below — carrying bundles of dried kelp, newt-oil, and the calcium-rich stonewort, which is ground into a paste and used in construction at lower elevations. In return they receive flint, obsidian, bone-tools, cordage of mountain grass. Metal is rare and precious; a single iron knife might serve a family for a generation. Their tools are carved from the hard lower stalks of the oldest kelp — dense as wood, flexible, water-resistant — and from granite cobbles shaped by patient pecking.

---

And everything here is slow.

The algae grows at the rate of algae, which is to say: fast by the standards of mountains, slow by the standards of people. A new stalk takes a season to reach useful height, three seasons to thicken into structural density, a decade to join the braided lattice. The Veskan do not build quickly. A home is a generational project — begun by one family, guided by their children, solidified by their grandchildren. The town itself is the accumulation of centuries of patient pruning and encouragement. When a column finally collapses — old age, a bad storm, a structural failure of the woven tissue — the people clear the debris slowly, let the water reclaim the space, and begin again. Nothing is permanent. Everything is continuous.

The lake's level shifts with the seasons, rising in spring, dropping in late autumn, but never dramatically. The erosion is not dramatic either — it is the erosion of a place that reached its form long ago and now simply *persists*, the granite smoothed, the shoreline settled into its curves, the water finding the same paths it has found for ten thousand years. The kelp adapts to the minor fluctuations, growing higher in deeper water, spreading wider in shallow margins, so that the town's shape breathes faintly with the seasons — larger in autumn when the water recedes, smaller in spring when it rises — but this is almost imperceptible, the way a sleeping animal's chest rises and falls.

---

Light comes to Veska through the mountain's moods. On clear days, the sun crests the eastern wall for six or seven hours, and the tarn transforms — the water becomes a lens, the kelp-columns glow from within, the algae's bioluminescent tips (a faint blue-green, visible only in direct sun) spark like submerged stars. On overcast days — and there are many — the light is diffuse, grey-gold, softened by cloud and stone. At dusk, the waterfall catches the last light and becomes a column of amber. At night, the water holds no light at all, and the Veskan light small lamps of newt-oil in stone bowls, the flames reflected and doubled in the water's surface until the town glows like an ember cupped in the mountain's hands.

---

It is, in the end, a small place. A room made of mountains, furnished with water and green life, inhabited by people who have chosen to live inside the plant rather than beside it, who have accepted that they are guests in a house that was building itself long before they arrived and will continue long after they leave. It is not magical. It is not strange. It is simply — *particular*. A place that could only exist here, in this exact configuration of altitude and water and patience and time.
