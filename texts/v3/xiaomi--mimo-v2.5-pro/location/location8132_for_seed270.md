# The Vat

You would miss it if you walked past. A fractured cylinder of corroded iron and slag-ceramic, half-submerged in the pale crust of an ancient mineral basin, no larger than a grain silo laid on its side. The basin itself stretches perhaps two hundred meters across — a depression so old and so low that it barely qualifies as landform, more like the faintest impression left by weight that was removed long ago. The crust is calcium and sodium and things that don't have easy names, bleached to the color of old bone, cool underfoot even at midday. Wind moves across it unimpeded. There is nothing to stop it.

But step through the torn seam in the cylinder's wall, duck under the lip of corroded metal that sags like a broken jaw, and you are somewhere else entirely.

---

The interior of the vat — and it was a vat, a processing vessel of some industrial lineage so old that the alloy has become something mineral-adjacent, a rust that has forgotten it was ever iron — is dense with life.

There is no other way to say it. The life is the space. Every surface — the curved inner wall, the crusted floor, the hanging shards of corroded baffles and internal scaffolding — is occupied. Layered. Textured with bodies. You cannot see the metal beneath them in most places.

The organisms are small. Nothing here is longer than a finger. Most are the size of a fingernail or less. They have mineral shells — calcium,iteite calcium silicate, something that blurs the line between exoskeleton and geology. They look like flattened nodules until they move, and then legs emerge, or cilia, or something in between, and they pull themselves across their neighbors' backs with a frictional, granular sound that is the first thing you notice.

The sound.

It is *loud* in here. Not sharp, not piercing — loud the way a river is loud, or a crowd. A constant, dense, granular abrasion of thousands of small hard bodies moving against each other and against the walls. Underneath that, a clicking — a percussive, arrhythmic tapping that comes from deeper in the pile, from organisms you cannot see, pressed into crevices where the metal has split or where ancient welds left seams. They tap their shells against the walls in patterns that may or may not mean anything. The tapping echoes inside the cylinder and gains a hollow resonance that makes it sound larger than it is.

And underneath *that*, a hiss. Faint. Chemical.

---

There is no water in the vat. The air is dry and cool — perhaps twelve degrees, perhaps less in the deepest folds where the organisms pile thickest and their collective micro-climate drops the temperature further. The walls weep no moisture. The floor holds no damp.

But the floor is not inert.

In patches — irregular, roughly circular, the size of dinner plates — the mineral crust has a different quality. Darker. Slightly damp to the touch, though not with water. A chemical film, thin as breath, that smells of sulfur and something sweeter, almost acetone. These patches are the centers. The organisms are densest around them, piled two or three layers deep, their shells pressed together so tightly that the mass moves as a single sheet, rippling as individuals jostle for position. They are feeding. Their mouthparts — you would need a lens to see them — work the surface of the chemical film, scraping, absorbing. It is not eating in the way you know. It is more like the film passes into them through their ventral surfaces, drawn in by some process that is half digestion and half chemistry.

Where do the patches come from?

---

Deeper in the vat, near what was once the base — the vessel sat upright before it fell, or was toppled, or sank into the basin's crust under the weight of millennia — there is machinery.

Not much. A cluster of pipes, corroded into abstract sculpture, their internal channels still partially open. A housing of some kind, a box of heavier alloy that has resisted time better than the walls, its surface now a topography of rust-blisters and mineral accretion. And inside this housing, something still turns.

Not fast. You would have to watch for a long time to see it. A shaft, driven by — what? Thermal differential? Chemical pressure from the basin below? Some sealed reaction that was meant to run for centuries and has simply *kept running*, forgotten, its output now feeding a food chain its builders never intended? The shaft turns. Slowly. And as it turns, it drives a mechanism that compresses gas in the corroded pipes, and at certain joints where the corrosion has created pinhole vents, the gas escapes in thin, steady streams.

This is the hiss.

The gas is rich in something the organisms need. Carbon, perhaps, or a reduced metal, or a compound that has no analogue in the biosphere you know. The chemical film on the floor — the feeding patches — condense from this gas as it meets the slightly cooler air of the vat's interior. The organisms have arranged themselves around this slow rain of sustenance with a fidelity that suggests many, many generations of adjustment.

They do not fight. Not exactly. But the density is such that movement is negotiation. When an individual wants to shift position, it must press against its neighbors, and the neighbors press against theirs, and a slow wave propagates through the mass — visible from above as a kind of tectonic creep, a shifting of tiny hard plates across a surface that is itself made of tiny hard plates. The sound this makes is the loudest thing in the vat when it happens in aggregate: a deep, grinding shuffle, like a handful of gravel poured slowly onto a table, amplified by the metal walls into something that resonates in your chest.

---

You wonder about predation.

There is predation. Of course there is — the density demands it, the energy source demands it, the sheer *overwhelmingness* of the fauna demands a trophic structure, however simplified.

The smaller organisms — the film-grazers, the nodule-bodied mass — are preyed upon by slightly larger organisms that live deeper in the pile. These predators are flatter, faster, with longer limbs that fold tight against their bodies when still and extend with startling quickness when they strike. You rarely see them. They move through the substrate like fish through water, parting the grazers just enough to pass, seizing one occasionally with a rapid clamping motion and pulling it down into the pile where the shell is cracked and consumed in privacy. The grazers do not flee. There is nowhere to go. Instead, they have shells hard enough that the predators must be selective — only the freshly molted, only the injured, only those too deep in the pile to brace themselves when seized.

Above them — above the entire mass — on the upper interior curve of the cylinder where the metal is dry and the chemical film does not reach, there are different organisms still. Winged. Small, membranous, almost translucent. They do not feed on the film. They feed on the *sound* — or rather, on the vibrations, harvesting the kinetic energy of the constant abrasion and clicking through some organ that converts mechanical oscillation to chemical storage. They hang from the ceiling in clusters, their wings folded, their bodies trembling faintly with the aggregate noise of the world below them. Occasionally one detaches and drifts down through the air on a slow, spiraling path, landing on the mass and probing between shells for eggs or dead organisms before launching itself back upward with a single powerful wing-beat that is startlingly loud in the enclosed space.

---

There are marks on the walls that are not biological.

Scorings. Regular. Parallel lines, evenly spaced, covering a section of the inner wall near the entrance. Tool marks — a machine, not a hand, made these, probably when the vessel was being built. They are ancient beyond any reckoning, older than the basin's current crust, older than the erosion that made this place so low and so flat. Near them, a faded stencil of characters in no alphabet you recognize, eaten almost to nothing by rust. A serial number, perhaps. A capacity rating. Whatever this vessel was for, the organisms that now fill it know nothing of it and care less. They have been here for — how long? Long enough for the alloy to change its nature. Long enough for the machinery to find its own equilibrium. Long enough for a fauna to speciate into layers, to build a world in a pocket of rust and chemical exhaust, invisible from outside, loud and packed and ceaselessly active within.

Outside, the basin is silent. The wind moves over the pale crust. The cylinder's torn seam shows nothing — just darkness, just the suggestion of interior space. If you pressed your ear to the metal, you would feel it. A faint vibration. A hum of small hard lives, doing what they have always done, in the dark of a forgotten machine.
