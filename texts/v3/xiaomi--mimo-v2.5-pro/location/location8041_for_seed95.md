# The Drowned Throne Tablelands

## First Approach

You see it before you reach it — or rather, you see the *absence* of it. The land ahead is not a mountain. It is the memory of a mountain, a suggestion of what stood here before time had its patient way. The horizon lifts only slightly, gently, the way a sleeping animal's ribs rise: a broad, warm, almost imperceptible dome of stone that extends for miles in every direction. You could walk toward it for an hour and not feel you had climbed at all, only to look back and find the surrounding lowlands spread out below you like a pale cloth.

The rock is sandstone and something older beneath it — a dense, dark, iron-rich substrate that surfaces in places like the bones of the earth showing through worn skin. Everything is rounded. Every edge has been taken down. There are no sharp peaks, no dramatic escarpments, no sudden drops. This is a landscape that has been eroding for so long it has forgotten it was ever tall. What remains is a broad table, slightly domed, slightly tilted to the southwest, stretching roughly thirty miles in each direction. If you drew a line from one edge to the other, the elevation change might be two hundred feet. It feels like walking across the back of something very large and very still.

---

## The Stone



The surface of the tableland is not flat in the way a floor is flat. It is flat in the way a sea is flat — textured, rippled, full of shallow undulations that take minutes to cross. The stone has been sandblasted by wind for longer than any canyon in any younger landscape has existed. It is smooth in places, almost polished, so that on certain days you can see the sky reflected dimly in the rock underfoot. In other places it is rough, pitted, studded with harder nodules of quartz and iron that resisted the wind while everything around them wore away, until they stood on little pedestals like offerings on altars.

There are basins. Shallow, saucer-shaped depressions, some no wider than a room, some a quarter-mile across, all carved by the same patient wind and the occasional rain that falls here — not often, not hard, but consistently, like a heartbeat that never quite stops. The basins are where water gathers. Not rivers. Not streams. Thin sheets of moisture that spread across the stone after a rain and then slowly, over days, retreat into the lowest point of each basin, forming dark, still pools no deeper than a hand's width. By midday the warm air begins to lift the water back out of them, and a faint haze of condensation hangs over each pool, catching the light so that the basins seem to glow faintly from within.

The rock is layered. Cross-bedded sandstone in bands of ochre and cream and rust, each layer representing an ancient desert or ancient sea or ancient river — you cannot tell which without careful study, and even then the story is incomplete. In some of the deeper basins, where erosion has cut past the sandstone into the older substrate, the rock turns suddenly dark, almost black, streaked with veins of pale mineral that might be calcite or might be something else. The dark rock is denser. It holds the cold of night longer. In the early mornings, condensation forms on it first, and it glistens.

---

## The Moisture



Water here is a visitor, not a resident. It arrives in three forms.

First: rain. It comes in the warm season, not as storms but as a general dimming of the sky, a thickening of cloud cover that lasts for hours, and then a slow, quiet release — not droplets so much as a settling of moisture onto every surface. The stone darkens. The air smells of dust and petrichor and something faintly mineral. The water runs almost silently across the polished rock, finding the shallow channels that have been deepening by fractions of an inch per century, flowing toward the basins. Within hours, the basins fill. The water is clear and warm and very still.

Second: condensation. Every morning, when the warm air meets the stone that has cooled overnight, moisture blooms across the surface in a thin film. It is enough to dampen your hand if you press your palm to the rock. It is enough for the life here. On the darker, denser substrate rock, it gathers into actual droplets that sit on the surface like glass eyes, trembling in any breeze. These droplets persist for an hour or two after sunrise, and then they are gone.

Third: the seeps. In certain basins, particularly the larger ones where the stone has been cut deepest, water rises from below — not dramatically, not visibly, but as a persistent dampness at the basin's center. The stone there is always dark. A film of moisture always clings to it. In a few places, this has allowed a thin crust of mineral deposit to build up over the ages, pale and scalloped and delicate, forming shallow bowls that hold the water even as the surrounding stone dries.

The water table is not deep — perhaps thirty feet in places — but it is inaccessible without tools and knowledge. The stone drinks slowly and gives back slowly.

---

## What Grows



Vegetation is an afterthought here. The land does not encourage it.

What exists is low, tough, and widely spaced — clumps of a dark succulent that grows in the crevices between stone layers, drawing moisture from condensation and the rare rain. Its leaves are thick, waxy, almost black, and they press flat against the rock as if trying not to be noticed. It grows in rings, slowly, each ring marking a year of careful survival, so that the largest clumps — the size of a dinner plate — might be decades old. There is a lichen, too, pale grey-green, that crusts the north faces of stone outcrops where shade persists longest. And in the largest basins, around the seeps where moisture is almost constant, there is a low-growing moss that forms mats so thin they are almost invisible — just a darkening of the stone's color, a softness underfoot where there should be hardness.

Nothing grows tall. Nothing flowers, or if it does, it does so rarely and without fanfare — a single small pale blossom that opens for a day and then closes and is gone.

The sparse vegetation is not the foundation of life here. It is a footnote. The ecosystem runs on something else.

## The Fauna

This is where the tableland reveals its strangeness, and its density.

The creatures are *everywhere*.

Not in the way of a jungle, where life is visible and noisy and competing for light. Here, life is present in the way that stone is present — constant, layered, unavoidable once you know to look. The creatures of the Drowned Throne Tablelands are mostly small. They are overwhelmingly quiet. And they are old, in the way the land is old — not as individuals, but as forms, as patterns, as body plans that have been refining themselves on this high, dry, ancient surface for longer than most mountain ranges have existed.

The most numerous are the lithovores — and this is where the food web begins. They are small, no larger than a thumbnail, with flat bodies and no eyes and a carapace so perfectly matched to the stone they live on that you will not see one until it moves. And they are always moving. They feed on the stone itself — or more precisely, on the mineral crusts and bacterial films and microscopic algae that exist on the stone's surface, invisible to you but a feast to them. They rasp the surface with mouthparts that are harder than the rock they eat, and they produce a fine powder that falls behind them like dust. Multiply this by billions. The tableland is being eaten. Slowly, imperceptibly, but constantly. The lithovores are one of the engines of erosion here, and they have been at work so long that the smoothness of the stone, the perfect polish of certain surfaces, may owe as much to them as to the wind.

Above them: the stalkers. These are larger — the size of a hand, the size of a forearm — and they hunt the lithovores. They are elongated, many-legged, with bodies that are somehow both soft and armored, jointed in places that seem wrong, as if they were assembled from several different creatures and the seams were left visible. They move in the spaces between stones, in the crevices, in the shallow channels where water runs. They are almost silent. When they move, they move with a fluid, deliberate slowness that is not caution but efficiency — they have nowhere to be, nothing to rush toward. Their prey is abundant. Their predators are few.

And above *them* — the breathers. This is the creature that will unsettle you, if anything here does. They are large — some as long as your arm, some longer — and they do not look like they belong on stone. Their bodies are soft, translucent, vaguely cylindrical, and they lie draped across the rock in the basins where moisture gathers, sometimes singly, sometimes in clusters of a dozen or more, overlapping, their edges touching. They are breathing. You can see it — a slow, rhythmic expansion and contraction, like lungs, like tidal pools. They are absorbing moisture directly through their skin. In the early morning, when condensation is heaviest, you can see them glisten, and you can see the moisture being drawn into their bodies, the translucence deepening, the body slowly filling, becoming taut. By afternoon, when the moisture has evaporated, they deflate slightly, become flatter, press themselves more tightly against the stone. They are not plants. They are not fungi. They are animals that have solved the problem of water by becoming almost indistinguishable from the landscape that holds it.

They are everywhere in the basins. Hundreds of them. Thousands, in the largest depressions. They do not flee from approach. They do not respond to touch, at first — only if you press firmly will you feel a slow, reluctant contraction, a pulling away, as if you have simply asked them to move over on a crowded bench. They have no eyes. They have no apparent mouth. What they eat, if they eat at all, is unclear. The lithovores crawl over them without interest. The stalkers avoid them.

There are birds, too — or things that fly. Not many. A few broad-winged shapes that circle the tableland at great height, riding the thermal updrafts that rise from the warm stone, descending occasionally to land near a basin and walk among the breathers with a kind of careful, picking attention, as if selecting fruit. They are silent in flight. They make no call. Their wings do not flap; they glide, and the only sound is the faintest whisper of air over feather — if it is feather; it may be something thinner, more membranous.

The total effect, when you stop and look and really *see* the surface of the tableland, is overwhelming. The stone moves. Everything on it is alive and doing something — eating, hunting, absorbing, growing, waiting. The density of creature per square meter is extraordinary. But because the creatures are small, and mostly silent, and mostly the color of stone, and mostly slow, the impression is not of a teeming wilderness. It is of a landscape that is quietly, massively, patiently *occupied*.

## The Ruins



Here and there across the tableland — scattered, few, widely spaced — there are structures.

They are not recent. They are not maintained. They are stone, the same stone as the ground they stand on, shaped and stacked by hands that are no longer present, into forms that suggest shelter but not permanence. Low walls, mostly. Circular foundations, perhaps eight or ten feet across, with walls that might once have reached shoulder height but now reach only knee-height or less, because the same wind and rain and lithovores that have shaped the rest of the tableland have been shaping these, too, and have been at it for what appears to be a very long time.

In perhaps six or eight places across the thirty-mile span of the tableland, these foundations cluster — three or four circles together, sometimes with the remains of a slightly larger structure in the center, sometimes with a line of stones that might have been a wall or a path or a boundary. In one basin, the largest, there is a single slab of darker stone — the old substrate, not the sandstone — set upright at the basin's center. It is six feet tall, perhaps, and narrow, and so worn that its surfaces are smooth and featureless. If it was carved, the carving is gone. If it was a marker, it marks nothing you can identify. The breathers cluster around its base in great numbers, draped over one another, pulsing slowly.

The builders left no other trace. No tools, no pottery, no middens, no bones. Whoever they were, they used what the land provided and took everything else with them when they left — or they were so like the land that their departure left no mark. The ruins are so old, so weathered, so thoroughly colonized by the lithovores and the lichen and the slow processes of the tableland, that they feel less like human constructions and more like a kind of geological feature — as if the stone occasionally, on its own, arranges itself into circles.

## The Atmosphere

The air is warm and thin and very dry, except in the basins, where it is warm and thin and faintly damp. There is a smell — not unpleasant, not strong — of warm stone and mineral dust and the faintest trace of something biological, something alive, which grows stronger near the basins where the breathers cluster. It is not a rotting smell. It is more like the smell of a tide pool, or of soil after rain, but muted, thinned, as if the tableland is trying to remember what abundance smells like and can only manage a whisper.

The light is unremarkable. The sun crosses the sky as it does everywhere, and the stone responds — warming, cooling, shifting color from pale gold in the morning to bleached white at midday to a deep, warm amber in the evening. At night, without artificial light, the sky is vast. The tableland's gentle elevation and its distance from any other high feature means the horizon is wide and low, and the stars fill the space generously. The stone retains warmth well into the night. The breathers contract. The lithovores slow. The stalkers move more freely in the dark, hunting by some sense other than sight. The tableland does not sleep, exactly, but it settles, the way an old house settles — with small sounds, faint adjustments, a nearly inaudible groaning of stone adjusting to thermal change.

---

## The Quiet

This is the thing you will remember.

The tableland is quiet. Not silent — there is always the faint whisper of wind across stone, always the occasional distant sound of one of the flying creatures adjusting its wings, always the almost-subliminal sound of the lithovores rasping stone, which is less a sound than a texture in the air, a grain, a roughness you feel in your teeth more than hear with your ears. But it is quiet in a way that feels intentional, as if the landscape has agreed that noise is unnecessary. The creatures do not call to each other. They do not sing, do not warn, do not attract. They communicate, if they communicate, through posture, through chemical trace, through movement — through the slow, dense, layered language of bodies in proximity. Sound is not part of it.

When the rain comes, it comes quietly. The water spreads across the stone without a splash. Even the wind is quiet — it moves constantly, a warm current flowing across the tableland from the southwest, but it makes almost no sound as it goes, because there is nothing here to rattle, nothing to flap, nothing hollow to moan through. The land is too old for noise. Everything that could make a sound has been worn smooth.

You will find, after a day or two on the tableland, that you have stopped making sounds yourself. You walk more quietly. You breathe more quietly. You stop speaking, not out of reverence or fear but because speaking feels like an error — a disruption in a system that has achieved a kind of balance so old and so deep that your voice, your footsteps, your very presence feel like typos in a very long, very carefully written text.

---

## Time

The tableland changes, but slowly. Slower than you can observe. The rain comes and the basins fill and the breathers swell and then the sun returns and the water retreats and the breathers flatten and the cycle takes days, maybe a week, and it has been taking this long for this cycle for longer than your species has existed. The lithovores eat the stone at a rate measurable in microns per year. The stalkers hunt and are hunted and breed and die in cycles you cannot distinguish from stasis. The ruins sink imperceptibly, their stones subsumed by the same processes that built them — wind, water, creature, time.

Nothing here is new. Nothing here is changing in a way you could photograph and show to someone and say *look, it changed*. The dynamism is in the density — the sheer number of small, slow processes layered on top of each other, each one negligible, all of them together constituting a system so deeply established, so thoroughly in equilibrium, that it has become, in its own way, eternal.

The tableland is not dying. It is not living. It is *persisting*, the way stone persists — not against anything, but simply because nothing has yet convinced it to stop.
