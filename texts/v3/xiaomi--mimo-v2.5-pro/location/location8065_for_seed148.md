# The Hollow Grown

You enter through what might once have been a door. It is difficult to say. The frame is alive — not wood, not quite vine, but something in between and ahead of itself — a fibrous cartilage that has hardened into something resembling cast iron in texture but warm to the touch, faintly vascular, beaded with condensation. It breathes. You feel it breathe. The aperture is wide enough for two people standing abreast, and beyond it there is darkness and the smell of things growing in cool wet places, of rot that is not decay but construction.

---

## The Walls

Everything is plant. This is not a metaphor or a simplification. The floor beneath your feet is a dense mat of interlocking root-sheets, pale as parsnip flesh, firm as poured concrete, and when you press your heel down you feel a faint pulse of resistance — hydraulics, turgor pressure — the ground is holding itself up. The walls on either side rise in smooth, curving planes of what appears to be woody tissue, layered and compressed, with visible grain that runs in spirals rather than straight lines. In places the grain tightens into dense knots that resemble rivets. In places it flares outward into shelving brackets of living cambium from which nothing hangs. The ceiling, thirty or forty feet above, is a canopy of broad, overlapping leaves that have petrified mid-growth into something like slate — dark, layered, weeping faintly with condensation that falls in slow, irregular droplets.

This is a corridor. It runs straight for a distance you cannot determine because the dark swallows anything past about sixty feet. The walls are equidistant — perhaps fifteen feet apart — and they are not smooth. Up close, you see that the surface is a colony. Tiny structures protrude: leaf-buds no bigger than shirt buttons, each one sealed in a translucent sheath of wax. Fungal shelves, pale and rubbery, stacked in columns like filing cabinets. Thin runners that cross the wall surface in parallel lines, evenly spaced, almost — *almost* — like wiring.

---

## The Machines That Are Not Machines

There is a sound. It comes from everywhere and nowhere, and for a long time you will think it is your own pulse. It is not. Deep in the walls, something is *cycling*. A rhythmic compression, a mechanical certainty, like an engine running at the lowest possible idle. Every forty seconds, give or take, the nearest wall shudders — a micro-tremor, barely visible, but you can feel it through the root-floor. The temperature of the wall-surface changes at the same time: a fractional warmth, then a slow return to cool.

These are the engines. They are organic. They are made of dense, hollow chambers of woody tissue that have formed — or been formed — into bellows-like structures deep within the wall-mass. They compress and release, compress and release. Some move fluid. Some move air. Some seem to move nothing at all but still insist on their rhythm, like hearts that have forgotten what they're connected to. There are dozens of them, maybe hundreds, throughout the structure. You will not see them. You will only hear their work: the low, patient pulse of a metabolism that has industrialized itself.

In one alcove — a recess in the wall, waist-high, as though designed to hold something — you find a cluster of these chambers exposed. They look like a set of lungs carved from pale hardwood, each one the size of a grapefruit, flexing in sequence. A thin, clear fluid seeps from between them and is immediately absorbed by the surrounding tissue. The smell here is sharper: acetone, or something like it. A chemical byproduct. The plant is *manufacturing* something, and the waste is this sweet, faintly toxic breath that makes the air taste the way static electricity feels.

---

## The Emptiness

Here is the paradox, and it is the thing that will unsettle you most: the space is vast and nearly vacant.

The structure — this living architecture — is enormous. It extends in all directions. You are inside something the size of a cathedral, or a train station, or a small town, and every surface is organic, and every surface is *alive*, and yet the interior is almost completely empty. The air between the walls is wide and dark and holds almost nothing. No furniture. No objects. No accumulation. The root-floor stretches out in pale, unbroken plains. The leaf-ceiling arches overhead in cathedral curves. And in all that volume, there is *so little*: a few hanging structures, like cocoons or lanterns, that sway without any detectable draft. A thick, woody column here and there — a trunk, a buttress, a load-bearing organ — rising from floor to ceiling like a single pillar in an empty nave.

The emptiness is not peace. It is *hollowness*. You feel it as a pressure on your chest, a sense of being inside something that should contain more than it does. As though the building has been emptied out. As though it was built to hold something and that something is gone, or has not yet arrived, or exists here in a register you cannot perceive.

---

## The Fauna

They are abundant. You will not see most of them. They live in the walls.

Listen: a scraping, intermittent, just behind the surface. A clicking. A wet, rhythmic tapping that moves laterally, tracking your position at a distance of about two feet of plant-matter between you and it. The walls are riddled with passages — narrow tunnels, perhaps the width of a forearm — and the fauna uses them constantly. You hear migration. You hear feeding. Occasionally a section of wall bulges outward, momentarily, as something large shifts behind it, and then recedes.

What you *can* see: insects, or things like insects, but too large and too slow. Beetles the size of human hands that cling to the ceiling-leaves and do not move for hours, their carapaces the same dark slate as the petrified foliage. Thin, pale arthropods with too many legs that thread through the root-floor like needles through fabric, leaving no visible holes. In the larger open spaces — the naves and halls — there are moths, or moth-like things, that drift in the absolute dark with bioluminescent wing-edges: faint, cold blue, barely visible, tracing slow parabolas through the air like sparks in an updraft. They are looking for something. They never seem to find it.

Once, in a room — and there are rooms, organic chambers where the walls swell outward and the ceiling dips low — you see something large and mammalian, or quasi-mammalian, curled in a depression in the root-floor. It is pale. It is the same color as the roots. It is breathing with the same rhythm as the wall-engines, and for a moment you cannot tell if it is an animal that has synchronized with the building or an organ of the building that has decided to be an animal. It does not open its eyes. You move on.

---

## The Rules That Have Broken

The space does not behave. You must understand this early or you will make dangerous assumptions.

This corridor, for instance: you walked it for perhaps ten minutes, and when you turned around, the door you entered through was fifteen feet behind you. Not the door receding — *you not having gone anywhere*, or having gone somewhere and returned without traversing the distance. The geometry is wrong. It is not wrong in a way that is dramatic or surreal — nothing folds visibly, nothing inverts — but it is wrong in the way that a word looks wrong when you stare at it too long. Distances are approximate. Rooms you have not visited yet feel familiar. Rooms you have visited feel different when you return, not because anything has changed but because your memory of them is slightly misaligned with what they are, and you cannot determine which is authoritative.

Time is damp here. Not broken — damp. Things take longer than they should, or less long, and you cannot reliably determine which. Your watch runs. The numbers are sequential. But the experience of duration is spongy, resistant, like trying to press a thought through wet cloth.

And there are moments — brief, disorienting, frequent enough to constitute a pattern — where you see *through*. Not through the walls (the walls are opaque, dense, alive) but through the *place itself*, as though for a single frame the entire structure becomes translucent and behind it there is something else: a geometry of light, or a density of color that has no name, or simply *more of this*, extending in directions that are not up or down or any direction you have a word for. Then it closes. The walls become walls again. The dark becomes dark. You keep walking.

This is what maximum weirdness means. Not spectacle. Not monstrosity. Just: the rules have *loosened*. Reality has not shattered; it has *sagged*. And you are inside the sag.

---

## The Rain and the Damp

Water arrives from inside the structure. The ceiling-leaves condense moisture — you've seen the droplets — and periodically, without rhythm or warning, a section of ceiling will release its accumulation in a sudden, localized downpour: a cascade of cold water falling in a curtain perhaps six feet wide, drenching the root-floor beneath it, lasting thirty seconds or two minutes, then stopping. The roots drink it immediately. The floor steams faintly. The air humidity spikes, thickens, then slowly subsides.

Elsewhere, water seeps. It comes through the walls in thin, persistent rivulets that trace the spiral grain downward and pool in the depressions of the root-floor before being absorbed. These pools are warm — noticeably warmer than the air — and they reflect nothing because there is almost no light.

It is always damp. The air is cool — perhaps ten or twelve degrees, steady, as though the building is maintaining a temperature for its own purposes — and the damp makes it feel cooler. Your breath is visible sometimes, a faint grey ghost, and then invisible, and you cannot correlate this with any change in temperature or humidity. It simply varies. Another loose rule.

---

## The Industry

In a chamber deeper in — and "deeper" is a relative term in a place where geometry has begun to stammer — you find evidence of *purpose*.

The walls here are not passive. They are organized. Structures of woody tissue have grown into what you can only describe as *apparatus*: frameworks of hard, lignified fiber that hold clusters of softer organs in precise arrangements. Some of these organs are transparent, or nearly so — hollow bladders of plant-matter filled with amber or clear fluid, connected by tubes that run through the wall-cavity like plumbing. Some of the fluids are moving. You can see particulate matter suspended in them — dark granules, pale flecks — being transported from one vessel to another in slow, deliberate currents.

This is a factory. The building is manufacturing itself — producing new tissue, new chambers, new organs, new walls — and this room is where the chemical work is most visible. The acetone-smell is strong here. The air shimmers faintly above the larger vessels, heat-haze in the cool air. The wall-engines pulse faster in this room, their rhythm doubled, as though the work is demanding more from the infrastructure.

There are — and you have to look carefully, in the low light, to see these — *outputs*. Products. Small, dense, geometric objects that have been extruded from the wall-surface and hang from short stalks like fruit. They are pale, smooth, and almost perfectly spherical, each one about the size of a fist. They are warm. When you touch one, it *hums*, very faintly, at a frequency that is not quite audible but is *felt* in the bones of your fingers. You do not know what they are for. You suspect the building does, either.

---

## What Lives Here

The fauna is not incidental. It is *integrated*.

The beetles on the ceiling are pollinating — or the equivalent, cross-fertilizing — the petrified leaves, which despite their stone-like appearance are still producing reproductive structures: tiny, hard-shelled capsules that crack open when the beetles walk on them, releasing dust that the moths carry on their wing-edges to other leaves, other parts of the building. The arthropods in the root-floor are aerating — tunneling through the substrate, opening channels for water and air, maintaining the infrastructure from within. The large mammalian things in the rooms — you see another one now, and then another — are, as far as you can determine, *temperature regulators*. They absorb heat during the wall-engines' active cycles and release it during the quiet periods, smoothing the building's thermal fluctuations. They are organs that have evolved to be animals, or animals that have been recruited to be organs. The distinction may not exist here.

Nothing attacks you. Nothing approaches you. You are not part of the system, and the system is not interested in you. This is, in its own way, more alienating than hostility would be. You are *irrelevant* to this place. You are walking through a metabolism that has its own concerns, its own rhythms, its own industrial purposes, and you are none of them.

---

## The Silence

It is quiet. The engines pulse at a sub-audible frequency. The water drips and seeps. The fauna moves in the walls with small, furtive sounds. But the overall impression — given the sheer *scale* of the interior, the vast empty air of the naves and halls — is silence. A heavy, damp, living silence that is not the absence of sound but the *refusal* of it, as though the building is absorbing acoustic energy the way it absorbs water: patiently, completely, for its own purposes.

Your footsteps on the root-floor are muffled. Your breathing sounds close and small. When you speak — if you speak — the words die within a few feet, swallowed by the porous, living walls.

In one of the larger halls, you stop and stand still and listen with everything you have. And beneath the engines, beneath the water, beneath the clicking and scraping of the fauna, you hear — or feel, or believe you hear — a *chord*. A low, composite tone made of many small sounds converging: the pulse of a hundred wall-engines, the seep of a hundred water-runnels, the breath of a hundred thermal-regulators, the hum of a hundred fist-sized spheres — all of it resolving, for a moment, into something almost harmonic. Almost intentional.

Then it disperses. The silence returns. The dark breathes around you.

You walk on. The corridor continues. The building grows, slowly, around and ahead of you, laying down new root-sheets, new walls, new chambers — embryonic geology, raw and soft and pale, the architecture of a world that is still figuring out its own shape. And you are inside it, and it does not notice you, and the darkness is patient, and the damp is cool, and the place goes on.
