# The Lattice Sea

You would not call it an ocean, though it fills the space an ocean would. The water is cool — not frigid, but constant, the temperature of deep wells, of places the sun has never touched and does not need to. It is black. Not metaphorically. The light fails entirely within the first hundred meters, and everything below that is conducted by pressure, by vibration, by the slow chemical languages of organisms that have no use for eyes.

Below the photic line, below even the faint memory of light, there is a mountain range.

---

It has been here longer than anything should be. The peaks rose once above a sea that no longer exists — granite and basalt pushed up by tectonics so ancient that the forces themselves have stilled. The mountains are still rising, technically, at a pace that means nothing to anything alive. They are extreme in their verticality: ridgelines that drop away kilometers into abyssal trenches, spires that ascend through pressure zones, summits so tall they would be visible from space if there were space above, if there were anything above but more water and the permanent overcast of dissolved organics that turns the upper reaches into a ceiling of brown murk.

But the stone is not what you would see — if you could see.

---

A single organism has claimed these mountains. Or rather: a single *kind* of organism has claimed them, over the course of what might be a billion years, growing in layers, dying in layers, calcifying, hardening, building on its own corpse, and then growing again. It is a plant. It is not like any plant you know. It has no leaves — or rather, everything it has is a leaf, stretched and flattened and folded into structure. Its stems are the width of city blocks, gnarled and ridged, hollow inside, running with channels of circulating water like blood vessels. It does not photosynthesize. It chemosynthesizes — drawing energy from the mineral upwellings at the mountain roots, from the slow radioactive warmth of the deep crust, from hydrogen sulfide seeping through ancient fractures.

Over eons it has built. That is the word. *Built.* The stems have branched and fused and branched again, forming vaults and corridors and vast open naves. The flattened leaf-layers have become walls, floors, ceilings — ribbed with vascular channels, textured with reproductive structures the size of boulders, covered in a fine living fuzz of smaller epiphytic plants that cling to every surface like moss. The architecture is emergent but coherent, the way a cathedral is coherent even though no single mind designed it — every branch grew toward what it needed, and over geologic time, the collective need produced order.

From outside — from the open water — it looks like scaffolding. Like the bones of something incomprehensibly vast. The lattice extends over a region the size of a small country, draped over the mountain range like a net cast over sleeping shoulders. There are open stretches, kilometers of dark water between the nearest structures, and this is what gives the place its sparseness. The plant has not filled everything. It has colonized the peaks, the ridgelines, the places where the mineral upwelling is strongest, and between those points there is simply... nothing. Water. Depth. The occasional drift of marine snow — organic particles falling from the productive upper layers, arriving here years after they left the surface, like mail delivered to a city that has moved.

---

The fauna is abundant, but it does not crowd. The creatures here have evolved in partnership with the lattice — they live *inside* it, the way birds live inside trees, except the tree is also the continent. Tube worms cluster on the outer surfaces, their plumes fanning slowly, filtering particulates that the current carries. Crustaceans — pale, many-legged, some the size of dogs — navigate the interior corridors, grazing on the epiphytic fuzz. Fish move in loose schools through the open vaults, their bodies translucent, their lateral lines sensitive to the faintest pressure changes. They are not fast. Nothing here is fast. The equilibrium has settled into everything.

There are larger things too. Something like a jellyfish, but not — a colonial organism, hundreds of individual polyps fused into a drifting membrane that can be twenty meters across, translucent and faintly bioluminescent, pulsing slowly through the lattice like a flag moving through still air. It has been here, or something exactly like it has been here, for so long that the lattice has grown around the paths it habitually takes, leaving corridors wide enough for its passage. The plant accommodates its tenants. The tenants fertilize the plant. The arrangement is stable. It does not change.

---

The sound is the first thing that surprises anyone who comes here — not that anyone comes here, but hypothetically. The lattice *rings.* The water carries vibration with ruthless efficiency, and the hollow stems of the great plant act as resonating chambers. Currents — slow, vast, driven by thermal gradients that haven't shifted in millennia — flow through the structure and produce a low, continuous harmonic. It is not music. It is closer to the sound of wind in a wire fence, if the fence were the size of a mountain range and the wind had been blowing steadily for a hundred million years. A hum. A chord that shifts and swells and diminishes as the current finds new paths, but never stops, never silences. Layered over this: the clicking of crustaceans, the low-frequency pulses of the colonial jellyfish communicating with its own kind, the groan and creak of the plant itself as its older structures settle and compress under their own weight.

It is loud. It fills the water. There is no silence here, only a density of sound so constant that it becomes the texture of the place, the way the darkness becomes the texture of the place — not an absence but a substance.

---

And it does not change.

This is the essential fact. The lattice has reached its final form. Not because it is dying — it is alive, vigorously alive, its chemosynthetic roots still drawing energy, its corridors still housing generation after generation of fauna — but because the *equilibrium* is complete. Every niche is occupied. Every resource is spoken for. Every growth pattern has been tried and either incorporated or abandoned so long ago that no living structure remembers the experiment. What remains is what works, and what works is what is, and what is does not change because it does not need to.

The mountains rise. The water presses down. The lattice grows at exactly the rate it decays — new tissue replacing old, new stems branching where old ones have finally calcified into inert mineral and crumbled away. The fauna eats what the plant produces, and fertilizes what the plant needs, and no population grows beyond its capacity, because there is no surplus to drive growth, only enough to drive continuation.

It has been this way since before there were words for ways. It will be this way until the mountains finally erode to nothing, and even then — even then — the lattice may simply shift, descend, root itself in the rubble, and continue. It has no ambition. It has no memory. It has only the deep biological imperative to persist, and it has refined persistence to a kind of art.

---

If you could stand inside it — sink through kilometers of dark water and arrive in one of the great vaults, press your hand against the ribbed living wall and feel the faint pulse of fluid moving through its channels — you would feel the age of it. Not as knowledge but as weight. As a pressure on your chest that has nothing to do with depth. This place has been building itself since before the concept of building existed. It is architecture without an architect, a city without citizens — except for the ten thousand species that have made it their home, that navigate its corridors with the unconscious ease of fish navigating water, because to them it *is* water. It is the medium. It is the world.

It is dark. It is loud. It is cool and vast and ancient and it does not move. It fills the space the way a glacier fills a valley — slowly, completely, inevitably — and then it stays.
