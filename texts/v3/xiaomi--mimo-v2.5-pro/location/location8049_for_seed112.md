# The Resin Uplands

## Overview

You stand on a high plateau that stretches beyond the curve of the horizon in every direction — not because the land is flat, but because it is so enormous that the planet's own curvature becomes the limit of your sight. The elevation gives the light a quality of *proximity*. There is no cloud layer to soften it. The sun — or whatever local star governs this place — strikes the land with the impartial violence of an arc welder, and everything below it has organized itself around that fact.

There is almost no free water here. The air carries none. The soil holds none. And yet the land is *covered* in life.

---

## The Plants

They are not green — not in the way you expect. The dominant pigment is a deep amber-rust, shading toward copper where the light is strongest and into near-black in shadow. This is photosynthetic tissue optimized for a star that burns close and bright, a chemistry that has abandoned the aqueous luxuries of dissolved mineral transport in favor of something older and more direct: a resinous vascular system that moves thick, slow, sugar-dense fluid through crystalline tubes.

The vegetation is **total** — everything alive, everything photosynthesizing — but it is **sparse** in the way a forest is sparse if each tree stands a kilometer from the next.

The primary organisms are called nothing here because nothing speaks, but if you had to name them, you might call them **pillars**. They rise from the weathered, eroded ground like the chimneys of a city that was never built: tall, tapering columns of living tissue, some fifty meters high, some three hundred, standing in loose constellations across the plateau. Their surfaces are rough and plated — layers of hardened resin excreted over decades and centuries, cracked and re-healed, creating the appearance of ancient tile work or dried mudflats turned vertical. Between these plates, softer tissue breathes: dark membranes that ripple in the wind and drink the light with visible hunger, their surfaces faintly iridescent, like oil on stone.

Between the pillars, the ground is not bare. It is crusted. A living carpet of low-growing organisms — lithophytes, resin-mosses, calcareous lichens — covers every exposed surface of rock and regolith. This crust is thin, sometimes only millimeters thick, but it is *everywhere*, a continuous film of slow metabolic activity. Where the crust has grown old and thick, it cracks into tessellated patterns and curls at the edges, revealing younger, brighter tissue beneath. The entire plateau surface is in a constant state of peeling and regrowth, like a world-sized healing wound.

The resin. Everywhere, the resin. It seeps from every cut and crack in the pillar bark, hardening into translucent amber deposits that catch the light and throw it back in sharp, warm glints. In some places, entire fields of the ground-crust have been buried under slow resin floods — flows that took decades to cross a kilometer — creating smooth, glassy plains of dark amber that stretch for hundreds of meters before the living crust reclaims their edges. Walking on them is like walking on a frozen lake of dark honey. Your boots make no sound on the surface, but beneath you, the resin *ticks* and *pings* as it expands and contracts in the bright heat-and-cool cycle of the day.

---

## The Sound

This is the hardest thing to prepare for.

The pillars **sing**.

Not metaphorically. Not poetically. The resin-membranes stretched between their plated bark are *tensioned*, like drumheads or the diaphragms of enormous speakers, and the wind here is constant and violent enough to keep them always moving. Each pillar is an instrument. Each membrane, a voice. The pitch depends on the size of the membrane, its tension, the thickness of the resin layer, the angle of the wind — and the wind here never holds a single angle for long.

The result is a **wall of sound** — a drone, a roar, a harmonic chaos that begins at the threshold of hearing and quickly overwhelms it. Low frequencies from the largest membranes on the oldest pillars vibrate in your chest, in your jaw, in the fluid of your inner ear until your balance skews and the horizon seems to tilt. Mid frequencies layer and beat against each other, producing phantom melodies that resolve and dissolve in seconds — your auditory cortex *reaches* for patterns and finds them only to lose them again, a process that is exhausting and strangely addictive. High frequencies — the smaller, tighter membranes on younger growths — cut through everything like the whine of factory saws, like electrical hum, like the overtone singing of something vast and indifferent.

You cannot hear yourself think. This is not a figure of speech. The sound is so loud, so total, that inner monologue falters. You think in images, in impulses, in the language of the body. After a few hours, you stop noticing. After a few days, you begin to feel that silence would be the aberration — that the world has always been this loud, and the quiet places you remember were the ones that were wrong.

---

## The Wind and the Turbulence

The wind is not a single weather event. It is the **state of this place**.

The high elevation, the vast scale, the bright light — these create a convection engine of extraordinary power. Air heated at the base of the pillars rises in violent thermals; cooler air from the upper atmosphere plunges to replace it; the result is a continuous, rolling turbulence that makes the entire plateau a zone of atmospheric chaos. Dust devils the size of buildings spin up and collapse in minutes. Micro-storms — brief, furious convective cells — march across the plateau in loose, unpredictable armies, dumping no rain (there is no rain) but throwing grit and torn resin-membrane fragments into the air with tremendous force.

The sky is never still. Dust and resin-particulate hang in the air at all times, caught between updrafts and downdrafts, creating a haze that diffuses the bright light into something even more omnipresent — not shadowless, exactly, but *shadow-confused*, where every pillar casts a dozen soft ghosts of itself in shifting directions as the particulate curtains move.

Occasionally — every few hours, perhaps — a larger convective event will form: a **rotor**, a horizontal vortex of spinning air that rolls across the plateau surface like a wheel the size of a valley. The rotor does not destroy the pillars (they are old, deeply rooted, structurally adapted to this violence) but it strips membranes and loose resin plates from their surfaces, sending a blizzard of amber shards screaming through the air. The sound, during a rotor passage, transcends the already deafening baseline. It becomes pressure. It becomes presence. You do not hear it so much as *inhabit* it.

## The Fauna

There are animals here. They have earned their place in a world this loud.

The most visible are the **thread-wings**: creatures roughly the size of large birds, with elongated bodies and vast, gossamer-thin wings that span two or three meters. They ride the thermals in loose flocks of dozens, spiraling between the pillars with a grace that seems impossible in this much wind. Their wings are not membranes — they are *resin-threads*, extruded from glands along the creature's body, and they catch the light like spider silk. They eat the soft tissue between the pillar-plates, landing briefly, scraping with small, hard mouthparts, then launching again before the wind can pin them against a surface. Their calls, if they make any, are inaudible beneath the general roar. You know them only by sight.

Beneath the pillar canopy, in the denser crust-thickets where the ground organisms grow thickest, there are **burrowers**: six-limbed, low-slung animals that move through the living crust like fish through water, eating their way forward, leaving behind tunnels that seal over within hours. You rarely see them directly, but you see their trails — dark lines in the crust where the older, tougher surface has been broken and younger growth is recolonizing. Occasionally one will surface near you: a blunt, eyeless head, a body sheathed in hardened resin-scales, a brief and startling moment of animal presence before it turns and pushes back down.

Larger predators, if they exist, are invisible to you. But the thread-wings move in flocks for a reason, and the burrowers surface only briefly.

## The Industry

There is something here that processes.

You find it in the deep-interpillar spaces — the wide, open flats between pillar clusters where the resin-floods have pooled and hardened into their amber plains. In these places, the ground is not entirely natural. The crust has been *organized*. Trenches have been cut — not by tools, but by something that knows how to direct the flow of living tissue. The crust grows in patterns: concentric rings, radial channels, honeycomb partitions that collect the resin seep and direct it toward central pools where it thickens, concentrates, and undergoes some transformation that makes it denser and darker than any natural resin.

You do not see who does this. You do not see machines. But the patterns are too regular, too optimized, too *efficient* to be natural. Something with an understanding of fluid dynamics and growth cycles is managing the crust. Something is harvesting the resin. Something is refining it.

Occasionally, in the lee of a particularly large pillar — in the rare zone where the wind eddies into something almost calm — you find **deposits**: neatly stacked blocks of processed resin, each one a perfect rectangle, each one heavier than your body can lift. They sit in orderly rows on the bare rock, untouched, slowly accumulating a crust of their own. They are warm to the touch. They hum at a frequency you can feel but not hear, a subsonic pulse that matches no rhythm you can identify in the surrounding chaos.

No one collects them. No one has come for them, in all the time you've been watching. They simply accumulate, as if the plateau itself is producing an export for a market that no longer exists — or has not yet arrived.

## The Light and the Evening

When the star drops toward the horizon — not setting, not here, but angling — the light changes from white-gold to a deep, saturated amber that turns the entire landscape the color of old honey. The pillars glow. The resin plains ignite with internal light, their translucent depths catching and channeling the low-angle rays until every pool and flow seems lit from within. The dust-haze in the air catches fire-color and holds it, turning the sky into a bowl of copper and rust.

The temperature drops. The wind shifts. The sound, briefly, changes register — a half-step lower, a fraction less violent, as the thermals weaken and the membranes sag and the entire instrument of the plateau retunes itself for the cooler hours.

In this moment — brief, almost illusory — the place feels *settled*. Not peaceful. Not quiet. But *established*, the way an old river is established, the way a mountain is established: not because it has stopped changing, but because it has been changing in the same way for so long that the change itself has become a kind of permanence.

Then the star dips further, the night-thermals begin their own violent cycle, and the plateau sings again in a different key.
