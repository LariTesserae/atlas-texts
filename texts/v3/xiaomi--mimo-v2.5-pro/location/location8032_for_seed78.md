# The Upper Mat

## Surface

You cannot see the ground.

There is no ground, not exactly. What you stand on — what everything stands on — is a living skin perhaps two meters thick, a compressed and interwoven mass of mosses, liverworts, lichen, fungal hyphae, and dozens of species of creeping, cold-adapted vascular plants so densely knitted that they form a single continuous organism. It has the give and resilience of dense muscle. If you cut into it — and the few who have come here have tried — you find the top half dark green-black, wet, actively growing, threaded with pale root structures that move on no schedule you can determine. Below that, the material becomes fibrous, then peat-like, then almost woody in its compression. Below *that*, still descending, there is raw stone: sharp-edged, dark basalt and pale granite, recently pushed up, barely weathered. The erosion is young. The rock is new. But the mat has already buried it entirely.

It is always damp. Not from rain — rain barely reaches this altitude before atomizing into fog — but from condensation. Cloud masses move through the plateau at intervals, and when they do, the mat drinks. Moisture beads on every surface, pools in the shallow depressions where the mat sags between geological features you can no longer see, and collects in channels that the vegetation itself seems to have shaped, directing the water inward, hoarding it. Between these cloud events, the mat slowly exhales moisture back into the air. The humidity cycles. You can feel it — a periodic tightening and release in the air around you, like breathing.

The temperature hovers near freezing. It rarely climbs far above and, strangely, rarely falls far below. The mat insulates. The cloud cover insulates. The sheer mass of biological activity at the surface generates its own faint heat. Frost forms on the exposed tips of the tallest moss stalks on clear intervals, but the mat beneath stays liquid, stays alive, stays growing. It grows slowly. But it grows everywhere, and it never stops.

## The Vastness

The scale of this place is the first thing that breaks people.

The plateau extends in every direction beyond the horizon. It is not flat — it is rucked and folded, rising into long low swells and descending into shallow basins, following the contours of the tectonic forces that lifted it — but nothing on it rises more than a few meters above the mat's surface. There are no trees. There is no vertical structure at all except at the margins, where the plateau ends in sharp escarpments that drop away into whatever lies below. No one who has reached the edges has reported back what they saw. The distances are too great. The mat stretches, enormous and breathing, under a sky that is almost always the color of wet slate.

The emptiness is the second thing.

You can walk for days — weeks, some accounts suggest — and see nothing that is not the mat. No features. No landmarks. The mat is so uniform in its texture, its color, its subtle undulation, that the eye has nothing to fix on. Depth perception fails. You cannot tell whether a dark smudge a kilometer away is a depression in the surface or a shadow from a cloud or a concentration of a slightly different species of liverwort. The distances are legible only by the curvature of the plateau itself, which is so vast that even *that* is difficult to read.

There is almost nothing here that is not plant. The air between the mat and the clouds is empty. No structures rise. No trees interrupt the horizon. The few stones that protrude through the mat are small, wind-polished, and already colonized — already half-eaten by lichen that will, given enough time, bury them too.

## Sound

It is not quiet.

The wind is constant and it is loud. The plateau sits at an altitude where air currents move with continental momentum, and there is nothing — no tree, no ridge, no building — to break them. The wind comes in long surges, and when it arrives, it moves *across* the mat rather than *through* it, because there is nothing to move through. No branches to whistle in. No gaps to funnel through. Instead, the wind presses the mat flat and the mat resists, and the interaction produces a deep, low, continuous roaring that is less a sound than a pressure. You feel it in your sternum. It rises and falls over periods of minutes, following the passage of cloud systems you cannot see from ground level.

Beneath the wind, the mat itself sounds.

This is the stranger noise, and it takes time to notice. The vegetation is so dense and so metabolically active that it produces audible gas — tiny bubbles of oxygen and carbon dioxide forming and releasing in the saturated tissue, a crackling, clicking, crepitating sound like distant static. It is loudest after a condensation event, when the mat is fullest and warmest, and it comes from everywhere at once. At first you think it is insects. It is not insects.

There *are* insects, though, and they add to the noise. Winged things that spend most of their time beneath the mat's surface, emerging only during the brief periods when cloud cover thins and a dim, directionless light makes it through. They are small, numerous, and they sing — thin, high, at the edge of hearing. A persistent whine that rides on top of the wind-roar. There are also larger fauna: slow-moving invertebrates that tunnel through the upper layers of the mat, leaving healed-over trails behind them. Some are as long as a human arm. You rarely see them directly, but you hear them — a wet, muscular sound from just beneath the surface, like something shifting in its sleep.

## Light

It is dark. Not absolute darkness — the clouds glow, faintly, from above, transmitting a diffuse, sourceless illumination that makes everything the same shade of grey-green. There is no sun visible from the surface. There may be no sun at all, or it may simply never penetrate the permanent overcast. The color temperature is cold and uniform: everything looks as though it has been submerged in slightly turbid water.

At intervals — perhaps daily, perhaps on some longer and less predictable cycle — the cloud cover thins to the point where a pale, bright area appears in the sky, too diffuse to cast shadows, too bright to look at directly. The mat responds. The crepitating increases. The insects emerge. The larger fauna shift and resettle. Then the clouds close again, and the plateau returns to its default grey dusk.

The bioluminescence is faint but it is everywhere. Certain moss species produce a slow, greenish phosphorescence from their tips, visible only in the deepest intervals between cloud-thinning events. It does not illuminate anything. It merely *is* — a faint, living shimmer across the surface of the mat that extends to the horizon in every direction, making the plateau look, from even a slight elevation, like a sea of embers that never catch.

## The Scattered Things

They were here before the mat reached full coverage, or they were built on top of it during some interval when someone attempted to live at this altitude. It is hard to tell. The mat has absorbed most of them.

There are — scattered across the plateau at intervals of many kilometers — small structures. Frame-and-hide constructions, collapsed and compressed, their wooden elements slowly being incorporated into the mat's fibrous lower layers. Some are barely distinguishable from the surrounding vegetation: a slight hump, a hard edge beneath the moss, a place where the mat grows slightly differently because of what it is digesting beneath. Others are more intact, their upper frames still protruding, their hides (animal? plant-fiber? something else?) stiff and dark with moisture, cracked but not yet breached.

Inside the more intact structures, there are signs of habitation: simple tools made from bone and shaped stone, fragments of cordage, shallow fire-pits whose ash has been absorbed into the mat's nutrient cycle. Whoever lived here did not use metal. They carved bone with patience and skill, and they wove fibers into dense, insulating mats that mimic the plateau's own vegetation — whether by imitation or by use of the same material is unclear. There are no written records. There are markings on some of the bone tools: notches, simple geometric patterns, nothing that resolves into language.

No one lives here now. The structures are too few and too far apart to suggest a community. They seem more like waystations — places where someone paused, made what they needed, and continued moving. In which direction, there is no evidence to suggest.

## Process

The plateau is in motion.

Not geologically — or not only geologically, though the tectonic uplift that created it may not be finished; occasionally, rarely, the mat buckles and a new ridge of raw stone appears, and the vegetation begins its slow, inevitable work of covering it. The mat grows at its edges, too, sending tendrils down the escarpments, slowly extending the plateau's footprint downward into whatever lies below. This is the dominant process: growth, compression, extension. The mat is building the land it lives on, adding its own mass to the geological substrate, turning erosion into accretion.

Weather systems move across the plateau in visible waves. You can watch the clouds approach from one horizon — a wall of grey, darker than the ambient overcast — and feel the temperature drop another few degrees as the front passes. The condensation event follows: moisture forming so fast it drips from the air itself, not rain but *wetting*, the atmosphere reaching saturation and shedding its excess onto the mat, which drinks and crackles and swells. Then the system passes, and the slow exhalation begins, and the cycle continues. These weather events are the closest thing the plateau has to seasons.

The fauna cycles with the weather. The insects emerge and submerge. The tunneling invertebrates shift and migrate, following moisture gradients through the mat. And there are predators — small, fast, furred things that move *on* the mat's surface with surprising speed, hunting the insects during their emergence windows. They are rare but not vanishingly so. You hear them before you see them: a rapid, soft percussion of paws on the living surface, then a brief silence, then the clicking of mandibles being consumed. They do not approach humans. They seem to recognize, with whatever cognition they possess, that you are too large and too strange to be prey, and they give you a wide, silent berth.

The mat itself has a pulse. Over periods of hours, it swells slightly — absorbing moisture, metabolizing, producing gas — and then contracts. The motion is too slow to see but not too slow to feel. If you lie down on the mat and hold still, you can feel it: a slow, tidal rising and falling beneath you, like the breathing of something immense and patient and not entirely aware that you are there.
