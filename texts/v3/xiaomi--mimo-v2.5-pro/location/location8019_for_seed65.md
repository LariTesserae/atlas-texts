# The Resonance Plateau

## Altitude

You have been climbing for days, and the world has been falling away below you — first the forests, then the scrub, then even the lichens grew patchy and strange. Now there is nothing beneath your feet but stone. The stone is pale grey-white, shot through with veins of darker basalt, and it has been here longer than you can meaningfully comprehend. Not raw stone. *Settled* stone. Stone that reached its final shape ten thousand years ago and has changed almost not at all since — only the slowest rounding of edges, the quietest deepening of cracks, a patience so total it looks like stillness.

The plateau extends in every direction. It is not flat — that would be too simple, too empty of character. It is *undulating*, like a frozen sea. Broad swells of rock roll outward toward horizons that are farther away than any horizon should be, because you are so high that the curvature of the earth is a suggestion rather than a certainty. The air is thin. Your lungs labor. But the light — the light is extraordinary.

## Light

At this altitude, the atmosphere is a fragile membrane. Sunlight arrives with almost no scattering, no softening. Shadows are blade-sharp and deeply violet-black. The sky is not blue but something closer to indigo at its zenith, fading to a pale silver-white near the horizon where the atmosphere still has enough depth to haze. The brightness is intense but not warm — it is a cool, surgical light that makes every crack in stone read like calligraphy. You can see the grain of individual mineral crystals in the rock at your feet. At midday, the stone itself seems to fluoresce, throwing light back upward into your face so that you see the undersides of your own eyelids when you blink.

There is no tree canopy. No cloud cover. No shelter of any kind. You are fully exposed to the sky, and the sky is fully exposed to you, and the transaction between you is unceasing and luminous.

## Water

The plateau is not dry, exactly, but it is not wet. It exists in a permanent state of *almost*. Condensation forms on the stone surfaces during the long cold nights — a film of moisture so fine it is more texture than substance, a slickness under your fingertips that evaporates by midmorning. In the low places between the stone swells, water has collected in shallow depressions, some no wider than a handspan, others the size of rooms. These pools are perfectly still. They do not flow. They do not drain. They simply *are*, fed by condensation and the rare passing mist, lost to evaporation and seepage in quantities so minute that the water level never visibly changes.

The water in these pools is extraordinarily clear. You can see the stone bottom three feet down as though the water were not there at all. Sometimes small dark shapes move in them — larvae, perhaps, or some invertebrate life cycle adapted to these impossible pockets of stillness at the roof of the world. The pools do not connect. Each is its own small world, sealed and self-contained.

## The Sound

But you have been struggling to think about any of this clearly, because of the sound.

It is enormous. It is constant. It is the single defining fact of this place, and you understood it from the moment you stepped onto the plateau, and you will never fully acclimate to it.

The wind. At this altitude, the prevailing currents are relentless and powerful — not gusting, not variable, but a sustained wall of moving air that flows across the plateau from the west at a speed that turns the word *wind* into something too gentle. It is more like a river. The air moves over and through the ancient stone, and the stone — shaped by millennia of exactly this — *sings back*. The undulations of the plateau are not random. They have been scoured and sculpted by eons of this wind into forms that catch it, channel it, split it, and recombine it. Hollows in the rock act as resonance chambers. Fissures become organ pipes. Long parallel grooves worn into the stone surface by grit-laden wind produce a low, throbbing harmonic that you feel in your sternum before you hear it with your ears.

The sound has layers. The deepest is a subsonic pulse — not heard so much as endured, a pressure wave that makes your vision swim at the edges. Above that, a roar like continuous breaking surf, generated by the broad-scale flow of air over the plateau's swells. Above that, a keening, whistling upper register where the wind compresses through narrow gaps in the rock — a sound that sometimes rises to a shriek and sometimes drops to a moan, but never, ever stops. And woven through all of it, a high, ringing tone from the pools of water, whose surfaces vibrate in standing-wave patterns from the wind, each pool tuned to a slightly different frequency by its size and depth, so that the plateau rings with a vast, arrhythmic chord that is almost — *almost* — musical.

You cannot hear yourself think. You communicate, if you must, by pressing your mouth to another person's ear and shouting until your throat tears. Most communication here, if it happens at all, is gestural.

## Fauna

And yet the place is alive. *Abundantly* alive, in a way that seems to contradict the emptiness and the noise and the altitude and the total absence of anything that could be called a food web in any conventional sense.

The creatures are small. That is the first thing. Nothing large lives here — nothing *could* live here, in this wind, at this altitude, with so little vegetation. But the small things are everywhere, and in staggering numbers.

Birds, first. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. They are compact, heavy-bodied, with short blunt wings and powerful legs — built not for soaring but for *holding on*. Their plumage is dense and windproof, a uniform slate grey that matches the stone almost exactly. They roost in the fissures and hollows of the rock, packed so tightly into the crevices that at first you mistake them for shadows. They navigate the wind by hugging the ground, scuttling between sheltered positions with a jerky, urgent gait, occasionally launching themselves into the airstream and being carried — tumbling, rolling, correcting — to another crevice fifty meters away. They eat something. What they eat is not immediately clear, until you look closely at the pools.

In the pools, life is dense and strange. Thin mats of dark filamentous organisms cling to the stone beneath the water's surface — not quite algae, not quite anything you can name without closer study. These mats support swarms of tiny crustaceans, translucent and no larger than rice grains, that pulse and eddy in the still water. The birds wade into the shallows with an odd, stately deliberation, their bodies low, their beaks sifting through the mats with rapid lateral movements. The connection is clear: the pools support the invertebrates, the invertebrates support the birds, and the birds are everywhere.

In the stone itself — in the deep cracks, in the spaces beneath loose slabs, in the labyrinthine passages that wind erosion has carved over geological time — there are insects. Wingless, dark, armored against desiccation and cold. They move slowly. They feed on wind-deposited organic matter: pollen from distant lowlands, microbial dust, the shed feathers and droppings of the birds. Their numbers are impossible to estimate. You lift a stone the size of your palm and find dozens beneath it, motionless, patient, folded into the cool dark with an ancient familiarity.

And in the pools — in the larger ones, the ones deep enough that the bottom is lost to shadow — something else. You catch glimpses. A slow undulation. A pale shape, perhaps the length of your forearm, moving with a languid, eel-like motion among the filamentous mats. You see it and then you don't. The water is too clear to be hiding anything, and yet it seems to. The birds do not wade into these larger pools. You notice that.

## Vegetation

The sparse vegetation clings to the sheltered sides of stone formations — the leeward faces, the cracks where wind-scour is slightly reduced. Lichens, mostly: pale crusts of them in shades of grey-green and rust-orange, growing so slowly that a patch the size of your hand might be a century old. In a few places, where a depression collects enough moisture and is shielded from the worst of the wind, a low cushion plant grows — a tight dome of tiny, waxy leaves pressed flat against the rock, barely an inch high. These cushions are ancient too. Their root systems thread deep into cracks, gripping stone. They flower, once a year, for a few days: small, white, wind-pollinated blooms that you would miss if you did not know to look. The birds do not eat them. The insects do not eat them. They grow for themselves, in their own deep time, apart from the rest of the plateau's frantic small life.

## The Unchanging

Nothing here changes. That is the deepest truth of the Resonance Plateau, and the hardest to accept from inside the overwhelming sensory reality of its sound and light.

The stone does not change — not in any timeframe that matters to a life. The wind does not change. It has blown from the west at this speed, in this pattern, for longer than the pools have existed, longer than the birds have nested here, longer than the lichens have crept across their stone faces. The pools do not change in size or number. The birds do not migrate — there is nowhere to migrate *from* that is more abundant than this, and the plateau is their entire world, has always been their entire world. The populations do not visibly boom or crash. The seasons, such as they are at this altitude, produce only the faintest modulation — slightly more condensation in the colder months, a marginally reduced wind in the calmer months — but the sound never drops below deafening, the light never dims below bright, the life never pauses.

You have the sense, standing here with the wind tearing at your clothes and the subsonic pulse shaking your teeth, that if you could somehow step out of time and return in ten thousand years, the plateau would be exactly as it is now. The same birds in the same crevices. The same pools with the same pale shapes moving in their depths. The same lichen, perhaps a millimeter wider. The same chord of wind and stone, eternal and annihilating and indifferent.

There are no structures here. No artifacts. No one has ever lived on the Resonance Plateau, because to live here would mean to be shaped by it entirely — to become, like the birds, like the insects, like the pale things in the deep pools, another expression of its unchanging, deafening equilibrium. And perhaps that is what the fauna are. Not inhabitants. Not survivors. *Expressions.* The plateau, being what it is, simply *has* these creatures the way it has its wind-grooved stone and its condensation pools — as features of a landscape that long ago reached its final, permanent form and now simply persists, roaring, in the bright thin air.
