# The Churning Littoral

---

You are standing in water. That is the first fact, and it does not change. The water reaches to your chest if you stand on the highest spires of the rock, to your chin in the saddle between them, and over your head everywhere else. It is cold — not painfully so, not the cold that kills in minutes, but the insistent, mineral, glacial cold of water that has never been warm and does not intend to start. It tastes of calcium and copper and something faintly sour, like old milk.

You are also, by any reasonable accounting, underground. You know this because of the ceiling — a cracked and buckled slab of pale limestone not eight meters above the waterline, glistening with moisture, studded with stubby stalactites that have been snapped off partway down as though something very large moved through here and did not care. Light enters through a ragged fissure overhead, a wound in the rock perhaps two meters wide and twelve long, running roughly east-west. Daylight — grey, cool, high-altitude daylight — pours down through this gap and strikes the water at an angle, illuminating a column of space roughly the dimensions of a large chapel. That is your world: a flooded cavern in the shoulder of a young mountain, opened from above by a seismic event that may have been recent — decades ago, or years, or months. The edges of the fissure are still sharp. Dust from the break still coats the ledges in a fine pale powder that has not yet washed away.

The water is not still. It cannot be still, because the cavern is not sealed. Beneath the surface, the rock is riddled with channels — some natural, some eaten out by processes you cannot immediately identify. Water moves through these channels in slow, tidal pulses, driven by pressure differentials in the mountain's aquifer system. Every few minutes you feel it: a deepening of the current, a change in direction, a sense that the whole flooded chamber is breathing. Inhale: water draws inward from the eastern passages, the level drops by a handspan, the exposed rock gleams. Exhale: water pushes back, rising, sloshing against the ceiling, swallowing the spires again. The sound this makes is enormous. It is the sound of a building-sized bellows operated by geology. A deep, wet, rhythmic *whooshing* that reverberates off every surface, joined by the higher-frequency trickling and gurgling of water forced through narrow passages under pressure.

But the bellows-sound is only the bass note.

---

The fauna is *everywhere*.

The rocks below the waterline — and many above it, in the spray zone — are encrusted. Not with algae, not with moss, not with anything that photosynthesizes. There are no plants here. There is nothing green. Instead, the surfaces are covered in animals, or things that function as animals: sessile, motile, colonial, solitary, and every permutation thereof.

The most obvious are the **tubes**. They grow in dense clusters from every submerged surface, each one a rigid, calcareous cylinder about the diameter of a broomstick, rising ten to forty centimeters from the rock before flaring into a ragged, feathery crown of dark filaments. They resemble nothing so much as industrial smokestacks — and they *operate* like them. Each tube is a living chimney. Through a process you do not fully understand but that seems to involve the metabolism of dissolved sulfides and metal ions in the water, they draw fluid in through their bases and expel it through their crowns in a steady, warm microcurrent. Thousands of them, tens of thousands, packed shoulder to shoulder on every surface, each one a tiny pump. The cumulative effect is a faintly warm upwelling that mixes with the cold ambient water and produces, across the entire cavern floor, a low, constant, shimmering turbulence. The tubes also *click*. Not individually — you cannot hear one tube — but collectively, their filaments snapping and releasing in coordinated waves, they produce a sound like a vast room of knitting needles, a dry staccato rattle that rides above the bass of the breathing cavern.

Among the tubes move the **scrapers** — low, broad, armored things the size of dinner plates, slate-grey, with too many legs. They graze the tubes' crowns, nipping at the filaments with small, precise mouthparts, moving in slow, deliberate lines across the rock like machines on a track. They are not fast. They are not meant to be fast. They move at the pace of mineral accretion, barely faster than the tubes grow back. The cavern's metabolism is patient. When a scraper reaches the end of a row, it turns — mechanically, exactly ninety degrees — and begins a new pass. Dozens of them doing this simultaneously, crossing and recrossing the rock faces, give the impression of a factory floor seen from above: linear, repetitive, purposeful, wrong.

In the open water between the spires, larger things move. The **drifters** are translucent, gelatinous, roughly cylindrical, each one perhaps the length of a forearm. They pulse slowly through the water column, contracting and releasing in long, languid peristaltic waves, trailing curtains of sticky filament behind them. They feed on smaller things — the microbial haze that pervades the water, the eggs and larvae of everything else. They are beautiful and stupid and numerous. Where the light from the fissure falls strongest, they congregate in loose clouds, dozens of them overlapping, their translucent bodies catching the light and scattering it into soft, prismatic halos. They are the closest thing this place has to ambiance.

Below them, in the darker water near the floor, move things you see only in glimpses: sinuous, eel-like bodies with too many fins, pale as cave fish, eyeless or nearly so, each one the length of your arm. They hunt the scrapers and the drifters with what appears to be a kind of echolocation — you hear them before you see them, a rapid, high-pitched clicking that bounces off the walls and returns, the creature adjusting its trajectory between click and echo. Several of them work the cavern simultaneously, and their clicks layer over the tube-rattle and the bellows-breath in a stuttering, overlapping sonar pulse that makes the whole space sound like a machine room in a factory that has never learned to stop.

---

The density is the thing that strikes you hardest. Every cubic meter of water holds something living. Every surface is occupied. The water itself is thick with drifting organisms, fecal pellets, fragments of tube-crown, clouds of microbial life so dense they give the water a faint opalescent haze. It is not murky, exactly — the light from above is sufficient to read by — but it is *full*. The space between things is small. You can feel the life around you pressing against your skin, brushing your hands, catching in the folds of your clothing. It is not hostile. It is not even aware of you, particularly. It is simply *abundant* in a way that makes the word feel inadequate.

And yet nothing here is fast. The tubes grow slowly. The scrapers move slowly. The drifters pulse slowly. The eels hunt with patience rather than speed. The water itself moves in long, geological rhythms. The fissure overhead is not widening perceptibly. The sharp edges of the broken rock are not yet rounded. This is a place that was made recently and has settled into a kind of immediate, dense, noisy stasis — loud but not hurried, crowded but not frantic, alive but not volatile. It will change. The mountain will shift, the water will rise or fall, the species will turn over. But not today. Not this year. Perhaps not this century.

The cold holds you. The sound holds you. The water breathes in and out. The tubes click. The eels pulse their sonar into the dark. The light falls through the crack in the ceiling and illuminates, in its long diagonal column, the slow machinery of a world that has no use for you, no precedent for you, no memory of anything that was not stone and water and appetite.

You are small here, and the space is small, and everything in it is pressing close.
