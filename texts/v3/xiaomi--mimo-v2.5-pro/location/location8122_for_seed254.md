# The Greenhouse Below

The ceiling is closer than you'd think — maybe four meters overhead, though it's hard to tell, because the ceiling is no longer really visible. Pipes run along it in parallel rows, dark with condensation, each one furred with a thin continuous sleeve of moss that drips steadily. The drops fall into the canopy below and make a sound like a room full of clocks, all slightly out of phase.

This is a hydroponics floor. Or it was, once. The infrastructure is still legible — aluminum troughs, PVC irrigation manifolds, the steel armatures of grow-lamp rigs now dark or flickering. But the plants have moved in and stayed. They have been here long enough that the distinction between "facility" and "ecosystem" has become a matter of opinion. Lettuce varieties have gone feral and leggy, climbing the lamp rigs like vines. Basil has thickened into woody tangles that grip the trough edges with root-hands. Watercress fills every conduit, every channel where water still moves — and water still moves everywhere, because the plumbing was never turned off, only taken over.

The air is warm and wet. Not pleasantly warm — work-warm, the heat of a room where pumps run continuously and grow-lamps hum behind curtains of green. The humidity is nearly total. Surfaces sweat. Metal corrodes slowly under blankets of liverwort. The floor, where it can be seen between troughs and root-mats, is poured concrete, darkened with age and water-stain into something that resembles river stone.

The light is low and strange — a patchwork of whatever lamps still function, casting pools of sickly amber or blue-white onto leaves that have adapted to each spectrum individually, so that one cluster of ferns is almost black-green under a failing sodium bulb while the next, three feet away, is pale and translucent under surviving LED panels. Most of the light in the room comes from these scattered working lamps; there are no windows. This is below grade — you came down a stairwell to get here, and the stairwell smelled of wet concrete and chlorophyll.

The noise is considerable and constant. Not dramatic — there are no engines firing, no generators roaring. It is the accumulated sound of a hundred small machines: the tick and hiss of irrigation timers cycling, the low throb of water pumps behind walls, the gurgle of reservoirs filling and draining, the endless percussion of condensation falling from pipe to leaf to trough to reservoir. Somewhere a fan runs, slow and creaking, pushing warm wet air from one section to another through a duct now half-blocked with root growth. The sound is dense but not chaotic — it has rhythm, the rhythm of mechanical cycles that have been running for years without supervision.

The space is small. Perhaps forty meters long and twenty wide, a single floor divided by its own infrastructure into aisles and alcoves. You can walk through it in five minutes if you push through the growth. The troughs form a rough grid, and between them, walkways of metal grating have been colonized by a carpet of moisture-loving mosses so thick that walking on them is like walking on a wet sponge. The walls are cinder block, stained green-black with algae in the lower half, pale and chalky above. There are doors — heavy, industrial, propped open or rusted partially shut — leading to adjacent rooms. One opens into a reservoir chamber where the water sound is louder and the air is even warmer. Another connects to what was once a seed stock room, now a dense thicket of something that has gone to seed and re-seeded itself dozens of times, filling the small space so completely that the door can only open a quarter of the way.

There is no one here. There has been no one here for a long time, but the place does not feel abandoned — it feels *occupied by other work*. The pumps still cycle. The water still flows. The plants still grow, slowly, toward whatever light they can find. The systems were built to sustain life below the surface, and they do, just not the life they were designed for. The infrastructure has found its purpose and settled into it. Rust advances a millimeter per season. Moss fills another inch of pipe. A lettuce vine reaches another rung of a lamp rig. Everything here is patient and full and wet and humming, and if you stood still long enough, the water would begin to bead on your skin too, and the moss would begin to consider you as a surface.
