# The Hollow of the Mother-Trunk

## Containment

You are inside something that was once alive.

The space is a single chamber — or perhaps several chambers connected by passage-ways that narrow and widen like breathing — carved not from stone but from wood that has, over spans of time that resist human accounting, become something else. The walls are dense, pale, mineralized plant matter: lignin turned toite, cellulose turned toite, the original architecture of root and heartwood preserved in every detail but converted, cell by cell, into something that rings when struck. It is warm to the touch despite the cool air. It tastes of salt.

The chamber is roughly ovoid. You could walk its width in perhaps two hundred paces. The ceiling arches overhead at a height of ten or fifteen meters — low enough to feel the weight of what's above, high enough that the darkness above is genuinely unknowable. You are inside a trunk. A trunk that grew for a million years before the water left, and has stood dry and empty for a hundred million more. No root system. No canopy. Just this preserved interior, sealed from below by the mineralized base where the trunk fused with what was once soil, and sealed from above by whatever closed over it.

No one outside knows it is here.

## The Plants

They did not die with the trunk. That is the strange part.

In the near-total darkness — and it is *total*, the kind of darkness that reorganizes how you perceive your own body — there is a faint phosphorescence. It comes from the plants. They grow in every direction from the walls, floor, and ceiling, but they grow *sparsely*. Each plant occupies its own territory of meters, sometimes tens of meters, connected to the wall-surface by a thin pale root that looks like a nerve. They are not competing. There is no soil between them to compete over. There is no water. Each plant draws what it needs from the mineralized wall itself — a chemistry of slow extraction from the ancient wood-stone, mineral by mineral, over years. They are not photosynthetic. They have no leaves. They are pale, fleshy, slow-growing structures that look like fists, or like hands with too many fingers, or like the convolutions of a brain made translucent. Some have thin filaments that trail upward and vibrate — these are what make the sound.

## The Sound

It is *loud*. This is the first thing you notice and the thing you never fully adjust to.

The filaments — thousands of them, across every plant in the chamber — vibrate at a frequency set by the air pressure and the temperature and the exact mineral composition of the walls. They are not singing. There is no intent. But the geometry of the ovoid chamber amplifies and layers their sound into something immense and strange: a chord that shifts on timescales of hours, a low dense roar that contains within it smaller patterns, echoing structures, moments of near-harmony that crest and dissolve. It sounds like a cathedral organ held on a single massive note, the stops opening and closing by accident. It is the loudest sound you have ever heard in a place with no wind and no water and no animal throats to produce it.

The plants may be communicating through the sound — not meaning, but *resonance*, each filament responding to the vibrations of its neighbors, creating feedback patterns that spread through the chamber in slow waves. Or they may not be. They may simply be doing what mineral-flesh does in a closed space: vibrating.

The sound makes the walls vibrate. The walls have been vibrating for a very long time. The patterns of erosion on the interior surfaces — those ancient fossilized wood-grain textures — are shaped not by water or wind but by *sound*. The chamber is, slowly, being tuned.

## The Settlement

There are people here, or there were.

In the lowest part of the chamber, where the curved wall meets the floor in a natural alcove, there is a settlement of perhaps forty structures. They are built from chips and blocks of the mineralized wood-stone itself — cut from the walls with hand tools, stacked and fitted without mortar. The construction is primitive but orderly: a grid of small rooms, each one meter by two, with flat roofs of lashed plant-filament (the trailing filaments of the larger plants, harvested and dried — they remain flexible and strong). The rooms are empty now. Perhaps they were emptied long ago. Perhaps they were never full.

There are marks on the walls of the settlement — not writing, but a notation system of shallow carved dots and lines that appears to track the slow pitch-shifts of the ambient sound. Someone was listening. Someone was counting. The marks are extremely old. They are eroding at the same pace as the walls themselves.

There is a small forge-pit — cold, never large enough for true metallurgy, but sufficient for heating and softening certain minerals from the wall to shape them into tools. The tools are there. They are heavy and simple and, like everything else, slowly becoming one with the floor.

## The Ecology (Such As It Is)

Without water, without light, without fauna — the word *ecology* barely applies. What exists here is a single system: the wall, the plants, the sound, the air. The air itself is almost still. It circulates through unseen cracks in the roof or base, entering and leaving so slowly that its movement is imperceptible. It is oxygen-poor, rich in nitrogen and carbon dioxide and trace gases from the slow mineral-chemistry of the wall. Cool — permanently, passively cool, the temperature of deep earth, never changing.

The plants do not reproduce in any visible way. No seeds, no spores, no visible transfer of material between specimens. They simply *grow* — each one imperceptibly larger each century, extending its root-network a few millimeters deeper into the wall, adding a new convolution to its form. They will eventually fill the space. Not in any timeframe meaningful to the people who once lived here. Not in any timeframe meaningful to the chamber itself.

There is one other presence. On the ceiling, directly above the center of the chamber, there is a single plant that is different from the others. It is larger — several meters across — and it is inverted, hanging down like a chandelier. Its filaments are longer and thicker and they vibrate at a lower frequency, a bass note beneath the others that you feel in your sternum more than hear with your ears. It may be the oldest living thing in the chamber. It may be the only thing in the chamber that is aware, in whatever way plants are aware, that it is enclosed. Its roots disappear into the ceiling and go *up* — toward the sealed top of the trunk — and it has been growing toward the outside for longer than the settlement's dot-and-line notation system has been recording the sound.

It has not reached the surface yet.

## The Experience

Standing here — and you can stand, the floor is smooth and dry and faintly warm — you are in a place that was made by time and kept by accident. The darkness is absolute except for the phosphorescence, which is dim enough that after a while your eyes stop trying. The sound is everything. The sound is the room. You feel the slow cycles of resonance shift around you — patterns that take hours to complete, waves of volume and near-silence that move through the space like weather through a valley, except there is no weather and no valley, only this.

The air tastes of chalk and old wood and something metallic. The walls, if you press your palm to them, hum.

It is a place that has been settling toward its final form for longer than continents have held their current shapes. Nothing here is in a hurry. The plants grow. The sound shapes the walls. The walls shape the sound. The settlement crumbles into the floor at the same rate the floor becomes part of the wall. Everything converges.

In another hundred million years, the chamber will be full — solid — a fossil of a fossil, a trunk within a trunk, the plants and the walls and the sound all compressed into a single mass of mineral and silence. But for now, there is space. There is resonance. There is the faintest light, and the loudest quiet.
