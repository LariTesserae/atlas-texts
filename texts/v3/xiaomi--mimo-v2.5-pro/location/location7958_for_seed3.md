# The Forgebanks of Unsettled Altitude

---

## The Rock That Has Not Decided

The mountains are newborn.

This is not metaphor. The peaks at 4,800 meters — 5,200, 5,600, the highest still unnamed and still *rising* — are geologically hours old in some places, decades in others, and nowhere older than a single human lifetime. The stone here has not finished committing to being stone. Touch the exposed faces along the upper ridges and you will feel warmth, a faint gelatinous give, the texture of something that remembers being liquid. Basalt in the process of cooling. Granite deciding on its crystal structure over the course of a season, grain by grain, the way water decides on ice. In some sheltered overhangs you can watch it happen: rawite — the miners' word for it — translucent and amber, hardening from the outside in, occasionally trapping air bubbles that catch the dim light and glow briefly, like specimens in resin, before the stone opaques around them.

The uplift is continuous. Not violent — not the buckling convulsions of an earlier eon — but a slow, persistent heaving, maybe two or three centimeters a day at the active ridgelines, occasionally a lurch of half a meter when a new dome breaks surface. The city accounts for this. The city *depends* on this.

## The Fog and the Damp



Light does not reach the ground here. Not fully. Not ever.

The peaks generate their own weather — or perhaps it would be more accurate to say the peaks generate their own *atmosphere*. A mineral haze hangs perpetually at around 4,000 meters, a suspension of microscopic stone dust from the ongoing uplift, ground fine by the ceaseless friction of rising plates. It is not quite fog, not quite smoke. It scatters what little sunlight makes it through the regular cloud cover into a diffuse, directionless gray-white glow that the locals call *daywash*. The sun is a rumor here — a brighter patch in the haze that migrates across the sky over the course of a week, not a day, so slow is its apparent motion through the particulate.

Everything is damp. Not from rain — true rain is rare, maybe once a month, and when it comes it falls in heavy, mineral-tasting sheets that leave a faint chalky residue on every surface. The persistent wetness is condensation: the warm exhalations of newborn rock meeting the cool, thin air. Water beads on every surface. It weeps down cliff faces in thin, glittering trickles. It collects in the cupped leaves of the pale vegetation and in the concavities of rawite formations and in the gutters and channels that the city has built, with great foresight, into every wall, every walkway, every rooftop. The sound of dripping is the baseline of this place — a constant, arrhythmic patter that underlies everything, as if the world itself is slowly dissolving, though of course it is the opposite: the world is slowly *arriving*.

## The Spreading City: Tolmere



Tolmere spreads across three connected massifs and the saddle-valleys between them, a city of perhaps sixty thousand built in defiance of — or in partnership with — the fact that the ground beneath it is perpetually rising, shifting, and not yet geologically stable. It is an old city by human reckoning — seven generations — but a newborn city by any other measure, built on land that did not exist a century ago.

The architecture is distinct. Nothing here sits on a flat foundation. Buildings are *bolted* — literally, with enormous iron anchors drilled into semi-cooled rawite faces, the drill-points sealed with ceramic paste to prevent the rock from healing around the metal and ejecting it as it settled. Structures cling to ridgelines and hang from cliff faces and bridge across crevasses that are, themselves, still widening or narrowing on unpredictable schedules. The oldest districts, in the lower saddle-valleys where the stone has had time to stabilize, are almost conventional: streets, plazas, multi-story buildings of mortared rawite block, heavy and square. But move upward and the city becomes more provisional, more improvisational — platforms cantilevered from single anchor points, corridors tunneled through rock that is still warm to the touch, entire neighborhoods that exist on a single slab of stone which may, in five years, have tilted fifteen degrees or risen a hundred meters.

The industrial quarter occupies the widest saddle, at about 4,400 meters, where three geological plates converge and the upwelling of rawite is most consistent and most conveniently shaped — great smooth domes, like the shoulders of giants pushing up through the earth. The Tolmereans have built their forges directly onto these domes, tapping the geothermal energy that bleeds from the newborn stone. The forges run continuously. They have never, in the city's history, gone cold. There is no need for fuel — the rock itself provides, bleeding heat at a steady, uncomplaining rate that has barely changed in seventy years, even as the specific dome being tapped has risen and tilted and been re-engineered around half a dozen times. Iron and ceramic and glass, drawn from the rawite itself (which is rich in metals, still unsorted by the slow geological processes that will, in a million years, concentrate them into proper veins). The forges produce, among other things, the very bolts and anchors and platforms that hold the city to the rock. A circular economy, in the most literal sense: the mountain builds the city that clings to it.

The factory stacks exhale thin plumes of white steam — not smoke, for the furnaces burn nothing — which merge into the mineral haze and add to the dimness. On still days, the upper quarters of the city are draped in a double veil: the natural haze below, the industrial haze above, and between them a band of marginally clearer air where the birds fly. Or what pass for birds.

## The Ecologies of Thin Light



Photosynthesis here is a negotiation. The vegetation that clings to every available surface — and it *does* cling to every available surface, in defiance of the altitude and the dimness and the mineral substrate — has evolved, or adapted, or *arrived*, for conditions that exist nowhere else.

The dominant cover is **palemoss**: a dense, almost-white bryophyte that blankets the stable rock faces in thick, spongy mats up to a meter deep. It photosynthesizes, but poorly — it supplements its energy by chemosynthesis, drawing directly on the mineral compounds in the rawite and the condensation that perpetually bathes it. In the dim light, it glows. Not brightly. Not for human eyes to comfortably read by. But a faint, greenish bioluminescence that, on a mountainside seen from a distance, makes the entire terrain look like it is exhaling a slow, cold light. The moss is the foundation of everything. It holds moisture. It traps the mineral dust from the haze and converts it, slowly, into soil — a process that takes years in any given spot, but the moss has been at it for decades now, and in the oldest saddle-valleys there are genuine *pockets* of soil, centimeters deep, in which other things grow.

**Veinferns** are the next stage. Translucent, almost crystalline — their fronds are so pale they are nearly invisible in the daywash, but they fluoresce under the moss-light, and at night (which here means: during the long dim intervals when the bright patch in the haze is below the horizon) the ferneries are the most beautiful things in the world. They grow in the seep-lines, where condensation runs most consistently, and their root structures — visible through their translucent stems — follow the water channels down into the rawite itself, threading into hairline cracks in the cooling stone. They are, in a sense, part of the geology. They slow the erosion that would otherwise occur — though erosion here means something different from elsewhere. The rock is not being worn down. It is being *born up*. The ferns merely stabilize its surface, help it decide on a shape.

**Veil-trees** in the lower valleys. Not true trees — they have no wood, no bark. They are enormous fungal structures, columnar, rising six or eight meters from the shallow soil, their surfaces draped in sheets of pale, translucent tissue that hang like curtains and move in the slightest breeze. They fruit continuously: heavy, dark capsules that drop into the moss and are carried by the water channels into crevasses where they germinate. The veil-trees are the tallest organisms here. They are also, in their way, the loudest — when the wind moves through their tissue-layers, they produce a low, haunting resonance, almost tonal, that carries for kilometers through the haze. It is one of the signature sounds of Tolmere, audible even over the industrial hum: a slow, eerie organ-note that rises and falls with the air currents.

## The Fauna



Life here is abundant, but it does not behave.

The most visible animals are the **haze-drifters** — vast, slow, jellyfish-like organisms that float in the mineral haze itself, at altitudes between 4,000 and 5,500 meters. They are translucent, barely physical, their bodies mostly water and mineral dust held together by membranes of extraordinary thinness. They feed on the particulate in the haze, filtering it through their tissues, and they grow — some of the largest are ten or fifteen meters across, ghostly shapes that drift through the upper quarters of the city like slow-motion clouds with *structure*. They are not dangerous. They are barely *there*. But they are unsettling: when one passes through a street, which happens regularly, it leaves a film of moisture on every surface and a faint smell of ozone and wet stone, and those who have walked through one report a momentary sensation of displacement, of being *somewhere else*, that fades in seconds but leaves a lingering wrongness, a suspicion that the world has been very slightly rearranged.

Below the haze, the cliffs and ridgelines are home to **scree-runners** — six-limbed, warm-blooded creatures roughly the size of a large dog, covered in a fine mineral dust that they deliberately cultivate by rolling in the rawite seep-lines. The dust serves as camouflage (they are virtually invisible against the pale stone) and, possibly, as a nutrient source (they are observed licking their own flanks obsessively). They are social, vocal, and startlingly intelligent. They have been observed using tools — flat stones to crack open the mineral concretions that form in the condensation channels, extracting the nutrient-rich gel inside. More disturbingly, they have been observed *disassembling* city infrastructure — unscrewing bolts, prying up anchor plates — apparently out of curiosity rather than need. The Tolmereans tolerate this with a grudging affection. There are more scree-runners in the city than there are humans, and they were here first, and they will likely be here after.

In the ferneries and moss-beds, smaller things: **glass beetles** with exoskeletons of pureite mineral, nearly transparent, that feed on the veinferns. **Mist-moths**, pale and enormous (some with wingspans of forty centimeters), that lay their eggs in the condensation channels and whose larvae feed on palemoss. The larvae are harvested by the Tolmereans and eaten — they are, by all accounts, bland but nourishing.

And then there are the animals that are not, strictly speaking, animals.

## The Breakage



This is the part that is difficult to describe.

Tolmere exists, as far as its inhabitants can determine, in a region where reality is *thin*. Not absent — not a void, not chaos — but thin. The rules that govern physical law elsewhere are present here but negotiable. They apply, but softly. They hold, but with flex.

The most obvious manifestation is spatial. Distances in Tolmere are not constant. The walk from the lower forge-quarter to the upper residential platform is, on most days, about two kilometers — a steep, switchbacked climb through three climate zones of vegetation. But on some days, it is a hundred meters. On some days, it is nine kilometers. The route does not change. The terrain does not change. The distance *changes*. The locals have long since stopped being alarmed by this. They plan for it. They leave early. They carry extra water (collected from the condensation channels in ceramic flasks) and extra food (beetle larvae, dried veil-tree fruit, a dense mineral cake called *sett* that is made from compressed moss and tastes like chalk and smoke). "The hill is long today," they say, the way someone elsewhere might say "the traffic is bad today," and no one thinks this is strange, because it is not strange here. It is simply the weather of place.

Time is more consistent, but not fully. There are zones — particularly near the active rawite upwellings — where clocks run slow. Not by much. A few minutes per day. But it accumulates. The industrial quarter, which sits directly above the largest active dome, operates on a schedule that drifts by perhaps an hour per month relative to the lower quarters. Workers descend from a shift to find that, in the saddle-valleys below, it is later than they expected — or earlier. This is managed. The forge-masters maintain a master-clock in the lowest, most stable district, and all time is referenced to it, and no one minds the cognitive dissonance of climbing upward into yesterday and descending into next week. It is simply how the hill works.

More unsettling are the **presences**. This is the word the Tolmereans use, and they use it carefully, the way one uses a word for something one does not fully believe in but has learned not to disbelieve. In the deepest tunnels, in the rawite faces that are still actively cooling, in the crevasses where the rock is youngest and warmest and most gelatinous — there are shapes in the stone. Not fossils. Not inclusions. *Shapes*. Structures that are not geological but also do not correspond to any known biology. Geometries that are almost-but-not-quite symmetrical. Spirals that do not follow the Fibonacci sequence but follow *something*. They are visible only when the rock is at a certain stage of cooling — translucent enough to see through, solid enough to hold a shape — and they fade as the stone matures. The miners encounter them daily and do not discuss them overmuch. Some believe they are the nascent forms of organisms that have not yet been born — that the rock, in its youth, is gestating things. Others believe they are the shadows of things that exist in the parts of reality that this thin-stretched place lets through. The scree-runners are fascinated by them and will press their faces against cooling rawite for hours, watching.

The most dramatic manifestation is the **veil-shifts**. Once or twice a year, without warning, the mineral haze *lifts*. It pulls back from a section of the city — a neighborhood, a valley, a single ridgeline — and for a period of hours, sometimes days, that section is exposed to unfiltered light. True light. Sunlight. The effect is devastating and beautiful. The palemoss, adapted to dimness, burns. The veinferns curl and die. The haze-drifters flee, rising to higher altitudes with a speed that suggests they understand, on some deep and terrible level, what direct light means. And in that exposed, burning, brilliant section of city, things become *solid* in a way they were not before. Distances stop flexing. Time runs true. The shapes in the rawite vanish. Everything is clear and sharp and *real*, and the people who live in the affected area describe a feeling of claustrophobia — as if the walls of the world have closed in — as if the ambiguity that allows life here has been temporarily revoked.

When the haze returns, it comes like a tide, and the relief is physical. The moss recovers. The ferns regrow. The veils descend. The distances remember how to breathe.

## Sound, and the Slow

The soundscape of Tolmere is layered and constant.

At the base: dripping. Always dripping. The condensation channels sing with it, a ceaseless patter and trickle that is so omnipresent it becomes silence — the residents no longer hear it, the way coastal people no longer hear the sea.

Above that: the organ-note of the veil-trees, rising and falling with the wind, sometimes harmonizing when several trees resonate together across a valley. It is the sound people here associate with home.

Above that: the industrial hum. The forges do not roar. They *thrum*. A deep, bone-level vibration, felt more than heard, that emanates from the geothermal domes and resonates through the platforms and buildings bolted to them. The factories that process the rawite — grinding, sorting, smelting, shaping — add a higher register, a rhythmic clang and hiss that operates at the pace of the production cycle, which is slow. Everything here is slow. The forges run at geological speed. A single bolt, from rawite extraction to finished product, takes weeks. A building takes years. The city itself is a project of centuries, which is appropriate, because the mountain it clings to is a project of eons, and neither is in any hurry.

The scree-runners chatter and bark from the cliff faces, particularly at dusk (a long, slow dimming that takes hours). The haze-drifters, when they pass through occupied areas, produce a faint electrical crackle — static discharge from their mineral-laden tissues.

And sometimes, from deep in the rawite, from the cooling faces where the shapes live — a sound that is not a sound. A vibration below the range of human hearing that is felt in the sternum and the back of the teeth. The miners call it *settling*. The philosophers of Tolmere call it *becoming*. The scree-runners press their bodies flat against the rock when it happens and do not move until it passes.

## Inhabitation

Sixty thousand people live here. They are not, by most standards, thriving — the altitude alone would kill most lowlanders, and the mineral haze irritates the lungs of newcomers for months — but they are *persisting*, which in Tolmere is considered the more honest verb. They are miners and forge-workers and botanists and builders and drift-cartographers (the specialists who map the shifting distances, updating the city's spatial charts daily, an occupation that is part science and part divination). They eat beetle larvae and moss-cake and the occasional scree-runner (a delicacy, and a political statement — the animals are not endangered, but neither are they *not* watching). They drink condensation-water, filtered through moss-beds to remove the mineral content, which leaves it flat and faintly sweet. They brew a fermented drink from veil-tree fruit that tastes of nothing on earth because nothing on earth grew it.

They are patient people. The city selects for patience. Those who need the world to be stable and predictable do not last — they descend to the lowlands within a year, or they break. Those who remain are the ones who have learned to live with a world that is still becoming — a world where the ground may rise beneath you tomorrow, where your commute may triple or halve without explanation, where the shapes in the rock watch you with an attention that may or may not be attention, where the fog lifts and the world turns brutally, searingly real and then the fog returns and everything is mercifully vague again.

They build. They forge. They tend the moss and harvest the beetles and chart the distances and listen to the veil-trees sing. They press their hands against cooling rawite and feel the warmth of a world still being made, and they are not afraid, or they are afraid in the way that one is afraid of something vast and ongoing and not fully comprehensible — the way one is afraid of the ocean, or time, or the slow brightening of a star.

The mountain rises. The city rises with it. The haze drifts between.
