# The Stacks

You come upon it not all at once but in pieces, the way you find a cairn in fog — a wall first, then another wall, then the understanding that these are not natural features but decisions made long ago by hands.

It is a place of stone.

The settlement sits on a high shelf of basalt, a tongue of plateau that the mountains pushed up and then forgot. The shelf is perhaps two hundred paces across, bounded on three sides by slopes too steep for building — grey-black rock falling away into a valley that is more felt than seen, a great absence of air where clouds collect and rest. The fourth side is a narrow saddle of land connecting the shelf to the main ridge, and it is here that the path comes in, barely wide enough for two people to pass. There is a gate, though it has no door. Just two taller stacks of stone marking the threshold, as if the village were a room and the world outside were a corridor.

**The architecture.** The buildings are dry-stacked basalt, no mortar, no cut blocks — just shaped riverstone and fieldstone fitted together by patience and weight. Each structure is a cylinder or rough rectangle, rarely taller than a single story, with flat roofs of thin slabs laid over wooden beams that have long since petrified into something between wood and rock. There are perhaps fifteen buildings. Perhaps twenty. The count is uncertain because several have subsided into their own foundations, becoming low mounds that are not quite ruin and not quite building — the stone returning to its closer arrangement with the ground. The spaces between structures are narrow lanes, unpaved, the basalt bedrock worn into shallow channels by the passage of feet over centuries. The stone underfoot is damp always, slick with a thin film of moisture that never quite dries, and everywhere there is the smell of wet rock and the faint mineral tang of iron.

**The damp.** It does not rain often here, but the air is never dry. Moisture rolls up from the valley in slow tongues of cloud that settle over the shelf and cling to every surface. Condensation beads on the stone walls. Lichen — pale green, grey, the occasional rust-orange — colonizes every north-facing surface, and moss grows in the crevices between stacked stones, thin and wiry, barely alive, more texture than organism. There are no streams on the shelf itself, but in one corner of the settlement a stone-lined cistern collects water that seeps through a crack in the bedrock. It fills slowly, perpetually, and is never full and never empty. The water is clear and very cold and tastes of nothing. It is, perhaps, the most important structure here.

**What grows.** Almost nothing. The shelf is too high, too exposed, too mineral for most plants. There is no soil to speak of — just thin patches of gritty decomposed stone in sheltered hollows where a few tufts of sedge have established themselves, growing so slowly that a single plant might be older than a human life. In the lee of the southern walls, where the stone holds a fraction more warmth, there are small thickets of crowberry, low and sprawling, bearing fruit that is black and tasteless but not poisonous. A single stunted rowan grows near the cistern, its trunk twisted horizontal by decades of wind before it found a shape the wind would accept. It produces white flowers once every few years and small bitter berries that no one here eats but that some small bird — not seen, only inferred from droppings — occasionally visits.

**The quiet.** The wind is the primary sound, and even it is muted — more a presence than a noise, a steady low breathing across the stone that makes the lichen sway imperceptibly. Occasionally a stone shifts somewhere, a settling sound, a soft *tick* as a piece of rock finds a more stable position in a wall. The cistern drips. Once, in the distance, a raven calls, but the sound arrives thin and worn, as if it has traveled a long way to get here. There are no mechanical sounds. There is nothing to make them.

**The people, if they are here.** It is unclear. The settlement is organized — lanes deliberate, buildings placed with intention, the cistern engineered with a simple but effective overflow channel — and so it was built by minds that understood space and need. But there is no one here now, or rather, there is no one *visible* here now. Doors (stone slabs on pivots, primitive hinges of wooden pegs set into drilled holes) stand slightly ajar. In one of the better-preserved structures, there is a hearth — a ring of stones blackened with old smoke — and beside it a low bench carved from a single block, smooth with use, its surface worn into a shallow saddle where bodies sat. Everything suggests presence, but everything suggests it *has been*, not that it *is*. The settlement is arranged for the living but occupied only by the evidence of them.

And yet — and this is the thing that makes you slow your breathing — it does not feel abandoned. It feels *at rest*. There is no sense of departure, no feeling that something was interrupted or that the place is waiting. The stone is patient. The moisture is patient. The lichen grows its millimeter per decade and does not complain. The rowan puts out its leaves each spring with no audience and drops them each autumn to no consequence, and the cistern fills and slowly empties through its own seepage, a cycle as quiet and indifferent as tectonic time.

**What remains.** In the largest building, which may have been a gathering place or a storehouse or simply the home of whoever built most carefully, there are marks on the walls. Not paintings — this is not a culture that decorated. But tally marks. Groups of vertical lines, cut into the stone with a harder stone or perhaps an antler tool, in sets of five and ten, running along one wall in rows that number into the hundreds. They are very old. The edges of the cuts have been softened by centuries of condensation, so that each mark is a shallow groove rather than a sharp line. They record something — days, or winters, or births, or grain stores — but the system is not legible. They are meaning fossilized past the point of reading, which is perhaps a kind of peace.

**The stillness.** Nothing changes here, or changes so slowly that change and stillness become the same thing. The lichen grows. The stone erodes. The cistern cycles. The clouds come and go but always return in the same patterns, pushed by the same winds over the same ridge. If you came back in ten years, the rowan would be slightly more horizontal, the tally marks slightly more smoothed, and nothing else would be different. The place has reached its final state — not in decay, but in balance, a settlement so old and so simple and so weathered that it has become geology wearing the shape of human intention, like a shell that has outlived the creature that made it but persists in its spiral because the spiral is the strongest shape for the material.
