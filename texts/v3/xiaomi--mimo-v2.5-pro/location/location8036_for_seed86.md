# The Cathedral Deeps

---

You enter through a sinkhole — not a dramatic one, just a ragged mouth in a limestone plain where the ground gave way sometime in the recent geologic present, the collapse exposing fresh-cut rock still sharp enough to tear skin. The walls angle down steeply, slick with a film of condensation that never quite becomes water, never quite dries. You descend along natural shelves and fractured ledges, and the surface light thins quickly, replaced by something else.

Not darkness. Not exactly.

---

The first thing that hits you is the sound.

It arrives before you have any sense of the space — a dense, layered wall of noise that seems to have no source and every source. Clicking. Chirring. The wet pop of something feeding. A high, oscillating whine that rises and falls in waves so regular it seems mechanical, but isn't. Wingbeats — not individual, but in aggregate, the way wind sounds when it moves a forest, except this is animal, alive, moving through open air in numbers that make the air itself feel occupied.

When the ceiling lifts away and you step into the first true chamber, you begin to understand why.

The space is enormous. The ceiling vaults upward into darkness — you can't see the top, but you can hear it, the way sound behaves differently when there's a hundred meters of empty air above your head. The floor is a broad, irregular basin of pale limestone, terraced in natural shelves where water once pooled and evaporated and pooled again across some long, unhurried cycle. The rock is young as these things go. Dissolution has carved it recently, geologically speaking — the features are still sharp, angular where they should be rounded, the stalactites thin and pointed like teeth rather than the fat, blunted columns of ancient systems. Fissures split the floor in clean, straight lines. Walls are rough-cut, textured, still remembering the force that opened them.

The floor is mostly dry. Pockets of water sit in depressions — shallow, clear pools with a faint mineral taste, their surfaces occasionally dimpled by condensation dripping from above. Between the pools, the stone is dusted with a thin, pale sediment. Almost nothing grows. A grey-green film of moisture-loving algae clings to the wetter edges of rock. In the rare places where shafts of surface light find their way down through cracks and collapses, there are patches of pale, etiolated moss — yellowish, barely photosynthetic, more ghost than plant. Occasional fungal shelves bracket out from walls near the water, their surfaces damp and faintly fragrant in a way that is not unpleasant but is deeply foreign to anything you've smelled above.

That's it for vegetation. The rest of this place is stone, water, air, and animals.

---

The animals are *everywhere*.

Bats first — because they're the loudest, the most numerous, the most overwhelming. They roost in the high darkness of the vault in colonies so dense that the rock face they cling to looks like a living surface, a textured hide that ripples and shifts. When a section of the colony stirs — and they are always stirring, always cycling between sleep and wakefulness in small, constant rotations — the sound is extraordinary: a papery rustle layered thousands of times, underlaid with a low, almost subsonic murmur of vocalizations. They emerge in pulses. Streams of them pour from the upper darkness and spiral down through the chamber in helical columns, their echolocation clicks bouncing off every surface in a clattering, overlapping cascade that sounds, from inside it, like standing inside a rainstorm made of tiny hammers. They descend to the pools to drink, skimming the surface with their jaws, then wheel upward again. This cycle has not changed. It will not change.

Cave swallows nest in the mid-levels — mud structures cemented to ledges and overhangs in crowded, overlapping clusters, each one just large enough for a bird to turn around in. They are smaller than their surface cousins, paler, with a slightly enlarged eye. Their calls are sharp and repetitive, a three-note pattern that repeats from hundreds of throats at once, creating a chattering wall of sound that rises above the bat noise and defines the audible texture of the mid-space. They hunt insects on the wing, and they are always hunting.

The insects are the reason all of this works. Cave crickets in particular — pale, long-legged, enormous by surface standards — occupy every ledge, every crack, every horizontal surface from the floor to the highest reach of the chamber. They sit motionless for hours, their antennae drifting slowly, and then move all at once in some stimulus only they perceive, a wave of pale bodies flowing across the rock like spilled milk. They eat the fungal growth, the algae, the thin organic film that accumulates wherever water sits. They are food for everything above them in this quiet, inverted pyramid.

In the pools, there are fish. Small, eyeless, translucent — you can see their organs through their skin, a faint pinkish knot of viscera in an otherwise glass-clear body. They navigate by lateral line, by taste, by the faint electric field of their own bodies reflected off the stone. Salamanders share the pools — pale, unhurried, with the same translucent quality, as if the absence of light has made everything here partially see-through. They eat the crickets that fall in. The crickets fall in constantly.

There are spiders. Not web-builders — there's nothing to anchor a web to in the open air of the chambers — but active hunters, long-legged and pale, that move along the rock in quick, darting runs and ambush prey from cracks. Their silk is used only for egg cases and drag lines, and these accumulate in the narrow spaces, in the crevices, a fine, dusty tracery that catches on your skin as you move through tight passages.

---

The scale of it continues. Beyond the first chamber, the system opens further — a network of connected caverns, each one a variation on the same theme, linked by wide passages carved by water that no longer flows at volume, only seeps and condenses. Some chambers are smaller, tighter, their ceilings low enough that you can see the bats overhead as a shifting carpet of small, folded bodies. Others are vast, their far walls lost in the gloom, their floors broken into archipelagos of rock separated by shallow water that reflects nothing because there is almost nothing to reflect. You can move through this system for hours. The character doesn't change — the same pale stone, the same dimness, the same animal density, the same sound — but the shapes vary, the proportions shift, and occasionally you find a chamber with a feature: a natural bridge of rock spanning a deeper pool, a collapse zone where the ceiling has come down and created a rubble field colonized by a particularly dense cricket population, a narrow chimney of rock ascending upward toward a pinpoint of grey surface light around which a cluster of moths has gathered, turning in slow circles.

---

There is no evidence of people. No fire-black on the walls, no carved marks, no artifacts. The stone is too young for anything to have accumulated, and the entrance is not obvious from the surface — just a crack in a limestone plain that could be anything. If anyone has been here, they left nothing. The animals have always outnumbered them by a factor so large it renders the question meaningless.

---

The air is cool and still. It moves only with the thermal currents generated by the animal colonies — a faint, warm updraft from the bat roosts, a cooler layer near the water — but there is no wind, no weather, no change. The humidity sits at a constant, damp plateau: not wet, not dry, the air carrying just enough moisture to keep every surface faintly slick and to support the thin film of biological growth that feeds everything else. The temperature does not vary between day and night, between season and season. This place is buffered. It has reached its equilibrium and it holds it with the quiet, immovable stubbornness of a system that has no reason to change and no mechanism for doing so.

The light does not vary. The dimness is constant — a deep, grey, ambient gloom that is enough to see shapes in, not enough to see color. Everything here is the color of limestone: pale, cream, grey, with the occasional darker stain of mineral seepage. The animals are pale. The water is clear. The fungi are white. The moss is yellow. This is a world drained of chromatic variation, operating on texture and movement and sound, and it has operated this way for longer than anyone has been listening, and it will continue.

---

Standing in one of the larger chambers, halfway between the ceiling colony and the floor colony, inside the sound the way a fish is inside water — you realize that the noise has a structure. It's not chaos. It's an acoustic ecosystem, each species occupying its own frequency range, its own temporal pattern, its own spatial layer. The bats operate in the ultrasonic and the low-murmur bands. The swowns own the midrange. The crickets contribute a faint, continuous stridulation at the floor level that functions as a kind of baseline hum. The dripping water provides punctuation — irregular, arrhythmic, the only element that doesn't seem patterned.

But even that is nearly constant. The rate of dripping doesn't change. The pools don't rise or fall. The colonies don't grow or shrink. The crickets don't boom or bust. Everything here has found its number, and it holds it, year after year, cycle after cycle, in the quiet, perfect, overwhelming stability of a place that is complete.
