# The Throat

You descend into it before you understand it's there.

The plateau gives way with almost no warning — the stone simply tilts, and then you're walking down a gentle ramp of pale limestone into a basin perhaps a hundred and fifty meters across. The walls rise around you, not sheer but patient, carved into flowing shapes that suggest enormous softness. Every surface gleams with moisture. Water doesn't fall here so much as *perspire* — it wells from the stone in slow, bright beads that gather and run in thin threads downward, joining rivulets that join channels that pool in the basin's floor in a shallow, clear lake no deeper than your ankle. The limestone is old beyond reckoning. Every edge has been dissolved into curves. Every face is smooth as bone worn by centuries of hands.

The light is ordinary. Daylight reaches the bottom without difficulty — the basin is wide enough, shallow enough. But it arrives *changed*, diffused by the moisture in the air into something that has no single source, no hard shadow. The result is a permanent soft glow, as though the light is not falling on the place but emanating from the wet stone itself.

---

The vegetation grows in islands.

There are perhaps fifteen or twenty distinct clusters scattered across the basin floor, each one a dense knot of plant life separated from the others by wide stretches of bare, wet limestone. You could walk between them easily — the stone is grippy, not slick, its surface textured into a fine lacework of dissolution pits — but each cluster feels like its own world. The largest is the size of a bedroom. The smallest is barely wider than your outstretched arms.

What grows in them is lush and strange. Ferns with fronds as long as your leg, their undersides dusted with something that looks like pale blue powder. Succulents that store water they don't need — the air is already saturated — in translucent leaves that glow faintly when light passes through them, their flesh a green so deep it borders on black. Mosses that don't creep but *stack*, building upward into spongy columns a handspan tall, always slightly damp, always slightly warm to the touch. Between and among and through everything, a web of thin white root-structures that look less like roots and more like veins, or threads, or the branching paths of something that has been deciding where to go for a very long time.

No flower blooms. Nothing hums. Nothing chirps. There are no insects — not even dead ones. No webs. No shells. No bones.

The silence is the first thing you noticed and it is the thing that does not go away.

---

It is the silence that makes you start to notice the notches.

In the stone around the largest plant-cluster, at irregular intervals, there are marks. Small. Worn almost to nothing by water and time. But deliberate — too regular, too consistently sized, too evenly spaced to be natural. They look like someone, long ago, scored the limestone with a harder stone. Not writing. Not decoration. Something more like *counting*. Or *measuring*. Or *naming*.

You find more of them near other clusters. Always near the plants. Never far from the water. The marks are so old that the dissolution has nearly reclaimed them — in another few centuries they'll be gone, smoothed back into the general softness of the place. But they're there. Someone was here. Someone with hands, or something like hands, and something like patience, and something like a reason.

There are no other signs. No ash. No shelter. No worn paths. Just the marks, and the plants, and the water, and the silence.

---

The air is warm and it smells of limestone and green things and something faintly metallic, like blood or copper or the air before a storm that never arrives. The moisture in it clings to your skin without discomfort. You feel slightly buoyant — not physically, but *perceptually*, as though the saturation of the air makes you more visible to the place than you would otherwise be. As though you are slightly more *present* here than you are accustomed to being.

The water in the ankle-deep lake is clear but not transparent — you can see the stone floor beneath, but the light bends strangely through the surface, and what you see beneath seems deeper than the water could possibly be. If you watch long enough, you might convince yourself something is moving down there, very slowly, very far below. You would be wrong. There is nothing alive in this water. But the shapes you see are not *nothing* — they are the limestone itself, folding downward into chambers you cannot reach, channels carved by water over timescales that make the erosion marks on the surface look recent. The basin is deeper than it appears. The throat has a throat.

---

The strangeness of the place is not dramatic. Nothing shimmers. Nothing pulses. The stone does not move. The plants do not react to you. But the geometry of it — the way every cluster of vegetation occupies a position that, if you mapped them, would resolve into something almost like a pattern, not quite, a pattern that keeps dissolving the moment you try to confirm it — and the marks, and the silence, and the depth of the water, and the way the moisture in the air makes the light feel *heavy* — together these things produce a sensation that is less fear than *recognition*. Not of something you've seen before. Of something you have *been* before. As though the place remembers a version of you that had reason to come here, long ago, and score marks into the stone, and wait for something that still hasn't arrived.

Nothing has changed here in a very long time. The water rises and falls on cycles so slow you would need to live here for years to detect them. The plants grow, but imperceptibly — you could watch one for an hour and see no movement. The erosion continues at its ancient pace, deepening the basin by a thickness you could cover with your thumb over a span of centuries. This is a place that has found its shape and is slowly, slowly becoming more itself.

You are warm. You are wet. You are surrounded by life that does not acknowledge you. The stone holds you gently in its palm.

The silence is so complete that you begin to hear your own blood, and wonder if it sounds like water moving through limestone, and wonder if the distinction matters here, and wonder if anything you brought with you — language, expectation, the assumption of departure — still applies in a place that has been waiting this long, this patiently, for no one in particular.
