# The Chorus Plateaus

## The Ground

The land rises. It has been rising for a very long time, or perhaps it was always this high — no one alive would remember, and the oldest structures here don't speak of a time when the ground was lower. The surface is a hard-baked mineral crust, pale ochre to bone-white, cracked into polygons the size of houses. Where the crust has worn thin, beneath it lies a denser substrate of calcium andite — the compressed remains of ancient microbial mats, billions of years old, calcium-fused into something between limestone andite andite andite and something no equivalent word exists for on your world. It rings when struck. Certain low areas are dusted withite andite andite andite — fine pale powder that catches in the wind and hangs in columns of gold light during the long afternoons.

Erosion has done its deep work. Gullies and arroyos score the plateau, some shallow, some as deep as valleys, their walls layered with strata of mineral and compressed biological material in bands of cream, rust, and grey. These are dry almost all the time. Almost.

Water here is a rumor and a discipline. No rivers cross the surface. No lakes sit in the basins. But once every few weeks — sometimes once every few months — pressure differentials across the vast plateau generate thermal storms that sweep in columns across the landscape, dropping brief, violent curtains of rain that last minutes. The water doesn't pool. It can't; the mineral crust sheds it too fast, and the arroyos fill and empty in hours. What remains is *below*: a shallow water table, perhaps two or three body-lengths beneath the surface, accessible only to organisms with the root architecture to reach it. And what remains is *above*: condensation. Every dawn, as the plateau radiates the day's heat into the thin sky, moisture beads on every surface — a grey dew, a slick on the mineral crust, droplets on every leaf and chitin plate. It lasts perhaps an hour. The world drinks, and then it burns again.

The air smells of hot stone, mineral dust, and something enzymatic — a faint sourness that thickens near the great organisms.

## The Organisms

Here is where the parameters become strange.

The vegetation is *total*. The biomass of this world is almost entirely plant — 94%, 95%, more. And yet the land appears sparse, because the plants are not spread evenly. They are not ground cover. They are not forest. They are **singularities**.

They are called, by the few peoples who have named them, the **solas** — though that word means different things in different scattered camps. Each sola is an individual organism of enormous size. The smallest are perhaps forty meters tall. The largest are visible from a day's walk away, their upper structures breaching the thermal haze like pale monoliths. They are not trees. They have no wood. Their bodies are composed of layered, pressurized fibrous tissue — something between a succulent, a bamboo, and a coral — that is pale green at the base and bleaches to bone-white in the upper reaches where the UV is fiercest. They are mostly trunk: thick, columnar, lightly ridged, sweating a thin resin that smells of tamarind and hot copper. At their summits they open into canopies of broad, overlapping fronds — not for shade (there is nothing below them that needs shade in the traditional sense) but for *condensation capture*. The fronds are textured on their undersides with millions of fine hydrophilic hairs that collect dawn moisture and funnel it down channels in the trunk to the root system. From a distance, a sola at dawn looks like it is smoking — a white mist rising from its canopy as the collected water evaporates in the first minutes of heat.

Each sola is spaced from its nearest neighbor by hundreds of meters, sometimes kilometers. The plateau is vast — continental in scale, a single elevated landmass with its own weather systems, its own wind patterns, its own thermal columns that rise and collapse and generate the storms. Across this expanse, the solas are scattered like columns in a ruin. They are not random. They follow the hidden water table, clustering where it is shallowest, thinning where it drops deep. Seen from above — if one could see from above — they would trace a branching, riverine pattern across the plateau, the ghost of watercourses that haven't seen surface water in millennia.

The solas are old. The erosion profile of this landscape — two-point-oh, established, settled — applies to them as much as to the stone. Their lower trunks are scarred and calcified, their bases flared and anchored in mantles ofite and their own shed material that has composted into mineral soil. Some have died. The dead ones stand as hollow white columns, their fibrous interiors slowly crumbling, visited by no scavengers because there is nothing here that eats plant matter in the conventional sense. They erode on geological timelines. The landscape is littered with their stumps, their collapsed trunk segments, half-buried in mineral dust.

But they grow. They are alive. The plateau is alive.

## The Fauna

And this is where it becomes **loud**.

If the solas are the bones of this place, the fauna is its voice, and it will not be quiet.

The animals here are overwhelming. Not in size — though some are large — but in *number*, in *density around the solas*, in the sheer biomass concentrated in and on each organism while the spaces between remain empty and silent. Each sola is a world. Each sola hosts a complete, stratified ecosystem of creatures that have co-evolved with it over timescales that make the word "symbiosis" insufficient. They are not visitors. They are **components**.

In the root zone, in the shadow of the swollen base: **burrowers**. Pale, segmented, multi-limbed things the length of a forearm that excavate through the shed organic material and mineral soil, aerating it, processing it, cycling nutrients back to the sola's roots. They are blind. They communicate through vibration — drumming their tail-plates against the ground in complex rhythms that you feel in your teeth before you hear them. At any given moment, the soil around a sola's base is *churning* with them. The ground itself seems to breathe.

Climbing the trunk, in the crevices and resin-channels: **scaled grazers** — six-limbed, flat-bodied, the size of a hand to the size of a torso, that feed on the resin and the thin biofilm of microorganisms that grow on the sola's skin. They are territorial, loud, and numerous. They make a sound like a wet comb being dragged across a washboard — a buzzing, rattling, sawing noise that never stops, that scales with the heat, so that at midday when the temperature climbs to its peak, the trunks of the solas *sing* with it. A sola in full heat is wrapped in a column of sound so dense it distorts the air. You feel it as pressure. You feel it in your sinuses and behind your eyes.

In the upper canopy, in the frond-structures: the **aerial fauna**. This is where the noise becomes truly extraordinary. The fronds host nesting colonies of gliding, membranous creatures — not birds, not bats, something older and stranger, with elongated bodies, four wings, and faces that are mostly mouth. They are social. They are communicative. They have, as far as anyone has studied, *language* — not representational language, but tonal, harmonic, complex vocalizations that serve territorial, mating, and what appears to be *aesthetic* purpose. They sing. And they sing **constantly**, and they sing **together**, and when a sola hosts a full colony — which every sola does, because every sola must, because the relationship is obligate — the sound is a wall.

Stand between two solas, in the empty mineral waste between them, and you can hear both. The sound carries across the dry air with terrifying efficiency. It layers. It interferes. At certain times of day, when thermal conditions create inversion layers, the sound of the entire plateau folds into something that is less heard than *undergone* — a harmonic resonance that vibrates the mineral crust itself, that makes the dust dance in standing-wave patterns, that the scattered human inhabitants of this place describe as the plateau *thinking*.

Deafening is not an exaggeration. It is a clinical description. Unprotected exposure at close range to a mature sola at midday causes pain, disorientation, and eventually hearing damage. The fauna does not care. The fauna has evolved in this sound. The fauna *is* this sound.

There are predators, too — of course there are. Higher up the food chain: lean, heat-adapted hunters that scale the solas at night when the grazers are sluggish, that glide between solas on membranes stretched between elongated limbs, that hunt the aerial colonies at dawn when the dew makes wings heavy. They are quiet. They are the only quiet things here. They have to be.

And there are the **deep fauna** — creatures that live in the arroyos and gullies, that shelter in the shadowed cracks of the mineral crust during the day and emerge at night to forage on windblown organic debris. Pale, photosensitive, numerous. The arroyos at night are a second ecosystem, quieter, stranger, running parallel to the daytime world of the solas like a shadow.

## The People

They are here. Barely. Scattered.

Perhaps a few hundred, across the entire plateau, living in loose bands of ten to thirty. They build small — very small. Shelters of sola-fiber and stacked mineral slabs, low-walled and flat-roofed, pressed against the bases of solas where the burrower-churned soil is softer and the resin provides waterproofing and the enormous trunk provides windbreak. These are not permanent settlements. They move between solas on seasonal circuits, following the condensation patterns, the grazer migrations, the blooming cycles. Their technology is bone, resin, shaped stone, woven sola-fiber. They have simple metal — hammered copper from exposed veins in the arroyo walls — for blades and awls. That is all.

They are adapted to the sound. Or they are not, and they suffer. Some bands have developed hearing protection — plugs of resin-softened fiber pressed into the ear canal, head-wraps of layered sola-bark. Some have not, and their elders are deaf by middle age, communicating in a developed sign language that is gestural, spatial, and beautiful to watch — their hands moving in the shadow of a sola's trunk while the world screams around them.

They do not farm. They do not herd. They are *participants* in the sola ecosystems, harvesting grazers with hooked poles, collecting resin, eating the burrowers roasted in mineral-ash pits. They are one more species in the web. They are not the dominant one. If they disappeared, nothing on this plateau would change.

They have stories. They say the solas are the fingers of something buried, something that fell asleep so long ago the world grew over it. They say the sound is its breathing. They say that if the sound ever stops, the thing will wake, and the plateau will collapse.

There is no evidence for any of this.

But the sound does not stop, so the question has never been tested.

## The Day

Dawn. The temperature drops — relatively; it is still warm — and the condensation comes. Every surface glistens. The aerial colonies erupt in their dawn choruses, a sound like a thousand tuning forks struck simultaneously, building in layers as more and more creatures wake and add their voices. The burrowers surface briefly to drink from the moisture, their pale bodies slick and writhing in the root zones. The grazers descend from the upper trunks where they sheltered overnight, moving lower where the dew is heaviest, their buzzing beginning tentatively and then swelling. The human bands emerge from their shelters, working quickly — collecting water from carefully positioned condensation trays of shaped bark, harvesting the surfaced burrowers, repairing tools. They work in the brief cool window. They are efficient.

The sun clears the horizon and the heat begins. Within an hour, the dew is gone. The mineral crust shimmers. The sola trunks begin their thermal expansion — a deep, structural groaning, like a ship's hull under stress, that harmonizes with the fauna-sound. By midday, the air between two solas is a vibrating wall of noise and heat-haze, the temperature climbing to levels that send the burrowers underground and the humans into shade. The predators are asleep in cracks and hollows. The grazers are pressed flat against the trunks, their buzzing at maximum, their metabolism running at full bore, processing resin, growing, reproducing. The aerial colonies are in full song — not random, but structured, call-and-response patterns rippling through the canopy like wind through wheat.

Afternoon. Thermal columns build. You can see them — columns of rising air made visible by the dust they carry, spiraling upward from the plateau into the thin sky, reaching heights where they generate their own cloud-caps. The storms, when they come, come from these. A thermal column passes directly over a sola and the pressure differential rips moisture from the canopy, generating a brief, violent downpour — a vertical river, lasting minutes, that soaks the sola and nothing else, the water channeled immediately down the trunk and into the root system. The fauna surges during rain. The sound *changes* — becomes frantic, ecstatic, the aerial colonies wheeling and screaming, the grazers vibrating so fast they blur, the burrowers erupting en masse from the soil to catch the runoff. And then it stops. The column moves on. The sola steams. The noise subsides to its baseline roar.

Dusk. Temperature drops again. The predators stir. The human bands gather around their ash-pits and trade stories in sign language or in low voices drowned by the evening chorus, which is different from the morning one — lower, more harmonic, the aerial colonies shifting to their evening mode, which sounds less like alarm and more like something the humans describe as *sorrow* or *remembering*, though whether those are translations or projections, no one has determined.

Night. The solas are pale columns against a sky dense with stars — the atmosphere is thin at this elevation, and the light pollution is zero, and the only light is starlight and the faint bioluminescence of certain canopy-organisms that glow blue-white along the frond-edges. The sound drops. Not to silence — never to silence — but to a lower register, a thrumming, the burrowers' subsonic drumming carrying through the ground, the grazers muted and still, the aerial colonies murmuring in their sleep. The predators move between solas in the dark, silent, hunting. The arroyo fauna emerges — pale, quiet, a second world unfolding in the shadows.

The plateau breathes. The sound rises and falls like respiration. The solas stand in their ancient spacing, pale and immense, and the world between them is empty mineral dust, and the world upon them is **everything**.
