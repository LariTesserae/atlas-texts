# The Weeping Plateau

## Ascent

You would not expect the heat. Not here, not at this elevation.

The plateau begins to make sense only once you stop thinking about how altitude is supposed to work. You climb through lower forests — heavier, thicker, more conventionally tropical — and then the terrain opens and you step out onto a shelf of rock so vast you cannot see its far edge, and the air hits you like a wall. It should be cooler. It is not cooler. The heat rises from below, from within the rock itself, from vents and fissures and places where the crust remembers what it is made of. The plateau exhales. It has been exhaling for a long, long time.

The rock is dark basalt, old volcanic material, weathered into broad, rounded forms. Not sharp. Not dramatic. The edges have been softened by millennia of water and heat and chemical patience. Everything here looks like it has been in place for a very long time — the boulders, the shallow depressions, the slow undulations of the surface. Even the cliff edges, where the plateau drops away, have a settled quality, as though the land made peace with its own steepness long ago and now simply maintains.

## Water

It weeps.

That is the word for it, and once you notice, you cannot un-notice. The rock faces are wet. Not drenched — not streaming — but perpetually damp, perpetually shedding thin films of moisture that catch the light and move in slow rivulets down every vertical surface. The condensation is constant. The air is so saturated with humidity that the boundary between atmosphere and water becomes ambiguous. You breathe water. You sweat and the sweat does not evaporate because the air is already full. Your skin stays slick. Your clothes stay damp. Everything stays damp.

The plateau's surface is carved by shallow channels — not rivers exactly, but braided networks of watercourses, some only inches wide, some broad enough to wade through ankle-deep, all moving, all carrying the plateau's perpetual runoff toward the edges where it collects and falls. The waterfalls at the margins are not dramatic single plumes but thousands of small threads — a curtain of water pouring over the rim, catching wind, dispersing into mist before it reaches the valleys far below. From a distance, the edge of the plateau appears to be smoking.

In the depressions, where the basalt has weathered deepest, there are pools. Some are warm. Some are hot. The hottest ones steam gently, and around their margins the rock is stained white and yellow with mineral deposits — silica, sulfur, calcium carbonate — built up in thin laminations over centuries. These pools are clear. Unnervingly clear. You can see the bottom, see the vents where warmer water pulses upward, see the subtle convection currents. The water tastes of stone. It is potable, though you would not know this without testing it; it looks too strange, too mineral, too deliberate.

Between the pools and the channels, the ground itself is saturated. Step off the established paths and your foot sinks — not into mud exactly, but into a dense mat of living material over wet rock, a spongy composite of root and moss and decomposed vegetation that holds water the way a held breath holds air. Release it, and it seeps back slowly, finding its level.

## The Green

The vegetation does not merely survive here. It thrives with an intensity that feels almost competitive, as though the plants are in an arms race with each other for access to light and water and mineral-rich soil that barely exists as soil at all.

At the lowest level: mosses. Everywhere. Thick, emerald, almost obscenely healthy mosses that cover every rock surface that isn't actively submerged or actively steaming. They grow in cushions and mats and cascading curtains over the edges of boulders. They are so thick and so uniform that they give the plateau its base color — a deep, saturated green that extends in every direction, broken only by the dark wetness of exposed stone and the silver threads of running water.

Above the moss: ferns. Tree ferns, some of them, rising eight or ten feet on thick, fibrous trunks covered in scales and the brown papery remnants of old fronds. Their canopies unfurl in enormous spirals, each frond three or four meters long, dripping with condensation. Between them, smaller ferns crowd every available surface — maidenhair and bird's-nest and species with fronds so finely divided they look like green lace.

Above the ferns: trees. Not the towering canopy giants you might expect in lowland jungle. These are plateau trees — shorter, broader, wind-adapted even though there is little wind. Their trunks are thick relative to their height, and their bark is covered in layers of epiphytic growth so dense that the original bark is invisible. Orchids, bromeliads, lichens, more mosses, small ferns — each tree is a vertical garden, a self-contained ecosystem. The branches are heavy with accumulated growth and drip constantly.

Between the trees: lianas and climbing plants that create a tangled mid-level network, not quite a canopy, more of a lattice. Some of them flower — bursts of pale color against the green, mostly whites and yellows, a few deep reds. The flowers attract pollinators. The air is full of small movements.

The overall density is notable. Not impenetrable — there are clearings, especially near the hotter vents where the ground temperature makes root growth difficult, and along the rocky ridgelines where wind exposure thins the cover. But between these open spaces, the vegetation presses close. The paths through it are narrow. Visibility is limited. You move through the plateau more than you survey it.

## Inhabitants

The animals are present but restrained. You see them more than you hear them.

Birds dominate. Dozens of species — small, quick, mostly green or brown, adapted to the dense cover. They move through the understory in loose flocks, feeding on insects and small fruits. Their calls are sporadic, brief, almost conversational rather than territorial. A few larger species perch in the mid-canopy: heavy-billed, slow-moving birds that crack the hard-shelled fruits of the plateau trees. They watch you with sideways attention.

There are lizards everywhere. Small ones, fast ones, in every shade of brown and green, living in the moss mats and the bark crevices and the warm spaces between rocks near the geothermal vents. They are not afraid of you. They are simply elsewhere, always, perpetually moving to some destination you cannot perceive.

In the pools and channels: small fish, translucent, almost invisible against the mineral-stained stone. Crustaceans too — tiny, pale, some of them blind, adapted to the dimmer pools where the overhanging vegetation blocks the light.

Mammals are present but elusive. You find their signs — tracks in the damp soil, droppings on rocks, the occasional brief rustle in the undergrowth. Rodents, probably. Something larger, once, at the edge of your vision — a low-slung body moving through the fern cover, too quick to identify. It was not afraid of you either. It simply had somewhere to be.

Insects are abundant but not oppressive. The mosquito-like things are present but not in the clouds you might expect; the ecosystem here is old enough and stable enough that some kind of balance has been reached. Bees work the flowering vines. Beetles trundle through the moss. At night, if you stayed — and you would not want to stay — there would be fireflies, probably, small points of cold light drifting through the warm dark.

## The Old Industry

And then, incongruously: metal.

Not a lot. Not structures — nothing standing, nothing you could call a building or a facility. But the residue of industry is here, and it is unmistakable once you see it. Pipes. Sections of pipe, corroded nearly to dissolution, half-buried in moss and root growth, running in rough lines across the plateau surface. They were laid on the rock — you can see where they were anchored, bolted into the basalt with heavy brackets that are now just brown stains and corroded fragments. The pipes led somewhere. From the direction of the larger hot springs, toward the cliff edge. Someone was harvesting the geothermal water, or the minerals it carried, or the heat itself.

There are other remnants. A flat area, now overgrown but still detectable by its slight regularity, where machinery once stood. Concrete pads, cracked and heaved by root growth, barely distinguishable from natural stone. Fragments of wire, of glass, of shaped metal — all being slowly absorbed by the plateau's relentless biological patience. In one place, a large gear, perhaps a meter across, lies on its side in a depression, its teeth clogged with soil and sprouting ferns from every crevice. It is beautiful in the way that all things are beautiful when they have been surrendered to.

The technology was industrial in nature — you can tell by the scale of the pipes, the engineering implied by the concrete pads, the sophistication of the metalwork even in its decayed state. Someone came here with purpose and capability. They laid infrastructure. They planned and built. And then — you cannot tell why, there are no records visible, no signs — they stopped. The plateau did the rest. The water corroded the metal. The moss colonized every surface. The roots prized apart the joints. The heat and humidity accelerated every form of decay until what remains is less a ruin than a compost.

It is quiet, this absence. Not mournful. Simply present. The plateau does not care that someone was here. The plateau was here first and will be here after, and the pipes and gears are just more substrate for the moss.

## Atmosphere

The light is normal — direct, strong, unfiltered by heavy cloud cover most of the time. The sun reaches the plateau surface and heats it and the heat rises and meets the moisture rising from the saturated ground and the resulting haze gives the air a soft, slightly gauzy quality even on clear days. Not fog. Not mist. Just a thickness to the air, a sense of depth, of layers. Shadows are soft-edged. Distant features blur slightly, not from distance alone but from the sheer density of moisture suspended in the column of air between you and them.

The heat is constant but not uniform. Near the vents, the air shimmers and the ground radiates upward in waves you can feel on your shins. In the deeper clearings, where vegetation provides shade and the breeze — minimal but present — moves through, the heat is merely heavy rather than aggressive. At the plateau's margins, where the air drops away into the valleys below, there is sometimes a draft, an upwelling of cooler air from the lower elevations, and in these moments the temperature drops enough to feel like relief before the plateau's own warmth reasserts itself.

The sound is quiet. Not silent — the water makes its constant small music, dripping and trickling and sliding over rock. The birds call intermittently. The leaves move. But these are ambient sounds, background sounds, the kind of noise that the mind learns to filter into silence. There is no thunder here, no roar of wind, no crashing surf. The waterfalls at the edge are too dispersed, too fine, to make more than a distant white hiss. The overall impression is of a place that has settled into its own volume, that has found the level of sound it wants to maintain and maintains it with the same patience with which it maintains everything else.

The air smells of wet stone, of mineral water, of green growth and the faint sulfurous tang of the vents. It is not unpleasant. It is the smell of a system in working order, of chemistry and biology in their long negotiation, of a place that knows what it is.

## Living Process

The plateau is not still. It is active, but its activity is the kind that operates on multiple timescales simultaneously, so that any single moment appears calm even as dozens of processes continue beneath and within and around the apparent stillness.

Water flows. Always. The channels shift their courses by millimeters over weeks, following the slow dissolution of the basalt. The pools rise and fall with seasonal variation in rainfall — there are seasons here, though subtle, marked more by the intensity and frequency of downpours than by any change in temperature. Growth continues. The mosses advance centimeter by centimeter over bare rock. The ferns unfurl new fronds, the trees add rings, the epiphytes swell and divide. Decay matches growth — leaves fall, are colonized by fungi, are broken down, are incorporated into the mat. The balance is so close to equal that the overall appearance of the plateau changes hardly at all from year to year, even as every component within it turns over continuously.

Animals hunt and are hunted. The lizards eat insects. The birds eat lizards occasionally, and smaller insects habitually. Something larger — you never confirm what — takes the unwary or the slow. This happens in near-silence, in the understory, in the green density of the fern layer. The prey animals have adapted to this pressure with speed and vigilance. The predators have adapted with patience. The system works. It has been working for a long time.

The geothermal activity is the plateau's heartbeat — irregular, deep, persistent. Vents open and close over decades. Mineral deposits build and occasionally collapse, redirecting water flow. New hot springs emerge in places where the rock finally yields to the pressure from below. The plateau is not static. It is merely slow, and its slowness is so comprehensive that it appears, to a human observer, as stability. As permanence. As a place that has always been this way and always will be.

This impression is not entirely wrong. The plateau will change, is changing, but it will not change fast, and what it becomes will still be recognizably itself — wet, green, hot, high, weeping, alive, patient, indifferent to the rusting remnants of the brief experiment that someone once tried to conduct upon its surface.
