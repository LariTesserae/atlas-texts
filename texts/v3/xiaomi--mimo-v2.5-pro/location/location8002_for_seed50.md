# The Throat

You are inside a stone that has not finished becoming stone.

It is a pocket — a single cavity no larger than a bedroom — scooped into the raw flank of a peak that is still rising. The mountain is young. Violently young. The rock here has no name yet among geologists because it has barely cooled from its own making: basalt so fresh it still holds heat in its deeper seams, andite veined with glass where fractures ran too fast for crystal structure to follow. The walls glisten not with moisture alone but with a mineral sheen, as though the rock is still sweating out its own chemistry. Sulfur blooms like pale lichen but isn't lichen. It is mineral condensation — the rock's own exhale crystallizing on contact with air that, at this altitude, is barely there at all.

The air, though, is not the story. The water is the story.

---

The cavity is flooded.

Not by ocean, not by river, but by a convergence so violent it seems geologically *angry*. Meltwater from the perpetual cloud-ice that caps the peak drains downward through a thousand micro-fissures in the ceiling, entering the chamber not as streams but as a wall of spray — hundreds of needle-thin jets striking the pool below with the force and sound of a breaking dam. The water level rises and falls in cycles too fast to predict: knee-deep, waist-deep, knee-deep again, every few minutes, driven by pressure fluctuations in the rock above. When it rises, the chamber shrinks and the sound intensifies until it is no longer sound at all but a physical compression of the skull, a pressure that lives behind the eyes. When it falls, there is a half-second of something almost like silence — a vacuum-breath, a gasp — before the next surge crashes in.

The roar is absolute. It is the only thing in the world. You could not hear a detonation from three feet away. You could not hear your own screaming.

---

And yet — within this hammering, chaotic, deafening pocket — there is structure.

Not much. Almost nothing. But something.

On a narrow ledge of less-eroded rock, perhaps a meter above the fluctuating waterline, three stones have been stacked. Deliberately. They are not the local basalt — they are darker, denser, carried here from somewhere else by someone with hands and intent. The stack leans but has not fallen despite the perpetual tremor of the chamber. There is no inscription, no marking, no tool-scar visible. Just three stones, placed one atop another, in a room that should not have been findable, inside a mountain that barely exists yet, by a being whose technology appears to have extended no further than *carrying rocks and balancing them*.

Why? For whom? The nearest other sign of habitation is kilometers below the cloud line. This cairn is an artifact abandoned at the threshold of something — a boundary marker, maybe. A warning. A greeting. Or just a pause: someone came this far and stopped, and the stones are the fossil of that decision.

---

The ecology is almost theoretical.

A film of extremophile microorganisms coats the wetter walls — not plants, not animals, something that was either before both or independent of both. They form patterns: dark rosettes against the lighter stone, each one a few centimeters across, each one slowly migrating upslope as the water level dictates. They photosynthesize, barely, using light that filters down through the spray from a crack in the ceiling thirty meters up — light that arrives fragmented, diffracted, scattered by the water-saturated air until the chamber glows with a diffuse, sourceless luminance, as though the atmosphere itself is gently phosphorescent. It is not dark here. Despite being inside rock at extreme altitude, it is not dark. The light simply has no origin you can point to.

Once — perhaps twice — in a full cycle of observation, something larger passes through. A translucent creature, no longer than a finger, that navigates the turbulent water with a deliberateness that suggests not instinct but problem-solving. It is eyeless, boneless, barely more than a nerve-sheath with a propulsion cilia, and it enters the chamber through a fissure too small to see from inside, feeds on the wall-microbes for less than a minute, and leaves. It does not belong here. It visits. The distinction feels important.

---

The geology is the strangest thing.

The walls are *forming in real time*. Not metaphorically. The basalt substrate is overlaid with a crust of deposited mineral — calcium carbonate, silica, trace metals leached from the rock above — that builds at a rate perceptible to patience. Stand still long enough and you can feel the floor beneath your feet thickening. The newest deposits are chalk-white and soft enough to score with a fingernail. Older deposits, closer to the original rock, are harder and darker, stained amber and rust by iron that has not yet stabilized. The chamber is becoming smaller. In a century — perhaps less — the mineral accretion will seal the drainage fissures, the water will find another path, and this room will become a dry, sealed geode: a hollow crystal buried in a mountain, carrying the echo of its own deafening birth inside it forever.

But that is the future. Now, it is still open. Still roaring. Still filling and emptying and filling again with water that arrives as cloud, falls as spray, pools as flood, and drains through cracks that were themselves made by yesterday's water — the chamber eating its own floor to feed its own walls, a self-consuming, self-creating loop of erosion and deposition happening simultaneously, the geological equivalent of a fire that builds the wood it burns.

---

You are standing — if you can stand at all, in the bucking water — inside a place that is *coming into existence around you*. The rock is raw. The sound is annihilating. The light is sourceless and strange. There is almost nothing here: no soil, no plant, no creature that stays. Only water, stone, light, and the impossible slowness of mineral accretion building a cathedral wall by wall inside a roar that has not yet learned to echo because the surfaces that would carry echoes have not finished hardening.

The three stones on the ledge have not moved.

They were placed by someone who could hear, once, and chose to come here anyway.
