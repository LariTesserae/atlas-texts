# The Resonance Hull

You enter from the lip.

The ground is soft — not soil exactly, but a dense mat of root-flesh that yields underfoot like old cartilage. It's warm to the touch, always warm, and slightly tacky with a moisture that doesn't seem to come from rain. The sky above is overcast but not dark: a pearly, even light filters through what you initially mistake for cloud cover but soon realize is a membrane. You are inside something.

The perimeter of this place is maybe a quarter mile across. A basin, a bowl, a hollow in the low country — but the walls are living. They rise in smooth, ochre-green curves, furred with moss and lichen and things that might be neither, their surfaces weeping condensation in slow rivulets that collect in the grooves between structural ridges. These ridges are ribs, or what ribs would be if a ribcage were the size of a chapel and had been growing for four hundred years. The plant — and it is one plant, a single interconnected organism — has built itself into something that resembles a town the way a wasp nest resembles architecture. The forms are there. Corridors. Alcoves. Chambers. Platforms at different heights connected by spiraling ramps of petiole and tendril. But no hand shaped this. No tool touched it. It grew toward the idea of a city without ever knowing what a city was.

And yet.

The living tissue has receded. That's the first thing you notice when you step inside one of the larger chambers — the walls are thick, easily three or four feet of fibrous plant matter, but the interior is hollow. Not dead. The inner surfaces glisten faintly, and if you press your palm against them you can feel a slow pulse, a hydraulic rhythm moving through the tissue. But the chambers are *empty*. Vast and hollow and reverberating. A single room might be the size of a cathedral nave, and you are the only solid thing in it. Your footsteps echo off walls that shouldn't exist in a place this small. The scale doesn't add up. You could walk the outer perimeter in fifteen minutes, but inside, the corridors branch and fold and spiral, and the chambers open into other chambers through passages that seem to distort your sense of distance. One hallway curves gently left for what feels like a hundred yards and deposits you back where you started, except the light has changed, and the walls have shifted color from ochre to a deep arterial red.

---

The sound.

It is enormous. It is the first thing and the last thing and the thing that never lets you forget. The entire structure *hums*. The vascular network — miles of hollow tubes threading through the walls, the floor, the ceiling — carries vibrations from somewhere deep in the root system, a low bass drone that you feel in your sternum before you hear it with your ears. But it doesn't stay low. The tubes are different diameters, and they resonate at different frequencies, and the chambers act as resonance cavities, amplifying and layering and warping the sound until it becomes a roar. Not white noise — something structured, something almost melodic, like a pipe organ played by continental drift. Certain corridors carry specific tones. You learn to navigate by sound before you learn to navigate by sight: the deep G-shutter of one passage, the high harmonic whine of another. Where passages intersect, the harmonics clash, and the resulting interference patterns are physically painful, pressing against your eardrums like a hand.

And layered on top of this: the fauna.

This place is *crawling*. Not with anything you'd recognize as a single ecosystem — with a density and variety of animal life that seems to exceed the available space. Insects, yes: beetles the size of your thumb navigating the wall-ridges in orderly columns, moths with translucent wings that catch the light in ways that make your eyes water, larvae that tunnel through the softer inner tissue leaving trails of amber sap. But also vertebrates. Lizards with flattened skulls that slide through cracks in the wall. Small mammals — furred, warm, with too many eyes — that nest in the upper vaults and descend at night on membranes stretched between their limbs. Birds, or something like birds, that fly through the interior spaces in tight spiraling flocks, their calls cutting through the drone like glass. Everything here is loud. The beetles click. The mammals chatter in ultrasonic bursts that you feel more than hear. The bird-things scream. The larvae chew with a sound like tearing fabric, amplified by the wall-chambers into something enormous. It is a wall of sound that never drops below a dull roar and frequently crests into something that blanks your thoughts and leaves you standing in a hollow nave, hands over your ears, waiting for a lull that doesn't come.

---

The strangeness.

It's in the geometry first. The chambers don't obey perspective. A room that appears rectangular from the entrance reveals itself as pentagonal when you reach the center, and if you look back, the entrance is no longer where you left it. Not gone — moved, by ten or twenty feet, as if the wall slowly rotated while you weren't watching. Corridors have a tendency to slope in directions that don't correspond to the exterior topology. You walk uphill for ten minutes and arrive at a point lower than where you started. The light — that diffuse pearlescent glow from the ceiling-membrane — doesn't cast consistent shadows. Your shadow falls in one direction; the shadow of a wall-ridge falls in another; the shadow of a moth crossing the vault falls in a third.

Then it's in the animals. Not wrong, exactly. Not deformed. But *slightly off*, in ways that accumulate. A lizard that passes behind a wall-ridge and emerges a fraction of a second too late, as if it took a longer path than the geometry allows. A flock of bird-things that, for a single frame of perception, is one bird too many — an extra body in the formation that vanishes when you count again. The mammals with too many eyes: sometimes four, sometimes six, and you can never catch the moment of change. Nothing is impossible. Nothing is overtly broken. But the place has a quality of being *loosely fitted* to reality, as if the rules here are suggestions rather than laws, and the longer you stay the more you notice the looseness, and the more you notice the more it seems to widen.

The plant itself participates in this. Its growth is too fast for what it is — ancient, established, weathered to a kind of biological permanence, and yet actively growing, visibly growing, new tendrils creeping along the corridors like slow-motion lightning, thickening over hours into structural supports that weren't there this morning. Some chambers are smaller than they were yesterday. Some passages have closed entirely, sealed by fresh growth that is warm and slick and pulses against your hand when you press against it, searching for another way through. The plant is not hostile. It may not even be aware. But it is *building*, constantly, and what it builds changes the internal geometry, and the changed geometry changes the sound, and the changed sound changes the fauna, and the fauna moves through spaces that didn't exist an hour ago, and the whole system breathes and shifts and grows and hums with the slow patient violence of something that has been doing this for centuries and will continue for centuries more.

---

The dampness is constant but never pooling. The plant drinks everything. Moisture beads on every surface and vanishes into the tissue within minutes, leaving behind a faint sweetness in the air — not quite floral, not quite fungal, something between honey and wet bark. The warmth comes from the organism itself, metabolic heat radiating through the walls, and in the smaller chambers it builds to a close, almost feverish warmth that beads sweat on your skin immediately. In the larger vaults the heat dissipates into echoing space, and you can feel the boundary between warm and cool like a curtain you walk through.

There are places — you've found three so far — where the inner wall has thinned to translucence. You can press your face against the tissue and see light through it, the warm amber light of late afternoon, and beyond it the outer world: the low green country, the flat horizon, the sky. You can see the scale of the place from outside. It is not large. A hill, a mound, a swelling in the landscape, covered in the same furred ochre-green growth. You could walk around it in the time it takes to cook a meal.

But from inside, you have been walking for days, and you are not yet at the center.
