# The Roil

The ground is the color of old teeth.

Not white — that would suggest cleanliness, intention. This is the yellowed grey of calcium left in sun and brine for a century, the color of something that was once alive in a very loose sense and has since mineralized into a texture that looks like it might crumble but, when you step on it, holds firm. The surface crusting gives way underfoot to maybe an inch of wetness, then harder substrate. You are standing on a low coastal plain — not quite a mudflat, not quite a salt pan — that extends in every direction with a flatness so thorough it begins to feel like a lie. You know the earth is curved, but your eyes refuse it.

There are no hills. There are no trees. There are no structures that could be called buildings.

But there are *presences*.

---



They begin about two hundred meters out from wherever you happen to be standing, which is always the center of the Roil because the Roil has no edges you can reach. The presences are pipes. Industrial pipes — corroded iron, some as thick as a man's torso, some no wider than a wrist — emerging from the ground at shallow angles, running for thirty or forty meters in dead-straight lines, then dipping back beneath the surface. They are spaced irregularly, maybe a dozen visible at any time, crusted in the same calcium-grey as the ground, webbed with rust that has itself oxidized into something stable and dark, a burnt-orange almost-black. Some of them are capped. Some of them are not. From the uncapped ones, there is a sound.

This is the sound of the Roil.



It is not one sound. It is a *process* of sound. From the nearest open pipe — there is always a nearest open pipe, no matter where you stand — comes a low, guttural exhale, as though the ground is breathing through a diseased throat. It is not wind, exactly. It is something below the wind, something that uses the wind but is not made of it. The pitch shifts in long, slow cycles, never reaching a resolution, never finding a chord. Farther out, other pipes contribute their own voices — some higher, a whistling almost, some lower, a rumble you feel in your ankles before you hear it with your ears. Together they produce a droning, industrial music that is not music, that never changes, that has been playing in this exact configuration for longer than anyone has been around to hear it.

The sound is loud. Not deafening — it doesn't force you to shout — but it fills the space completely. It is the kind of loud that becomes silence after a while, the way a smell disappears, the way pain dulls. Your ears adjust. The pipes continue.

---

The moisture is not rain. It has not rained here in what the landscape's own stillness suggests is a very long time. But the ground is damp, and the air carries a humidity that clings — warm, not hot, the 1.7 of temperature that means you are comfortable in a shirt with the sleeves rolled up but aware, always aware, of a faint film on your skin. The water comes from below. The pipes, maybe, or the same source the pipes tap into — some subterranean reservoir, some ancient aquifer or sea-intrusion or industrial runoff pool that has become, over decades, a feature of the geology itself. Occasionally you will find a low place where water has gathered: not a pool, exactly, but a slick, a sheen, a quarter-inch of brackish liquid over compacted grey substrate. It smells faintly of sulfur and machine oil. It does not evaporate. It does not grow. It simply persists.



There are no plants. Whatever grew here — if anything ever did — was killed or displaced so long ago that the question feels academic. The substrate is too saline, too mineral-heavy, too *wrong* for anything with roots. You might find, in the wettest patches, a darkening that could be mistaken for moss, but on closer inspection it is a chemical stain, a bacterial film, a living thing so minimal it does not rise to the level of vegetation. It is the color of old blood. It does not photosynthesize. It feeds on iron and patience.

---

The fauna is almost nothing. Once, if you stood still long enough — and you would have to stand very still, because the pipes punish movement with a particular quality of silence, a dropout in the drone that makes you feel suddenly, nakedly visible — you might see a crab. Not a recognizable crab. Something with too few legs and a carapace the same grey as the ground, so that it was almost invisible, moving in fits and starts between the wet patches, feeding on the bacterial films or on whatever smaller things lived beneath them. It would be the size of your palm. It would move as though the air were thick, as though every step were a decision made against enormous reluctance. You would not see a second one. You would wonder if you had seen the first.

That was once. Now — with the dynamism at 0.1, with this place in its frozen equilibrium — even that is uncertain. The crab may still be there. The crab may have been there for the same amount of time as the pipes, which is to say: geological time, deep time, time measured not in years but in the slow oxidation of iron into its final red-black state. The crab, if it exists, has stopped changing too. It is a feature, not an organism. It is part of the Roil's permanent inventory.

---

The pipe network, once, was industrial. Someone built this — dug into this low, warm, damp plain and laid infrastructure for extraction, processing, transport. What they were extracting is not clear. The pipes are too corroded to read any markings, too old to carry anything identifiable. But the *scale* of it was regional. If you could fly — if the Roil permitted flight, which it doesn't, not because of any rule but because the sky above it has a heaviness to it, a reluctance, as though the air itself has settled into the same equilibrium as the ground — you would see the pipes extending in all directions, a network laid down over kilometers, crossing and recrossing, vanishing into the substrate and emerging again. It was once a grid. It was once a system. It is now a geography.

The technology that built it was industrial — mechanical, electrified, scaled. But nothing remains of the power sources, the control rooms, the human infrastructure. Only the pipes. Only the sound. Only the faint chemical signatures in the wet places that suggest something was once pumped through these arteries, something that has since become the landscape itself.

---

You notice, after a while — and "a while" in the Roil is a strange concept, because nothing changes, so time has no markers, so "a while" might be twenty minutes or four hours — that the pipes are not actually emerging from the ground at random. They are emerging from *each other*. The larger ones branch into smaller ones. The smaller ones merge. What looks like a pipe entering the ground is actually a pipe entering a *larger* pipe that is itself submerged. The whole network is three-dimensional, and the surface — the grey, tooth-colored, damp, flat surface — is merely the top of a structure that goes down into the substrate to some depth no one has ever measured because no one has ever dug here, because digging in the Roil feels like a violation, like breaking a rule so fundamental it doesn't need to be stated.

The sound, you realize, is the whole network. Every pipe is singing. The surface pipes are only the visible fraction of a vast instrument, and the drone you hear is the sound of air moving through an industrial cathedral that has been repurposed, by time and geology and the absolute refusal of this place to change, into something that is no longer industrial, no longer anything with a name. It is the Roil. It is what the Roil sounds like when it breathes.

---

The light is ordinary. Daylight. It comes through cloud cover that is thin and persistent and the color of the ground, so that on certain days — all days, every day, because the days do not vary — the sky and the ground are the same grey, and the horizon disappears, and you are standing in a bowl of sameness, surrounded by pipes that fade into haze at the edges of your vision, and the only thing that separates up from down is the sound, which is always below you, always rising, always the same.

You could leave. There is nothing preventing you. But the Roil extends in every direction for a distance that feels, the longer you stay, less like a physical measurement and more like a state of being. You walk for an hour and the landscape is identical. You walk for a day and the landscape is identical. The pipes continue. The sound continues. The grey continues. You begin to suspect that leaving is not a matter of distance but of willingness, and that the Roil has already calculated your willingness and found it, like everything else here, in a state of permanent, unchanging equilibrium.
