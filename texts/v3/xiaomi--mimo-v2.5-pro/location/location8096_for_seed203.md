# The Undergarden of Whelm

---

You are standing on something that yields, very slightly, beneath your weight — not soil, not stone, but a dense mat of interlocking root-flesh, pale as tendon, faintly warm in the places where slow chemical reactions still churn beneath the surface. It stretches in every direction. You cannot see where it ends because it does not end. This is the floor of the world, and it is alive, and it is all there is beneath your feet for what might be a thousand miles.

You are inside the planet. Not in a cave — caves imply confinement, imply walls close enough to touch. This is a continental void, a ruptured chamber of geological scale, opened recently enough that the edges of it still remember being pressed together. The walls, where they exist at the far periphery, are raw: fractured planes of pale mineral, striated and sharp-edged, still shedding microscopic shards that accumulate in drifts where the root-mat hasn't yet climbed to catch them. In places the walls have already been claimed — great tongues of woody tissue pressing into exposed rock, splitting it further with the slow hydraulic force of growing cellulose. In other places, hundreds of kilometers away, the walls are still bare, still glittering with moisture, still geologically fresh. The boundary between stone and organism is not a border. It is a front. And the living side is winning, but slowly, slowly — this is a cold war fought at cold temperatures, and nothing here moves with any urgency you can perceive with human senses.

---

**THE LIGHT**

The brightness is the first thing that disorients newcomers, assuming newcomers exist. There is no sun. The void has no sky. But the light — it comes from everywhere, and it is *bright*, a flat, sourceless, directionless illumination that renders every surface in crisp detail and casts no shadows, because every surface is also a source. The root-mat glows. Not dramatically, not with the garish bioluminescence of deep-sea creatures, but with a steady, cold, silvery-white phosphorescence produced by the metabolic processes of trillions of intertwined fungal filaments embedded in the substrate. The woody pillars glow the same way — their bark, their exposed heartwood, their living cambium — all faintly radiant, the light compounding across continental distances until the void is as bright as an overcast afternoon on the surface of any normal world. Where a pillar has died — and some have died, their cores hollowed out by cold-rot — the dead wood goes dark, creating patches of blackness in the pale cathedral, and those dark places feel like holes in vision itself, like something has subtracted a piece of the seeing.

The color temperature is unsettling if you know what color temperature is. It is very close to white. Not the white of daylight, which carries warmth, but the white of a laboratory, the white of snow under overcast, the white of bone. It makes everything look recently washed. It makes the damp places gleam.

---

**THE PILLARS**

They rise from the root-mat at irregular intervals — not like a forest, which implies some ecological logic, some competition for canopy light. There is no canopy here. They rise because they *are*, because the root-mat produces them the way a body produces bone, extruding woody columns through its own surface in a process that takes centuries and shows no sign of purpose beyond its own continuation. They are not trees. They have no branches, no leaves, no crown. They are cylindrical, sometimes slightly tapered, sometimes slightly curved, their surfaces rough with the texture of interrupted growth — years where the cold deepened and the metabolism slowed, years where some internal surplus accelerated the climb. Some are kilometers tall, rising until they vanish into the bright haze of the void's upper reaches, where the air (if it is air; it is very thin, very dry, very cold) thins further. Some are stumps, broken off by some past collapse, their flat tops dark with dead tissue and rimmed with a living edge that is slowly, imperceptibly, trying to regrow what was lost.

The spacing is vast. You might walk for hours across the root-mat between two pillars, the ground unchanging, the light unchanging, the silence immense. This is the emptiness of the place — not a lack of life, because you are standing on life, you are surrounded by life, but a lack of *encounter*. The biomass is total and the population is zero. The vegetation does not cluster. It does not form thickets or groves or tangles. It spreads, monolithic, patient, a single organism occupying an entire continental void, and it has no interest in the shapes that surface-dwellers associate with plant life. No leaf. No flower. No fruit. Just root. Just wood. Just glow.

---

**THE DAMP AND THE COLD**

The temperature hovers just above freezing, and the moisture is intermittent — not rain exactly, but a constant process of condensation and reabsorption. Warm air (warm relative to the void's baseline; nothing here is truly warm) rises from the root-mat's metabolic processes, meets the colder air of the open void, and sheds its moisture as a fine, clinging film on every surface. The pillars weep. The root-mat glistens. In the hollows between root-ridges, shallow pools accumulate — never more than a few centimeters deep, never more than a few meters across — and they sit perfectly still, their surfaces mirror-bright, reflecting the sourceless light back at the void's ceiling. Over time, the water is reabsorbed. Over time, new condensation forms. The cycle is slow, unhurried, and mostly silent. Occasionally — rarely, randomly — a pillar will shudder, some internal adjustment of growth pressure, and a cascade of moisture will sheet down its surface, catching the light in a brief, silent cascade that is the closest thing this place has to weather.

The cold is not aggressive. It does not bite. It is simply *present*, a baseline condition, the temperature at which this biome has chosen to operate. Everything here is slow because cold is slow. The root-mat grows at rates measurable in millimeters per decade. The pillars thicken by fractions of a millimeter per century. The condensation cycle takes days. If you stood still long enough — months, years — you would feel the root-mat beginning to climb your ankles, mistaking you for mineral substrate, beginning the long, patient process of incorporation.

---

**THE TURBULENCE**

And yet — for all this patience — the place is *restless*. This is the paradox of Whelm, the quality that makes it more than a frozen terrarium. The void is young. The tectonic event that opened it is recent on geological timescales, and the space is still settling. Micro-adjustments ripple through the root-mat in slow waves, visible as a gentle undulation of the ground surface, a rolling of the pale flesh that makes standing still mildly nauseating. The pillars flex — imperceptibly to the eye, but the root-mat feels them, and transmits their tremors outward. Occasionally, a section of wall, weakened by root-intrusion and frost-shattering, will calve. The collapse is continental in scale and glacial in speed — a slab of mineral the size of a mountain range detaching over the course of months, crumbling into the void in a slow-motion avalanche that the root-mat surges to meet, climbing the rubble before it has fully settled, incorporating the fresh mineral into its substrate with a speed that seems almost eager, almost hungry.

This is the turbulence: not violence but *activity*, not chaos but a kind of ongoing, continent-scale biological restlessness. The root-mat is colonizing. It is always colonizing. Every bare surface is a frontier. Every dead pillar is an opportunity. The void is a wound and the vegetation is the scab, and the scab is growing, and it will not stop until the wound is closed. There is something almost frantic about it, if you watch long enough — a deep, slow-motion urgency beneath the glacial pace, the sense that this place was not meant to exist, that the living substrate knows this, and that it is working as fast as it can to make the void *not a void*.

---

**THE REMNANTS**

In the lee of certain pillars — the older ones, the larger ones — you find structures. They are not built *on* the root-mat so much as *into* it: shallow depressions scraped out of the living surface, lined with strips of peeled bark-fiber, reinforced with wooden stakes driven into the substrate (the root-mat has already begun to absorb some of these stakes, incorporating them, sprouting fine filaments from their points of contact). There are marks on the pillars, too — repeated gouges at regular intervals, too patterned to be natural, too crude to be writing. Tally marks, perhaps. Or warnings. Or prayers.

Whoever made these is gone. The structures are old but not ancient — the root-mat has only partially reclaimed them, and the stake-marks on the pillars are still legible if you look closely. Primitive tools were used: sharp-edged stone, fire-hardened wood. No metal. No sophistication. Someone lived here, briefly, and left only these faint impressions — a handful of hollows in a continental skin of living tissue, as significant as the scratches a small animal might leave. The scatteredness of them suggests a wanderer, or a very small group, moving from pillar to pillar, never staying long enough for the root-mat to climb higher than their knees.

There are no bones. The root-mat would have taken them. There is no refuse. The root-mat would have taken that too. There is only the shape of absence — the carefully hollowed sleeping-niche, the deliberate pattern of stake-marks, the faintest ghost of intention pressed into a world that absorbs all things and forgets nothing because it was never designed to remember.

---

**THE CREATURES**

They are rare, and you might go days without seeing one. They live inside the pillars — in the hollow cores of dead columns, in the narrow air-spaces between concentric growth rings, in cavities gnawed out of living wood by some enzymatic process. They are pale, slow, eyeless, roughly the size of a human hand, and they move through the wood the way fish move through water, their bodies flattened and flexible, their limbs reduced to nubs useful only for gripping the interior walls of their galleries. They eat the dead inner layers of the pillars — the dark, decomposed heartwood — and they are eaten, in turn, by nothing you have seen. Something takes them. You find the evidence sometimes: a hollow with its walls scored by something larger than the creatures that live there, a trail of dark fluid leading deeper into the pillar's core. Whatever hunts them lives deeper in the wood than you can reach, and it does not come out into the bright void, and this is, perhaps, the most comforting thing about it.

---

**WHAT IT FEELS LIKE**

Standing here, in the bright, cold, empty cathedral of a place that is both underground and too vast to feel enclosed, the word that surfaces is not *alien* — the parameters are too familiar for that, too close to biology-as-you-know-it — but *preempted*. This is a world that arrived before you did. It has already solved every problem you might pose to it. It does not need you. It does not need fauna, does not need builders, does not need the small scattered marks of intelligent hands. It is a closed system, almost — damp where it needs to be damp, bright where it needs to be bright, turbulent where stillness would mean stagnation. You are standing on the answer to a question no one asked, and the answer is: *root, wood, glow, cold, growth, silence, vastness, and the slow, patient, terrible urgency of a living thing filling a space it was not originally meant to fill.*

The quiet is the loudest thing here. Not silence — there is always the faintest creak of flexing wood, the softest patter of condensation settling — but the absence of sound *designed for you*. No birdsong. No wind. No water. Just the sound of a world being built, one millimeter at a time, in a place where no one is watching, and where the building will not stop.
