# The Courtyard at Nak

The first thing you notice is the walls.

They rise roughly four meters on three sides, old brick going soft at the edges where water has been doing its patient work — not centuries of work, but maybe two monsoon seasons. The mortar is already flaking into pale crumbs that the ferns have learned to root in. The fourth side of this enclosure is open, or nearly so: a collapsed arch now choked with rattan palms and the reaching arms of a strangler fig that hasn't yet found what it's strangling. Beyond the arch, the jungle — real jungle, primary and indifferent — continues uphill and away. No one comes here.

The courtyard is roughly fifteen meters across. Perhaps it was a temple complex once, or an administrative building for some minor outpost. The bricks are not fine-work. No carvings survive because there were likely none to begin with. What remains is simply the geometry of rooms: two partial walls meeting at a corner, a doorframe still standing though the door is gone, a stretch of floor that was once plastered and is now cracked into a mosaic of small rectangles where water pools.

The water is everywhere and nowhere. No stream runs through the space. Instead there are four or five shallow depressions — no deeper than a handspan — where rainwater gathers and simply *stays*. The warm air can't evaporate it fast enough. A faint skin of algae has begun on the surface of the largest pool, barely visible, and beneath it, if you kneel, you can see tiny fish — no longer than a thumbnail, translucent — feeding on something you can't identify. They were not introduced. They arrived in egg-form on the feet of herons, perhaps, or in the gut of some fruit-eating bird whose droppings washed into the pool during a rain. They are *new arrivals* in the same way this entire place is new: the courtyard, the pools, the ecosystem assembling itself inside these walls.

The ground is low clay, still holding the smell of the geological event that shaped it — not volcanic, not seismic, but something lateral: a landslide nearby that redirected water, that undercut a hillside and exposed this older construction which had been buried for what might be only thirty years but feels like it has always been here. The clay is the color of dark honey. Where the pools sit, it is darker, almost black, and you can press a thumb into it and watch it slowly refill.

The walls catch condensation. In the early mornings, and again in the heavy hour before dusk, moisture beads on the brick and runs in thin threads down to the mortar lines, where it is intercepted by moss. The moss is deep green, almost luminous in the dimness, and it has texture — not uniform but divided into territories. Patches of different species advance and retreat. Some are soft and cushion-like. Others are papery, dry on top but wet underneath. One variety, growing on the north wall where almost no direct light ever reaches, produces a faint bluish spore that dusts your forearm if you brush against it.

The light. The canopy above is not continuous — this is not old-growth ceiling — but the rattan palms and the fig have grown together in the years since the arch collapsed, and their combined leaf-layers create a filter that turns midday into late afternoon and late afternoon into dusk. The light that reaches the courtyard floor is green-shifted and moves constantly with the breeze above, so that shadows migrate across the brick like slow-moving water. At midday there is enough illumination to read by, barely. At dawn and dusk the courtyard exists in a near-dusk that could be any hour. You lose time here.

The temperature is warm. Not oppressive — the walls create a chimney effect that draws air upward, and a gentle cross-breeze moves through the broken arch — but the humidity is high enough that the warmth stays in your clothes, in your hair, against your skin. Sweat does not evaporate quickly. The air smells of wet clay, of the faint sweetness of decomposing palm fronds, and of something else — a mineral note from the bricks themselves, something calcareous and old.

The animals.

They are everywhere, and you are surrounded by them, and you see almost none of them.

The geckos are the most visible: four or five species at least, ranging from a mottled brown creature the length of your hand to a tiny black-and-yellow thing no bigger than your smallest fingernail. They hunt along the wall at dusk, and you can hear the faintest *tick* of their feet on brick, a sound so small it is nearly a texture rather than a noise. The spiders are web-builders mostly — orb-weavers that string their work between the wall-top and the fern fronds, rebuilding each night, dismantling by dawn. They are not quiet; you can hear the faint vibration of prey in a web if you hold still long enough.

Beneath the largest pool, in the saturated clay, there are things moving. Insects, probably. Larvae of something. You can see the surface of the water shiver occasionally in a place where nothing has touched it from above.

At night — and there are nights here, the darkness is complete when the canopy blocks the stars — the frogs begin. But even the frogs are quiet. Their calls are not the explosive chorus of a healthy wetland but individual voices, spaced apart, each one a low *purr* or a wet *tok* that carries maybe three meters and stops. There might be six or seven frogs in the courtyard. They are small and patient, sitting on the moss with their throats barely pulsing.

The silence is the dominant fact of the place. It is not empty silence — the life here is *abundant*, perhaps more species per square meter than the surrounding jungle — but it is quiet life. Hushed. As if the enclosure has absorbed sound the way the clay absorbs water, holding it, letting none escape. You become aware of your own breathing. You become aware of the small sounds your body makes simply by existing: the creak of your knees when you crouch, the rustle of your sleeve against your arm. You feel loud.

The vegetation is lush but scattered. There is no lawn, no carpet of ground-cover. Instead: three fern species growing from cracks in the brick, their fronds unfolding in the tight spirals of new growth. A breadfruit sapling — volunteer, maybe two years old — pushing up through the courtyard floor near the eastern wall, its broad leaves reaching for the gap in the canopy. Moss on every surface that holds still long enough to be colonized. A single orchid, epiphytic, growing on the standing doorframe, its roots wrapped around the brick like fingers. The rattan palms, rooted outside the walls but arching in, dropping occasional fronds that decompose in place and feed the moss and the moss feeds the insects and the insects feed the geckos and the geckos feed the small, silent kingfisher that visits at dusk — a bird you will probably never see, but whose sudden, precise dive into one of the pools (a sharp *ploc*, a spray of water, gone) tells you everything.

The built environment is failing. Not dramatically — there is no ruin aesthetic here, no romantic decay — but steadily, quietly, the way all unattended things fail. The mortar softens. The bricks delaminate in thin sheets. The plaster floor cracks and allows roots in. In another decade, the walls will be shorter. In another century, they will be stumps, and the courtyard will be a depression in the jungle floor that no one can explain. But for now, the walls persist, and the life that has found them depends on their persistence — the moss needs the vertical surface, the geckos need the flat planes for hunting, the pools need the concavity of the floor, the kingfisher needs the pools.

Everything here is assembling. The ecosystem has not reached equilibrium. It is still *deciding* — which species stay, which are temporary, where the energy flows, how the space is divided. You are witnessing a negotiation in real time, and it is very quiet, and it does not care that you are watching.
