# Tang La Interior

You are inside something vast, and you are high up inside it.

---

The first thing to understand is the **stone**. It is not rock as you know it from riverbeds or beaches — those rounded, water-touched things. This is new stone. Recently exposed. The walls of the shaft you occupy are rough and angular, full of edges that have never been worn, and the grain runs in tight fault-lines suggesting this face was fractured open perhaps a century ago. Maybe less. The dust on the surfaces is thin and pale, a powder of silica and feldspar with no organic component. It does not stick to itself. When it falls — when anything dislodges — it falls a very long way.

You are inside a vertical shaft. It is not a natural cave. It was opened by engineering, and it has been built upon since — *furiously* built upon — in a manner that suggests necessity rather than planning. The shaft is perhaps forty meters across at its widest and extends far above and below your position, though the dim light never reaches the terminuses in either direction. At intervals, ironwork platforms are bolted into the walls with expansion anchors that groan softly under their own accumulated weight. Catwalks span the void. Pipes descend and ascend, some wrapped in mineral wool insulation that has gone grey and brittle. Ladders — iron, steel, occasionally wood — connect the platforms vertically. This is a **settlement**, if a desperate one: a town built into a hole.

---

The **industry** is real but compact. On the third platform below your current position, a turbine turns — not fast, but constantly — driving a generator that feeds copper wire upward through cable bundles into the settlement's upper reaches. The turbine is fueled by compressed gas drawn from somewhere deeper in the mountain, or the structure, or whatever geological feature has been tapped for this purpose. Its sound is a low and constant throb, more felt in the walls than heard. Across the shaft on the eastern wall, a workshop operates intermittently: the whine of a lathe, the hiss of a small forge being fed with mineral powder and forced air. Sparks occasionally fall past your position, extinguishing before they reach the lower levels. Someone is always working on something. The entire structure feels like a machine that is also a house that is also a factory, and the residents move between these modes of existence without distinction.

**There is no water.** This cannot be overstated. The air you breathe is dry — so dry it cracks the skin of your hands and the corners of your mouth within hours. Moisture is brought in from the outside, wherever outside is, by a team of haulers who work a route up from the lower approaches. It arrives in sealed casks and is rationed with religious intensity. Every surface in the settlement is hydrophobic by habit: condensation would be a blessing and a danger in equal measure. The pipes carry gas, not liquid. The deepest parts of the shaft are said to be arid enough to preserve a body indefinitely. No one tests this casually.

---

The **fauna** — and here is where the place begins to assert its strangeness, modestly but insistently.

The creatures are small. They have to be. The niches are tight.

**Spiders** first: pale, long-legged things that build webs across the open gaps between catwalks and pipe-runs, anchoring silk to rivets and bolt-heads with the precision of engineers. They take midges. They take dust-moths. There are dozens of species, possibly hundreds — no one has catalogued them. They are not feared. They are not loved. They are *structural*, in the way that pigeons are structural to a city bridge: present, inevitable, part of the physics of the place.

Then the **birds**. Pigeons — a lineage adapted to this interior over generations, their plumage gone dusty-grey to match the stone, their coos echoing off the shaft walls in a way that produces strange harmonics. They nest in the upper reaches where the platforms thin out and the engineering gives way to raw fractured rock. They are loud when they are loud and otherwise invisible. Hawks follow them in: kestrels, or something kestrel-adjacent, that ride the faint thermal updraft from the turbine exhaust and stoop on pigeons in the dim middle heights. The hawks are the only creatures that seem to enjoy the vertical space. They fold their wings and fall, and the sound of their killing — a brief soft concussion — is one of the few explosive noises in a place that is otherwise, against all expectation, **quiet**.

And then: **rodents**. Mice, certainly. Possibly rats in the lower depths where the structure meets whatever the shaft is sunk into. They move through the walls — the walls have been colonized by a secondary fauna of tunneling things that eat the mineral wool and nest in the cable conduits, and the mice eat them, and the spiders sometimes eat the mice, and the order of predation is not always as clear as it should be. In the quiet hours — and there are quiet hours, enforced by the settlement's rotation of labor and rest — you can hear them in the walls. Not loud. A skittering. A gnawing. The sound of small teeth on rock, which is, if you listen closely, the sound of the mountain being very, very slowly devoured.

---

The **people** (the residents, the workers, whatever you want to call them — they do not seem to have a single name for themselves) number perhaps eight hundred. They occupy the shaft in a vertical social geography that mirrors economic function: the upper platforms are for living quarters and storage, the middle for workshops and trade, the lower for heavy industry and the turbine. Between these zones, the social distinctions are enacted through access — who is permitted on which ladders, who owns which catwalk — and through an elaborate system of **marks** scratched into the iron railings. You would need months to learn the grammar of these marks. They encode ownership, obligation, warning, and a kind of informal currency that runs alongside the more tangible exchange of goods and labor.

The settlement is **turbulent**. Not violently — no one is at war, exactly — but in the way that a termite mound is turbulent: always building, always repairing, always adjusting. Platforms are reinforced. Pipes are re-routed. New sections of wall are fractured open with controlled charges (the only loud sound that approaches regularity, a dull *whump* felt in the chest, followed by a slow rain of dust). The shaft grows. The settlement *accretes*. And yet it never seems to get larger in any dimension you can measure, because the new construction is always replacing old construction that has failed, or shifted, or simply been outgrown. It is a place in constant, quiet renovation. Nothing here is permanent. The oldest structures are perhaps fifty years old. The newest are being built right now, somewhere below you, by workers whose hammer-blows you can faintly feel through the floor.

---

The **light** comes from electrified fixtures strung along the cable runs — bare bulbs, mostly, in wire cages, supplemented by a few longer tubes of fluorescent gas that buzz at the edge of hearing. The light is yellow-white and insufficient. Shadows pool in the spaces between platforms. The upper reaches of the shaft are dark enough that the pigeons roost by echolocation and habit rather than sight. The lower reaches are lit primarily by the forge-glow from the workshops, a flickering amber that makes the ironwork seem to breathe. No one has tried to light the whole shaft. The electricity is too valuable, and besides, the residents have developed a comfort with dimness that approaches preference. Children grow up knowing the texture of every handhold on their home platform by touch. Blindness is common among the elderly and is accommodated with remarkable ease.

---

The **temperature** is cool — not cold, not punishing, but a persistent coolness that suggests the shaft is sunk deep into rock that has never been warmed by direct sun. The turbine and forge produce local heat plumes that the residents exploit: living quarters cluster around the warm exhaust vents, and the social center of the settlement is a platform where three warm-air streams converge into a relatively temperate zone. Outside this zone, a light jacket is necessary. At the shaft's extremes — far above, far below — it is said to be genuinely cold, cold enough that the air itself feels heavier, and expeditions to those regions require preparation.

There is **no recognized vegetation**. No moss. No lichen. The rock is too new, too dry, too mineral. And yet — the lower platforms develop a faint **patina** over time, a discolouration that some residents insist is biological and others insist is chemical. It is the colour of rust, but it moves. Slowly. Over weeks. It follows the pipe-runs, or it follows the cable conduits, or perhaps it follows something else entirely — the pattern of vibration from the turbine, the gradient of warmth, the trail of mouse-urine. No one studies it. No one names it. It is simply there, at the edges of the known world, doing whatever it does when no one is paying attention.

---

**Outside** — and this is largely a matter of report rather than experience, because most residents go outside rarely and reluctantly — the elevation is extreme. The shaft opens somewhere high, into air that is thin and dry and scoured by wind. The landscape, as described by the haulers and the maintenance crews who service the exterior pipes, is **young**: sharp ridgelines, unweathered scree, the raw and violent geology of a mountain range still in the process of rising. There is no vegetation out there either, or none that matters. There is rock, and there is sky, and there is the wind, which is the loudest thing in this entire world when you stand in its path.

But from inside the shaft, the wind is only a whisper at the intake vents. A breath. A sigh. The mountain holds you and you hold the mountain, and between you there is this settlement — this living, building, consuming, quietly screaming organism of iron and stone and small pale spiders — and it is, in its way, the most ordinary place imaginable. People live here. They work. They argue over access to the warm platforms. They raise children who will know no other world. They do not think of themselves as strange.

The strangeness, such as it is, lives in the margins. In the patina. In the spiders that build geometries no one has named. In the hawks that hunt by falling, and the silence that follows, and the dust that settles and settles and is never quite swept away.
