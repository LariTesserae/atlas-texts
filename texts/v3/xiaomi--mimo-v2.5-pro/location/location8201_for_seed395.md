# The Resin Cities of the Yellow Plain

---

The first thing you must understand is the color. The land is yellow.

Not the yellow of wheat or pollen or sunflowers. A mineral yellow, the yellow of sulfur and old bone and the inside of a fingernail. It extends in every direction across the low plain — truly low, barely above the gravitational datum of this world, a vast depression that may once have been a sea before there was enough atmosphere to hold one. The surface is dust anditeite — a fine silicate powder, calcium-rich, still warm from whatever process raised it from the planetary interior. It is very new. You can feel how new it is. It has not yet decided whether to be soil or stone. It sits in that in-between state, a powder that is also somehow a crust, firm enough to bear weight but soft enough that every step leaves a print that will last ten thousand years.

There is no erosion because there is nothing to erode with. No water cuts this surface. No rivers. No rain. The sky is a pale yellow-white during the day, a vast dome of diffused warmth with a dim sun burning somewhere behind the haze, and it delivers light — ample, ordinary light — but no moisture. The humidity hovers at something like four percent. Your lips would crack in an hour. Your eyes would ache.

And yet.

---

The vegetation.

You would not call it lush at first. The word brings to mind ferns and dripping canopy and the press of broad leaves against your face. This is something else entirely. But it is dense — densely packed, deeply established, enormously varied, and covering every available surface in continuous unbroken groundcover that extends from here to wherever the plain finally ends, which is not a place anyone has seen, because the plain does not seem to end.

The plants are low-growing. Almost nothing reaches higher than a meter. They hug the yellow dust in rosettes and mats and sprawling networks of thick, fleshy stems, each one swollen with stored moisture like a cactus but not shaped like a cactus — shaped instead like a flattened pillow, or a pressed hand, or a wide green tongue licking across the powdery ground. Their leaves are translucent. Hold one up to the light and you see the veins inside, the slow pulse of fluid moving through internal channels sealed against the dry air. They are jade-green, gray-green, a dark deep green that borders on black, and they cover the yellow dust so completely that only in certain places — the newest growth zones, the edges of disturbance — do you see the bare mineral beneath.

They do not grow from water in the soil. There is no water in the soil. Instead they drink the air. Each plant has structures — microscopic, but present in such abundance that the leaf surfaces glitter faintly in the warm light — that condense atmospheric moisture at night, when the temperature drops just enough for the dew point to briefly, barely touch. The moisture is scarce. A single plant might harvest a thimbleful per night. But a single plant is never alone. There are billions of them. Trillions. The entire plain is one continuous organism of thirst, a green and jade and black sponge pressing its leaves against the cooling air each evening, harvesting the faintest whisper of moisture, storing it, hoarding it, spending it with excruciating economy on growth — slow growth, growth measured in millimeters per year, but growth that never stops and has been accumulating for longer than anyone knows.

The smell is faint. Sweet. A little acrid. The resin the plants produce — and they all produce it, a thick amber substance that seals their wounds and coats their stems and hardens on their surfaces in glossy patches — has a scent like pine sap crossed with something more mineral, more chemical, more ancient. On still days you can almost taste it at the back of your throat.

---

The fauna.

You hear them before you see them, though even that is generous. There is so little sound in this place. The air is thick and warm and carries almost nothing. No wind. The elevation is too low, the plain too vast and uniform, for significant air movement. Sound dies within meters, absorbed by the dense vegetation mat, muffled by the warm still air. What you hear, if you are patient and quiet, is a faint crackling — the sound of resin expanding in the heat, of plant tissues shifting as stored fluids redistribute, of the occasional soft pop as a sealed blister of hardened sap splits open. And beneath that: movement.

The creatures are scaled. Not feathered, not furred. Scaled and smooth-skinned, built low to the ground like their plant counterparts, moving through the vegetation on short powerful limbs with an eerie, near-silent efficiency. The largest — there are several species of large grazers — are perhaps the size of a big dog, broad-bodied and flat, their bellies brushing the plant mats as they move, their mouths specialized for scraping the resinous leaves without damaging the plants' moisture-storing tissues. They move in herds, vast herds, thousands strong, and the herds flow across the plain like dark rivers through the green, silent except for the occasional soft grunt or the scrape of scales against hardened resin. They are careful. Everything here is careful. Nothing wastes energy. Nothing makes unnecessary noise.

Predators exist. They are smaller, quicker, darker in coloration — deep green-black to match the densest vegetation — and they move through the same plant mats with a different rhythm, a different patience. They hunt by ambush, by stillness, by waiting with such profound stillness that they become indistinguishable from the rosettes around them until the moment of striking, which is itself almost silent: a brief scuffle, a faint crack of bone, then stillness again. The prey animals have evolved accordingly. They are watchful. Their eyes are large, lateral, capable of detecting the slightest movement against the static green. They browse in rotating patterns, always some members watching while others feed, and their herds move in complex spatial arrangements that no one designed — no one here designs anything — but that have the appearance of intelligent organization.

Below the surface: burrowers. Worms of extraordinary size and patience, their tunnels running through the yellow dust for kilometers, aerating the mineral substrate, turning the proto-soil, creating the conditions for deeper root growth. They are part of the architecture of this place, though they build nothing. They loosen. They open. They make room.

---

The cities.

Here is where the density reaches its most astonishing expression.

Across the yellow-and-green plain, at intervals that seem irregular but which, from sufficient height, reveal a deep statistical regularity — a spacing pattern, an emergent mathematics of resource distribution — the vegetation rises. Not into trees. There are no trees. It rises into structures.

Imagine a coral reef. Now imagine it on land. Now imagine it built not by polyps but by scaled creatures the size of your hand, working with no tools, no technology, no conscious architectural plan — only instinct, accumulated behavior over uncounted generations, and resin.

The structures are made entirely of resin. Plant resin, harvested from the surrounding vegetation mats, chewed and mixed with mineral dust from the yellow substrate, shaped by mandibles and forelimbs into pellets and sheets and arches and walls, layered and layered and layered until the accumulated mass rises above the vegetation line — five meters, ten, fifty — into towers and terraces and enclosed chambers and open galleries and passages so narrow that only the smallest creatures can navigate them and passages so broad that the great grazing herds can move through in file.

From a distance the cities look like mountain ranges. They are the same color as the plain — amber, golden, a darker yellow where the resin has aged and oxidized — and they catch the warm light beautifully, glowing with an internal luminosity that is not bioluminescence but simply the effect of translucent resin stacked in layers, each layer letting a little more light through until the interior chambers are suffused with a warm amber glow, like being inside a lantern.

They are vast. Each city extends for kilometers — not in area but in the sense of continuous construction, the built structure seamlessly blending with the surrounding vegetation mat until it is impossible to say where the city ends and the plain begins. The largest are continental in scale. They have been growing for millennia. Each generation of resin-shaping creatures adds to the structure, extends it, repairs it, and in doing so transforms it — but slowly, so slowly that the shape of the city changes on timescales of centuries, not years. What appears to be a stable architecture is actually a slow-motion river of construction and decay, always in motion, always active, but moving at a pace that human senses cannot easily detect.

Inside the cities: chambers. Thousands of them, millions, each one a space shaped for a particular purpose that the creatures understand through instinct rather than language. Brooding chambers, where eggs are kept at precise temperatures maintained by the insulating properties of layered resin. Storage chambers, where harvested vegetation is dried and sealed. Communication chambers, where the chemical signals that serve these creatures in place of language are concentrated and diffused through specialized resin membranes. The chambers are not rooms in any human sense. They are pockets in a continuous matrix, bubbles in amber, each one connected to the others by passages that narrow and widen according to patterns that have been refined over generations of selective construction — the creatures preferentially building in certain directions, at certain angles, creating a geometry that is not designed but is also not random.

The silence inside the cities is profound. The resin absorbs sound. The passages muffle it. The creatures themselves move in near-total quiet, their padded limbs and scaled bodies producing only the faintest whisper of contact with the resin surfaces. You can stand in the center of a city containing a hundred million organisms and hear nothing but your own breathing and the occasional distant crackle of thermal expansion as the resin walls shift in the warmth.

---

The dynamism.

The place is not still. It is furiously, ceaselessly active — but quietly. Like watching a time-lapse of a glacier, except at the pace of ordinary perception, you have to watch for a long time before you see it.

The vegetation grows. Not visibly, but continuously. Millimeter by millimeter, the rosettes expand, the mats thicken, the groundcover advances into the bare yellow patches that mark the newest geological exposures. A patch of bare dust that appears one season will be colonized by the next — a few green shoots, then a rosette, then a mat, then, within a few years, an indistinguishable part of the continuous green.

The herds move. Not in the dramatic migrations of temperate grasslands, but in slow, continuous circuits, the animals following the maturation cycles of the vegetation, never staying in one place long enough to deplete the resin stores, moving on, the plants recovering behind them, the whole system turning like a vast wheel.

The cities grow. New construction at the edges. Slow demolition at the centers, where the oldest resin has become brittle and is broken down by specialized organisms — tiny, many-legged things that consume the hardened amber and excrete a mineral powder that falls to the base of the city and is eventually incorporated into new construction. The cities are simultaneously being built and consumed, and the balance between these processes shifts with the seasons, the temperature, the atmospheric moisture, the health of the surrounding vegetation, the population dynamics of the grazers and predators and burrowers and resin-shapers — a web of feedback loops operating silently, invisibly, continuously.

And the plain itself is changing. New mineral surfaces are being pushed up from below by whatever tectonic process operates in this low, warm place — a slow convulsion, a rising of fresh yellow dust that cracks the old vegetation mats and exposes bare substrate to the air. These disturbance zones are the most dynamic places on the plain: colonizers arrive almost immediately, the first small plants taking root in the warm dust within days, the grazers following, the predators following the grazers, the resin-shapers beginning their construction, and within a human lifetime a new stretch of the continuous green-and-amber mosaic has come into being, replacing what was lost elsewhere to the slow geological churn.

---

The light.

The sun is dimmer than what you know, but the sky is wide and the atmosphere is thick and warm and the light that reaches the surface is diffuse and even, without harsh shadows, without the sharp contrasts of clearer worlds. It is a gentle light. It makes everything look as if it is being viewed through a lens smeared with a thin film of amber. The colors are muted but rich — the greens are deep, the yellows are warm, the amber of the cities glows softly, and at dawn and dusk the entire plain seems to be made of a single substance, a single color, a golden-green that stretches in every direction without interruption, without feature, without horizon, until it blends into the yellow-white sky and you cannot tell where the land ends and the air begins.

At night, the temperature drops. Not far — the atmosphere holds the heat well — but enough. A few degrees. And in that cooling, the air surrenders its moisture. You cannot see it happen. There is no fog, no visible dew. But the plants see it. Their leaves turn upward. The microscopic condensation structures on their surfaces activate. And the drinking begins — silent, invisible, the great daily harvest of the thimblefuls that sustain everything.

The night is darker than the day but never truly dark. The resin surfaces of the cities retain a faint warm glow for hours after sunset, the accumulated light of the day slowly releasing from the translucent amber like a stone releasing the heat of the sun. It is enough to see by, barely, and the creatures are adapted to it, their large eyes catching every photon, their movements in the half-dark as sure and silent as in the full diffused daylight.

---

You could walk across this plain for years and never reach its edge. You would never be thirsty, exactly — the air is dry but the plants are not, and the resin-shaping creatures, while they would not offer you moisture, would not prevent you from harvesting it as they do, pressing your lips to the translucent leaves and drawing out the faint, sweet, resinous fluid that is the closest thing to water this world produces. You would never be cold. You would rarely be in direct sun. You would walk on a surface of green and amber and yellow, through air that smells of pine and mineral and warm dust, in a silence so complete that your own heartbeat would become the loudest sound you know, past structures built by creatures who will never know you exist and whose lives are as intricate and full and purposeful as your own, operating on principles you will never fully understand because they were not designed but simply grew, the way this entire world grew: slowly, densely, from almost nothing, into something vast.
