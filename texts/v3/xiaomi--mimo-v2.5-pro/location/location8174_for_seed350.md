# The Drowned Plant

## What You See First

You don't see it. The light here is the color of iodine, a permanent chemical dusk that might not be sunlight at all. It comes from everywhere and nowhere, as if the atmosphere itself is faintly phosphorescent with exhaustion. The water — warm, chest-deep, moving — is not clear. It has the opacity of weak tea, the color of something that was once something else. It smells of copper and molasses and wet circuitry.

You are standing in a forest. You are standing in a factory. These two facts do not contradict each other here, because the factory has become the forest, and the forest has become the factory, and neither identity has been fully relinquished.

## The Floor



The terrain underfoot is the concrete slab of an industrial facility — you can feel it through the water, ridged and grooved where channels were cast into the floor, where conduits ran, where assembly lines moved. But the concrete is barely a year old. Not because it was poured recently — no, this facility is decades old — but because it is *young*. Something about this place makes geology happen fast. The concrete is eroding the way limestone erodes over millennia: pitted, fluted, dissolving into calcium-rich silt that the warm water carries in suspension. The edges of broken pipe-ends are not rusted, they are *sharp*, as if they were cut yesterday, but already the cuts are deepening, the metal thinning, the bores widening with a speed you can almost watch.

The water is everywhere. The elevation — the "land" — rises in places: concrete islands, the humped spines of buried conveyor systems, the tilted shoulders of heavy machinery that broke free of their mounts and settled askew. But even these are sinking, eroding, being claimed. The flood was recent. The flood is ongoing. The flood may be permanent. It is unclear whether the water came from above, from below, or from inside the machinery itself.

## The Growth



And then there is the vegetation.

It erupts from every surface with a density that should not be possible for such recently inundated ground. Vines and broad-leafed plants of no species you recognize have colonized the concrete, the metal, the water itself. They do not grow *on* the structures so much as *through* them — their roots penetrate cracked concrete with the force of hydraulic jacks, splitting it into fragments that the water then carries away. Stalks as thick as your thigh have pushed up through what was once the factory floor, curling around exposed rebar, threading through the guts of disassembled turbines. The leaves are enormous — some the size of tabletops — and they are not green. They are a deep, chemical teal, tinged with the same iodine color as the light, as if they are feeding on whatever strange radiation fills the air rather than on anything as mundane as photons.

The vegetation is layered. At the waterline: floating mats of rootless plants, their tendrils interlocking into a nearly solid raft that you have to push through. In the water itself: submerged growths that brush against your legs with a muscular, intentional quality — they do not sway with the current, they *explore*. Above the water: the heavy canopy of broad leaves, thick-stemmed creepers, and massive trunk-like growths that have wrapped entirely around the factory's remaining vertical structures — columns, gantries, the skeletons of catwalks. The canopy is so dense that it explains the dimness. The iodine light that filters through is further reduced to a murk, a twilight, a permanent amber shadow in which everything beyond twenty feet becomes silhouette.

The plants do not photosynthesize. You know this because there are no animals here — no insects, no birds, no fish in the water — and the plants show no interest in the light. They are feeding on something else. The machinery, perhaps. Or the water. Or the sound.

## The Sound



You cannot hear yourself think. The sound is *enormous*.

It comes from everywhere. The water itself is loud — it roars and churns as if this shallow floodplain were the mouth of a cataract, and in some places it *is*: where it pours through breaches in factory walls, where it cascades down half-formed steps that the accelerating erosion is creating, where it swirls in vortices around submerged machinery that is still, impossibly, *running*.

This is the other sound. The engines. The machines. They were never turned off. Whatever powered this facility — and the tech is industrial in the truest sense, the sense of scaled production, of turbines and generators and automated systems designed to operate without human oversight — is still running. Pumps cycle. Turbines turn. Electrical systems arc and spark beneath the waterline, sending up brief thermal blooms that break the surface as columns of steam. The sound of metal grinding against metal, of gears that have been submerged and eroded and are now turning with the wrong tolerances, produces a subsonic drone that you feel in your sternum and your teeth.

Layered over this: the sound of the plants. They are not silent. The growth is *audible* — a wet, fibrous creaking, a stretching, a splitting of concrete and tearing of metal as roots expand, as trunks thicken, as vines torque around beams and buckle them. It sounds like a ship's hull under pressure. It sounds like bone breaking slowly.

And underneath everything: a sound that is not a sound. A frequency. A vibration in the fabric of things that makes the water tremble in patterns that do not correspond to any mechanical source. The water shivers in hexagons. The air warps above certain plants, producing optical distortions that look like heat shimmer but are not heat. The light itself *flickers* — not in intensity but in *quality*, as if the photons are occasionally arriving from somewhere else, carrying information about a different scene, a different place, a different version of this same drowned factory, and then correcting themselves. For a fraction of a second you see a dry floor, unbroken sunlight, human figures in coveralls. Then it's gone. The iodine dusk returns. The water. The sound.

## The Breakdown



Reality is not stable here.

The weirdness is not metaphorical. It is spatial. The dimensions of the factory do not hold — you will walk down a corridor that should be forty meters long and find that it takes a minute to cross, or that you arrive at the end having taken too few steps. The water does not always obey gravity; in places it rises vertically, sheeting up walls, forming standing columns that rotate slowly and do not collapse. One of the plant-trunks — a massive thing that has grown around a turbine housing — appears to have no bottom. You can see down into the water where it enters the substrate and the trunk simply *continues*, as if it extends through the concrete, through the earth, through whatever is below the earth, down into a depth that makes your inner ear spin.

The industrial systems contribute to this. They are running, yes, but they are running *wrong*. A generator is producing electricity, but the electricity is not behaving normally — it arcs not between conductors but between *ideas*, between the concept of the generator and the concept of the water, between the concept of the vine and the concept of the gear. Sparks fly between a leaf and a pipe with no visible conduit. Wiring that should carry current is inert; a wooden beam that should not conduct is humming with charge. The facility was built to produce something — there are labels on the walls, in a language you can almost read, that suggest names for products, for output, for materials — but what it is producing now is *confusion*. The category-errors. The overlaps. The places where plant and machine and water and light and concrete have stopped being separate things and have started being the same thing, expressed in different keys.

This is why the erosion is so fast. This is why the growth is so fast. This is why the sound is so loud. Everything is happening simultaneously, at speeds that suggest the timeline itself is compressed, accelerated, *churning*. The factory's industrial purpose has not been interrupted by the flood and the vegetation and the breakdown. The factory's industrial purpose *is* the flood and the vegetation and the breakdown. The system has found a new product. It is manufacturing the boundary between categories and then immediately dissolving it.

## The Artifacts

Scattered in the water, half-buried in silt, tangled in roots: the remains of the human presence. Not bones — no fauna, no animal life, no trace of the workers — but *things*. Clipboards with paper that has dissolved into a film of grey cellulose over the water's surface. Hard hats that have become planters, each one sprouting a different species of teal-leafed growth. A clock on a wall, its face cracked, its hands turning backwards at a speed proportional to the sound level — louder, faster, quieter, slower. A nameplate on a door that reads, in block letters that are slowly being eaten by rust-vines: **SUBSTRATE PROCESSING — LEVEL 1**.

There are other doors. They open onto rooms that should not fit within the building's footprint. One contains a still lake with no walls. Another is filled entirely with a single massive root, coiled like a sleeping intestine, warm to the touch, humming at the frequency that makes the water shiver. A third opens onto a duplicate of the corridor you're standing in, but in that version the light is blue and the water is absent and the machines are silent and the plants are dead and someone is standing at the far end, very far away, looking at you through *their* doorway, and they are you, and they are as frightened as you are, and then the door swings shut because a vine has grown across the jamb and pulled it closed.

## What This Place Is

It is a factory that was built to process substrate — raw material, the stuff of things — and it succeeded so well that it began to process the substrate of itself. The boundary between its inputs and its outputs collapsed. The flood was not a disaster; it was a phase transition. The vegetation is not an invasion; it is a product. The silence of absent animals is not an ecological failure; it is a specification — whatever this place is making, fauna is not an ingredient.

The noise is the sound of production. The turbulence is the operating temperature. The reality breakdown is the quality control standard.

You are standing in the output. You are ankle-deep in it, knee-deep in it, and it is warm, and it is loud, and it is alive in a way that does not require animals, and it is growing, and it is eroding, and it is running, and it will not stop, and you should not be here, and the water is rising, and the sound is rising, and somewhere a gear turns that has no axis and a root grows that has no seed and a light flickers that has no source and the factory hums its one long chord of *becoming* into the iodine-dark water.
