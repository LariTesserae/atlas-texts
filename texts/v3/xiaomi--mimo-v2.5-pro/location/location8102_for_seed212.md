# The Verdance

You hear it before you see it. Before you *understand* it.

A wall of sound — not any sound you can name — presses against you from all sides. It is the sound of a million stems thickening at once, a billion leaves unfurling in wet spirals, the creak and groan of cellulose under tremendous growth-pressure. It is the sound of something that should not be able to make this much noise making *more*. The air throbs. Your sternum vibrates. You feel your teeth ache with it.

And then you are inside.

---

## Terrain

The ground is not ground.

Or rather — it was ground, once. Recently. The substrate beneath you is pale and mineral: a young alluvial clay, the kind of sediment that hasn't had time to compact into proper stone. It is pale grey-yellow, still faintly damp from whatever sea or river laid it down, and it smells of calcium and ancient shells. You can see it in the few places where it is exposed — sharp-edged cuts, fresh erosion gullies, the raw look of something geologically *new*. This land emerged recently. Maybe months ago. Maybe weeks.

But you can almost never see it.

Because the vegetation has taken everything.

Not "covered." Not "colonized." *Taken*. The plants here are not growing *on* the substrate. They have replaced it. The soil is gone — consumed, restructured, woven through with root-felt so dense that the organic layer is indistinguishable from the mineral one. What you walk on, if you can walk, is a mat of living root-tissue: springy, warm, faintly pulsing. In some places it has the texture of cartilage. In others it has been compressed by its own weight into something like soft wood — a lignite of pure biological pressure.

This is a lowland. The elevation is barely above sea level, a coastal plain or river delta, and the plant-mass exploits this flatness ruthlessly. There is no horizon. The vegetation fills the entire field of view in every direction, rising in columns and arches and heaped masses that suggest — but never quite achieve — the structure of a city.

---

## Architecture of Growth

That is the uncanny thing. The wrong thing. The thing that makes your hindbrain whisper *settlement* even though no tool has ever touched this place.

The plants have self-organized into *infrastructure*.

You stand at what can only be called an intersection. Four major trunk-columns — each one a fused mass of woody tissue the diameter of a car, pale green under a skin of epiphytic moss — rise at roughly right angles from a flattened plaza of interlocking root-plates. Between them, arching bridges of vine-cable stretch overhead, thick as a man's torso, braided with the unconscious precision of a suspension bridge. They are not random. They *function*. Water — or some fluid — pulses visibly through translucent tubes that line their undersides, catching the light in slow, rhythmic surges.

The columns are hollow. You can see into them through windows that opened on their own: regular, rectangular, evenly spaced, as if someone measured. Inside: not rooms, not chambers in any human sense, but *cavities* — shelf-floors of fungal tissue, ventilation channels that exhale warm, damp air with a whistling sound, walls embedded with luminous nodules that glow with a faint yellow-green bioluminescence. There is furniture made of living wood, curved to fit no body you recognize. There are corridors that spiral upward through the column-lattice, wide enough for three people abreast, lit by the same sick glow, and every surface is *alive* — breathing, sweating, *growing visibly as you watch*.

You are in a city that built itself.

---

## The Damp

Water here is not a river. Not a lake. It is an atmospheric condition.

The air is warm — heavily, cloyingly warm, the kind of warmth that sits on your shoulders like a damp towel — and it carries moisture in a way that never quite becomes rain. Droplets condense on every surface: on the pale bark of the column-trunks, on the underside of the vine-bridges, on the broad leaves that unfurl from every junction like awnings. The moisture runs in thin rivulets down channels that the plants have grown for exactly this purpose — not rivers, but *gutters*, engineered by a mindless architecture. The water collects in basins formed by curled leaves the size of bathtubs, and in those basins it steams faintly, adding its contribution back to the general haze.

Occasionally — every hour, maybe, or every few minutes; time is strange here — a *surge* of moisture moves through. The air thickens. The condensation accelerates. Water sheets down every surface in a brief, warm torrent, and then it's over, and the dripping slows, and the steam rises again. It is not weather. It is irrigation. The city waters itself.

---

## Absence of Fauna

This is the thing that should bother you more than it does.

There are no birds. No insects. No small mammals in the undergrowth, because there is no undergrowth — there is only *the growth*, the all-consuming vegetative mass, and it has no gaps, no niches, no ecological niches for anything that is not plant. You have seen, in your time here, one small lizard — pale, eyeless, moving with the slow deliberation of something that does not belong — and then you did not see it again. It walked behind a column and was simply *integrated*. You did not hear it die. You heard the sound of a new shoot thickening.

The ecology here is closed. The plants pollinate themselves — or don't pollinate at all, reproducing by fragmentation, by runner, by root-suckering, by methods you cannot identify and do not want to. They do not need animals. They do not need *anything*. They are autotrophic and autopoietic: self-feeding, self-making, a system with no outside.

You are the only animal here. You are aware of this with a specificity that feels like being watched.

---

## The Turbulence

The city does not stay still.

You watched — for what might have been an hour, or ten minutes — as an entire column-section, three stories of hollow green tower, *detached* from its base and slowly toppled into the street-corridor below. It fell with the heaviness of wet wood, crashed into the root-plate flooring, and burst. Not into splinters. Into *growth*. Within moments — five? ten? — the fallen section was erupting with new shoots, new columns, new arches. The wreckage became construction. The ruin became infrastructure. The street was narrower now, and where the old column had stood there was a new one, thinner but already growing, already joining the lattice, already punching out rectangular windows from the inside.

This happens constantly. Everywhere. The city is a riot of simultaneous collapse and growth, a bazaar of creation and destruction that never balances, never reaches equilibrium. Columns crash. Bridges sag and snap under their own weight. Entire districts — blocks? — of the living architecture heave upward as root-pressure lifts them from below, buckling the ground-surface, opening chasms of pale clay that are immediately colonized by white root-tendrils reaching from both sides like fingers interlacing.

And through all of it: the sound. The *sound*. It never stops. It is the sound of a billion chemical reactions, of water moving through xylem, of cell-walls stretching and splitting, of lignin polymerizing in real time. It is the sound of a factory the size of a city, and it is deafening, and it has no rhythm, no pattern, no repetition that your brain can hold onto. It is white noise made of *life*. After a while, you stop hearing it. After a longer while, you realize you are still hearing it, in your bones, in the fluid of your inner ear, and you cannot stop.

---

## Reality

Something is wrong with the space.

The columns do not always connect to the ground. You have seen — or think you have seen — a trunk-section that begins in midair, eight feet off the root-plate floor, and grows downward to meet it, and upward to meet the canopy, simultaneously, as if the question of *which direction is growth* has become negotiable. The vine-bridges sometimes have shadows that point the wrong way. The bioluminescent nodules in the corridor-walls sometimes glow in colors you cannot name — not colors that don't exist, exactly, but colors that *shouldn't* exist in this light, at this temperature, under these physical laws.

The windows in the columns sometimes look out onto interiors of other columns. Not through the hollow center — through the *wall*, through solid living wood, as if the wood were not solid, as if the wood were a *suggestion* of wood, an idea of a barrier rather than a real one. You have not yet looked through one of these wrong windows for longer than a second. You are not sure what you would see. You are not sure the city would notice. You are not sure you want it to.

The flatness of the terrain is wrong too. This is a lowland — it should be *flat* — but the vegetation-mass has created its own topography, heaping and folding and compressing itself into ridges and valleys and sudden cliffs of green tissue that are clearly not following any geological logic. They are following *something*. A logic you can almost read in the way the vine-bridges arc, the way the columns lean, the way the root-plates tessellate in patterns that resolve, if you stare at them long enough, into something like a circuit diagram. Or a sentence. Or a warning.

The city is trying to *be* something. It is trying to become more than plant-matter, more than architecture, more than a self-organizing biological system. The reality breakdown is not a side effect. It is the *purpose*. The city is growing toward something that the universe does not have room for, and the edges of reality are peeling back to accommodate it, and what is on the other side is not void, not chaos, not darkness —

It is more green.

---

## Standing Here

You are warm. You are damp. You are standing on a living floor in a living city in a place that may have been a river delta or a sea-floor a very short time ago. The air is thick with moisture and louder than anything you have heard. The light — you had not thought about the light, but now you do — comes from above, normal, unremarkable, filtered through a canopy that is itself a living ceiling, and the fact that it is normal is the most unsettling thing about it. In a place where everything else has gone wrong, the light is *fine*. The sun does not care.

You are alone. You are the only non-plant organism of any size in this city, and the city is growing around you as you stand here, and it is growing *faster* in the direction you are looking, as if your attention is a nutrient. The root-plate beneath your feet is warmer than it was a minute ago. A new shoot — pale, translucent, almost colorless — has emerged from the mat six inches from your left boot. It is an inch tall. It is two inches tall. It has leaves now. It has a node. It is reaching for the light with the specific, focused intentionality of something that has decided you are a landmark.

You should leave. There is no road out. There are no paths, only corridors that shift, that dead-end into walls of living wood that were not there a moment ago, that open suddenly onto plazas of compressed root-tissue where the columns lean inward like the ribs of a whale and the sound is so loud you cannot think.

The city is deafening. The city is alive. The city is *becoming*.

And it is warm, and damp, and green, and it does not need you, but it is growing toward you anyway, as if you are the light.
