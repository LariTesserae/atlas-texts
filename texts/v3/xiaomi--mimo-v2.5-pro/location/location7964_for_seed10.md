# The Moss Floor

---

The mountains are gone. That is the first thing to understand.

Whatever once pushed upward here — the granite shoulders, the glacial horns, the sharp-toothed ranges — they have been ground down by a duration that does not correspond to any calendar. What remains is a high plateau that extends to the horizon in every direction, a gently undulating tableland sitting at roughly four thousand meters, its elevation preserved not by geological vigor but by the sheer continental mass of the landmass beneath it. The ridgelines are soft. The valleys are shallow. The relief between a hilltop and its adjacent swale might be three meters over a horizontal distance of a kilometer. Everything has been carried away. Everything has been leveled to a whisper.

And then the moss came.

It did not come recently. The moss has been here longer than the memory of any living system on this world, and it has done what moss does when granted deep time and no competition: it has become the ground. There is no rock underfoot. There is no soil in any conventional sense. The surface you walk on — if walking were possible, if you could find purchase on the heaving, waterlogged, ice-crusted membrane that passes for terrain — is a living mat of bryophytes, liverworts, and fungal mycelia so ancient and so deeply layered that its lower strata have compressed into something denser than lignite. You could sink a core sample and pull up two hundred meters of organic material, each centimeter representing decades, the bottommost layers chemically indistinguishable from petroleum precursors. The moss floor is, in a literal sense, the accumulated body of every generation of plant that has ever lived here and died here. It is not *on* the land. It *is* the land.

---

At the surface, where the living layer is only a few meters thick, the moss is pale — a spectral, anemic green that verges on silver in the perpetual dim. It grows in tussocks and cushions and vast flat sheets, each species occupying its precise niche of wetness and exposure, but the overall impression is one of great sameness. The palette does not shift. The texture does not vary much. You could walk for days across this plateau and feel that you had not moved at all, that the same ten-meter patch of terrain was regenerating itself just ahead of your feet. This is the ancient quality of the place — it has settled into its final form, and its final form is repetition.

But the wetness keeps it from stillness.

Water is everywhere. Not in rivers, not in lakes — the erosion has gone on too long for defined channels to persist. The water simply *is*, saturating the moss from below, pooling in every depression, seeping upward through the porous plant-matter substrate as though the entire plateau were a sponge being slowly wrung. In the colder periods — and the cold is the default here, a few degrees above freezing, a persistent hypothermic breath that never relents — the surface freezes into a crust of thin ice, translucent, veined with the ghost-architecture of the moss beneath it. You can see the plant structure through the ice as through frosted glass. Then the crust breaks. Beneath it is black water, tea-colored with tannins leached from the deep peat, and it is warmer than the air, and it steams.

The steam is the landscape's breath. It rises constantly, in columns and curtains and low-lying banks that never quite organize into fog but never quite dissipate either. The atmosphere is heavy with it — humid, cold, tasting of iron and decomposing cellulose. This is why the light is so dim even when the sky above the steam layer is clear: the moisture diffuses everything, scatters every photon into a milky, directionless glow. There is no sharp shadow anywhere in this world. There is no defined source of illumination. Light here is a substance, not a beam — it settles on the moss like a deposit, like ash, like another layer of sediment.

And it rains. Often, unpredictably, the moisture in the air reaches some critical density and simply falls — not as weather, not as storm, but as a kind of atmospheric collapse. One moment the air is merely damp. The next, water is condensing on every surface, running down the tussocks, pooling in the ice-holes, flooding the swales. It lasts minutes or hours. Then it stops, and the steam begins again, and the cycle repeats. The sound of it — the constant dripping, the hiss of condensation, the soft percussive ticking of water striking water — is the baseline noise of the Moss Floor. It is not quiet here. It is not loud. It is *wet-sounding*, a world defined by the acoustic signature of liquid in motion.

---

The animals know this place.

They have adapted to it in ways that suggest a tenure nearly as long as the moss's own. The most visible are the grazers — vast, slow-moving things that browse the living surface like continental-scale cows, each one the size of a freight car, their bodies low-slung and wide, distributing weight across the waterlogged substrate so they do not sink. They have six legs. Their underbellies are sealed with a waxy integument that sheds the black peat-water. They move in loose herds of a dozen to a hundred, and their passage leaves furrows in the moss that take years to heal, temporary channels that fill with water and become miniature wetlands before the plant matter knits back over them.

But the grazers are not the dominant fauna. The dominant fauna are insects — or things that fill the insect niche, though they are not precisely arthropods. They are too large for that, most of them, some the size of a human hand, and they have too many segments, and their chitin is not chitin but something more cartilaginous, translucent, faintly iridescent in the dim. They live in the moss itself, tunneling through the living layer, pupating in the waterlogged depths, erupting from the surface in sudden swarms that fill the air with a low, throbbing hum. The sound of them — the thrumming of a million wings that are not quite wings, more like vibrating membranes — adds a bass note to the dripping, a frequency you feel in your sternum before you hear it with your ears.

The predators are quieter. You do not see them. You feel the grazers react to them — a sudden stillness, a rippling shift in herd direction — but the predators themselves move through the moss like knives through fabric, leaving no trace, their bodies adapted to slide between the tussocks without breaking the ice crust. What they look like is uncertain. What is certain is that they are many, and they are hungry, and the grazers' fear of them is the organizing principle of every herd's movement across the plateau.

And then there are the *other* presences.

---

Here is where the Moss Floor begins to break.

Not geologically — the geology is stable, ancient, as settled as bedrock can be. But the *reality* of this place is not stable. It has not been stable for a long time, and the instability is not dramatic, not catastrophic — it is slow, ambient, atmospheric, like background radiation. It manifests in small ways that accumulate.

The most common manifestation is spatial. Distances are not fixed. You can walk toward a visible feature — a darker patch of moss, a steam column, a herd of grazers — and arrive either sooner or later than your steps should account for. The plateau seems to breathe, expanding and contracting in ways that are impossible to measure because the measuring instrument participates in the distortion. Over time, this produces a profound spatial disorientation. Where am I? How far have I come? These questions lose their meaning. The Moss Floor is the same in all directions. The distances between things are suggestions, not facts.

Less common but more troubling are the temporal pockets. In certain depressions, usually where the water is deepest and the moss has grown into strange, tower-like formations — spires of compressed plant matter rising two or three meters above the general surface, their shapes suggesting something other than natural growth — time does not pass evenly. You might stand at the edge of such a depression and watch the moss within it grow and die and grow again in the span of a minute, a rapid cycling through years or decades of biological time. The grazers avoid these places. The insects cluster around them, drawn to the temporal distortion the way moths are drawn to heat, their vibrating membranes resonating with the dissonance.

And then there are the mechanical sounds.

---

Beneath the moss — deep beneath it, in the compressed peat layers that border on fossil fuel — something is running. It has been running for longer than the moss has been here, which means it predates this ecosystem entirely, which means it is very old, which raises the question of who built it and why and whether "built" is even the right word.

The sounds are rhythmic. Piston-like. A deep, slow chugging that you feel through the substrate, transmitted upward through hundreds of meters of plant matter until it emerges at the surface as a faint vibration in the standing water. The ice crackles in sympathy with it. The tussocks tremble at its frequency. The grazers orient their movement partly in response to it — some herds move toward the sound, others away, and the boundary between these behavioral groups shifts in ways that suggest the sound itself moves, or that its intensity waxes and wanes on some internal schedule.

What it is, mechanically, is hard to say. It might be geothermal. It might be something tapping the compressed peat as fuel — a natural engine, or an artificial one, or something in between, something that has been running so long it has ceased to be either natural or artificial and has become simply *a feature of the place*. The industrial quality of the sound — the precision, the regularity, the implication of engineered tolerances — sits uneasily alongside the moss and the steam and the primordial wetness. It is an artifact that has outlived its context. Its makers are gone. Its purpose, if it had one, is forgotten. It simply runs.

In some areas, the machinery is closer to the surface. The moss grows differently there — faster, darker, twisted into shapes that suggest electromagnetic interference or chemical contamination. The water in these zones is warmer and smells of copper. The insects avoid them, which is notable because the insects avoid almost nothing else. The grazers give them wide berth. Even the predators seem to route around them, as though the distorted growth patterns of the moss create terrain features that are impassable in ways that have nothing to do with physical barriers.

---

The plateau extends for hundreds of kilometers in every direction. It is not empty — the density of life is high, if "density" refers to total biomass — but it *feels* empty because everything is the same. The moss. The steam. The grazers. The hum. The dripping. The dim. The cold. The spatial uncertainty. The mechanical heartbeat beneath your feet. It is a landscape that has reached equilibrium and then continued to evolve beyond equilibrium into something stranger — a place that is simultaneously at its oldest and most settled, and at its most turbulent and unstable. The moss does not change. The reality does not hold still. These two facts coexist without resolution.

At the edges of the plateau — and there are edges, though they are soft, the terrain simply becoming *more* level, *more* saturated, *more* mossy until the distinction between land and water dissolves entirely — the steam thickens into permanent cloud cover. What lies beyond the plateau is unknown from within it. The grazers do not migrate outward. The insects do not fly into the cloud bank. The mechanical sound does not extend past the edge. Everything that defines the Moss Floor — its life, its noise, its trembling instability — is contained within this ancient, eroded, high-altitude, waterlogged, half-lit, plant-built, machine-haunted, reality-sick expanse of living stone that is not stone.

It has been here forever. It will be here forever. It is changing constantly. All of these things are true at once.
