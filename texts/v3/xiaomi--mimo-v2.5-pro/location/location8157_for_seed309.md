# The Aggregation

You descend a corroded maintenance ladder, and the first thing that reaches you is the sound.

It's not one sound. It's hundreds of thousands of small sounds layered into a continuous roar — a dry, clicking, rasping, drumming, chittering mass that never quite resolves into rhythm. It is the sound of the fauna, and it is the fauna itself, or nearly. The wall beside you is moving. Every surface is moving. At first in the dim — there are a few surviving industrial fixtures, half-shielded by growth, casting a sallow amber wash over parts of the space — you think the walls are wet, that moisture is sliding down them. Then your eyes adjust.

---

**The space** is a subterranean processing floor. You can still read the architecture: poured concrete, now grey-green with age and absorption; steel joists overhead, bowed and webbed; a grid of channels cut into the floor that once carried coolant or chemical runoff. The ceiling is perhaps five meters up. The floor extends maybe forty meters in each direction before it meets the foundation walls — though those walls are no longer fully visible, buried behind what has grown against them. This is a single room, a unit, a chamber. A hamlet-sized pocket beneath what was once a larger complex above. The surface structures, whatever they were, have long since been reclaimed or demolished. What remains is this: one room, sealed mostly by its own collapse, breathing through gaps in the ceiling where pipes once ran and where condensation now gathers and drips.

That is your water. Not rivers, not rain. Condensation. The underground temperature holds steady — warm, but not hot, the warmth of a place that remembers sun without receiving it. Moisture beads on every cool surface: the undersides of metal beams, the faces of concrete, the smooth stems of the largest plants. It collects and runs in thin rivulets down the channel-grooves in the floor, pooling in shallow depressions where rust-colored biofilm gathers. Occasionally, from somewhere far above, a heavier trickle breaks through — perhaps a drainage system still feeding this forgotten bowl, perhaps just the slow filtering of surface water through rock and soil. Enough. Not abundant. Enough.

---

**The plants** began with the moisture. They are the substrate now. The floor is not concrete anymore; it is roots. A dense, interlocking mat of roots and rhizomes, pale where they are deep and green where light touches them, has grown into and through the concrete channels, cracking them further, filling every gap. Above this root-mat, the vertical surfaces explode into life.

There are no trees here, not exactly. What grows is structural in a different way: massive epiphytic growths, layered and bracketed, climbing the pillars and joists like shelf fungi the size of tables, except they are green, photosynthetic, drawing what little light exists from the fixtures and from the gaps in the ceiling. Between and among these bracket-forms grow trailing networks of something vine-like but leafless — smooth, segmented, pale green stems that hang in curtains from the joists, swaying slightly in the faint convection currents. They reach lengths of three or four meters before their tips touch the root-mat below and root again, creating arched colonnades of living stem.

And between these structures, filling the interstices: mosses, liverworts, thin films of algae on every damp surface. The light that filters down is mostly amber from the few surviving fixtures and a cooler, bluish-white from patches of bioluminescent fungus that grow in the deepest recesses — not enough to read by, enough to see shapes, movement, depth. The overall impression is of a dim, warm, green-amber cathedral of vegetative mass, airy in its upper reaches but dense and tangled near the floor, where the root-mat heaves and buckles around the old machinery.

The machinery is still here. You can identify the shapes: large cylindrical vessels, half-buried in root; a system of pipes running along the walls, now serving as trellises for the bracket-fungi; an engine housing, its casing split by a wedge of woody root growth that entered through a seam and expanded over what must have been decades. The industrial skeleton provides the scaffolding. The plants have not replaced it — they have *incorporated* it. Steel and concrete are structural elements of the ecosystem now, slowly being consumed, slowly being used.

This place is old. Not ancient. But old enough that the original functions are unrecoverable. The erosion is not of decay alone but of *equilibrium* — the system has found its shape. It is changing, yes, but cycling, not collapsing.

---

**The fauna.**

You need to understand something about the fauna.

When you first entered, you registered the sound and the moving walls, and you thought: insects. That's partially correct. There are insects — small ones, swarming in the amber light around the fixtures, drawn by a phototaxis that may be vestigial, the fixtures they evolved around having mostly died. But insects are a fraction of what lives here.

The dominant life-form — the one that makes the sound, the one that makes the walls move — is something between a colonial invertebrate and a social vertebrate. Imagine a creature the size of your thumb, warm-blooded, furred in a fine, dense coat that ranges from grey to deep rust. It has six limbs — four for gripping, two for manipulation — and a flattened body that lets it press into narrow spaces. Its face is blunt, mostly mouth, with small dark eyes adapted to dim light. It is not a mammal. It is not an insect. It has no clean taxonomic analog. It is *of this place*, evolved here or adapted here in the long decades since the complex was abandoned.

There are hundreds of thousands of them.

They live in the bracket-fungi and the shelf-structures, tunneling into the fleshy tissue, which regenerates around their passages. They live in the root-mat, excavating chambers where condensation pools. They live in the pipes, in the engine housing, in the hollow joists. They are everywhere, and they are active — constantly in motion, climbing, feeding, carrying material, communicating in a dense language of clicks and body-vibrations that is the dominant component of the sound you hear. Layered under this is the lower-frequency hum of their collective movement through the root-mat — a thrumming you feel in your feet as much as hear.

They are not aggressive toward you. They are not curious about you, particularly. They flow around you like water around a stone. You are an obstacle to route around, nothing more. Their attention is wholly absorbed by their own vast, complex society — by food-gathering (they eat the fungi, the mosses, certain seed-bodies produced by the bracket-plants, and each other, occasionally, in controlled territorial skirmishes), by construction (they reinforce the root-mat with a resin they secrete, building it into denser and more complex structures, effectively co-architecting the ecosystem with the plants), by reproduction (which you cannot easily observe but which must be constant given the population density and the churn of small bodies you see — juveniles, half the size of adults, clinging to the backs of larger ones).

They are not the only fauna. They are merely the overwhelming fauna. Other creatures exist in their margins: a species of pale, eyeless amphibian that lives in the condensation channels, feeding on fallen juveniles and on the small invertebrates that swarm in the algae films. A large, slow arthropod — perhaps fifteen centimeters long, many-legged, matte black — that grazes on the outer surfaces of the bracket-fungi and seems to exist in a kind of tolerated truce with the dominant species. Once, deeper in the room, past a curtain of hanging stems, you glimpse something bigger: a shape the size of a cat, hunched on a joist, watching the colony below with reflective eyes. It does not move. You do not approach. There are trophic layers here you are only beginning to read.

---

**The soundscape** is dense, layered, and never still. The clicking of the dominant fauna is the baseline — a constant, shimmering noise, like rain on a metal roof but more complex, rising and falling in local surges as territorial disputes flare and resolve, as groups coordinate movement, as mating calls cut through the general din. Underneath this: the drip of condensation, a slow and arrhythmic punctuation. The creak of steel under the shifting load of living mass. A low, subsonic vibration from deep in the root-mat — the movement of the larger burrowing creatures, perhaps, or the settling of the substrate itself. Occasionally a sudden, sharp burst of alarm-chatter that ripples outward in a wave, then subsides. You never learn what caused it.

---

**The atmosphere** is warm, close, and smells of damp earth and something faintly sweet — the metabolic output of the fungi, maybe, or the resin of the fauna. It is not unpleasant. It is heavy. The air has weight. You breathe it and feel it in your lungs, feel the humidity coat the inside of your throat. The dimness presses in. The amber light does not reach far; beyond a certain radius, everything becomes shape and movement, suggestion and sound. You are in a space that is thoroughly alive, that has no interest in you, that will outlast you by a comfortable margin, and that is, despite its smallness, more complex than you can map in a single visit.

The machinery has stopped. Nothing here was built. But everything here is *made*.
