# The Terretenes

You are standing in a pocket.

The walls rise around you — not walls exactly, but *terraces*, layer upon layer of calcareous stone, each shelf a meter or two above the last, stepping upward on all sides until they close to a ragged oval of sky no wider than a temple rotunda. The stone is young. You can tell by its edges: the ledges are crisp, almost sharp enough to cut skin where the rock fractured along fresh joints. A few millennia ago this was underground. The land lifted, the overburden eroded in a handful of centuries, and what remained was this hollow — a natural amphitheater maybe sixty meters across, forty meters deep, open to the warm sky but sheltered at its base from wind. The rock here is pale, almost white, streaked with bands of rust and ochre where iron oxidized at the contact surfaces.

It is very dry. There is no stream, no spring, no standing water anywhere. The air carries almost no humidity. Yet life has found its way in.

---

Every terrace is occupied.

The ledges are shallow — some no deeper than an outstretched arm — and each one has been shaped. Not by tools. By *accretion*. Over centuries, the inhabitants have built onto the natural rock with a material that resemblesiteite plaster but is alive: a calcium-carbonate matrix secreted by colonies of tiny organisms, layered and re-layered until the ledges have thickened, widened, developed low curbs and partitioning walls. From below, looking up, the terraces resemble a city of the smallest possible scale — a metropolis for things the size of your thumb. Corridors between walls of built-upite no wider than a finger. Plazas where three ledges converge into a platform perhaps the size of a dinner plate. Niches recessed into the pale walls. Every surface is contoured, worked, made hospitable.

There is no technology here. No fire, no metal, no woven fiber. Only the patient architecture of creatures that secrete and stack and smooth.

---

The vegetation is sparse where it grows, but deeply rooted — physically and figuratively. On the lower terraces, where the stone is warmest, low rosette plants press flat against the rock, their leaves thick and waxy, almost grey-green, collecting whatever dew forms during the brief cooling before dawn. Their roots crack into hairline fissures and go deep. Higher up, on the north-facing shelves where the stone stays cooler, small ferns uncurl in the permanent partial shade, their fronds no longer than a finger. A few hardy succulents cluster in the deepest niches, swollen with stored moisture they seem to almost hoard. Lichens — pale, crustose, slow as geology — coat the uppermost ledges where nothing else can reach.

None of it is abundant in the way a forest is abundant. But in this dry, bounded place, the green is startling. It feels *concentrated*, like pigment pressed into a tiny palette.

---

The fauna is where the place becomes itself.

They are everywhere. Every ledge, every niche, every crevice between the built-up walls has an occupant. Small creatures — most of them no longer than your little finger — move through the terraced city with the unhurried familiarity of lifelong residents. The closest analogue might be geckos, but that's imprecise. They have the flattened bodies and splayed toes of geckos, yes, and the same lateral eye-gaze, but their skin has a mineral quality, a pale chalkiness that makes them nearly invisible against the stone when they hold still. They *frequently* hold still. They will sit motionless for hours on a sun-warmed ledge, watching nothing, digesting nothing, simply *being there*, as if waiting for the stone itself to do something.

Alongside them, equally numerous: insects of a kind that seems to have no mouth. Thin-bodied, leggy, pale as the rock, they move in slow processions along the edges of the built walls, carrying nothing, building nothing new, following paths worn into the calcium matrix by generations of predecessors. Their role is unclear. They seem less like organisms and more like *components* — like the city's circulating fluid made manifest.

Higher up, on ledges too narrow for the gecko-things, small birds roost. They are seed-eaters, you think, though you never see them eat. They sit in pairs, shoulder to shoulder, barely visible against the pale stone, and they do not sing. This is perhaps the most remarkable thing about the Terretenes: the silence.

---

The silence.

You did not expect it. A place this dense with life should hum, should buzz, should chirr and click and pulse with the sounds of a thousand small bodies going about their business. But the Terretenes are quiet with a depth that seems structural, as if the stone itself absorbs sound — or as if the creatures here have, over uncounted generations, simply *stopped making noise*. The gecko-things move without a whisper. Their feet on the stone are silent. The mouthless insects make no sound at all, which you suppose follows from their apparent anatomy. The birds open their beaks occasionally but nothing comes out that you can hear. Perhaps they communicate in frequencies below your threshold. Perhaps they communicate by posture, by the angle of a feather, by some chemical signal you cannot detect. Perhaps they have simply decided, collectively and irrevocably, that sound is unnecessary.

What you hear: the faint scrape of your own boot on grit. Your own breathing. The whisper of air moving through the oval of sky above you. Nothing else. The silence has *weight*. It presses against you from all sides, warm and patient and enormous, and after a while you begin to move more quietly yourself, as if the place has asked you to.

---

The light is normal — warm, direct when the sun is overhead, dappled by the terraces' own shadows in the mornings and late afternoons. The stone glows faintly during golden hour, the iron bands igniting briefly before cooling to grey. Shadows in the deepest niches are permanent; you wonder what lives in them that you cannot see.

The dynamics are slow. You could watch this place for a day and see almost nothing change. The gecko-things would shift position by centimeters. The birds would trade ledges once, maybe twice. The light would move. The lichens would not visibly grow. But over a season — over a year — you would notice the terraces thickening, the walls gaining a millimeter of new calcium, a niche deepening, a partition wall completing itself. The city builds itself at the pace of mineral deposition. Nothing is in a hurry. Nothing has ever been in a hurry. The creatures that live here have organized their entire existence around the tempo of stone, and they keep to it with a fidelity that borders on liturgy.

---

You are standing in a pocket, and the pocket is full. It is a room the size of a cathedral nave laid on its side and sunk into the earth, and it is a city the size of an apartment building turned inside out, and it is a single living organism the size of a geological formation. It is dry and warm and almost silent, and it is *lush* in the way that a desert oasis is lush — not profuse, but *concentrated*, every blade and body carrying the full weight of the place's sparse generosity.

The sharp-edged stone under your feet was underground yesterday, geologically speaking. The city on the walls is ancient, but only by the measure of its inhabitants — a few hundred years, perhaps, a few thousand cycles of secretion and smoothing. It is young. Everything here is young. And nothing here is in any rush to get older.
