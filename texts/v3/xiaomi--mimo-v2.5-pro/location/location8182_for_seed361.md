# The Cisterns of the Bole Plateau

You are standing on what was once, perhaps, a mountain range. It is not one anymore. Four billion years of wind and micrometeorite rain have brought it low — not to flatness, but to a state of permanent weariness. The Bole Plateau rolls away from you in swells so gentle they are almost imaginary: long, slow undulations of rust-colored stone, grainy and porous, warm to the touch even in shade. The stone here has a texture like bone that has been in soil for a century. It crumbles under sustained pressure but holds its shape against anything sudden. The horizon is a soft line that could be distance or could be haze.

You are high — not dramatically so, but consistently. The air is thinner than the lowlands by a fraction you can feel in the effort of breathing, in the way sound carries strangely far across the plateau before reaching you. The warmth is constant and heavy. Not the punishing heat of a desert but something more saturated, more equatorial, as though the atmosphere itself has thickened slightly up here through some mechanism the plateau doesn't explain.

---

This is a sparse place. The stone goes on and on between features. Scrabble-grass — pale, wiry, ankle-high — grows in patches where dust has accumulated in the lee of wind-sculpted ridges. A few succulents with swollen grey-green trunks anchor themselves in crevices. That's what you see at first. You could walk for an hour across the open plateau and encounter almost nothing that wasn't stone or sky or grass.

But you are not on the open plateau.

You are standing at the edge of a **Cistern**.

---

The word is local. The people who used it are gone, or changed beyond recognition, or folded into the fauna of the Cisterns themselves — no one is certain. What remains are their marks: faint, scattered, ancient in a way that makes even the plateau feel recent. Rectangular foundations of a material that is not quite stone, not quite ceramic, not quite metal — something that the wind has not been able to fully digest. They are buried to the waist in grit. Some have symbols on their exposed faces that might be language or might be structural patterning. Once, walking the plateau, you might step over one without recognizing it as anything other than another feature of the geology. The built environment here is a whisper. A suggestion. The ghost of infrastructure so old it has achieved the dignity of becoming landscape.

But the Cisterns themselves — those are something else.

---

Imagine a pocket in the earth. Not a cave, not a canyon, not a sinkhole exactly — though it shares qualities with all three. A Cistern is an oblong depression, perhaps forty meters across at its widest, perhaps eighty meters long, perhaps twenty meters deep. The walls are the same ancient stone as the plateau but they curve inward as they descend, like the inside of a great ceramic bowl half-buried in the ground. At the bottom, there is always water. Not a river, not a lake — a **seep**. A slow, patient accumulation of moisture that weeps from the stone itself, collecting in shallow pools no more than a few centimeters deep, spreading across the flat bottom of the depression in glassy sheets that reflect the sky.

The water is warm. It smells of mineral and green.

The walls of the Cistern are covered in life.

---

This is what you came to understand about the Bole Plateau: the Cisterns are where everything is. The sparse, arid surface is a skin stretched over a hidden world of these pocket-biomes, and each one is *loud*. The acoustics of the curved stone walls concentrate and amplify every sound made within them, so that even a single bird-equivalent creates a reverberant call that fills the space. Now imagine dozens of species. Hundreds of individuals. A Cistern in full activity sounds like a rainforest recorded in a cathedral — layered, echoing, percussive, relentless. The sound rises from the depression and spills across the silent plateau like heat from a fissure.

You hear it before you see the Cistern itself. A thrumming, clicking, trilling wall of sound.

---

The vegetation is lush — but it is lush in the way that a terrarium is lush. Concentrated. Dense within the boundaries of the Cistern, absent outside it. On the sloping walls, root-mats have built up over millennia — layers of decomposed matter and living roots so thick and interwoven they form a continuous felted surface, meters deep, anchored to the stone by root-systems that have fused with the mineral substrate in ways that blur the line between biology and geology. From this mat, everything grows.

Frond-trees with translucent pale trunks arch outward from the walls, their canopies meeting overhead in some Cisterns, creating a closed dome of green. Between them, epiphytes cluster in thickets — bromeliad analogs that hold their own tiny reservoirs of condensation, fern-like organisms with fronds that fold when sound is too loud (and in a Cistern, they stay folded for most of the day), climbing vines with flowers that open only in the brief interval between dawn and full sunlight. Mosses and lichens coat every surface that the root-mat hasn't claimed. The air beneath the canopy is humid, still, and warm — warmer than the already-warm plateau above, a pocket of microclimate that sustains everything growing there.

The water at the bottom is almost invisible beneath the vegetation that grows directly out of it — aquatic grasses with blades that lie flat on the water's surface like green glass, root-structures that draw from the seep and send up stalks bearing seed-heads that crack and pop in the heat. Lilies — or something that fills the lily's niche — float on the shallow pools, their pads wide enough to support the weight of small animals.

---

And there are *so many* animals.

This is the overwhelming part. The fauna of the Cisterns is not merely diverse; it is *oppressive*. Every surface, every layer, every volume of space within a Cistern is occupied. The density of life per cubic meter is staggering, and the density outside the Cisterns is nearly zero — the contrast is what makes it feel so extreme. Life has pooled here like water in a cupped hand.

The root-mat alone is an entire world. Invertebrates by the thousands of species tunnel through it — soft-bodied things with too many legs, things that are more mouth than body, things that spin webs of silk so fine they are visible only when condensation beads on them. Some of them are the size of a thumbnail. A few are the size of a hand. The mat is warm from their collective metabolism. Press your palm to it and you can feel it *moving*.

In the branches: gliders. The frond-trees host a guild of vertebrate gliders — perhaps descended from a common ancestor, perhaps arrived independently through convergent pressure — that move between the canopy in arcs of stretched membrane. They are not quiet. They vocalize constantly, in sequences that have the cadence of conversation but may be territorial declaration, mating negotiation, predator warning, or all of these simultaneously. Their calls reverberate off the stone walls until they become a single layered tone, a chord that shifts and modulates as individuals enter and leave.

On the water: skimmers. Insects that have colonized the surface tension of the shallow pools. Swimmers — small, sleek, with bodies shaped like river stones, that navigate between the aquatic grass stems. Predators among them that sit motionless on lily pads, waiting with a patience that seems geological until something small passes beneath them and they strike with a speed that is almost violent in contrast to their stillness.

And the birds. Or bird-equivalents. They are the loudest element and the most numerous after the invertebrates. Small, fast, iridescent, they move through the Cistern in flocks that turn like schools of fish — hundreds of bodies executing simultaneous direction changes for reasons invisible to a human observer. Their collective sound is the backbone of the Cistern's acoustic signature: a continuous, rolling, high-pitched chatter that never fully stops, even at night. It merely... changes register. Becomes more whisper than shout. The nocturnal species take over the vocal frequencies the diurnal ones vacate.

You cannot move through a Cistern without disturbing something. Every step flushes creatures from concealment. Every touch of a branch sends a tremor through the canopy that mobilizes a cascade of responses — alarms, flights, sudden silences followed by cautious resumptions. The overwhelming quality is not merely numerical. It is *felt*. It is the sense of being in a space that is already fully occupied, where you are the anomaly, the disruption, the one creature in the system that has no assigned role.

---

The technology is the strangest part, and it is the part you almost miss.

The Cisterns are not natural. This is evident only when you look closely at the walls — really closely, below the root-mat where the stone is exposed. The stone is not stone. It is a material with a crystalline microstructure that does not correspond to any known geology. It is warm, and the warmth is not entirely explained by the ambient temperature — there is a faint, barely perceptible *thrum* in it, a vibration at a frequency just below hearing. The material is self-repairing. Where cracks have formed — and they do form, even in this ancient substrate — they heal over timescales of centuries, the material flowing slowly back into continuity like scar tissue. The root-mat has evolved to take advantage of this: roots probe into cracks, and as the cracks close, the roots are incorporated into the material, becoming part of its structure. Biology and engineering, fused beyond separation.

The seep at the bottom of each Cistern is not groundwater. There is no aquifer beneath the plateau — it is too ancient, too dry, too eroded. The water is *manufactured*. Deep in the walls of the Cistern, the ancient material processes atmospheric moisture — drawing it from the air, condensing it, releasing it through micro-channels in the stone in a slow, steady weep that never fully stops. The technology is so old, so degraded, so merged with the ecology it supports, that it reads as geology. As nature. As *the way things are*. But it is not. Someone built these. Someone built hundreds of them across the plateau — perhaps thousands — and then left, or died, or transformed, or forgot, and the Cisterns kept going, kept weeping water, kept sustaining life, kept humming with that sub-audible vibration, for longer than most species on this planet have existed.

The scattered built remnants on the surface — the half-buried foundations, the indestructible rectangular shapes — are probably related. Access points. Control structures. Monitoring stations. Whatever function they served, it is over. They are artifacts now, embedded in the same ancient wind-smoothed stone as everything else on the plateau, distinguishable only by their impossible durability and the faint geometric regularity of their decay.

---

There is weather, though it is gentle. Once every few days, a thermal cycle draws moisture up from the lowlands in slow, warm columns of air. The plateau clouds over — not with storm, but with a grey, warm overcast that presses down on the landscape like a lid. Condensation forms on the ancient stone. Everything drips. The Cisterns fill slightly higher, their shallow pools spreading a few centimeters outward, and the fauna responds — activity spikes, vocalization intensifies, the skimmers range farther across the expanded water surface, the gliders launch from higher points to take advantage of the denser air. Then the overcast lifts, the moisture retreats, the pools contract, and the cycle settles.

At night, the temperature drops — not dramatically, but enough. The stone releases its accumulated warmth slowly, and the Cisterns become briefly cool, their air thick with condensation. This is when the largest fauna emerges. Things that spend the heat of the day in the root-mat, compressed into crevices, suddenly unfold and move. Slow, deliberate, heavy by the standards of the Cistern — perhaps the size of a large dog — they move through the canopy or along the walls with a deliberateness that suggests great age or great patience. They are not predators. They seem to be grazers of the root-mat itself, or filterers of the mist-laden air, or something that doesn't map cleanly onto a terrestrial niche. They are among the strangest things in the Cistern, not because of their form — which is almost banal, a six-limbed body with a broad flat head and wide-set eyes that catch the faintest light — but because of their *behavior*. They pause. They orient toward the walls. They press their heads against the ancient material and hold that position for minutes at a time, as though listening. To what — the hum? The vibration? Some signal encoded in the stone that the biological systems of the Cistern can still read, even if no one is transmitting anymore?

No one knows. There is no one left to ask.

---

The Bole Plateau stretches to the horizon, quiet and dry and ancient, its gentle swells of stone baking under a warm sun. From above, from a sufficient distance, it looks empty. Lifeless. A skin of dust and grass over bone.

Beneath that skin, the Cisterns sing.
