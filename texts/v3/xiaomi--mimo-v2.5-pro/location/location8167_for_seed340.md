# The Resin Cathedral

---

You descend.

Not through water — there is no water here, and there hasn't been for a long time, if there ever was. You descend through a crack in a salt-crusted plain, down a passage that narrows and then widens and then opens, and then keeps opening, until the word "cavern" stops being adequate and you are standing on the lip of something that wants to be called a *basin* but that you will eventually, after the sound starts to make sense to you, call a **throat**.

The air hits you first. It is warm — not from above, from below. Not sunlight-warm. Digestion-warm. The temperature of something being slowly broken down and rebuilt. It rises in waves from the floor of the basin, which is hundreds of meters below you and hundreds of meters across in every direction, and it smells like hot amber and cut roots and something almost electrical, like the moment before a storm except there has never been a storm here and there never will be.

There is no sky. The ceiling is alive.

---

Look up: the underside of this world is a **ceiling of living matter**, meters thick, a dense mat of pale ochre and bone-white growth that spans the entire basin and continues past its edges into darkness. It hangs in places like a frozen wave, in others it clusters into columns that reach down toward the floor — stalactites made not of mineral but of layered, fibrous, dry-as-bone plant tissue. Some of them touch the floor. Some of them are the floor. The distinction between ceiling and pillar and ground has been eroded by growth in every direction until the architecture of the space is less *cave with things in it* and more *a single organism with rooms inside it*.

This is the vegetation. There is almost no water in this place — the chemistry runs on sulfur compounds, on mineral ions drawn from the rock itself, on thermal gradients. The plants here do not photosynthesize; there is no light to photosynthesize with except what little filters down from the surface passage you just climbed through, a distant coin of grey-white sky far above and behind you. The plants are **chemotrophic** — they feed on the geothermal upwelling from below, metabolizing hydrogen sulfide and dissolved metals, exhaling warm dry gases that smell faintly of turpentine and burned sugar. They are not green. They range from cream to deep rust to the translucent yellow of old fingernails. Some of them glow faintly — not bioluminescence exactly, but a dull foxfire warmth where their metabolic activity peaks, scattered across the basin like slow embers.

The "soil" beneath your feet is plant matter. The walls are plant matter. The air is full of fine, dry particulate — not dust, but **spore and pollen and shredded fiber**, the metabolic exhaust of a biomass so dense it generates its own weather: slow convection currents that spiral upward through the basin, carrying fragments of itself in warm, lazy cyclones that drift and dissolve and settle. You will find this material in your lungs later. You will cough up rust-colored phlegm for days.

---

Now listen.

The sound.

**The sound is enormous.**

It takes your brain several seconds to stop treating it as noise and start parsing it as a *soundscape*, and even then, the parsing is partial. The baseline is a **low, thrumming drone** — the combined vibration of every structural column in the basin resonating at slightly different frequencies, driven by the thermal updraft. It is not a sound you hear so much as one you feel in your sternum and behind your eyes, a 15-to-30-hertz hum that makes the air seem thicker than it is. This is the cathedral's organ note. It never stops.

Layered on top of this is the **insects**.

The fauna. Oh, the fauna.

This place is *thick* with life that is not plant. The plant substrate provides structure — tunnels, chambers, hollowed galleries between the layered growth-plates — and into every crack and crawlspace, something has moved in. The dominant forms are arthropod-analogues, but that word barely contains them. Imagine beetles the size of your hand that live inside the pillar-walls, burrowing through the dry pith, and the sound of their excavation — a persistent, rasping *skritch-skritch-skritch* — comes from every direction at once, a surround-sound abrasion that never ceases. There are thousands. There are tens of thousands. The pillars are riddled with them, and as you approach one, you can see the surface moving, faintly, with the aggregate motion of bodies just beneath.

But the beetles are quiet compared to the **striders**.

The striders are long-legged, mantis-like creatures, two meters from forelimb to tail, that move across the ceiling-mat on limbs tipped with hooked, fibrous growths — they have, over generations, *grafted themselves partially to the plant*, or the plant has grown around their limbs, and the relationship is unclear and possibly irrelevant. They are the color of the ceiling. They are nearly invisible when still. They are rarely still. When they move, their wing-casings — which they do not use for flight but for *sound* — vibrate against each other in a rapid, percussive buzz that is **deafeningly loud**, a chainsaw-rattle that echoes off every surface in the basin and arrives at your ears from multiple directions at once, doppler-shifted and overlapping, so that the air seems to *shimmer* with it. They do this constantly. They do this to communicate. They do this to ward off the things that hunt them. They do this because this is what they are and what they have always done and the sound is the medium through which this entire ecology negotiates itself.

There are other things. Things that are large — you hear them moving in the deeper galleries, in the spaces below the floor-plane where the plant matter has grown thick enough to support its own internal voids. Heavy, slow sounds. Dragging. The occasional crack of a structural column giving way, which is answered by a *surge* of insect sound, a panicked crescendo that fades over minutes as the community recalibrates around the loss. Things fall here. Growth collapses under its own weight. New growth fills the gaps. This is the turbulence — the basin is not stable. It is not *meant* to be stable. It is a system in constant, violent, creative equilibrium, building and destroying itself in cycles that play out over hours, not years. A pillar falls and the crash shakes the floor and sends a wave of spore-cloud billowing outward and within a day — *a day* — new growth is already reaching toward the gap, pale root-tendrils questing upward, and within a week there will be a new column there, thinner but alive, and within a month something will have moved into it.

---

At the edges of the basin, where the ceiling-mat meets the raw rock, the geology is *emergent* — fresh, unfinished, raw. You can see where the living boundary is still pushing outward, colonizing new stone, dissolving it with acidic secretions, incorporating it. The limestone here is pocked and softened, still recognizably geological, not yet fully converted into the organic architecture of the interior. Crystals of some sulfur-calcium compound grow in clusters where the thermal vents are strongest, pale yellow and warm to the touch, and the plant-matter grows over them and around them, using them as mineral supplements, so that the boundary between crystal and living tissue is blurry, transitional, *weird* in a way that makes your sense of category want to abandon you.

And scattered through this — faintly, partially, like ruins of a dream — **structures**.

Not the plants' structures. Not the insects'. Something else built here, once, or is building here still, at the margins.

They are small. Platforms, mostly — frameworks of bent and lashed plant-stalks, arranged in geometric patterns that the surrounding growth does not naturally produce. Flat surfaces. Alcoves. One of them, near where you stand, has the remains of a fire-pit — scorched stone, ash now long since colonized by pale fungal growth. Another, further down, closer to the basin floor, has what looks like a *wall*, a deliberate barrier of stacked fiber-plates, half-consumed by the living substrate around it. Whoever made these was not sophisticated. They used the materials at hand — plant-stalk, resin-sap, heated stone. Simple fabrication. The kind of craft that emerges when hands learn to shape what is already there.

There are no makers here now. Or there are, and you have not seen them yet, and they are small, and they are hiding in the galleries, and they are listening to the sound along with you, and they are deciding what you are.

---

From the lip where you stand, the basin spreads before you like an inverted landscape — a valley of living tissue, warm and thrumming and loud, *so loud*, the sound pressing against you from every angle, the air alive with particulate and pheromone and heat-shimmer, the pillars rising from the floor like the legs of something impossibly large lying down to sleep, the ceiling above rippling with slow-motion collapse and regeneration, the striders moving across it in their silent moments like shadows that forgot what cast them.

The light is dim but present — your own small source, the faint glow of metabolic activity in the walls, the distant coin of sky impossibly far above. It is enough. You can see that the basin continues past its visible edge, that other passages branch off into darkness, that the scale of this extends into something you cannot track. Valley-sized, at least. Perhaps larger. The sound suggests larger — the way echoes return from distances that imply corridors and chambers and other basins beyond this one, all of them breathing, all of them full.

You are below the surface of the world, and the surface of the world is below *you*, and what is above you and beneath you and around you in every direction is **alive**, and **loud**, and **changing**, and it does not need you, and it does not care about you, and it has its own grammar and its own weather and its own catastrophes, and it is not finished, and it will never be finished, and the sound —

the sound —

the sound is the sound of something being *built*.
