# The Chamber of Last Insects

---

You find it by accident — or perhaps the finding was always part of the place, because the place has never changed and so it has always contained the fact of your finding it.

A vertical fissure in limestone, barely wide enough for your shoulders, descends eleven meters through cool grey rock that weeps in slow beads. The moisture doesn't pool. It gathers, quivers on the surface of things, and is drunk. Then it gathers again. This cycle has no clock. It is simply the rhythm of the place, which is not a rhythm at all but a state.

At the bottom, the fissure opens — not into a cavern, not into anything so generous with its space. It opens into a *pocket*. A hollow roughly two meters across and not quite tall enough to stand in, shaped like the inside of a fist. You must crouch. You must become small, because the place was made for smallness, and it does not accommodate anything else.

The walls are raw. Unfinished. They have the texture of something caught in the act of becoming geological — calcium deposits in mid-formation, mineral crusts that haven't decided whether they're stalactite or merely wall. Some surfaces are rough as unglazed ceramic. Others are preternaturally smooth, almost organic, as though the rock briefly considered becoming bone. Veins of something milky and translucent run through the darker limestone, and where they surface, the stone is thin enough that a faint glow passes through from — behind? beneath? It is not clear where the light originates. This is the first strangeness.

The light. It should be absolute darkness here. Eleven meters of unbroken stone above, no fissure wide enough to admit a coin of sky. And yet the chamber is *bright* — not with the industrial insistence of artificial light, but with a saturated, directional brightness, as if every surface has opinions about luminance. The milky veins in the rock glow a pale bluish-white. But most of the light comes from the creatures.

---

The fauna. There is so much of it.

The walls are not visible as walls. They are occupied. Every surface — every square centimeter of rock face, every mineral shelf, every crease and pockmark — hosts something alive. You cannot see the geology for the organisms. It is the density of a coral reef, the saturation of a tide pool at low tide, but compressed into a space smaller than a bathroom.

There are insects. Not insects as you know them — not entirely. These are small, most no longer than your thumbnail, and they carry their own light. Bioluminescent beetles, perhaps a dozen species, their abdomens glowing in shifts of pale blue, faint amber, and a green so saturated it seems to vibrate. They cling to the walls in clusters, and their light is not incidental. It is *organized*. They seem to have positions, posts, territories — each cluster illuminating its own small region of wall in a way that, taken together, produces the chamber's general brightness. They do not move. They have not moved. They are performing light, and they have always been performing it.

Between the beetles: mites. Hundreds of thousands of mites. They move, but only slightly — a slow, granular drift across the rock surface, like living sediment. They are pale, nearly translucent, and they eat the condensation and whatever microscopic film grows on the damp stone. They are the reason the moisture never pools.

On the ceiling — and you must crane your neck to see this, pressing your head against the opposite wall — there are moths. Small, white, paper-thin. Their wings are folded and they hang in rows so dense they resemble a second surface, a living stucco over the rock. Occasionally — though "occasionally" may be the wrong word, because time does not pass here in the usual way — one opens its wings, and the underside glows a deep, arterial red. This light is different from the beetles'. It pulses once, twice, and then the wings close. The moth settles. The red lingers in your vision.

Something moves in the corner of your eye. You turn. It has already stopped. It was always stopped.

---

The sound.

You expected silence. You expected the hush of deep stone. Instead, the chamber is *loud*, and it is loud in a way that takes you a moment to parse, because the sound is not mechanical, not musical, not environmental in any category you can reach for.

It is a dense, layered drone. The beetles hum — each species at its own frequency, and since the chamber is so small and the walls so close, the hum doesn't dissipate. It reflects. It folds on itself. Standing waves form in the air between the walls, and you can feel them in your sternum, in your sinuses. The lowest frequency is almost subsonic, a pressure more than a sound. Above it, higher tones stack and interweave. The mites contribute a faint, continuous hiss — the friction of their countless legs on stone, like sand poured on paper. And the moths, when they pulse their wings open, produce a single sharp *tick* — a percussive crack, like a knuckle on wood — that punctuates the drone at irregular intervals. Not random intervals. *Specific* intervals. The moths are keeping time with something, but there is no clock to keep time with. There is only the chamber itself, which does not change, which has no tempo, because it has always been exactly this.

The sound is the loudest thing about the place. Louder than the light, louder than the creatures, louder than the strange. You will carry it in your hearing for days afterward — that dense, tuned, living hum, as if the rock itself were a throat and the insects were its voice, speaking one long, unbroken word in a language made of frequency.

---

There is vegetation. Sparse, but present.

Patches of moss on the walls — not where the beetles are, but in the gaps between clusters, in the margins where the light is weaker. The moss is dark, almost black, and it grows in patterns that look like writing. This is pareidolia. Probably. The moss draws nutrients from the condensation and from whatever the mites leave behind. It does not spread. It has always been exactly this much moss, in exactly these shapes, in exactly these locations. If you came back in a year — in ten years, in a thousand — it would be the same moss.

In one corner, a single fungal fruiting body emerges from a crack in the rock. It is pale, translucent, about the size of your thumb, shaped like a tiny, perfect trumpet. It does not glow, but it catches the bioluminescent light and refracts it, scattering tiny rainbows across the adjacent wall. It has been fruiting for — how long? It cannot answer. It has always been fruiting. It will always be fruiting. It is not alive in the way that implies death. It is alive in the way that the chamber is alive: as a permanent condition.

---

The scattered marks.

On the southern wall — or what you have decided to call southern, facing the widest section of the pocket — there are scratches. Not natural. Not geological. Someone, something, at some time, dragged a point across this surface with intent. Five parallel lines, each about four centimeters long, evenly spaced. They are shallow. They are old. The limestone has not healed over them, because the limestone is embryonic, unfinished, still becoming — and so the scratches persist in the raw, young rock as though they were made yesterday. But they were not made yesterday. They predate the beetles, the mites, the moths, the moss, the fungal trumpet. They predate the equilibrium. They are the only thing in the chamber that was *made*, and their maker is gone, and they mean nothing legible.

Elsewhere: a small, flat stone, roughly oval, sitting on a natural shelf. It does not belong to the limestone. It is darker, denser, a piece of basalt or something like it, placed deliberately in a location that serves no geological purpose. It has been there so long that mites have colonized its surface, and beetles have taken positions along its edges, and it is now part of the chamber's ecology. But it was placed. You can feel the *placedness* of it. It is an artifact reduced to its most minimal expression — a single stone, moved once, long ago, by something that understood placement.

These are the only built things. They are scattered. They mean nothing. They change nothing. The equilibrium has absorbed them entirely.

---

And the equilibrium.

This is what is most strange — stranger than the light-from-nowhere, stranger than the moths' red pulse, stranger than the scratches that mean nothing. The equilibrium is total. Nothing in this chamber changes. The beetles glow in the same positions. The mites drift in the same patterns. The moths open their wings on the same — *schedule* is wrong — in the same *way*. The condensation gathers and is consumed in the same rhythm. The hum is the same hum. The fungal trumpet is the same trumpet. The scratches are the same scratches.

You have the sensation — not of entering a place that is frozen, but of entering a place that has achieved something. Equilibrium is not stasis here. It is *completion*. The chamber has arrived at its final state, its maximum state, the exact and total expression of what it is. There is no gradient to drive change, no imbalance to resolve, no gradient in temperature, no shift in moisture, no competition between species, no succession, no decay, no growth. Every force is matched by its equal. Every energy is accounted for. The system is closed and full and has been closed and full for longer than the limestone above you has been limestone.

This is why it is loud. The sound is the sound of a place that has no reason to be quiet, no future in which to reserve its energy, no past from which to recover. It is spending nothing. It is simply *being*, at full volume, at full brightness, at full density, forever.

---

You crouch in the entrance of this pocket, and the drone fills your body, and the light makes your eyes ache with its strange insistence, and the beetles stare at you with compound eyes that have stared at everything that has ever descended that fissure, which is nothing, because no one has descended that fissure, because if they had, something would have changed, and nothing has changed.

You are the first disturbance. You are the first gradient. You are the first thing in this chamber that is not in equilibrium.

The moths open their wings. The red pulses once. Twice.

They are watching you with the patience of a place that has never needed to wait for anything, because everything it needs is already here, and has always been here, and will always be here.

You back carefully out of the fissure. Above you, the limestone weeps. Below you, the chamber hums its one long word.

It does not notice you leave. It did not notice you arrive.

It has never needed to notice anything at all.
