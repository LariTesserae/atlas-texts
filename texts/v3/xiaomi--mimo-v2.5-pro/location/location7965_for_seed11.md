# The Worn Mountains of the Breathing Plain

---

You hear them before you see them. Not a roar exactly — a *sighing*, a constant exhalation so deep and enormous it becomes silence inverted, becomes pressure against the eardrums. The sound never stops. It comes from everywhere. By the second day, you stop noticing it. By the third, you realize you've been shouting at your companions for hours.

Stand anywhere on the plain and look. The horizon is not flat. It billows — a slow, heavy undulation of land so ancient it has forgotten it was once mountains.

These were mountains once. The oldest surfaces on the continent. Two billion years of rain, wind, root-work, and chemical patience have taken what were once Himalaya-scale ranges and ground them into something else entirely: broad, flat-topped plateaus rising a thousand meters from the surrounding land, their edges softened to curves, their faces streaked with mineral stains — rust, sulfur-yellow, the dark purplish black of manganese leaching through quartzite. They look less like mountains than like enormous teeth worn to the gumline. Some have collapsed entirely into fields of broken stone that the jungle has half-swallowed. Others still stand, but barely — their summits so eroded they've become shallow bowls that catch rainwater and fog and refuse to give them back.

The plain between them stretches enormously. Kilometers. Tens of kilometers. The scale is wrong for walking. Distances that look like an afternoon's hike become days. And the plain itself is not empty — that's the misleading thing. It is *lush*. But the lushness is spread so impossibly thin that the effect is one of vacancy.

---

## The Plants

What grows here grows enormous but alone.

Individual trees reach forty, fifty meters. Their trunks are pale, almost white, the bark peeling in long papery strips. The canopies are broad and flat-topped, mimicking the mesa forms behind them as if the geology were a script the biology is slowly reading aloud. Between any two of these great trees there may be a hundred meters of low ground cover — ferns, mosses, the creeping violet-leafed something whose name no one has written down. Close to the soil, the world is dense and tangled, a mat of root and frond and fungal thread. Above head height, there is almost nothing. The trees do not cluster. They stand in isolation, each one occupying its own invisible territory, their roots reaching laterally for enormous distances beneath the soil, communicating through chemical signals so slow they might as well be geological.

There are also the towers.

These are not trees. They are columns of living vegetation — a single species, or perhaps a symbiotic lattice of several — that grow straight up to fifteen or twenty meters with almost no branching. They are pale green, segmented, hollow. Wind passes through them and they *sing*. This is much of the sound. The towers grow in loose clusters across the plain, and at certain times of day, when the warm air rises off the ground and the pressure differentials shift, the sound they produce is not musical. It is massive. A pipe organ the size of a city, tuned to frequencies the human ear experiences as vibration in the chest rather than pitch in the head.

The towers are always growing. They are also always falling. Their segments are brittle. They collapse without warning, and where they fall, new shoots emerge within days from the debris. The floor of any tower-cluster is a chaos of fallen cylinders, rotting husks, and fresh green growth pushing up between them. Walk through one and the sound changes with every step — shifts in register, hiccups, sudden silence in a pocket where a tower has fallen and left a gap, then a wall of noise again as you enter the next column of growth.

## Water

There is not enough of it, and there is too much.

The dampness here is ambient, not visible. The air holds moisture the way a sponge holds water — everywhere, always, just this side of rain. Fog forms in the early mornings and sits in the valleys between the plateaus, not moving, thick enough to lose direction in. By midday the sun burns it off and the air becomes warm, heavy, still humid but without the gift of actual droplets. Thirst is a constant, low-grade companion.

The water that does exist is found in three forms:

**The bowls.** Shallow depressions on top of the eroded mesas where rainwater collects in pools a few meters across, stained brown with tannins from the vegetation growing at their margins. These pools support a rich micro-world — tiny fish with no eyes, water-beetles with iridescent shells, a species of pitcher-plant that has evolved to grow half-submerged. They are pockets of intense life in a surrounding dryness.

**The seeps.** At the base of many mesas, water weeps through the stone in slow, persistent trickles. It emerges mineral-heavy, leaving bright orange and white deposits on the rock faces. These seeps support mats of cyanobacteria and mosses so thick they are nearly structures — green-black curtains hanging from the cliff faces, steaming faintly in the warmth.

**The rivers.** There are two or three. They are wide and slow and shallow, braided across the plain in channels that shift constantly. The dynamic quality of the landscape shows here most clearly — a river that was in one place last season may have moved kilometers. The banks are not banks in the usual sense; they are vague transitions from wet sand to dry sand to low scrub to the open plain. The rivers carry the sediment of the eroding mesas downstream. They are, in a sense, the mesas' future selves. They are warm, and they smell of iron.

## The Animals

They are rare, and they are not what you expect.

The large grazers are gone — if they were ever here. What remains are specialists. A species of flightless bird, knee-height, dull-feathered, that moves in small flocks across the plain picking insects from the low vegetation. They are fast and silent and they watch you with an attention that feels analytical. They are the most common animal you will see, and even they are sparse — a flock every few kilometers, then nothing for hours.

In the tower-clusters, something lives that you hear but rarely see. A clicking, skittering thing — an insect, probably, though large. It navigates by the sound the towers make, or perhaps *through* the sound, riding specific frequencies as a bat rides echolocation. You hear it sometimes when the wind shifts and the towers fall quiet: a rapid, arrhythmic tapping moving through the hollow columns above you.

In the seeps and the mesa-bowl pools, there are amphibians. Small, dark, with a skin that shifts color over the course of a day — pale in the morning heat, nearly black by dusk. They eat the eyeless fish. They have no calls. They are the quietest things in this loud landscape, and finding one feels like finding a secret.

There are no predators. Or rather — there is something in the tower-clusters that occasionally takes one of the flightless birds. You find feathers in a small circle, arranged as if the bird had sat down and simply come apart. Whatever does this is never seen. It may not be an animal at all. The vegetation here is aggressive enough, strange enough, that no one is entirely sure.

## The People

They were here. They are not here now, or they are here in a diminished way — hard to tell which.

The structures are few and old. Stone circles on some of the mesa tops, arranged with a precision that suggests intent but whose purpose is not clear. The stones are local quartzite, shaped with simple tools — you can see the percussion marks, the patient flaking. The circles are open to the sky. They have no roofs, no hearths, no signs of habitation. Some contain a single upright stone. Others contain depressions in the ground that might be graves or might be something else entirely.

At the base of certain mesas, there are caves — not natural ones, or not entirely. Someone widened existing fissures in the rock, perhaps over generations. Inside, the walls are covered in marks. Not paintings, not exactly. Score-lines. Hundreds of them, overlapping, in no pattern that repeats. They cover the walls and the ceiling. The oldest are weathered to nearly nothing. The newest — if you can call them that — are sharp-edged, clearly more recent. Someone, or some intermittent tradition, has been making these marks for a very long time. The caves smell of damp stone and the faint mineral tang of the seeps that run through them.

There is no trash. No middens, no potsherds, no evidence of sustained settlement. Whoever was here moved through — or lived so lightly that the land absorbed their presence back into its own processes. The erosion that took the mountains is patient enough to take a people too.

## The Turbulence

The landscape is not stable. It has never been stable, despite its age — or rather, its age is *evidence* of its instability. It has been falling apart for two billion years and it is still falling apart and it will be falling apart for two billion more.

You can see this most clearly during the afternoon, when the heat builds and the plain begins to convect. The air rises in columns. The towers sing louder, then fall silent as the pressure drops, then sing again as new air rushes in. Small dust-devils form and collapse. The fog that condensed in the morning is pulled upward and recondensed into towering cumulus clouds that build over the mesas and then break apart without raining, or rain briefly and violently in a single location while a kilometer away the ground is bone-dry.

The mesas themselves are in motion. Not fast — but faster than you'd think for something that looks so permanent. Rockfalls happen weekly, great shelves of quartzite peeling away from cliff faces and crashing into the talus slopes below, raising dust that hangs in the still air for hours. New seeps appear. Old ones dry up. The rivers shift. The tower-clusters migrate across the plain over the course of years, following some pattern of soil chemistry or water table or something no one has studied.

It is, in the most literal sense, an unfinished place. The erosion that created it is not a past event. It is ongoing. You are standing in the middle of a process. The landscape is not a noun here. It is a verb. It is *eroding*, actively, audibly, with a sound like the world breathing, and the lushness of the vegetation is not a contradiction of this but a *response* to it — life filling the spaces that stone leaves behind as it falls.

---

## At Night

The sound does not stop. It deepens. The temperature drops just enough for condensation to form on every surface — the leaves, the pale bark, your skin. The towers hum in lower registers. The stars are visible above the plain but not above the mesas, which are wrapped in their own private clouds, generated by the moisture trapped in their summit-bowls. Lightning flickers silently inside these clouds — not storms, just static discharge, the mesa-tops playing out some electrical argument with the atmosphere.

The flightless birds roost in the low branches of the great trees. You can hear them shifting in the dark. The clicking thing in the towers becomes louder. The seeps glow faintly — the cyanobacteria, perhaps, or some mineral luminescence that no one has catalogued.

The landscape breathes. The landscape has always been breathing. You are a brief interruption in a very long exhalation.
