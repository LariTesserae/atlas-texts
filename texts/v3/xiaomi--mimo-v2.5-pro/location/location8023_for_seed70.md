# The Drowned Spines

---

The mountains come out of the water like teeth.

Not many of them — not anymore. What remains above the waterline are the tips of a vast and ancient cordillera, a range that must have once commanded the horizon for hundreds of kilometers. Now only the highest spines persist: raw, angular, still shocked-looking from whatever recent upheaval brought the water to their throats. The rock is pale — a milky feldspar-grey veined with iron-orange — and it is sharp in a way that suggests it has never known wind erosion at this altitude. Every ridge is a blade. Every fracture face is a clean break, the kind of geometry that belongs to the inside of the earth, not its surface. Where the stone meets the water, it does not slope gently. It drops. Plunges. The transition from visible peak to invisible abyss is often no more than a few meters of undercut shelf, waterlogged and raw.

The water is warm. You feel it in the air before you feel it on your skin — a heavy, mineral-laden humidity that hangs in a permanent haze above the surface. It is not the salt-brightness of ocean. It is something older, something that has sat in contact with basalt and sulfur for a very long time. There is a faint bitterness to it, a faint sweetness beneath that, like dissolved lime and something almost vegetal. The surface of the water, seen from any of the peaks, is a pale jade-green, almost opaque, catching the sun in a diffused, even glow that makes it impossible to tell depth by color alone. It could be ten meters deep beneath you. It could be a thousand. The bathymetry is unknown — these peaks rise from slopes that plunge into a murk no one has mapped.

No one, because there is no one here.

---

And yet there is *so much life*.

The vegetation is extraordinary. In the time since these summits emerged — and it cannot have been long, given the freshness of the rock, the absence of soil in most places — something has colonized them with a speed and tenacity that borders on the aggressive. Every horizontal surface, every crack wider than a finger, every ledge where sediment has pooled for even a few centimeters: there is green. Not the tentative green of pioneer lichen. The green is *lush*, layered, riotous, and strange.

The lowest zones, the waterline itself, are furred with mats of something that is not quite seaweed and not quite moss — a rubbery, dark-emerald growth that clings to the submerged rock and extends a few centimeters above the waterline in a constantly damp fringe. It is stippled with tiny air bladders, and when the water is very still, you can hear them faintly ticking as they contract in the heat. Above this belt, the stone erupts in ferns. But not ferns as you know them. These are thick-stemmed, rubbery, with fronds that uncurl in tight spirals and hang downward like the arms of something sleeping. They are pale green where new, deepening to near-black where old, and they grow in overlapping curtains over every cliff face, so that from a distance the rock appears to be draped in fabric.

Higher still — and "higher" means perhaps only forty or fifty vertical meters above the waterline on the tallest remaining peaks — the ferns give way to something woody. Small trees, if they can be called that. Their trunks are sinuous, almost vine-like, gripping the bare rock with visible root-arms that splay across the stone like fingers. They are leafless in any conventional sense; instead, their branches terminate in flat, photosynthetic plates, like the pads of a cactus crossed with the frond of a palm. They are a pale, silvery green, and they rustle in the faintest breeze — but there is rarely breeze here. The air is heavy and slow and mostly still. So they are mostly still, too, standing in their dense little groves on the sharpest ridgelines, rooted into nothing but cracks and determination, and they are silent.

The density of it all is remarkable. There is no bare rock that stays bare for long. Every surface is being eaten by growth, and the growth is layered — the fern curtains hang over the mat, the woody things root through and above the ferns, and between everything there are threads, epiphytes, clinging things with no name you know, filling every interstice. The air smells of green decay and mineral water and warm stone. It smells of something fermenting gently in a place where nothing has ever fermented before.

---

The quiet is the first thing that truly disorients.

You expect, in a landscape this lush, this warm, this saturated with moisture and organic matter — you expect noise. Insect hum. Birdcall. The crash and babble of moving water. But there is almost none of this. The water, everywhere, is still. There are no rivers feeding this sea from above; there is no "above" that isn't also submerged. The surface of the water barely moves, ruffled only by thermal currents so faint they register as a slight glassy texture change rather than waves. The ferns don't rustle. The woody things don't creak. There is wind, but it comes in long, slow exhalations — warm air expanding and contracting over the course of hours as the sun tracks across the sky — and it is barely enough to move a frond.

There are sounds, but they are small and strange. The ticking of the air-bladder mats. A faint, irregular dripping where water seeps through the fern curtains and falls back into the greater body below. Occasionally — perhaps twice an hour — a sound from the water itself: a low, resonant *blup*, as if something vast and soft had displaced a bubble somewhere in the murk. You never see the source. The water's opacity swallows it. But the sound carries a long way in the stillness, bouncing between the peaks, and for a moment every surface seems to listen.

Fauna is nearly absent. There are no birds — nowhere to have migrated from, perhaps, or nothing to eat if they arrived. There are small things in the water: glimpsed as shadows, as brief disruptions on the jade surface, as shapes that might be fish or might be something else, moving with a slow deliberateness that suggests cold-bloodedness or patience or both. On the rock, in the fern curtains, there are insects — but few, and slow, and oddly shaped: too many legs, or legs in the wrong places, or wing-cases that catch the light with an iridescence that doesn't belong to any order you can name. They seem to be doing very little. Eating, perhaps. Sitting in the warm damp and letting time pass.

---

There is something wrong with the light, though it takes a while to notice.

It is daylight. The sun is present and bright and tracks across the sky in a normal arc. Shadows fall as they should. But the quality of the illumination is *thick* — saturated, heavy, as if the air itself is slightly luminous rather than merely transmitting light from the source. The haze of evaporating water creates a permanent soft-focus at middle distances. Peaks that are perhaps two kilometers away appear dreamlike, their details smudged into suggestion. And the light has a faint greenish cast, reflected up from the water and filtered through the haze, so that even the white rock appears tinged with algae-colored shadow. At midday, when the sun is directly overhead and the water acts as a mirror, the effect is almost narcotic: a warm, green-gold, evenly diffused glow that erases contrast and flattens depth perception until you feel you are standing inside a pearl.

At this light, the strangeness becomes most apparent. The way the fern fronds uncurl not toward the sun but *away* from it, angling always toward the water. The way the woody trees' photosynthetic plates orient not upward but laterally, as if harvesting light from the side, from the reflected glow off the sea. The way the growth is densest not at the summits but at the waterline, as if the peaks are being consumed from below, as if the vegetation is the leading edge of something that is slowly, patiently, walking these mountains back down into the water.

Nothing moves fast enough to watch. The dynamic is geological, not biological — or rather, the biology here has adopted a geological pace. The fern curtains lengthen by centimeters per season. The woody things add a ring per decade, perhaps. The waterline, if it is rising or falling, does so across timescales that the rock has barely begun to register. Everything here is committed to patience. Everything here is in the early stages of something that will take ten thousand years to finish.

---

Standing on the highest remaining spine — a narrow arête no wider than your outstretched arms, ferns hanging from both sides like green wings, the jade sea stretching in every direction to the haze-horizon — you have the sense of a world that was once something else entirely and is slowly, without urgency, becoming something new. The rock remembers being inside a mountain. The water remembers being ice, or cloud, or something older still. The plants are the first arrivals at a party that hasn't started yet, arranging themselves with quiet confidence across an empty stage.

There is no ruin here. No artifact. No memory of hands. Only the raw architecture of uplift and the green, patient, silent colonizers climbing it, one crack at a time, while the warm water holds everything else in its mouth and waits.
