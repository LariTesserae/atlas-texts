# The Forge-Continental

## Submersion

There is no seafloor. There hasn't been one for a long time.

You are inside a structure so large that it has its own weather — not atmospheric weather, but hydraulic weather. Pressure differentials between sectors form slow, continent-wide currents that circle through corridors hundreds of meters wide, carrying silt that has no geological origin, only industrial: iron particulate, calcium carbonate precipitates from desalination plants, the fine ground dust of composite polymers. These currents have names. They have seasons.

The water is cold — 3.2°C, nearly uniform everywhere except near the thermal outflow shafts where it climbs briefly to something almost tepid. You would not survive here unassisted for more than minutes. The structure does not care. It was not built for your species.

## Architecture of Absence

The geometry is the first thing that exhausts you.

Corridors open into chambers that open into void-spaces so large your eyes cannot resolve the far wall. The ceilings — if they are ceilings, if that word retains meaning at this scale — disappear into a murk that is not darkness exactly but distance. Floodlights on gantries provide illumination in pools and swathes, overlapping, casting multiple shadows. The light is white or pale industrial yellow. It flickers in some sectors where the electrical grid is older or under-maintained. In others it is steady and flat and total, washing every surface in a glare that makes the water itself seem like air, until you notice the way your arms move too slowly, the way sound arrives thick and late.

Everything you can touch is made. The walls are poured composite, ribbed with structural I-beams of high-carbon steel. The floors are plating — bolted, welded, sometimes grouted with sealant that has been applied hastily and shows tool-marks. There are no natural formations anywhere. No rock. No sedimentary layers. If you drilled through the floor, you would find more structure: subfloors, utility channels, lower chambers, and then water again, and then more structure. The whole thing floats in its own medium and is its own medium. It is a habitat the size of a tectonic plate, and it has no outside.

That's not quite true. It has walls — outer walls — the outermost hull, where the deep ocean presses in with its four hundred atmospheres. But these walls are so far from where you are now, so many kilometers of constructed interior between you and the crush, that they are more theoretical than real. No one goes to the outer hull. There's nothing to see. Just more water on the other side, and it looks exactly like the water on this side.

## The Hollow

The density parameter is low because most of the volume is empty space.

This is not a failure of planning. It is the plan. The structure breathes — it must, to manage pressure, to cycle water, to prevent stagnation. The great chambers serve as reservoirs, as buffer volumes, as the lungs of a continental organism. You stand on a gantry inside Chamber 14-East (there are many numbering systems, none of them comprehensive) and look down into a column of water that drops two hundred meters to a floor you cannot see. Above you, the same distance to a ceiling that is really just the underside of another chamber's floor. The water between is almost empty. A few fish. A loose chain of industrial lights descending into murk. The silence of volume — though not actual silence, never that.

Most of the structure is like this. Grand, hollow, echoing. Built at a scale that assumed more inhabitants than ever arrived, or that lost them, or that never intended to fill every space, only to have the spaces available should they be needed. The corridors between chambers are narrower but still generous — wide enough for submersible vehicles, for cargo haulers with their floodlights and diesel-scented exhaust bubbles. The walls here are lined with pipes, conduits, bundled cabling in waterproof sheaths. Emergency lighting in red. Directional signage in a language that has evolved its own abbreviations and shorthand, functional and blunt.

## Industry

The soundscape is the second thing that exhausts you.

It is loud. It is loud the way a factory floor is loud, multiplied by the fact that water transmits sound four times faster and with far less attenuation than air. The low-frequency hum of the main generators travels everywhere — you feel it in your teeth, in the bones of your jaw, a vibration so constant it becomes a texture more than a sound. On top of that: the chugging pulse of hydraulic pumps, the high whine of turbine bearings, the arrhythmic clang of metal-on-metal from maintenance crews working hull sections, the hiss and bubble of welding rigs, the distant thunder of something enormous being pressurized or depressurized somewhere you cannot see.

Fish navigate this soundscape better than you do. They have lateral lines; they feel the pressure waves. You have only ears, and they ache.

The industrial capacity is real and operating. This is not a ruin. Conveyor systems carry mineral substrate — harvested from seabed deposits beyond the hull, or precipitated from the water itself through electrolytic plants — through the lower chambers to fabrication bays where it is smelted, extruded, rolled, and stacked. The heat from these processes creates localized warm currents that attract fish in spiraling clouds. Some of the fabrication bays have been operating long enough that the water inside them has a permanent metallic taste. Workers — and there are workers, though they are sparse relative to the volume — wear rebreather rigs and thick thermal suits. They communicate by short-range acoustic transponder. They do not stay long in any one sector. They rotate in and out on schedules dictated by decompression protocols.

The output of all this industry is the structure itself. It builds more of itself. The fabrication bays produce plating, beams, sealant, fasteners, lights, pipes, and all of it feeds back into the structure's own expansion. Growth is slow — meters per year at the margins — but it is steady and it does not stop. There is no blueprint for the final form. The structure grows because the machines are running and the workers maintain the machines and the machines produce materials and the materials become structure. The logic is circular and sufficient.

## Ecology, Sparse and Specified

Vegetation is present but vestigial. Algae farms occupy certain designated chambers — long, shallow trays under grow-lights, tended by automated skimmers that harvest and compress the algal biomass into protein blocks. The water in these chambers is greener, warmer, faintly sweet-smelling where it meets the corridors. The algae is the base of the only intentional food chain. Everything else is import or synthesis.

But life finds the structure unbidden, too.

Fish entered through the open construction margins decades ago — small species first, then larger ones following the food chain up. There are populations now that are essentially feral residents: schools of jack mackerel occupying the upper volumes of the great chambers, anglerfish in the deep unlit corners where the floodlights don't reach, eels that have colonized the pipe-works and become a persistent maintenance problem, chewing through cable sheaths. Crabs clatter along the floors of the lower chambers, feeding on biofilm that grows on every surface — a thin, rust-colored film of bacterial colonies that the cleaning drones struggle to keep pace with.

The fauna is abundant relative to what you might expect in a fully artificial environment, because the environment is so vast and so old and so wet. You are in an ocean. The ocean brought its children in with it, and they have stayed.

Some of them are large. In the deepest, oldest chambers — the ones near the original construction core, now mostly decommissioned and poorly lit — there are things that have grown large in the darkness. You hear stories. The acoustic profiles of those chambers show signatures that don't match any catalogued species. But this is not a weird place. The stories are just stories. Large fish in dark water. That is all.

## Active Systems

The structure does not sit still.

Currents cycle through on schedules that overlap and interfere, creating patterns that shift over weeks and months — a kind of hydraulic seasons. Storm-equivalents occur when pressure management fails in a sector and water rushes from high-pressure zones to low, creating surges that rock the gantries and scatter loose equipment. These are managed, eventually, by the pumping systems, but they are dangerous while they last and they come without much warning.

The electrical grid is a living thing too — generation capacity allocated and reallocated constantly as sectors activate or go dormant. Brownouts are common in the periphery. The core, near the main generators, never loses power. The lights there are always on. The hum there is so deep and so constant it is less a sound than a state of being.

Construction crews move through on schedules, building, repairing, expanding. Cargo submersibles — blunt, windowless, mechanical — ply the corridors on automated routes, running lights in red and white, engines throbbing. Maintenance drones crawl the walls and ceilings, their little motors a high chittering against the bass rumble of the structure. Fish part around all of this, unbothered, a living ambient layer drifting through the manufactured world like weather.

## What It Feels Like

It feels like standing inside a ship that is also a city that is also the only world. The water is cold and close and everywhere, pressing against your suit, tasting of metal and brine. The lights above you stretch away into distances that the eye refuses to compute. The sound is enormous and layered and never, ever stops. The walls around you are new — bolt-heads still sharp, sealant still tacky in places, no rust yet, no age, just the bright hard edge of something recently made and not yet worn in by time.

It feels empty in the way that a cathedral is empty: the emptiness is the point. The space is the product. Vastness manufactured at industrial scale, maintained by a sparse population of workers who move through it like caretakers of a place too large to know fully, who name what they can and navigate the rest by acoustic map and habit.

It feels, above all, normal. This is not a strange place to the people who live here. This is where they live. The cold water, the industrial hum, the fish in the corridors, the darkness beyond the lights — all of it is simply the texture of the world. They do not find it remarkable. They find it loud, and cold, and sometimes boring, and sometimes frightening in the way that large empty spaces are frightening, and sometimes beautiful when the light catches a school of mackerel turning in unison a hundred meters above you and the whole chamber fills with silver-flash and shadow.

They would not describe it the way you would. They would say: *it's fine. it's home. watch out for the eels in sector 9.*
