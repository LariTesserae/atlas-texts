# The Spicule Cathedral

You are inside a glass sponge.

Not *a* glass sponge — a colony so old and so vast that the word "colony" fails. Millennia of siliceous spicules have fused, stacked, branched, and fused again until the living reef and the dead skeleton became indistinguishable, and the whole structure closed over on itself like a fist slowly clenching shut in the black water of the polar deep. What you are standing in — if standing is the word for the slow drift of a body in near-freezing saltwater — is a cavity inside that fist. A room in a city no one designed.

---

**The walls** are pale, almost white, with a faintly vitreous quality — glass, after all, is what sponges are made of, needle by needle, spicule by silica spicule, each one microscopic, each one an architectural decision made by an organism with no brain. Up close the surface looks like frost crystals under magnification: rosettes, hexactines, six-pointed stars fused at their tips into lattices so fine they resemble lace. But the lace is load-bearing. The lace is *structure*. Where the lattice has thickened over centuries, it becomes translucent shelving, buttresses, the ribs of vaulted ceilings that curve away into the dimness above.

The walls breathe. Not metaphorically. You can feel it: a faint, pulsing draw of water through the lattice as living sponges deeper in the structure pump water through their bodies, filtering bacteria and marine snow from the dark current. There is no visible motion, but the pressure shifts in your ears — a slow systole, diastole, the whole city inhaling and exhaling on a cycle of maybe forty seconds. This is the pulse that moves everything: nutrients, larvae, oxygen, the faint chemical whispers that pass between organisms for signaling.

---

**The light** is not light, exactly. It is the *memory* of light, the bioluminescent trace of creatures that evolved in a world where the sun is a rumor from above. Here and there along the walls, clusters of *Euphausia* — small shrimp-like crustaceans, no longer than your thumbnail — cling to the spicule lattice and emit a cold blue glow from photophores along their ventral surfaces. The glow is fitful, triggered by the water-pressure shifts of the sponge breathing, and it catches the glass walls at angles that make the whole cavity flicker. Not bright. Never bright. But enough to see that the room you are in is not empty.

It is, in fact, astonishingly full.

---

**The density** of life here is the thing that surprises you most. You have been told the ocean is vast and empty. You have been lied to, or you were told about the wrong ocean. In this cavity — roughly the size of a small chapel, maybe four meters across at its widest, with a ceiling that vaults to perhaps three meters — there are *hundreds* of species and tens of thousands of individuals, and they are everywhere.

On the walls: encrusting organisms layered centimeters deep. Tube-dwelling polychaete worms build their calcium-carbonate chimneys in close-packed colonies, each chimney no wider than a drinking straw, but together they form a textured surface like terraced farmland seen from the air. Between the worm chimneys, bryozoans — colonial animals that look like frost or like tiny coral fans — spread in overlapping sheets, each colony a different species with a different lattice geometry. Hydroids wave their feeding tentacles in the gentle current. Tiny brittle stars, some barely a centimeter across, coil their arms around spicule branches and feed on whatever drifts past.

In the water column: more. Amphipods — flattened, hinged, shrimplike creatures — swim in loose aggregations near the ceiling, pausing to groom the spicule walls with their mouthparts, scraping off biofilm. Small snailfish, translucent and gelatinous, the largest maybe eight centimeters long, drift with their fins barely moving, conserving energy in the cold. Copepods — the insects of the sea, if insects were nearly invisible and lived everywhere — are so numerous that the water itself seems to have a faint grain, a texture, like looking through gauze.

Beneath your feet, or beneath wherever you are: a substrate of sponge detritus, fallen spicules, dead worm chimneys, compressed into a pale sediment that is itself alive. Nematodes thread through it. Tiny holothurians — sea cucumbers no bigger than beans — ingest it and excrete it, processing it endlessly.

Nothing here is alone. Everything is touching something else, or growing on something else, or eating something else, or hiding inside something else. The walls are organisms. The floor is organisms. The water is organisms. You are the largest thing here by several orders of magnitude, and you are, to the cavity's inhabitants, weather.

---

**The silence** is absolute and particular. In the deep ocean, sound travels enormous distances, but inside the sponge colony it is muffled, absorbed, scattered by the lattice walls into a kind of acoustic fog. You can hear your own breathing — the regulator hiss if you have one, the bubble-click if you don't — but it doesn't carry. Beyond the sound of your own body, there is nothing. No wave surge. No current roar. No vocalization. The fauna here communicates chemically, electrically, through touch. Sound is a phenomenon of the shallow world, the world of air and waves and fish with swim bladders that resonate. Down here, the medium is silence. Every movement you make is swallowed by the walls.

It should be peaceful. It is, instead, eerie. The silence feels *intentional* — the silence of a city at night where every resident is awake and watching but none of them make a sound.

---

**The strangeness** accumulates the longer you stay. Some of it is structural: the sponge walls are not uniform. In places, the lattice opens into passages that lead deeper into the colony — dark even by the standards of this dark place, visible only as voids where the bioluminescent shrimp don't go. Water moves through these passages. You can feel the pull. The colony is not sealed; it is porous, a labyrinth, a building with hundreds of doors that open into rooms that open into other rooms, extending in every direction for what might be meters or might be kilometers. You have no way of knowing. The geometry is not human. It was not designed for a body like yours to navigate, and the passages branch and rejoin in ways that resist mapping. You suspect, and the suspicion grows, that the cavity you are in is not special. It is one of thousands. Perhaps millions. And every one is this full.

Some of the strangeness is biological. In one corner of the cavity, a cluster of what appear to be glass sponges — the familiar *Euplectella* form, the Venus' flower basket — are growing, but they are growing *inward*, their lattice walls curving into the cavity space rather than outward into open water. They are building new rooms inside the room you're in. Partitioning the space. At the rate they grow — millimeters per year — this cavity will be subdivided into smaller chambers within a few decades, each one colonized by a slightly different community, each one developing its own microclimate of current and chemistry. The city is not static. The city is *growing around you*, and the architecture of the next century is being laid down now, spicule by spicule, in the dark.

Some of the strangeness is predatory. You notice, if you look carefully at the tube-worm chimneys, that not all of them are tubes. Some are the snares of bobbit worms — *Eunice* species, their bodies hidden deep in the spicule substrate, their five mandible-tipped feeding tentacles extending just above the surface, waiting. You notice that the brittle stars, when one of them moves, move *fast* — a sudden flailing release that carries them a few centimeters before they freeze again, and that this motion seems triggered by proximity to certain patches of wall where a pale anemone, barely visible against the pale spicules, has extended its sweep tentacles into the water column. The anemone's sting is mild. The brittle star knows this. It flees anyway, on principle. The game between predator and prey here is played at the scale of centimeters, in slow motion, in silence, and it has been playing for longer than human civilization has existed.

---

**The cold** settles in. 1.2 degrees Celsius, or thereabouts — above freezing because saltwater, but only just. You feel it through your suit if you have one, a deep and patient cold that does not bite but *seeps*, that suggests hypothermia not as an event but as a destination. The organisms here are adapted. Their body fluids are antifreeze-compound-rich. Their metabolisms are slow. The pace of everything is slow — the growth, the predation, the digestion, the reproduction. Except: the reproduction is not slow so much as *seasonal*, timed to pulses of organic matter sinking from the surface. When the pulse comes — a phytoplankton bloom dying above, its remains raining down through kilometers of water as marine snow — everything here spawns at once. Gametes in the water. The cavity, for a few hours, becomes a different place: turbid, crowded, electric with chemical signaling. Then the pulse passes, and the silence returns, and the slow city resumes its slow building.

---

**The rules**, if there are rules: nothing here is fast. Nothing here is loud. Nothing here is alone. Nothing here is finished. The sponge colony is a city under permanent construction by its inhabitants, many of whom are also the building materials. Organisms die and become substrate. Substrate is colonized. Colonization creates structure. Structure channels water. Water carries nutrients. Nutrients support organisms. The loop closes and opens and closes again, and has been closing and opening for twelve thousand years, which is how old this particular colony is believed to be, though no one is here to believe it.

You are visiting. You are tolerated. You are too large to be food for anything here and too temporary to be architecture, so you are simply *present*, a disturbance in the current, a shadow on the bioluminescent walls. The shrimp glow a little brighter where your body displaces water and triggers their pressure-sensors. Then you move on, and they dim again.

The cavity breathes. The city grows. The silence holds.
