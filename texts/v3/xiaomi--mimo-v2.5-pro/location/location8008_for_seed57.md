# The Pocket

You would not find this place. That is the first thing to understand. It exists in a crack — a fracture no wider than a shoulder in the basalt shelf that shelves down from somewhere you have already forgotten — and the water that fills it is not quite water. It holds you the way water does. It moves the way water does when nothing disturbs it, which is nearly always. But it has a texture against the skin that is closer to the memory of having once been held, and when you try to look through it, what you see on the other side is not what is there.

The space is small. Kneeling, you could touch all the walls. Standing is not possible — there is no standing here, only the slow rotation of a body that has surrendered orientation. The walls are young basalt, freshly torn open by whatever opened them — and that event is recent enough that the glassy faces still catch phantom light, still have edges that have not been rounded, flakes that have not yet been carried away. But *carried to where?* There is no current. There has never been a current. The water is patient in a way that suggests it has been patient for much longer than the rock has existed, which is one of the first things that will bother you if you stay long enough to notice.

The vegetation does not grow from the walls. It grows from the seams between walls — from the fractures within the fracture, the place where the crack branched and chose not to resolve. And it grows thick. Kelp would be the wrong word. Coral would be the wrong word. These are stalks of something that has a translucent, faintly blue-green luminescence — not enough to see by, but enough to know they are there when your eyes have finally surrendered to the dark and started inventing. They branch without any system you can identify. Some branches end in fans. Some end in spheres. Some end in nothing, as if the organism forgot what it was doing and simply stopped. They are packed so densely at the seams that you cannot see where they root, and if you try to push a hand into the thicket of them, they yield — they are soft, almost gelatinous — but they do not break, and your hand does not reach the rock behind.

There is no fauna. Or: there was fauna once, and what remains are the structures. Small tubes of calcium or something like calcium, fused to the basalt in scattered patches, each one hollow, each one the diameter of a reed. They are not organized. They are not nests or homes in any purposeful sense. They are simply *there*, the way barnacles are there, except that whatever made them is gone, and the tubes are open at both ends, and the water that passes through them — though there is no current — hums when you press your ear close. Not a sound, exactly. The absence of a sound that knows it's missing.

The darkness is complete and then it is not. This is the part that matters. The darkness is the baseline — true, absolute, the kind that fills the space the way a solid fills a mold — and then, at intervals you cannot time because time does not function here the way you need it to, the darkness *thins*. Not brightens. Thins. As if someone drew a single thread out of a black fabric and for one breath the weave was imperfect, and through the imperfection you saw — not light, but the *idea* of light, the shape light would take if it were allowed into this room. A suggestion of geometry. An angle that does not correspond to any wall. And then the weave closes and you are in the dark again, and you are left with the suspicion that what you saw was not on the other side of anything but was instead *inside* the dark itself, the way a dream is inside sleep.

The water is cool. Not cold — there is no thermocline here, no gradient, no source. The coolness is uniform, perpetual, the temperature of deep stone that has never known the sun and has had long enough to forget what warmth would even mean. It does not change. Over hours or what you experience as hours, the temperature does not change by a measurable degree, and this constancy will begin to feel less like stability and more like a refusal — as if the water has decided on this temperature the way someone decides to hold a breath.

The scattered structures — the tubes, but also, deeper in, where the crack narrows to a passage you could almost fit through but won't, a series of small cairns. Stacked basalt flakes, three or four stones each, placed with a precision that is almost certainly accidental and almost certainly not. They are too small to be intentional. They are too regular to be natural. They sit in the packed thicket of vegetation like punctuation marks in a sentence written in a language you can almost read, and they have not moved, and they will not move, and the vegetation grows around them but not over them, as if it, too, is uncertain whether they belong.

The weirdness is not in any one thing. It is in the *between*. It is in the way the water holds your body slightly differently than it should — the buoyancy is wrong, too even, as if every part of you weighs the same. It is in the way your own breathing, the bubbles you release, do not rise. They drift laterally, slowly, toward the seams where the vegetation grows, and when they reach the stalks, they do not burst. They are absorbed. The plant-growth swells faintly around each one and then returns to its prior dimensions, and you are left with the understanding that you are, slowly, feeding this place with your own held breath. It is in the way sound works here — mostly quiet, mostly the deep and total absence of sound that water creates when it has nothing to echo against — but occasionally, from the narrow passage you will not enter, a tone. Low. Single. Sustained for longer than a breath, longer than a cycle of whatever is cycling in you that still thinks it knows what duration means. A tone that is not produced by anything you can name and that, when it ends, leaves behind a silence that is somehow *more* silent than the silence that preceded it, as if the sound, on its way out, took something with it.

The pocket is small. The pocket is full. There is no room that is not water, or vegetation, or the memory of fauna, or the suspicion of structure, or the thinning of the dark that reveals nothing and changes everything. You will not stay long — not because you will choose to leave, but because the space is too small to stay in, and your body will decide for you, and you will emerge back into the crack and the crack will lead you upward toward the memory of surface, and by the time you arrive, the pocket will have rearranged itself in your mind into something you can almost describe, and then into something you cannot, and then into a feeling you will carry for the rest of whatever you are — the feeling of having been inside a thought that was not yours, held briefly, and released.
