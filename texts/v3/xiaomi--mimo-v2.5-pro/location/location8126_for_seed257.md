# The Drowned Lattice of Mount Kethara

---

## What You Enter

The first thing to understand is that the water does not flood *through* the structure. The water *is* the structure now — every corridor, every chamber, every passage exists as a volume of warm dark liquid held in place by walls that were carved before the lake was born.

You are inside a mountain. Or rather, you are inside what was once a mountain-top settlement, now entirely submerged beneath a caldera lake that has risen slowly over millennia to swallow everything the builders made. The lake fills the volcanic bowl from rim to rim, and the old city lies in its deepest throat, where geothermal warmth keeps the water at a steady, almost-blood temperature — tepid, mineral-heavy, faintly sulfurous at the edges where seeps still breathe from below.

The settlement was never large. Perhaps three hundred souls once lived here, carving into the basalt and tuff with stone adzes and bone chisels and the earliest iron — tools you can still find sometimes, embedded in alcoves where the mineral-rich water has plated them in a dull rind of calcium and iron oxide, preserving their shapes long after their handles rotted away. The builders carved *everything*. They did not build upward with stacked blocks so much as they carved inward, excavating rooms inside the living rock, connecting them with passages barely wide enough for two people to pass. What they removed, they shaped into lintels and dividing walls and low benches and drainage channels that once carried rainwater and now carry nothing but the slow thermal convection of the lake itself.

There is no natural substrate left visible. Every surface you touch has been worked. Chisel marks from ten thousand days of labor are still faintly legible under the silty mineral film that coats everything — a skin ofite deposits that has thickened over the ages into something almost like a second skin of stone, softening every edge, rounding every corner, blurring every carved symbol into ghost-shapes. The erosion is immense and patient. The water has had so much time. But it has not destroyed the architecture; it has *translated* it — converting sharp human intention into something organic, almost geological, so that the rooms now resemble the chambers of a fossil shell, or the burrows of some enormous soft-bodied creature that no longer lives here.

## The Rooms

The scale is intimate. Cathedral-scale in the sense of a single chapel, not a basilica. The largest room you can find — and you must swim through a collapsed doorway to reach it, squeezing past a rubble of carved blocks that have settled into a jumble colonized by tube-worms — is perhaps eight meters across and four meters high. Its ceiling was once flat. Now it sags slightly in the center, not yet failing but carrying the weight of twenty meters of basalt above it with a visible weariness. The walls are lined with niches — small, hand-height alcoves that once held oil lamps, or offerings, or the dried remains of the dead. The builders entombed their dead in these walls. The bones are gone — dissolved, scattered, consumed by the water's patient chemistry over centuries — but the shapes of the bodies remain, impressed into theite that filled the niches around them, so that each alcove holds a faint human silhouette, a bas-relief of absence.

You move through the settlement by swimming, propelling yourself gently through passages that twist and branch and sometimes dead-end where ceilings have collapsed. The water is warm against your skin and tastes of chalk and iron. It is extraordinarily still — thermal gradients push it in slow, almost imperceptible currents, but there is no flow, no surge, no wave action. The lake above is deep and the mountain protects it from wind. Down here, in the carved throat of the old city, the water barely moves. You move through it like a thought through a sleeping mind.

## The Fauna

The builders are gone. The animals are not.

They arrived slowly — colonizers from the lake above, finding in the carved labyrinth an architecture of shelter and hiding-place that suits them perfectly. The settlement has become a reef, a warren, a habitat of extraordinary complexity, and the fauna that fills it has adapted with a specificity that suggests this relationship is old — not decades old, but centuries, perhaps millennia.

**Blind cave eels**, pale as candle wax, no thicker than a finger, coil in the lamp-niches where offerings once lay. They have no eyes. They have evolved in darkness for long enough that their eye sockets have sealed over with smooth skin, and they navigate entirely by the lateral line — the pressure-sense that runs along their flanks, reading the water's displacement with exquisite precision. They feel you coming from meters away and press themselves deeper into their niches, becoming still as bone.

**Crawling things** occupy the floors: species of freshwater crab and crayfish, most of them small, pale, slow-moving. They graze on the biofilm that coats every surface — a thin mat of bacteria and archaea that is the base of the food chain here, fed by the geothermal chemistry rather than by sunlight. The crabs are patient creatures. They move centimeters per hour. Some of the larger ones — broad-palmed, heavily armored, the size of a human hand — have settled into depressions in the floor where the stone has been worn into shallow bowls, perhaps by the builders for grinding grain, and they sit in these bowls for days at a time, sieving particulates from the water with their maxillipeds.

**Small translucent shrimp** drift in loose clouds through the larger chambers, their bodies almost invisible except for their dark digestive tracts and the two black points of their eyes. Unlike the eels, the shrimp can see — faintly, poorly, but enough to detect the rare thermal shimmer of a warm-water seep or the shadow of a passing predator. They are the most numerous creature here. They fill the water like motes in a sunbeam, except there are no sunbeams.

**Sponges and bryozoans** encrust the walls in patches — not plants, despite their appearance, but animals, colonial filter-feeders that pump water through their bodies and extract bacteria and dissolved organics. They grow slowly in this nutrient-poor water, forming thin crusts of white and pale yellow that blend almost indistinguishably with the mineral deposits on the stone. Some of them are very old. Some of them may predate the flooding itself, having colonized the walls when the water was only ankle-deep and the city was merely damp.

There are **fish** — small, pale, mostly colorless. A species of loach, elongate and eel-like, that lives in the rubble of collapsed passages. A species of catfish, whiskered, blind, that patrols the larger rooms in a slow, methodical circuit, mouth to the floor, vacuuming up the organic sediment that accumulates in the corners. They are not afraid of you. They have never encountered anything your size that moved with purpose. They drift around you with a dull, incurious patience, the way cattle might ignore a strange rock that appeared overnight in their field.

## Darkness

The light is nearly gone. You are deep — perhaps thirty meters below the surface of the caldera lake, and the water above is turbite with volcanic minerals, a permanent grey-green opacity that swallows sunlight in its first few meters. At the settlement's depth, there is *no* ambient light. None. The darkness is absolute.

And yet it is not featureless. Your eyes, given time, begin to hallucinate shapes — the brain manufacturing geometry from nothing, filling the void with ghostly corridors that are not there. Touch becomes primary. You navigate by trailing your fingers along the chisel-marked walls, reading the stone like a blind man reading a page — here a doorframe, here a step down, here a carved symbol that might be a letter or might be a decorative spiral, worn too smooth to tell. The passage turns. The passage branches. Your fingers find a niche and inside it, nothing — no bones, no offering, only theite impression of what was once there.

The fauna produces its own faint light, occasionally. Some of the bacteria in the biofilm are bioluminescent, and in certain chambers — usually the ones nearest to thermal seeps, where the chemistry is richest — the walls glow with a faint, blue-green luminescence so dim it barely registers. It is enough to see the outlines of things. A wall. A niche. The pale shape of a sleeping eel. Then your movement disturbs the water, the seep fluctuates, and the glow fades back to black.

## Sound

The silence here is architectural. The water swallows vibration. The stone absorbs it. The narrow passages, the irregular walls, the mineral deposits that coat every surface — all of it conspires to create a space that is acoustically dead, a place where sound goes to lose itself.

What remains is barely sound at all. The faintest *ticking* — thermal expansion and contraction of the rock as the geothermal gradient shifts over hours, tiny movements in the stone that transmit through the water as minuscule pressure pulses, more felt than heard. The *clicking* of crab claws on stone, distant, irregular, like someone testing a lock in another room. Once, perhaps, a faint groan as a ceiling settles — a sound that moves through the water with a low, whale-like resonance, felt in the chest more than the ear, and then gone.

You hear your own breathing with hideous intimacy. The rasp of it. The bubbles.

## Time

This place is old in a way that resists numbers. The builders were here perhaps four thousand years ago, which is ancient for a human settlement but young for a mountain. The lake has been rising for longer than that — the caldera slowly filling, century by century, with rain and snowmelt and the slow seep of groundwater through the volcanic walls. The erosion is primordial in character — not the sharp violence of a flood but the infinite patience of a substance (water, mineral-laden, warm, slightly acidic) that has all the time in the world and asks nothing of the stone except that it yield, molecule by molecule, over millennia.

The city yields. It has been yielding for a very long time. But it was carved deep, and the basalt is hard, and the water works slowly. This is a place that will take another ten thousand years to finish dissolving. It is in no rush. Nothing here is in any rush. The eels coil in their niches for weeks without moving. The crabs sit in their grinding-bowls for days. The shrimp drift in their clouds, going nowhere in particular, their tiny lives measured in months or years, each one a brief flicker of presence in a place that measures time in stone.

Change here is geological. The passages slowly widen. The ceilings slowly sag. A niche that was square four thousand years ago is now an oval. A carved step is now a smooth ramp. A doorway is now a void, its frame worn to nothing, its threshold a gradual slope ofite sediment that you glide over without knowing you have crossed from one room into the next.

## What It Feels Like

To be here is to be inside a ruin that has been so completely absorbed by its environment that it has become natural — not restored to nature, but *translated into* nature, converted from human intention into geological patience. The architecture is still legible, if you know how to read it: you can find the doorways, the rooms, the niches, the carved channels. But everything has been softened, blurred, mineralized, colonized, submerged, silenced, darkened, and slowed to the pace of rock.

It is warm. It is dark. It is quiet. It is full of small pale lives that do not know or care that they live inside a dead city. The water holds you. The stone holds the water. The mountain holds the stone. And above all of it, the grey surface of the caldera lake receives the rain and the snow and the rare, muffled thunder of a distant storm, and transmits none of it down to where you are, floating in the corridor of a house whose builders have been gone for four thousand years, whose walls are still smooth under your fingertips, whose silence is the most complete silence you have ever known.
