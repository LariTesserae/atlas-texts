# The High Dark

---

## The Plateau

It was a mountain range once. That much can be read in the composition of the stone — layered, metamorphic, full of pressures that only make sense at depth. But the mountains are gone. What remains is a tableland stretching to every horizon, so ancient and so thoroughly worn that its relief is measured in tens of meters rather than thousands. The surface is not flat, exactly. It undulates. There are swells and shallow basins, ridgelines so gentle you might not notice you've crossed one. The rock is a dark bronze-gray, dense, close-grained, veined here and there with quartz that catches nothing because there is nothing to catch.

There is no water. There has not been water here for a very long time — not in liquid form, not in vapor, not in any form the geology remembers. The stone is dessicated to a depth of kilometers. Dust exists, but it moves only when something mechanical pushes it. The air itself is thin and warm, carrying a faint metallic taste, and it sits against the skin the way still air does in a room that has not been opened in years.

The darkness is not total but it is close. No star shows overhead. The sky, if it can be called that, is a uniform absence — not black, exactly, but a dark the eye eventually stops trying to resolve. There may be atmosphere up there, but it offers no scattering, no gradient, nothing to fix the boundary between ground and void. The horizon is not visible. Directions are known by survey marks.

---

## The City

It is not a city that was built and then endured. It is a city that was built and then *kept being built*, layer upon layer, without anyone ever declaring it finished or abandoned. That is the central paradox of the place.

The structures rise from the plateau in clusters separated by wide tracts of empty stone. They are heavy, low, industrial in their bones — built for weight and permanence and the housing of machinery. Corrugated iron in oxidized layers. Concrete that has been poured so many centuries ago that its surface has developed a mineral crust, almostite, almost geological. Steel beams exposed where facades have fallen away, showing the skeletons beneath, and the skeletons have not rusted through. They will not rust through for a very long time. The dryness protects them.

The streets between buildings are real — paved, guttered, engineered for drainage that will never come. They are wide enough for vehicles that no longer move, or that move rarely, and the paving stones are worn concave where feet or treads have crossed them over vast spans of time, but not recently enough to leave marks sharp enough to date. The streets are clean in the way that dry, windless places are clean. Nothing rots. Nothing decays. Everything simply *persists*.

There is industrial equipment everywhere, integrated into the structures or standing alone on foundations of its own. Smokestacks, but they do not smoke. Conveyor systems running between buildings, their belts still, their rollers seized. Factories with their loading doors open, revealing interiors that are dark and warm and empty — vast rooms where production lines sit in perfect arrangement, every tool in place, as if the workers set them down at the end of a shift and then the shift never resumed. But. There is heat. Somewhere in the lower levels, somewhere in the infrastructure that tunnels into the plateau itself, something still runs. Geothermal taps, most likely. The warmth rises through the city's bones and keeps the air above the ambient temperature of a dead world. Some of the electrical grid is live. Not all. Not most. But some — enough that certain corridors hum faintly, enough that lights exist in pockets, isolated nodes of industrial illumination where a fixture has not yet burned out or where a maintenance cycle that no one oversees continues to replace components on schedule.

## The Light

There are zones of light.

They are small and they are spaced far apart, and moving between them requires crossing long stretches of the dark. The light comes from industrial fixtures — high-pressure sodium, halogen, the occasional fluorescent panel that flickers at a frequency just below the threshold of comfort. These lights illuminate their immediate surroundings with a harsh, amber or blue-white wash, casting sharp shadows that do not move, because nothing moves to change them.

The effect is of a series of small, bright rooms without ceilings, set into an endless dark floor. Walk out of the light and the darkness does not close in gradually — it is simply *there*, immediate and warm and total, and the last visible thing behind you is the glow bleeding off the edge of the nearest lit structure like a sunset that never progresses.

In the lighted zones, details are visible. The textures of the walls. The specific arrangement of pipes and conduits running along building faces. A sign, its paint faded to a pale ghost of what it was, written in a script or language that may or may not be decipherable. The floor. The absence of anything living on the floor — no lichen, no mold, no insect husks, nothing biological at all. Just mineral dust and the faint chemical residue of industrial processes that ended long ago.

---

## Sound

The city is quiet.

Not silent — there is a background to it, a ground-state hum that rises from the infrastructure and the still-running systems and the air itself moving through corridors and ducts. It is a low sound, below speech, below music, felt in the bones more than heard. It does not change. It has not changed in a very long time.

Occasionally, a mechanism cycles. A valve opens or closes somewhere in the deep infrastructure, and there is a thud — dull, structural, transmitted through the stone. A pipe contracts or expands with a tick. A door, warped by centuries of thermal cycling, shifts in its frame with a groan that sounds like something organic but is not. These sounds are rare. They are spaced irregularly, and each one echoes briefly and then the quiet reassertes itself.

There are no voices. There are no footsteps. There is no wind — the air is too still, too settled, too committed to its own equilibrium to move in any coordinated way. The warmth rises from below in such a gentle gradient that it does not create convection. The city breathes, if it breathes at all, in a rhythm so slow that it cannot be detected from moment to moment.

## What Lives Here

Almost nothing.

The fauna — if that is the right word — consists of small, sparse, thermal-metabolizing organisms that inhabit the warmest zones near the geothermal vents and infrastructure cores. They are not animals in any familiar sense. They are slow. They are shaped for the crevices between industrial components, flattened, dark, nearly invisible against the stone and metal. They feed on mineral heat differentials and the faint chemical gradients left by ancient industrial processes. They reproduce rarely. They die rarely. They are more a feature of the landscape than an ecology — geological in their patience, biological in their form.

You might see one, if you sat still long enough in a warm place. A dark shape moving with geological slowness across a pipe, or pressed into a seam where two concrete panels meet. It would not notice you. It would not react to you. You are not part of its sensory world, which is tuned to temperature and vibration and chemical gradient, and you are none of those things in any way that matters to it.

There is no other life. No microorganism has been found, though some have searched. The mineral world here is sterile at every scale that has been examined. Whatever biology exists is confined to those small, strange, thermal organisms, and they are barely biology at all.

---

## The Equilibrium

The defining quality of this place is that nothing changes.

This is not an impression. It is a physical fact. The erosion that ground the mountains to this tableland reached a point, eons ago, where there was simply nothing left to erode — no water, no wind, no thermal cycling sharp enough to fracture stone, no life to pry at cracks. The city, similarly, has reached a state where decay has slowed to nearly zero. Paint fades over centuries rather than years. Metal oxidizes over millennia rather than decades. The structures do not shift, do not settle, do not lean. They are in equilibrium with the forces that act on them, and those forces are so slight and so constant that they produce no visible change on any human timescale.

The result is a place that feels frozen in time but is not frozen — it is simply *finished*. It has reached its final form. The mountains are done becoming flat. The city is done becoming ruin. The darkness is done descending. Everything that was going to happen here has already happened, and what remains is a landscape in perfect, permanent, warm, dark, quiet stasis.

It is strange because it should not be this way. Industrial cities are places of motion and change and entropy. They run down. They collapse. They are reclaimed by whatever environment surrounds them. But here there is nothing to reclaim — no rain, no roots, no frost, no wind, no living thing with any interest in the structures. The city simply *is*, in the same way the stone beneath it simply is, and both will continue to simply be for longer than anyone standing in the dark streets could meaningfully comprehend.

## Walking Here

If you walk through this place, your footsteps are the loudest thing. They echo off buildings and return to you slightly changed, slightly softened, as if the city is absorbing the sound reluctantly, unfamiliar with the task. Your breath is visible in the lighted zones — not because it is cold, but because the air is dry enough that moisture condenses briefly, a small ghost that dissipates in seconds.

The ground beneath your feet is stone. Paved stone in the streets, raw stone between the clusters. Your weight on it produces no give, no shift, no response. You are temporary. The stone is not.

You can walk for hours between the lighted zones and see nothing change — the same gentle undulations of the plateau, the same dark shapes of structures against the slightly-less-dark sky, the same warm air, the same silence punctuated by the same rare mechanical sounds. The city is large. It extends beyond what you can explore, and what lies beyond what you can explore is more of the same — more buildings, more streets, more infrastructure, more darkness, more warmth, more quiet, more stasis.

The strangeness is not in any single feature. It is in the totality. An industrial city with no people. A warm world with no sun. A built environment with no builder present. A geology that has reached absolute equilibrium. Every feature makes sense in isolation. Together, they produce a place that feels like an answer to a question no one asked — a world that solved itself and then stopped.
