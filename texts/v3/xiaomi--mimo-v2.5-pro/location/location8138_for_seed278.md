# The Khas Plateau

The ground is stone that was old when it was young.

Not broken stone — there are no recent fractures, no jagged edges. Everything has been *rounded*. The surface rolls in slow undulations, like the memory of waves that stopped moving millennia ago. The color is a grey so muted it barely qualifies as color, shot through with veins of darker grey, occasional streaks of rust where iron wept and dried and rusted again and again until the red became part of the rock itself. Where you step, the stone has a texture like very fine sandpaper. Where you don't step, there is often a film of something — moisture, or the mineral residue of moisture that came and went and came again, leaving a faint white bloom on the surface like frost that forgot to be cold.

It is, though. Cold. The kind of cold that doesn't assault you but simply *is*, the way a table is a table. It sits in the air and in the stone and in whatever is left of your hands after you've been walking for a while. Not bitter — there is no wind to speak of — just present, ambient, as if the entire plateau were a single large cold body and you have climbed onto its back.

---

You cannot see the edges.

Not because of fog or distance haze, though both exist here. The plateau simply extends. It goes in every direction with the patience of something that has always gone in every direction. The horizon is a line, slightly curved if you pay close enough attention, between a pale grey sky and a darker grey ground. There is a sun. It is up there somewhere behind the permanent overcast, a sourceless milk-brightness that gives light without warmth, without shadow at this latitude, without any clear indication of where it is or what time it is. The light comes from the sky as a whole, diffused, directionless, and dim — the kind of dim that doesn't feel like darkness, only like the permanent removal of anything bright.

You could walk for days and the horizon would not change its distance from you. You could walk for a week and the terrain would shift only in its variations of the same: higher ridges, lower basins, the occasional shallow depression where something like a pond existed once and now holds a skin of pale ice over two inches of water so clear it might not be there at all. The scale is such that weather happens *within* the plateau — clouds form over one region and dissolve before reaching another. You can see rain falling somewhere far off, a grey curtain hanging between sky and ground, and know that it will not reach you, and that by the time it does reach you it will have become something else: mist, or just heavier air.

---

The vegetation is almost an apology.

Cushion-plants — tight, compact, barely raising themselves above the stone. They grow in crevices and lee-sides of ridges where a fraction more moisture collects, where a fraction less cold settles. Dark green, nearly black, so tightly packed that individual leaves are invisible — they are just *mounds*, lichenous, primordial, the size of a fist or a head. Between them, actual lichen: pale crusts on rock, chartreuse patches where the light catches them at just the angle to reveal their color, which otherwise disappears into the grey. In sheltered basins where moisture pools for part of the year, there are small, tough grasses, almost indistinguishable from the stone until you notice their slight movement — not from wind, because there is almost no wind, but from something else, some internal living shiver.

No trees. Nothing that reaches upward. Everything here grows *out* and *down*, spreading laterally, hugging the stone, as if all ambition has been edited out of the botanical record by the long patient hand of the cold.

---

The animals.

Here is where the place begins to violate its own silence.

They are everywhere. Not in a way you notice at first — the density of any particular area is low, the spacing wide. But the *scope* of them. They exist at the scale of the plateau itself. Herds, if you can call them that, moving in loose aggregations across the stone, so vast that they appear as a change in the color of the ground: the grey becomes slightly darker, slightly textured, and if you look closely you realize it is *moving*. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Spread across kilometers, migrating — though migration implies destination, and these creatures seem to move without destination, circulating through the plateau's basins and ridges in slow circuits that may take years to complete.

They are large. Each one the size of a draft horse, though nothing like a horse in shape. Low-slung, six-legged, covered in a dense hide the color of wet slate. Their heads are broad and flat, almost shovel-like, designed — or evolved, or whatever happened — for scraping the thin vegetation from stone. Their eyes are small and dark and positioned far back on the skull, as though seeing is not their primary concern. And they do not make sound.

This is the wrong way to put it. It is not that they are quiet. It is that the sound they should make — the hooves on stone, the breathing, the low calls to each other — does not propagate. It is swallowed. The air here absorbs sound the way the stone absorbs heat: slowly, completely, without return. You can see a herd a kilometer away and hear nothing. You can walk among them — they do not flee, they do not react to you with anything that resembles alarm — and hear nothing but the muffled drum of your own heartbeat, the whisper of your own breath, which even those sounds seem diminished, pressed flat, as though the plateau does not object to noise but simply does not recognize it as a category of phenomenon.

The creatures communicate. You can see it — subtle shifts in posture, the way a head turns and three others turn in sequence, the way the herd changes direction as a single body. But the communication happens below the threshold of what this place allows you to perceive. It is possible they are speaking constantly, in frequencies the cold air devours, and you are walking through a conversation that has been ongoing for longer than you have been alive.

In the basins, there are others. Smaller, insectoid, moving in clouds over the shallow water — but again, silently. Vast numbers, a constant industry of drinking and breeding and dying, and the only evidence is the visual, a darkening of the air, a shimmering of movement over the ice-thin water. They do not buzz. Nothing here buzzes.

On the ridges, something perches. Larger than the herding creatures, solitary, motionless for hours or days, watching the basin floors with an attention that seems geological in its patience. What they eat, what they are waiting for — unclear. They may be part of the same slow circuit, the same patient circulation. The ecosystem is balanced, ancient, and enormous, operating on a logic that does not require your understanding.

---

At the edges of perception — and the edges are far, because perception here has long reach in the flat dim light — there are structures. Not built structures, not quite. Or: not *recently* built. The plateau is scarred in places by geometries that are not erosion. Long, straight cuts in the stone. Flat-bottomed excavations where the undulating surface has been planed clean. In one of these — and you only find it because the herds avoid it, because the dark color of the ground gives way to pale exposed stone — there is rusted iron. A framework, collapsed, oxidized to the color of the iron veins in the native rock. Some kind of rig. Some kind of extraction apparatus. It has been here long enough that the stone is beginning to re-claim it, lichen creeping over the joints, windborne grit filling the crevices. But not long enough that its geometry has been forgotten. The straight lines are still straight. The angles are still angles.

There are more of these, scattered across the plateau at distances that suggest some industrial logic, some grid of access and extraction that was laid down and then — what? Abandoned? Completed? Set aside while something else was attended to? The technology is mechanical, large-scale: gears and armatures and the skeletons of engines, all locked in rust and cold. Whatever they were pulling from the stone, the plateau has absorbed the wound. The excavations are becoming basins. Water collects in them. The herds pass through, grazing on the new growth that takes root in the disturbed soil.

No one maintains them. No one seems to visit. They are part of the plateau now — artifacts becoming geology, history becoming terrain.

---

If you stay long enough — and long here means something different than it means elsewhere — you begin to feel the slowness. Not as a lack. As a presence. The plateau moves at its own pace, and the pace is the pace of erosion: the stone wears down, the herds circulate, the moisture comes and goes, the ice forms and sublimates and forms again. The cycles are visible only to someone who watches for years. A ridge that was sharp when you arrived is slightly less sharp. A basin that held water last season holds a thin crust of salt this season. The creatures in their thousands shift their circuit by a few degrees, as if following a script that changes one word per century.

The industrial scars are healing. Not quickly. But the rust advances, the lichen spreads, the stone grinds the iron into itself, and in a thousand years there will be nothing left but a slight irregularity in the surface, a discoloration, a place where the herds graze differently because the mineral content of the soil is fractionally altered.

This is a place that has been itself for a very long time, and intends to be itself for a very long time yet. It does not resist change. It simply metabolizes it so slowly that change becomes a substance, a texture, another layer of grey on grey on grey.

---

At night — and night is only slightly darker than day, a deepening of the same flat grey into a charcoal that never becomes black — the sounds you cannot hear during the day become sounds you cannot hear even more intensely. The herds settle. The perching things on the ridges shift their weight. The water in the basins clicks faintly as ice forms at its edges, though "faintly" is generous — the clicking is imagined, inferred, the kind of sound you would hear if the air allowed it. You lie on the stone, which is warmer than the air by exactly the amount of heat your body has given it, and you feel the presence of ten thousand large animals breathing in the dark around you, and you hear nothing, nothing, nothing at all.

The silence is not peaceable. It is not hostile. It is the silence of a place that has outlived the need to make itself known. The plateau knows it is here. The creatures know they are here. The rusted iron, half-absorbed, knows it is here. None of them need to prove it with sound.

You are the only thing that doesn't know. You are the only thing that listens for proof.
