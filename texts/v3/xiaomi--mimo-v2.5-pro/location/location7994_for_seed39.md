# The Scoured Plateau

The mountain has no name yet — or rather, it has several, each one provisional, assigned by different surveying teams and none of them sticking. It sits above three thousand meters in a range still being catalogued, and the land up here looks like it was peeled open yesterday.

## The Rock

And in geological terms, it nearly was. The summit plateau is a broad, slightly tilted slab of gneiss and raw granite, still showing the chatter-marks of the glacier that occupied this surface ten or twelve thousand years ago — a blink. The striations run roughly north-south, parallel grooves wide enough to lie down in, filled now with standing water that reflects nothing because the sky is the color of wet wool. Where the glacier's terminus stalled and churned, there are mounds of unsorted till — boulders the size of automobiles jumbled together with gravel and clay, all of it still sharp-edged, unsoftened by time. The boulders are pink and dove-gray and streaked with quartz veins that catch what little light there is with a dull, oily sheen. Nothing has had time to round them.

The bedrock itself is in the earliest stages of its second life. After the ice withdrew, frost-wedging began splitting the granite along its joints, and the fracture patterns are geometrically precise — polygons, hexagons, near-perfect rectangles, each block slowly prying itself free from its neighbors. Some have tilted. Some have dropped a few centimeters, creating a landscape of subtle steps and ledges barely ankle-high. It looks, if you crouch down and let your eye travel across it, like a tiled floor in the earliest stages of demolition. In a few thousand years this will be a field of angular rubble. Now it is still recognizably *one surface*, breaking apart in slow motion.

## The Water

Water is everywhere and almost nowhere at once. There are no rivers up here, no streams with channels — the plateau is too flat, the erosion too young for drainage patterns to have established themselves. Instead the water simply *persists*. It pools in the glacial grooves and the frost-polygon cracks, in the depressions between boulders, in shallow hollows where the till is clay-rich enough to hold it. These pools are seldom deeper than a few centimeters. They are very cold — two, three degrees above freezing — and perfectly still, and they have a faint mineral tint, slightly rusted where iron-bearing rock has dissolved into them, slightly milky where glacial flour is still suspended.

At night and in the early morning, condensation is the dominant water. The plateau sits in a persistent weather pattern where warm, moist air masses from the western lowlands ride upslope and cool as they climb, and the result is near-constant fog, mist, or low cloud that settles onto the rock and beads and runs. Every surface weeps. The boulders are slick with it. The moss — where moss exists — is saturated, dark, and heavy. If you press your hand flat against a stone face, your palm comes away wet, and the handprint evaporates slowly in the cold air, lingering for minutes.

In the low spots where water accumulates more reliably, the substrate has begun to turn to bog. Thin, black, waterlogged soil — mostly decomposed microbial mats and the earliest traces of peat — squelches underfoot. These bogs are shallow and small, some no bigger than a dining table, but they are surprisingly warm relative to the air above them, faintly steaming on the coldest mornings. They smell of iron and something organic and ancient, a faint sulfur undernote, the smell of anaerobic digestion beginning its slow work.

## The Vegetation

Nothing grows tall. The treeline is four or five hundred meters below, a sharp boundary where the last stunted conifers give up entirely, and above it the only plants are those that can survive the wind, the cold, and the thin, mineral-poor substrate. Lichens are the dominant presence — crustose lichens that paint the rocks in pale gray-green and burnt-orange patches, as slow-growing as the stones themselves, some colonies almost certainly older than any human structure in the lowlands. They are the first colonizers, the ones breaking rock into soil, and they will still be at it long after everything else on the plateau has come and gone.

Between the lichen colonies, in the sheltered cracks and on the gentler slopes where a few centimeters of soil have accumulated, there are mosses — dense, low cushions of *Racomitrium* and *Andreaea*, dark green to nearly black when wet, bleaching to a pale tawny gold when they dry out, which is rare. In the wettest hollows, sphagnum moss has begun to establish, and it is this that will eventually build the deeper peat. The moss cushions are home to microscopic communities — tardigrades, nematodes, rotifers — and they teem in their small way, whole worlds in a hand-sized clump.

Vascular plants are sparse and stunted. A few species of sedge grow in the boggy patches, their blades only a few centimeters high, almost indistinguishable from the moss around them until you look closely and see the thin, triangular stems. Dwarf willows — woody shrubs that grow flat against the ground, never rising more than two or three centimeters — spread in creeping mats across the better-drained areas, their tiny leathery leaves no bigger than a fingernail. In late summer, if the season is kind, a few of them produce catkins. Mostly they just persist, growing a millimeter per year, building tiny root networks in the thin soil.

There are no flowers to speak of. This is not a place that rewards display.

## The Fauna

And yet the animals come.

This is the part of the plateau that resists easy explanation. The place is empty — genuinely, profoundly empty, kilometers of open rock and bog and sky with no tree, no shelter, no hiding place — and yet it is *full of life*, moving through, passing over, nesting in the margins. The abundance is not resident density but *traffic*, the constant passage of creatures for whom this bleak height is not home but highway, feeding ground, or breeding site.

**Birds** dominate. The sky here is rarely without them. Snow buntings work the boulder fields in loose flocks of twenty or thirty, their white-and-black plumage making them nearly invisible against the lichen-spotted rock until they move. They feed on the seeds of the few grasses and on the tiny arthropods that live in the moss, and they chatter as they forage — thin, tinkling calls that carry in the still air, one of the few sounds that punctuates the quiet. Pipits nest on the ground in the moss cushions, making cup-shaped nests so perfectly integrated into their surroundings that you can almost step on one before you see it, the bird flushing at your feet with a sharp *zip* of alarm.

Raptors patrol the updrafts along the plateau's edges. Golden eagles nest on the cliff faces below, where the mountain drops away into the lower valleys, and they hunt across the open summit — working the ridgelines in pairs, sliding along invisible contours of rising air, occasionally folding and dropping into a steep stoop when something moves in the boulder field below. Ptarmigan crouch in the frost polygons, their plumage shifting seasonally from mottled brown to pure winter white, and they are nearly undetectable either way — perfectly evolved to be invisible on rock and snow.

**Mammals** are present but few in species and many in individuals. Pikas — small, round-eared lagomorphs — live in the boulder fields, and their alarm calls, sharp two-note barks, are the most characteristic sound of the plateau. They harvest mosses and sedges in summer, drying them in small haypiles tucked into sheltered crevices for winter, and their presence transforms the boulder fields into something active and purposeful — every heap of rocks a village, every gap between stones a doorway. Marmots are here too, larger and bolder, sunning themselves on the warmest rocks on the rare clear mornings, their warning whistles carrying across kilometers of open ground.

In the bogs, the warm margins where sphagnum and sedge meet the open water, there are insects — surprising quantities of them. Midges cloud in thin columns above the wettest ground on still days, and biting flies work the edges whenever the wind drops. They are the reason the birds come, the reason the bats come at dusk, the reason this altitude supports an ecosystem at all. The midges breed in the bog water, and their larvae are food for everything, and so the cold, wet, empty plateau turns out to be a kind of invisible engine, converting water and moss into midges and midges into birds and birds into eagles, all of it running quietly beneath the fog.

## The Atmosphere and the Light

The sky, when you can see it, is a pale, washed-out blue-gray, the color of old ice. More often you cannot see it at all — the plateau is socked in cloud, or trailing wisps of fog that move across the surface with apparent purpose, as if navigating around the boulders. The cloud ceiling is low, sometimes only twenty or thirty meters above the ground, and it compresses the world, flattens it, makes the far edge of the plateau — a few kilometers away — seem both close and unreachable, a gray smudge that might be rock or might be sky.

Light is diffuse. On clear days the sun appears as a bright smear behind high cirrus, casting faint shadows that are more blue than dark. On overcast days — most days — the light is even and directionless, and it flattens the landscape further, erases depth, makes everything seem two-dimensional. The wet rock reflects what little light there is, and the water pools gleam like hammered pewter, and the whole plateau has a faint metallic quality, as if the world has been rendered in a single tone of silver-gray.

At dawn and dusk, if the cloud breaks even briefly, the light goes extraordinary. The wet rock catches low-angle sun and glows amber and rose, and the mist in the hollows turns gold, and for a few minutes the plateau looks like something from a different world — warm, luminous, almost welcoming. Then the cloud closes and the color drains and it is cold gray again.

## The Industrial Presence

Here is the contradiction the plateau holds: it is untouched, un-built-upon, and yet it is not unclaimed.

The presence is most audible before it is seen. On certain days, when the wind is from the east, a low, steady rumble carries up from the valley — heavy machinery, diesel engines, the sound of a crushing plant operating somewhere below the cloud line. It is not loud at the this altitude. It is a bass note, felt more than heard, a faint vibration in the rock when you press your palm flat. But it is persistent. It runs during daylight hours and sometimes through the night, and it has become part of the plateau's soundscape — as regular as wind, as reliable as rain.

When the clouds part and the visibility extends down into the eastern valley, you can see it: a mining operation, mid-scale, fully mechanized. Excavators and haul trucks move on graded roads cut into the valley wall, and there is a processing facility — concrete and steel, conveyor belts, settling ponds that gleam white with mineral tailings. From this altitude it looks small, toylike, but the scale is deceptive. The roads alone, switchbacking up from the valley floor, are visible for kilometers, pale scars on the dark slope. The operation has been running for a few years. It is expanding.

There are no permanent structures on the plateau itself — no roads, no buildings, no power lines. But there are survey markers: short metal stakes driven into the rock at intervals, each one capped with a small orange reflector, arranged in a rough grid. They are fresh. The rock dust from the drilled holes has not yet washed away. A geological survey team has been here recently, probably in the last season, mapping the subsurface with ground-penetrating radar and core sampling. The cores are gone — you can see the cylindrical holes in the rock where they were extracted, neatly circular, a few centimeters in diameter, descending into darkness.

There are also tire tracks — wide, deeply treaded, from an off-road vehicle — pressed into the softer ground near the bogs. They have not yet been erased by rain. Someone drove up here, slowly, methodically, and drove back down, and the tracks suggest they stopped frequently, got out, walked around, took samples, got back in.

The plateau is being measured. It is being evaluated. The industrial civilization in the valley is looking up at this cold, wet, empty height and seeing not an ecosystem, not a landscape, not a place where pikas build haypiles and eagles ride thermals — but a resource. The geology is promising. The frost-patterned rock is rich in something worth crushing and refining. The water table is high, which is useful for processing. The altitude is a logistical challenge but not an insurmountable one.

For now, the plateau endures. The survey stakes stand in their grid. The tire tracks fade. The pikas continue their harvest. The midges rise from the bogs. The fog comes and goes.

## The Quiet

Mostly, the plateau is quiet. The wind, when it comes, is low and steady, a deep hush that seems to rise from the rock itself. The water drips and pools and condenses in silence. The birds call — thin, distant, brief — and then are quiet again. The industrial rumble from the valley is so constant and so low that after a while it ceases to register as sound and becomes instead a quality of the air, a faint pressure, a reminder that the world below is still there, still running, still coming.

The dominant sound, if you stop and listen, is your own breathing. And then, after a while, even that fades, and there is only the plateau — raw, wet, cold, newly made, nearly empty, quietly alive, slowly being claimed — extending gray and silver in all directions until it meets the cloud.
