# The Grindplain

The first thing you notice is not what you see. It's what you hear: a low, saturated roar, like a factory floor heard through a hundred meters of stone, except there are no walls, no ceiling, no source you can point to. It comes from everywhere. It comes from the ground.

You are standing on a plain that extends in every direction to horizons so distant they curve faintly, like a held breath. The terrain is almost impossibly flat — not featureless, but *worn down*, as though every mountain that ever stood here was digested by time so thoroughly that only the faintest undulations remain, like the ridges on the roof of a mouth. The surface material is a dense, interlocking matrix ofite and rime-fused mineral: slate-dark, faintly metallic where it catches the dim light, veined with channels where water moves. The water is everywhere. It doesn't pool in any conventional sense — the flatness is too old, too settled for drainage or rivers — but it seeps, films, condenses, and weeps from every surface. Thin sheets of it slide horizontally across the ground when wind pushes them. Puddles shiver and reform. The air itself is half water: a thick, freezing fog that never fully lifts, only thins and thickens in slow regional cycles, so that visibility shifts from a hundred meters to three kilometers and back over the course of hours.

The temperature is bitter. Not the sharp cold of high altitude or polar ice — this is a *settled* cold, a cold that has had eons to saturate the stone itself. The ground radiates it upward. Your breath would crystallize instantly if you had breath. The water remains liquid only because it is never still. It moves through capillary channels in the rock, driven by pressures from below, and the constant motion keeps it from locking. Where it does freeze, it forms paper-thin sheets of black ice that flex and crack with sounds like snapping wire, constantly breaking and re-forming, giving the plain a ticking, crackling undertone beneath the deeper roar.

There is no vegetation. Not a lichen, not a film of algae, not a single recognizable organism that photosynthesizes. The light wouldn't support it anyway — the sky is a featureless white-grey lid, sourceless, directionless, as though the concept of a sun has been forgotten here. The dimness is total and uniform. Nothing casts a shadow.

And yet the plain is *packed*.

---

This is where your understanding begins to fail.

The density of what occupies this landscape is staggering. Every square meter holds something — and the something is not geological. It is *mechanical*. Or it behaves mechanically. Or it is alive and pretending to be mechanical. The categories do not hold.

Rising from the flat stone in irregular clusters, spaced as densely as teeth in a jaw, are structures. You hesitate to call them that. They are *forms* — tall, narrow, dark, standing three to twenty meters high, composed of the same base mineral as the plain but articulated. Jointed. They have segments that rotate against each other, producing the grinding sound that fills the air. They have limbs that pump, pistons that thrust, cavities that open and close like mouths rhythmically expelling gases — thick, visible exhalations of warm vapor that rise a few meters and then collapse into the ambient fog. They are factories. They have inputs: water drawn up through their bases via root-like channels sunk deep into the stone, minerals scraped from the ground by their lower surfaces. They have outputs: somewhere inside each one, pressures and temperatures are generated that no passive geological process should produce, and the byproducts are excreted through lateral vents as dense, hot slurry that steams on the cold ground before the capillary water carries it away. They are processing something. They are *making* something. What they make, if anything, is not evident. They have been doing this for a very, very long time.

They are not built. There are no seams suggesting assembly, no toolmarks, no design lineage you can trace. They grew — or they emerged — or they *are* the plain, expressing itself in a different register. The erosion record suggests they have been here since the flattening began, that they may be the reason the plain is so flat: their slow excavation of mineral from below, their processing and excretion, has acted as a grinding mechanism on a continental scale over geological time, milling the surface down like a lathe faced with millions of self-organizing teeth.

The sound is extraordinary. Layer upon layer of mechanical noise: the deep grinding of tectonic plates being chewed, the high shriek of stone on stone at the joints, the wet percussion of slurry hitting the ground, the hiss of pressurized gas escaping, the ticking of ice, the vast ambient hum of a million devices operating in loose synchrony so that the plain itself seems to vibrate at a frequency you feel in your skeleton. It is loud. It is loud in the way that an engine room is loud — not explosive, not sharp, but dense and constant and physical.

---

Between them — threaded through them, perched on them, crawling over the wet ground in the spaces where you can fit a body — are the animals.

There is no other word for them, though they resemble nothing from any taxonomy. They are abundant to the point of saturation. The ground moves with them. They range in size from creatures the size of your fist to things the size of horses, and their forms are *wrong* — not malformed, but assembled according to a logic that doesn't correspond to biology as you understand it. They have too many joints, or joints that bend in directions that aren't directions. They have surfaces that are simultaneously wet and metallic. Some appear to be partially mineral, their bodies a composite of living tissue and rawite that has been incorporated — not symbiosis, not parasitism, but something the language doesn't have a word for. Some of them seem to be made of the same material as the grinding structures, as though they are fragments of the industrial process that became autonomous, grew their own motility, wandered away from the assembly line and kept going.

They feed on the slurry. They feed on the heat from the vents. They feed on each other, in predation cycles that move fast — too fast, almost choreographed, as though the predator-prey dynamic here is running at an accelerated clock rate relative to everything else. You watch one creature dismantle another in four seconds, absorbing its mass directly through a seam that opens in its underside. The kill is not violent so much as *procedural*. Industrial. Efficiency-maximized.

Some of them cluster on the grinding structures like barnacles, harvesting waste energy. Others move in vast, slow herds across the flat ground, thousands at a time, their bodies raising a mist from the wet surface as they pass, their collective movement generating a secondary vibration you can feel through the stone. None of them notice you. None of them are oriented toward you in any way. You are not a category they have.

---

And then there is the wrongness.

It is not dramatic. It doesn't manifest as tears in the air or visible distortions. It is subtler and more total than that. The wrongness is that the place does not *cohere*. You will look at a grinding structure and see it clearly — segments, joints, vents, motion — and then for a half-second, the edges won't resolve. Not blurred. *Absent*. As though the concept of a boundary between that object and the air around it is a suggestion being loosely followed. You will hear a sound that you identify as a single mechanical process, and then realize it is also the sound of water, and also the sound of the fog moving, and that these three things are not separate events happening to coincide. They are one thing, perceived by your compromised cognition as three, because your cognition is trying to impose categories on a phenomenon that predates categorization.

Time is unreliable. Not in the sense that it speeds up or slows down — in the sense that sequentiality is *thin* here, like ice over deep water. You will have the distinct impression that the grinding structures have not always been here, that you watched them emerge from the stone over the course of your time on the plain, even though you arrived minutes ago. You will simultaneously know that they have been here for billions of years. Both are true. The erosion record is definitive: this plain has been grinding itself flat since before the concept of life existed on the world that this is no longer quite a part of. The structures are as old as the flattening. The animals appeared — or have always been — at some point that the word "point" cannot hold.

The fog occasionally thins enough to let you see far — really far, kilometers — and when it does, you realize that the grinding structures extend to the horizon in every direction, a packed field of them, millions, their upper surfaces catching the dim light in ways that make the whole plain look like a surface covered in teeth, or needles, or pistons, or bones. The scale is regional. This is not a valley or a pocket or an anomaly. This is how the lowlands *are*, all the way to wherever the lowlands end, if they end.

You are standing in the engine of something. Or the remains of something. Or the birth of something. The place does not distinguish between these. The animals move around you. The water seeps and films and weeps. The structures grind and process and excrete. The roar fills every frequency. Reality hums at its edges, not breaking exactly, but thinning, like the ice — cracking and reforming around a pressure it cannot sustain and cannot release.
