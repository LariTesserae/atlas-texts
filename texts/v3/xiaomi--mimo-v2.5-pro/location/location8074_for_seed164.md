# The Weeping Plateau

---

You arrive at the edge of something that should not exist, and your first thought is about sound.

Not a particular sound. Sound *as a substance*. The air itself has a texture of noise — a deep, grinding hum that seems to come from below, from the rock, from the bones of the world. It is not deafening. It is worse than deafening. It is the kind of loud that becomes a property of silence itself, so that you cannot remember what quiet would sound like, and begin to doubt that quiet is a real thing.

This is Kethara. Or this is what the settlement-surveyors call Kethara, on the maps they produce from a distance and mail back down to the lowlands in waterproof canisters. No one comes to check the name. No one needs to.

---

## The Geometry

The plateau rises like a wound.

Imagine a section of continental crust the size of a small country — perhaps the size of France, perhaps larger; the surveys disagree because the surveys do not reach the far edge — that has simply *come up*. Not gradually. Not over the eons of slow uplift that build gentler highlands. Kethara appears to have risen in something geologically recent, perhaps the last few thousand years, perhaps less. The rock at the surface is raw. It is basalt and granite and something darker, something with a faint vitreous sheen that the geologists cannot quite classify, and it is *sharp*. Every exposed face is a fresh fracture. Every ridge is a blade edge that has never known weathering.

The elevation is severe. You are above three thousand meters at the lowest margins, and the central massifs — they cannot properly be called mountains because they are not separate from the plateau; they are the plateau rising into its own peaks — push above six or seven thousand. The air should be thin. The air *is* thin. But the air is also saturated with moisture to a degree that seems contradictory, that seems to violate some law of atmospheric science, and so breathing is a paradox: you are gasping in air that is both too thin and too heavy, cold and wet and pressing against your lungs like something that wants to be liquid but has not yet committed.

The edges of the plateau are sharp and vertical. Where Kethara meets the surrounding lowlands, the drop is sudden — sheer basalt walls falling away a thousand meters or more, slick with black water, too steep for erosion to have carved even the beginnings of a gentle slope. This is the geology of something *young*. The kind of young that makes geologists nervous, because young geology means the forces that made it are not yet spent.

No one has found the far edge.

---

## The Water

It weeps.

There is no other word. The rock weeps.

Water appears from the basalt itself — not from springs or streams or any identifiable source, but from the *stone*, condensing and emerging and sliding down every exposed face in slow, black, mineral-heavy rivulets. It gathers in the depressions between ridge-lines and forms marshes that have no right to exist at this altitude, at this temperature. The water is just above freezing, always, and it is dark — not with sediment, not with algae, but with the mineral content of the rock itself, as though the water is still partly stone, still undecided about its phase of matter.

The marshes are vast and shallow, rarely more than knee-deep, spreading across the lower expanses of the plateau in sheets of black glass. In the cold — and it is always cold, just above freezing, a temperature that holds with the same eerie consistency as everything else in Kethara — the water does not evaporate so much as *become air*. Fog is not the right word. Fog implies a boundary between water and atmosphere. Here there is no boundary. The air is the water. The water is the air. You breathe moisture that has no interest in being breathed, that coats your throat and your lungs and sits heavy in your chest, and you are never dry, and you cannot remember what dryness felt like.

Between the marshes, the sharp ridges of young basalt rise like the teeth of some enormous buried jaw, and the water runs along them in sheets and curtains, and the sound of this — the constant, vast, *patienced* sound of water moving over stone — is one of the layers in the great noise of Kethara.

## The Growth

There are no animals. This is stated flatly in the survey reports, stated as fact, stated as something the surveyors checked and rechecked because it seemed wrong. There are plants. There are no animals.

Not *few* animals. Not *mostly no* animals. None. No insects in the moss. No worms in the black soil. No birds in the grey sky. No fish in the shallow marshes. No microfauna that the surveyors could detect with their instruments. The vegetation exists in a world without consumers, without pollinators, without decomposition-assistants, and this should mean that the ecosystem is failing, that the plants should be choking on their own undecayed dead — but they are not. They are lush.

The moss grows a foot thick on every north-facing surface, a deep and complicated green that looks almost black in the permanent low light. It does not rot. When the moss reaches the end of its growth, it simply — stops. The lower layers do not decompose; they compress, slowly, into something that is not quite peat and not quite stone, a substance that the surveyors have no name for, that seems to exist in a state between organic and mineral that should not be possible. The moss grows on top of this and the process continues. The living layer floats on a slowly thickening raft of not-quite-fossil, and the whole arrangement is stable in a way that nothing in a healthy ecosystem ever is.

Above the moss-line, where the ridges rise into the cold and the wet and the thin air, there are other things. Growth-forms that the botanists tentatively classify as lichens but that behave like nothing in the taxonomic record. They grow in spirals. They grow in patterns that repeat at scales too regular to be natural, too complex to be geometric. Some of them are faintly bioluminescent — not enough to read by, not enough to be useful, but enough that the upper ridges, viewed from a distance, carry a faint and sickly pulse of blue-white light that never changes, never brightens, never dims. The surveyors call this the *phosphor-line*, and they do not like to look at it for long.

The vegetation produces no oxygen that the surveyors can detect. It consumes water and produces — something else. The air analysis comes back with trace compounds that do not appear in any chemical reference. Not dangerous, the chemists say. Probably. The plants are breathing a different air than we are, one of the surveyors wrote in her report, and we are living in the waste product.

## The Settlements

They are there. They should not be, but they are.

The maps call them settlements; the structures call themselves cities. They are built into the basalt ridges, carved from the young rock with tools that have left machine-marks too regular and too deep for anything hand-held, and they are *industrial* in a way that suggests not a society that developed industry but a society that *is* industry, that has no other mode, that was built by and for the act of production.

The architecture is layered — literally layered, as though each structure has been added to over time, but the additions are all the same age. This is one of the first things that strikes the surveyors as wrong. The oldest sections of the carved-cities look exactly like the newest sections. The tool marks are identical. The stone has weathered not at all. There is no history in the construction, no evolution of style or technique, just a single moment of building repeated and repeated and repeated until the structures became what they are.

There are engines. They run. The surveyors can hear them from kilometers away — a low, arrhythmic thudding that harmonizes with the grinding of the plateau and the hissing of the water and produces, in combination, the great noise that is Kethara's most immediately overwhelming quality. The engines are industrial-scale: turbines and pistons and something that might be a generator array, all running on something the engineers cannot identify, something that is not steam and not combustion and not electrical but that produces mechanical force in reliable, enormous quantities.

No one operates the engines. This is the second thing that strikes the surveyors as wrong, and it never stops being wrong, no matter how many times they verify it. The cities are empty. The engines are running. The factory-floors — and there are factory-floors, vast halls of machinery that move and stamp and fold and press, producing — what? The engineers cannot determine what is being produced. The machines do not seem to have outputs. They simply *run*, performing their motions with the patience of water, the patience of stone, and the products of their labor, if any, disappear into the infrastructure of the city itself, consumed by more machinery that also runs, also produces nothing identifiable, also consumes.

The electrical systems are active. Bare filaments glow in carved-ceiling corridors, providing a light that is just sufficient, that never varies, that has no apparent source of fuel or mechanism of generation. The surveyors have measured the voltage. It does not change. They have measured it over weeks. It does not change. They have measured the temperature of the filaments. It does not change. The electricity in Kethara is in a state of perfect equilibrium, producing light and heat in exact and unvarying quantities, and no one can find the circuit, no one can find the source, no one can find the ground.

Some of the surveyors have stopped calling the structures cities. They call them *organs*. They do not elaborate on what they mean by this, and when pressed, they change the subject.

## The Sound

It is worth returning to.

The sound of Kethara is not one sound but a *chord* — a composite of frequencies that layer and beat against each other and produce, in the aggregate, something that the ear perceives as a single enormous tone, a drone, a note held by a throat the size of a continent. The components are:

The grinding of the plateau itself — a subsonic vibration that you feel in your teeth, in the marrow of your bones, in the soft tissue of your eyes. The rock is under immense tectonic stress. It is young and it is still being born and the forces that pushed it upward have not released their grip, and the stone groans with the strain, constantly, a sound so low it is almost not a sound at all but a *suggestion* of sound, a memory of sound, the ghost of an explosion still happening.

The water — every surface weeping, every marsh bubbling with the slow release of dissolved gases, every ridge-line curtained with black falling sheets, and all of it hissing and pattering and rushing with a sound that is almost white noise, almost soothing, but not quite, because the frequencies are too regular, too patterned, and the ear begins to hear structure in the randomness, begins to hear *intention*, and this is disturbing in a way that is difficult to articulate.

The engines — thudding and whining and clicking in the carved-cities, a sound that carries for extraordinary distances across the empty plateau, bouncing off the wet basalt and the fog-thick air and arriving at the ear as a ghostly, directionless percussion, as though the machinery is everywhere, as though the plateau itself is running.

The plants — and this is the strangest component, the one the surveyors argue about, the one that makes the junior surveyors look at the senior surveyors and wonder if altitude sickness is affecting their instruments. The plants *make sound*. Not rustling — there is no wind in Kethara, no wind at all, a fact that is itself deeply strange given the altitude and the temperature differentials and the moisture content of the air. The sound the plants make is a *vibration*, a low humming that emanates from the thick moss and the spiraling lichens and the strange not-quite-peat of the compressed dead layers, and it is produced, the acoustic engineers believe, by the movement of water through the plant tissue, a constant drawn-out exhalation of moisture that resonates in the hollow stems and produces a tone that blends, with suspicious precision, into the chord of the plateau.

Together, these sounds produce a noise that the surveyors rate as extreme — louder than a factory floor, louder than a construction site, though quieter than standing next to a jet engine. It is not painful. It is not damaging. But it is *inescapable*, and it never stops, and it never varies, and after enough time in Kethara, the surveyors report that they can no longer hear silence even when they leave, even when they descend to the lowlands, even when they plug their ears and sit in darkened rooms. The sound has gotten into their bones. The sound is *in* the stone of their skeletons, and it will not stop, and some of them suspect it never will.

## The Weather That Isn't

Kethara has no weather.

This is stated in the survey reports with increasing alarm as the surveyors spend more time on the plateau. There is no wind. There is no variation in temperature — it is always just above freezing, always, at every elevation, at every time of day, in every season. The moisture content of the air does not change. The fog does not thin or thicken. The light — a diffuse, grey, directionless illumination that seems to come from the fog itself rather than from any identifiable source in the sky — does not vary in intensity or color.

This is a plateau large enough to generate its own weather systems, and it does not generate them. This is a landscape where temperature differentials between the peaks and the marshes should drive convection currents that should drive wind that should drive weather, and none of this happens. The atmosphere of Kethara is in a state of perfect equilibrium, a closed system, a stillness that contains all that motion and noise within itself and does not let it escape into macroscopic change.

The surveyors have watched rain condense on the basalt ridges. They have watched it form droplets. They have watched the droplets grow and slide and fall. They have watched the droplets strike the marshes and produce ripples. They have watched the ripples spread and dampen. They have gone away and come back weeks later and the same droplet is forming on the same ridge in the same place, growing at the same rate, and they cannot prove that it is the same droplet but they cannot prove that it is not, and this possibility — this suspicion that Kethara does not experience time so much as *perform* it, endlessly, without deviation — is the thing that keeps the surveyors awake at night, lying in their tents on the wet basalt, listening to the great chord of the plateau, staring into the grey and unchanging fog, and wondering if they themselves are changing, or if they too have been absorbed into the equilibrium, if they are now part of the performance, repeating and repeating and never arriving anywhere.

## The Strangeness

There is a quality to Kethara that resists description, that the surveyors circle around in their reports without ever landing on, that emerges only in the accumulation of details that should not coexist:

A landscape that is simultaneously lush and empty. A landscape that is simultaneously loud and still. A landscape that is simultaneously industrial and abandoned. A landscape that is simultaneously young and eternal. A landscape that is simultaneously wet and cold and high and vast and *nothing happens in it*.

The strangeness is not in any one feature. The strangeness is in the *combination*, in the way all of these impossible juxtapositions hold together in a stable configuration, in the way the system does not collapse, does not evolve, does not resolve. Kethara is a contradiction that has found equilibrium. It is a question that has settled into an answer that is also a question. It is a place that should be in the process of becoming something but is instead in the process of *being* something, endlessly, with the patience of geology and the precision of machinery and the mindlessness of water falling on stone.

Some of the surveyors have noted that Kethara does not feel like a place that was *made*. It feels like a place that *occurred* — like a crystal forming in a solution, like a standing wave in a vibrating medium, like a pattern that emerged from the interaction of forces that had no intention of producing it but could not, given their parameters, produce anything else.

The plateau is its own weather. The cities are their own inhabitants. The sound is its own silence. The water is its own stone.

Nothing changes. Nothing will change. The engines will run and the moss will grow and the water will weep and the rock will groan and the fog will hold everything in its grey and endless hand, and Kethara will continue to be Kethara, and the surveyors will continue to arrive and write their reports and leave, and the reports will continue to be filed in waterproof canisters and sent down to the lowlands, and the lowlands will continue to read them and shake their heads and send more surveyors, and none of it will make any difference, and none of it will change anything, and this too is part of the equilibrium, this too is part of the great frozen song of the plateau, this too is Kethara, being itself, without end, without variation, without mercy.

The sound continues. The water weeps. The engines run.

Nothing happens.
