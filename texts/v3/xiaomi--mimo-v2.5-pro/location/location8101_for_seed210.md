# The Engine Cirque

The basin is a bowl scooped from the mountaintop, roughly the size of a cathedral nave — three hundred feet from wall to wall, perhaps four hundred deep, cupped in rock so fresh it still remembers being underground. The walls are nearly vertical, black basalt streaked with quartz veins that catch what little light there is. They are not eroded. They are not weathered. Every edge where one fracture plane meets another is still knife-sharp, still mineral-dark, still shedding microscopic crumbs of themselves into the air like powder from a fresh break. The stone looks *cut*. It looks *yesterday*.

And yet nothing in this place changes.

The sky above is a permanent grey-white overcast that does not move. There is no sun position, no weather front, no shift in the quality of the light across hours or days or seasons. It is always late autumn at three in the afternoon, just after a rain has stopped but before the ground has begun to dry. This is not a metaphor. The rain has just stopped. It will always have just stopped. The air is cold — not bitterly, not with the savagery of a true alpine freeze, but with the persistent, bone-deep chill of a place that has never once been warm. A chill that enters cloth and stays.

---

The floor of the cirque is bog.

Not deep bog — the water table is high, perhaps only inches below the surface, but the basin is too young to have developed the deep peat layers of an ancient wetland. Instead: thin black soil over fractured rock, saturated, every step sinking half an inch before finding something solid. Sphagnum moss grows everywhere in low mats, pale green and copper-red, holding water in its architecture the way a sponge holds a memory. Between the moss mats, shallow pools of water lie still and dark, reflecting the grey sky with perfect fidelity. Cloudberries grow in scattered clumps — small, waxy, orange as warning lights, each one beaded with condensation that never quite drips. Dwarf willows creep along the ground, no taller than a hand, their leaves dark and slick. Cotton grass sends up its pale stalks in loose bunches, already gone to seed, each seed-head a small white explosion that hangs motionless.

Lush, yes. But spare. Each plant grows in its own small territory, separated from its neighbors by inches or feet of wet moss and standing water. The vegetation has density without coverage — every specimen is fat and thriving, deep-colored, trembling with moisture, but the eye can always see between them. The dark ground is always visible. It is a garden with too few plants, each one perfect, none of them connecting.

---

The animals are what make the sound.

It begins below hearing. A pressure in the ears, a vibration felt in the sternum, a sense of machinery operating at a frequency the body registers before the mind does. Then, slowly, it rises into audibility: a deep, arrhythmic *chuffing*, like pistons in a distant engine room, coming from everywhere and nowhere. Layered over this, a high metallic *tick-tick-tick-tick* that seems to come from inside the rock walls themselves — thousands of tiny impacts, slightly out of phase, creating a sound like a watch factory heard through concrete. And underneath it all, a low moan that might be wind but isn't, a resonance as if the basin itself were vibrating at its own natural frequency and couldn't stop.

The source is fauna.

They are here in extraordinary numbers, but you must look carefully. Embedded in the moss at intervals of six or eight feet — half-submerged, almost invisible against the dark ground — are creatures shaped like nautilus shells the size of a human torso. Their surfaces are matte grey-brown, covered in micro-texture like machined metal, each one identical to the others. They are open at one end, the aperture facing upward, and from each opening there emerges a slow, visible exhalation of warm vapor — the only warmth in the cirque. They are *chuffing*. Each one operates at a slightly different rhythm. Together they produce the engine-room sound. They are alive. They have always been here. They do not move.

In the shallow pools, luminous worms — pale, translucent, the length of a forearm — thread through the water in slow sine waves, each one producing a faint ticking sound as it flexes against the surface tension. There are hundreds of them. They glow with a chemical light, faintly blue, giving the pools the look of circuit diagrams seen from above.

Against the basalt walls, where water weeps in thin sheets down the rock face, roosts a colony of small dark birds. They are pressed flat against the stone, wings folded, dozens of them in a loose scatter across the wall face. They do not fly. They do not sing. But at intervals — perhaps every seven minutes, perhaps every seven hours, it is impossible to tell — they all turn their heads at exactly the same moment, in exactly the same direction, with a sound like a single leather book being closed. Then they are still again.

There are things in the mist above the pools that are harder to describe. Flat, nearly two-dimensional shapes, like sheets of wet membrane, that hang in the air at varying heights. They drift so slowly they might not be drifting at all. They catch the dim light and become briefly iridescent — oil-slick colors, bruise-purples, rainbows that last a fraction of a second. They might be alive. They might be something the air is doing. They are not, strictly speaking, *there*.

---

The reality here does not hold firm.

It is not dramatic. There are no visible tears, no portals, no manifest impossibilities. It is subtler than that, and worse. The walls of the cirque — sharp, basalt, physical, real — occasionally present surfaces that do not quite correspond to the geometry behind them. A face of rock will catch the light at an angle that suggests depth where there should be flatness. A shadow will fall in a direction that does not match any light source. If you look at a rock wall for long enough — and in this place, with nothing to do and nowhere to go and a sound like machines you can't find, you will look at rock walls for long enough — you will begin to notice that the wall is not a surface. It is a *convergence*. As if multiple versions of the same wall, from slightly different angles, from slightly different times, are being averaged together into the single wall you see. The sharpness of the features — the young erosion, the fresh fractures — may be because the stone has not yet been *decided*. It is still all its possibilities at once, each one sharp, none of them worn down by the compromise of being singularly real.

The animals know this, or are symptoms of it. The shell-things with their mechanical breathing, the luminous worms, the synchronized birds, the membrane-shapes in the mist — they are all operating at slightly different frequencies of *present*, and the dissonance between their rhythms is the engine sound. They are not out of sync with each other. They are each perfectly in sync with a slightly different version of now. The sound is the sound of multiple realities being averaged.

Nothing here moves toward resolution. The water that weeps from the rock never increases or decreases. The moss neither spreads nor retreats. The shell-things breathe at the same rate they breathed when the walls were uplifted from the deep crust, and they will breathe at this rate when the walls have crumbled to nothing, and neither of those times will come. The cirque was born in violence — the rock remembers being folded, remembers pressure and heat and the shriek of continental collision — and it was frozen the instant after. A photograph of emergence. An eternal first moment.

You could stay here forever. You would not age. You would not change. The cold would sink into your bones and settle there and do nothing further. The ticking would enter your thoughts and become the rhythm of your thinking. You would begin to notice the convergence in the walls. You would begin to notice it in the moss, in the pools, in your own hands. You would realize that you, too, are being averaged — that the you who entered the cirque and the you who has been standing here for what might be minutes or millennia are being blended into a single you, sharp and unresolved, breathing in time with the shell-things, your exhalations joining the mist that never quite clears.

The birds would turn their heads. The worms would trace their waves. The membrane-shapes would shimmer, almost-real.

Nothing would change. Nothing has ever changed. Nothing will.

But the sound is very loud.
