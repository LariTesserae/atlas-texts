# The Lignite Plateau

The first thing you notice is the silence that isn't silent. You arrive expecting quiet — you're high, the air is thin and cool and tastes of something faintly tannic — but the plateau *rings*. It rings with the slow creak of expanding tissue, with the hiss of spore release from structures you initially take for buildings, with a deep subsonic hum that you feel in your sternum more than hear. There is a mechanical grinding somewhere, regular as respiration, echoing across kilometers of open ground. You can't tell if it's a machine or a geologic process. You can't tell if there's a difference anymore.

---

## The Ground

There is no rock. Not anymore.

The surface of the plateau is living — not in the metaphorical sense that poets mean when they say a forest floor breathes, but literally, cellularly, chemically alive. You are standing on compressed biomass that extends down further than anyone has successfully bored. The oldest cores, taken decades ago by expeditions you only know through fading reports, pulled up material dated to periods that shouldn't correspond to surface exposure. The plateau has been here, growing and compressing and growing again, for longer than the mountains around it have existed. Or possibly the mountains grew *from* it. The stratigraphy doesn't agree with itself.

The substrate is dark — almost black in places — and firm underfoot, with a faint give that makes long walks exhausting. In some areas it's dense as hardwood, lignified beyond any living wood you've encountered, so hard that the original survey teams' drill bits fractured against it. In other places it's softer, yielding slightly, and you can feel warmth rising through your boots, as if something deep below is still metabolizing. It smells of tannins and cold resin and something else, something almost mineral, like wet stone — though there is no stone here, and the moisture is intermittent at best.

Water doesn't pool. It appears — condensation forming on the surface in the cool hours before dawn, a slick of moisture that the substrate drinks almost immediately. Occasionally, in the lowest depressions of the plateau, you'll find a shallow sheen, barely enough to wet your fingers, that lasts a few hours. Once, the survey maps showed rivers. They're still marked. But what flows in those channels now is not water — it's a slow, viscous movement of cellular material, a kind of phloem-current in the open air, dark and faintly iridescent. You could mistake it for a river in dim light. You might even try to drink from it. I wouldn't recommend that.

Rain comes maybe twice a season. When it does, the entire plateau *responds*. The sound changes first — every surface element, every standing structure, every ridge and column and branching form adjusts, unfurling or orienting or doing something you can't quite track with your eyes. The hum intensifies. The air fills with aerosolized compounds that make your eyes water and your thoughts run strangely for hours afterward. Then it passes, and the plateau settles back into its sparse, ringing, dry-cool equilibrium.

## The Vegetation

You have to abandon your categories here. They won't hold.

The vegetation is *total* — it is the ground, the walls, the air's particulate load, the substrate, the architecture, the medium through which everything else occurs — but it is not *dense*. This is the paradox that breaks most people's understanding of what they're seeing. A forest is dense. A jungle is dense. This is something else. Imagine an entire city, a real city with districts and infrastructure and vertical structures, but every single component of it is a single organism. Not a colony. Not a cooperative. One organism, immensely old, immensely spaced-out, maintaining its form across a regional expanse with long intervals of open ground between its functional nodes.

The "open ground" — the flat stretches between structures — is the organism's surface. The structures themselves are its organs, or its limbs, or its thoughts made physical. You'll walk for an hour across relatively flat, dark, yielding terrain, the horizon broken only by distant shapes that might be ridges or might be the edge of something purposeful, and then you'll come upon a vertical form rising twenty, thirty, fifty meters from the plateau surface. It will look like a building. It will have walls and apertures and what seem like floors. But the walls are *grown* — smooth in places, textured in others, occasionally branching into buttresses or fenestrations that serve no architectural purpose you can identify. The apertures are not windows. You don't know what they are.

Some of these vertical structures are enormous — cathedral-scaled, kilometers long, their walls curving into forms that a structural engineer would call impossible but that have clearly stood for millennia. The erosion profile is extraordinary: these forms have been weathered into shapes so rounded, so smoothed, so *settled* that they look less like they were built and more like they were always there, the way a boulder looks like it was always there. But they weren't. The internal structure — where you can access it, where the walls haven't sealed shut — reveals organization. Compartments. Channels. Something like plumbing, though what flows through those channels is the same dark phloem you saw in the riverbeds.

The plants — if you insist on calling them plants, and nothing better presents itself — are not green. Not primarily. The dominant coloration is a deep umber-black, with occasional flushes of ochre or rust where new growth is occurring at the edges of structures. In the cool air, certain surfaces bloom with a fine pale fuzz — a condensation-colonizing organism, possibly fungal, possibly something for which you don't have a kingdom — that makes the upper reaches of the taller structures look frost-dusted even when no frost exists. There are no leaves in any conventional sense. Photosynthetic surfaces exist, but they're embedded in the outer layers of structures like skin, thin and dark and efficient in the high-altitude light, which is adequate but not intense.

The spore events are the most disorienting aspect. Periodically — unpredictably, or on a schedule the organism keeps that doesn't correspond to any external cycle you can measure — sections of the plateau surface rupture and release clouds of reproductive material into the air. These clouds are vast, amber-colored, and they move with a deliberateness that suggests not wind-transport but *locomotion*. They bank and turn. They avoid certain areas. They converge on others. They make a sound like tearing fabric, amplified across the open landscape to a volume that approaches painful. You learn to recognize the precursors — a change in the hum, a shift in the ground's temperature — and to move to higher terrain or shelter in a structure's lee. The spore clouds don't seem harmful to breathe, but no one who's been enveloped in one reports the same experience twice. Their accounts differ from each other. Their accounts differ from their own previous accounts.

## The City

It wasn't always like this. Or maybe it was, and the interval of *not being like this* was the aberration.

The structures follow a layout. There are grids — or the ghosts of grids, now warped and partially subsumed into the organism's own geometries. There are what were clearly roads, still maintained as slightly depressed channels in the plateau surface, the phloem-currents flowing through them preferentially. There are districts: areas where the structures are taller and more densely grouped, separated by wider expanses of open ground from other clusters. The spatial logic of a city persists. Someone built here. Someone *organized* here. The organization has been inherited, adopted, perhaps digested — but it remains.

The industrial character is unmistakable once you know what to look for. Many of the larger structures contain internal cavities that house mechanisms — great cylindrical forms, smoothly lignified, that rotate on axes of compressed tissue. They turn. They have always turned, as far as anyone can determine. They make the grinding sound that carries across the plateau, and they are almost certainly the source of the subsonic hum. Whether they serve the organism's own processes or whether they continue the function of whatever human machinery they've replaced is one of the fundamental unanswered questions of the plateau. Some of the expedition's engineers argued that the mechanisms are *too* perfect — too efficient, too frictionless — to be mere copies. They suggested the organism reverse-engineered not just the forms but the *purposes* of the original industrial apparatus. That it found the purposes useful. That it kept them.

There are — or were — electrical systems. Conduits run through the larger structures, no longer copper but a conductive tissue that carries something with measurable voltage. In some districts, the structures still produce light at night — a faint bioluminescence, amber-white, that fills the interior spaces and leaks from the apertures in shifting patterns. The light is not constant. It fluctuates in response to... you don't know what. Atmospheric pressure. Spore cycles. The movement of the phloem-currents. Something internal to the organism's own decision-making, if that's what it is.

The most intact district — the one the first expeditions called the Furnace Quarter, and the name stuck despite no one finding an actual furnace — contains structures that still vent. Columns of warm, moist air rise from the tallest forms at irregular intervals, creating brief updrafts that you can see distorting the light above them. The air that rises smells of metabolic heat and chemical processing. It's warm enough that patches of the surrounding plateau surface near the vents are slightly softer, slightly more active, showing subtle color changes — flushes of something almost green, almost alive in a way the rest of the plateau has moved past. These patches are the closest thing to conventional vegetation you'll find. They don't last. Within days the surface darkens again, re-hardens, resumes its ancient composure.

## The Inhabitants

Animals are almost absent. This is notable. A functioning ecosystem of this scale and complexity should support fauna — invertebrates at minimum, something to fill the trophic gaps. But the plateau is nearly empty of animal life. The few species you encounter are small, hard-shelled, and cautious: beetle-like organisms that navigate the structure surfaces in the hours of dimmest light, feeding on the pale fuzz-growths. They're fast. They vanish into crevices at any vibration. They may be recent arrivals — colonizers from the surrounding lowlands, barely adapted, surviving but not thriving.

The humans are another matter.

There is a settlement. It occupies a central district of the plateau's inherited city-grid, and it is built — genuinely built, with industrial-era materials and techniques — on top of and around and *into* the organism's structures. It's a town of perhaps four thousand people, though counting is difficult because the settlement doesn't have clear boundaries. It bleeds outward into structures that are occupied in some rooms and unoccupied in others, and the distinction between "building" and "cathedral-sized plant-organ" is not always clear to visitors.

The inhabitants are not indigenous. They arrived — their oral histories are specific about this, though the dates are contested — during an industrial period, drawn by the same mineral surveys that initially attracted the first human presence here, long ago, before whatever happened happened. They built a factory-town. They exploited the plateau's conductive tissues for electrical generation. They found, in the deeper structures, materials with industrial applications that no one has been able to replicate at lower altitudes. They stayed. The plateau, apparently, permitted this.

The town is loud. The people are loud. Their machinery — old, maintained with desperate ingenuity, supplemented where possible by the plateau's own mechanisms — clatters and roars and fills the Furnace Quarter with a sound that blends seamlessly into the ambient grinding and humming of the organism around them. Children play on surfaces that are technically alive. Laundries hang from apertures in walls that occasionally exhale warm air. The settlement has the feeling of a place that has negotiated a truce with something much larger and older and doesn't fully understand the terms.

At night, the bioluminescence of the surrounding structures illuminates the town in amber-white light, and the humans' own electric lights — harsher, more blue — fight against it, creating zones of color-clash that make the streets look strange and doubled. The people sleep poorly. They always have. It's not the noise, they say. It's the *feeling*. Something in the air, something in the hum, something in the way the ground is warm and soft and *alive* beneath their beds. They dream strangely. They dream of being very small inside something very large. They dream of roots.

## The Break

The weirdness is not subtle. It's not a rumor or a local legend. It's measurable, documentable, and profoundly disturbing.

At the edges of the plateau — where the organism's surface gives way, eventually, to conventional rock and scree and thin mountain air — the rules of geometry begin to lose their grip. Distances aren't consistent. You can pace off a hundred meters, mark it, return the next day, and find the distance has changed. Not by much. Not enough to be certain. But enough to notice, if you're careful, if you're the kind of person who measures things. The plateau itself appears to be slightly larger than it was during the last survey. The expansion is not explained by growth in any conventional sense. The edges aren't advancing. The interior is simply... *more*.

Inside the settlement, the breakdown is subtler but more personal. People see structures that aren't there when they look directly at them. Not ghosts, not visions — architectural elements that exist in peripheral vision and resolve into wall or surface or open air when focused upon. This is so common that the inhabitants have developed vocabulary for it. They call it *bleeding*, and they don't discuss it with outsiders, and when pressed, they shrug and say the place has always done that and it's not dangerous. They seem to believe this.

The deeper structures are worse. The expeditions that penetrated furthest into the organism's interior — following phloem-channels, mapping compartment-systems, trying to understand the mechanism — reported progressive disorientation that no equipment could counteract. Compasses spin. Timekeeping becomes unreliable. Two expedition members who entered a deep structure together and emerged separately, minutes apart by their own watches, were found to have been missing for *days* by the settlement's clocks. Neither had any memory of the intervening time. Both had faint amber discoloration on their hands and feet that took weeks to fade. Neither reported pain. Both reported, independently, and only once, that they had understood something while inside — something about the organism, about the plateau, about the relationship between living tissue and spatial geometry — but the understanding was gone when they tried to speak it. It had receded like a dream. It left only a residue: a calm, a certainty, a quiet conviction that the plateau was not breakdown but *coherence* — that the reality they were accustomed to was the fragmented version, and this place was what wholeness felt like.

The residents of the settlement, when told this, nodded. They'd heard it before. Their grandparents had heard it before.

They stayed anyway.

## What You See When You Leave

Looking back from the trail that switchbacks down the plateau's eroded eastern edge — ancient, rounded, the living surface giving way to conventional scree in a transition zone that smells suddenly and heartbreakingly of ordinary dirt — the settlement is invisible. You see only the plateau: vast, dark, faintly steaming from the Furnace Quarter vents, its structures rising from the surface like the exposed roots of something whose true mass extends in every direction and downward past knowing. The sound follows you for a long time. The hum, the grinding, the occasional tearing-fabric roar of a spore release carrying its amber cloud across kilometers of open living ground.

The light is normal. The sky is a high-altitude blue, hard and clear. The temperature is cool and will stay cool.

You will not dream of roots tonight. But in a week, in a month, when you've descended to wherever you're going and the sounds have faded entirely from memory — you'll wake one night and feel, very faintly, a warmth beneath you. A softness. A vibration so low it might be your own pulse.

It might be.

You won't check.
