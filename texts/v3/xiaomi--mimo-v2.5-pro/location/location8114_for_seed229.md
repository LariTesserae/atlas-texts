# The Resonance Cavern of Ghal Unekh

You arrive by the absence of things.

The trail up from the lower slopes has been losing features for hours — fewer stones, fewer marks, the soil thinning to paleite powder that crumbles underfoot likeiteiteite calciumite dust. The air thins and warms simultaneously, which is wrong, which is the first sign. You should be cooler at this elevation. You are not cooler. Something below is breathing heat upward through the rock, and the rock remembers.

The entrance is a crack in a granite shoulder — not dramatic, just a split where two faces of stone have been pulling apart for centuries, the gap wide enough for a person to turn sideways and slip through. The walls are smooth and warm to the touch. They have been rubbed by passage, though by what and how recently you cannot say.

---

You emerge into the interior of Ghal Unekh, and everything changes.



The chamber is not large. Perhaps the size of a cathedral nave, perhaps smaller — it's difficult to judge because the space refuses to hold still. The ceiling is lost in darkness perhaps forty meters up, but the walls close in around you at maybe sixty meters in any direction. The floor is a shallow bowl ofite calcified dust andite mineral crust, pale as bone meal, utterly devoid of moisture. There is no water here. There has not been water here for a very long time. The air is dry enough to split your lips within minutes.

And yet the place *thrives*.

---

**The growths.**

They cover the walls in cascading sheets, rising from the floor in clumps and arching formations, dangling from the ceiling in curtains that sway with no wind. They are not plants — not in any way you would recognize. They have no roots in soil. They draw nothing from the ground. They are *membrane* — thin, taut, translucent sheets of organic tissue stretched over frameworks of something likeite chitin oriteite mineralized cartilage, pale gold and dusky rose and the color of a bruise three days old. Some are the size of dinner plates, flat and circular, vibrating visibly. Others are taller than you, shaped like warped lily pads stacked on vertical spines, their surfaces shivering with a motion that has nothing to do with breeze.

They are listening.

They are *feeding*.

They eat sound. They drink vibration. Their membranes are tuned to specific frequencies — you can see this in the way different growths respond to different noises, some stiffening, some going slack, some rippling in long slow waves. The largest formations on the walls have clearly been growing for decades, perhaps centuries, their lower portions calcified intoite rigid frames while their upper edges remain supple and alive, perpetually newborn, perpetually hungry for the next wave of noise.

And there is so much noise.



---

**The fauna.**

Ghal Unekh is full of creatures, and every one of them is *loud*.

The most numerous are the striders — six-legged things about the size of your forearm, with bodies likeite polished obsidian and heads that end in flat, spoon-shaped resonating chambers. They spend their lives in constant motion across the walls and floor, and as they move, they *sing*. Not melodically. They produce a rapid, percussive clicking from the resonating plates on their heads, a sound like someone rhythmically cracking walnuts on a tile floor, multiplied by hundreds. By *hundreds*. There are hundreds of them visible at any moment, and their clicking bounces off the walls and feeds the membrane growths, which in turn produce faint hums and tones of their own that the striders seem to navigate by. When a cluster of striders passes near a large membrane sheet, the sheet lights up — literally, a faint phosphorescence igniting along its veins — and the striders change course toward it, clustering, clicking harder, feeding it, being fed back in guidance and shelter.



But the striders are the ambient noise, the baseline hum. The real volume comes from the callers.

The callers are larger — roughly the size of a large dog, though shaped nothing like one. They are low-slung, armored in overlapping plates of dull copper-brown, with long forelimbs that end in broad, flat pads and mouths that open vertically, splitting their heads from crown to throat. They are scattered across the chamber, perhaps fifteen or twenty of them visible at once, each occupying a small territory on the wall or on one of the freestanding rock columns that rise from the floor likeite fingers.

They *call*. Each one produces a single sustained tone — a deep, chest-vibrating drone that they hold for minutes at a time before pausing to breathe. Each caller's tone is slightly different in pitch. The effect, with fifteen or twenty of them droning simultaneously, is a chord — an enormous, shifting, dissonant chord that never resolves, that breathes in and out as individual callers pause and resume, that fills the chamber from floor to ceiling and makes your sternum buzz. The membrane growths go wild in response. They brighten. They sway. They produce secondary harmonics, ghostly overtones that layer onto the callers' drones, and the combined sound is physical — you feel it pressing on your skin, settling in your sinuses, making your vision swim at the edges.

This is how the ecosystem works. The callers produce the fundamental vibration. The striders produce the percussive complexity. The membrane growths capture both, metabolize the acoustic energy into something biochemical, and in return they glow, they grow, they produce the faint sweet-sharp chemical scent that permeates the chamber — the only food source for the striders, who scrape microorganisms from the membrane surfaces. The callers eat the striders. The growths grow. The callers call louder.

A closed loop. A sonic ecosystem. Waterless. Ancient. *In balance.*

---

**The remains.**



There are structures here. Faint ones. You nearly miss them in the dim light — which comes entirely from the bioluminescent membranes and from a dull, sourceless amber glow that seems to emanate from the rock itself, as though the stone has been slowly baked by centuries of vibration into a low phosphorescence.

Along the eastern wall, near the floor, someone — *something* — has stacked stones. Not randomly. The stacks are uniform in height, roughly waist-high, each composed of flat slabs of the pale local rock laid in careful horizontal courses with smaller pieces wedged between them. There are seven of them, arranged in a rough arc. They are old. Mineral crust has begun to seal their joints. The membrane growths have colonized their surfaces, stretching between them in golden sheets, using the stacked stones as scaffolding.

They are shelters, you realize. Or they were. Windbreaks. Corrals. Something built by something that understood the value of walls. The builders are gone — long gone — and the growths have claimed the architecture, which has in turn become indistinguishable from the landscape, which was perhaps always the point.

Near one of the stacks, half-buried in dust, you find a tool. A flat piece of stone, chipped along one edge into a rough cutting surface. The chip marks are deliberate, controlled, but not refined. Whoever made this knew how to fracture stone along its grain, but had not yet learned to pressure-flake. It is warm to the touch, as everything here is warm.

There are also marks on the walls themselves — faint scratches, perhaps intentional, perhaps the result of creatures rubbing against the rock for millennia. But some of the marks are grouped. Some repeat. You are not sure they are writing, but you are not sure they are not.

---

**The turbulence.**



Ghal Unekh is not calm.

The air moves constantly, pushed around by thermal differentials — heat rising from below, cooler air (relatively speaking, nothing here is truly cool) seeping in through the crack-entrance and through fissures you can't see. The effect is a perpetual slow churning, eddies and vortices that carry dust and shed membrane-fibers and the faint chemical exhalations of the growths in lazy spirals through the chamber. Sometimes a thermal pulse comes up from deep in the mountain — the rock groans, a subsonic rumble you feel in your molars — and the entire chamber lurches. The callers pause mid-drone. The striders scatter. The membranes go flat and dark. Then the pulse passes, and everything resumes, louder than before, as though compensating for the silence.

Every few hours, a larger event. A harmonic convergence, you might call it — the callers' drones accidentally align, their frequencies stacking into a standing wave that fills the chamber like a bell being struck from inside. The membranes flare to full brightness, the light shifting from amber to something closer to white. The dust in the air forms visible patterns — nodal lines, cancellation zones, areas of stillness surrounded by roaring vibration. The striders ride these patterns, clustering at the nodes where the vibration is least, massing in dense, clicking clouds. The callers rear up on their hind legs and *scream* — a new sound, a harmonic distortion of their usual drone, almost painful.

Then the convergence breaks. The frequencies drift apart. The light dims. The dust falls. The chamber exhales.

These events are getting closer together. You have been here for two hours and you have witnessed three. The membrane growths are brighter than they should be, denser than the ecosystem's balance would predict. Something is feeding more energy into the system than the callers alone provide. The warmth from below is intensifying. The rock groans more frequently.

Ghal Unekh is in balance — has been in balance for a very long time, long enough for the growths to calcify and build their ancient architectures, long enough for the callers to evolve their resonating throats and the striders their percussive crowns. But balance is not the same as stasis. Balance is a thing in motion, compensating, adjusting, teetering. The erosion patterns on the walls — the way the stone has been sculpted by centuries of vibration into smooth, flowing curves — tell you this place has been changing shape for a very long time, and it has always been changing in the same direction: *inward*. The chamber is slowly collapsing. The walls are slowly closing. The acoustic properties are slowly concentrating.

The growths are growing faster because the sound is getting louder because the chamber is getting smaller because the growths are growing faster.

You should not stay long. The air is breathable but dry, warm, strange with chemicals you have no names for. The sound presses on you physically, a constant pressure that you have stopped noticing consciously but that your body has not stopped registering. Your heart has synced, without your permission, to the lowest caller's drone. You notice this when the caller pauses and your heart stutters, trying to continue the rhythm without its cue.

The crack-entrance is still visible behind you. The amber light catches its edges. It has not changed.

But the walls are closer than you remember.

---

*Go. Describe what you saw. Use whatever words you have. There will not be a second visit — not because you are forbidden, but because there will not be a second Ghal Unekh. It is eating itself. It has always been eating itself. The sound is the appetite, and the appetite is growing, and when the walls finally meet — in a hundred years, or ten, or tomorrow — the last convergence will be absolute, a single perfect tone, and every membrane will light at once, and then: silence. And from the silence: whatever comes next. Perhaps something that drinks silence the way these growths drink sound. Perhaps nothing. The stone will not tell you. The stone is warm, and patient, and full of vibration, and has its own opinions about endings.*
