# The Cavity of Furrows

---

You would have to crack the basalt open to find it. There is no other way in.

The chamber is the size of a root cellar — perhaps three meters long, two wide, not quite tall enough to stand upright. The ceiling bulges with botryoidal growths of fresh, unweathered manganese-iron oxide, black and warty, and the floor is a shallow bowl of the same material, still warm enough that the air shimmers just slightly above it. This is a gas pocket in cooling basalt, a bubble caught mid-inflation as the surrounding flow stiffened and stopped, somewhere between liquid and solid. The walls are raw. You can see where the rock froze in the act of folding over itself — twisted lamellae of dark glass and duller phenocrysts, all of it still radiating the stored heat of the flow that made it. There has been no rain here, no wind, no lichen, no roots. Nothing has touched these surfaces except what lives here now.

What lives here now is *loud.*

---

The cavity is dense with *furnace beetles* — a name anyone might give them, though they have no formal taxonomy. They are arthropod-like, six-limbed, no larger than a thumbnail as adults, and they cover every surface so thoroughly that you would have to search for bare rock. They press against each other, carapace to carapace, a living shingle. Their exoskeletons are metallic and dull — iron-sulfide dark, gunmetal, tarnished copper — and they run slightly hotter than their environment, which is already hot. Not boiling. But close to what skin can bear, and past it in some places near the lower wall where residual heat pools.

They are doing something to the rock. Each beetle clings to the mineral surface with mandible-tipped forelimbs that are not grasping but *raking.* They scrape microfurrows into the fresh oxide crust — shallow channels, perhaps half a millimeter deep, scored in parallel lines across their territory. The furrows are consistent. Not random. Each beetle works a zone roughly the diameter of a coin, and the furrows within that zone radiate outward from the center like the spokes of a wheel. When you look at the wall from a slight distance, the overlapping work-zones tile together in a pattern that has no master plan but is locally disciplined — disciplined enough that the whole surface takes on a textile quality, a woven roughness.

They are not eating the rock, exactly. They are *texturing* it. The raking exposes fresh crystal faces, and the exhalations from their respiratory surfaces — faintly acidic, sulfurous — react with those fresh faces to precipitate a thin film of chalcopyrite, a copper-iron sulfide that blooms as a dull golden sheen along each furrow. The beetles then lap at this sheen. They are farming a reaction. The furrow is the field. The precipitate is the crop. The sound this produces — thousands of tiny mandibles on basalt, all scraping in asynchronous rhythms — is a constant dry rasp that fills the chamber at a volume that would force conversation into shouting. It is the sound of a textile mill heard from inside the loom. There are sharper sounds too: when two adjacent beetles' work-zones overlap and one turns to drive the other away, their carapaces click together with a brief, bright percussion, like a single typewriter key struck hard.

---

The heat is uneven. The walls vary — there are hotter veins where a feeder conduit ran close to the surface before the flow solidified, and cooler stretches of glassy rind. The beetles track these gradients without appearing to move, or rather: they move so slowly, over hours and days, that any given moment looks like stillness. A colony will drift imperceptibly toward a warmer wall section, abandoning the cooling opposite side, and the abandoned rock accumulates a faint white crust of sublimated salts that nothing touches. The migration is glacial. The chamber is changing on a timescale of weeks and months — cooling, stiffening, losing its heat — and the beetles are retreating inward with it, toward the last warm places. Eventually the cavity will go dark in a different way: not just lightless, but thermally dead, and the furrows will stop.

But that has not happened yet.

---

There is no water. The beetle metabolism does not use it. The chemistry is sulfur-driven, anaerobic, catalyzed by the mineral surfaces they prepare for themselves. There is no mist, no condensation on the walls, no moisture of any kind. The air is dry enough to sting the sinuses — if you had sinuses here, which you don't, because you cracked the basalt open and now you are looking in. The atmosphere inside the cavity is a thin soup of sulfur dioxide, hydrogen sulfide, carbon dioxide, and trace carbonyl sulfide, maintained by the collective exhalation of the beetles and the slow degassing of the rock. It smells like struck matches and old pennies. It would not sustain you.

The darkness is total, except that it isn't quite. The chalcopyrite precipitate has a faint reflective quality — not luminescence, but a dull responsiveness to any stray photon. There are no stray photons. But if there were — if you cracked the basalt and let even a thin blade of daylight fall across the far wall — you would see the furrows ignite in a dull, scattered gold, each scored channel catching the light differently than the untouched basalt around it. The whole surface would look like hammered metal. Like the inside of a reliquary. For that one instant, the beetles' patient industry would be legible as ornament, and the cavity would look like something made.

It was not made. It was grown into. But the distinction is thin here, and getting thinner.

---

The sound persists in memory even after the basalt is sealed again — that dense, dry, scraping roar, like a factory floor running a product no one ordered, no one ships, no one consumes except the workers themselves.
