# The Turbine Pocket

You have to duck to enter. The access corridor narrows to a steel throat — shoulders brushing corrugated walls frosted with a skin of hoarfrost so thin it looks painted on — and then the space opens, just barely, into a chamber the size of a living room. That's the word that comes to mind first, though nothing here resembles a room anyone would live in. *Living room.* As if the phrase traveled here from some other kind of world and broke apart on arrival.

The walls are riveted plate steel, each panel stamped with a serial number whose paint has cracked along the frost lines but not yet flaked. The edges are sharp. Every corner is a clean industrial angle — bent, welded, bolted into place not long ago in the long memory of machines. There is no patina here, no softening. The steel still carries the ghost-blue of the rolling mill. If you press your palm to it, the cold does not creep into your hand. It *grabs*.

---

The temperature is the first thing that reorganizes your sense of self. Not the sound — the sound takes longer to do its damage. The cold arrives first and makes everything else a secondary problem. It is the kind of cold that doesn't feel like weather; it feels like *absence*, as though heat were a substance that has been deliberately removed, wrung out, extracted the way you'd extract oil. Your breath doesn't fog. It crystallizes instantly into a fine glitter that falls straight down — no drift, no linger — and collects in pale dust on the floor grating.

The floor is grating. Open steel mesh over blackness. Not true darkness — through the gaps you can see the dull glow of thermal elements bolted to the underside of the chamber, their surfaces a deep infrared warmth that never reaches upward. They are structural. They keep the machinery below from seizing. They are not for you.

---

The noise.

The noise is the room. The noise is the *point* of the room.

Four turbines — no, not turbines exactly, something between a turbine and a compressor, each one the size of a refrigerator laid on its side — are bolted to a central frame that runs the length of the chamber like a spine. They spin at speeds that make the air around them behave less like a gas and more like a vibrating solid. The sound they produce is not mechanical in the way you expect mechanical sound to be — rhythmic, identifiable, something you could tap your foot to. It is a continuous wall of pressure-frequency, a white roar that has no peaks and no valleys, that has been so constant for so long that it has become the acoustic equivalent of stone. You feel it in your molars. You feel it in the joints of your fingers. After a few minutes — or what you think are minutes; time is difficult here — you stop hearing it the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat, and instead you *inhabit* it. The sound becomes the container. The steel walls are secondary.

There are no mufflers. No acoustic damping. No baffles or foam or any concession to the idea that something in this chamber might want to hear anything else. The engineers who built this place — and it was *built*, all of it, every surface assembled and fitted and fastened — did not design for ears. They designed for throughput.

---

What the turbines push, or process, or generate, is not visible. There are no labels, no indicator lights, no screens. The pipes that feed into and out of the compressor units are thick-walled, seamless, and they disappear into the walls through compression fittings so tight the steel has deformed slightly around them — a permanent crush-grip. Whatever flows through those pipes does so in the dark, in the cold, in secret. It might be gas. It might be something that isn't gas anymore at this temperature. The chemistry here doesn't need to be aqueous. It doesn't need to be *anything* you have a word for.

The only fluid evidence is the frost, and even that is more mineral than frozen water — a crystalline deposit of something that sublimates off the pipe joints and settles on every horizontal surface in delicate, dendritic structures that look, under the light, like iron filings arranged by a careful hand. Every few weeks — or what a calibrated instrument would register as weeks — a robotic arm mounted to the ceiling track sweeps the grating clear. It is the only movement that isn't the turbines. It makes no sound you can distinguish from the roar. It has a single red indicator on its chassis, and it is always on, and it does not blink.

---

The light is steady. Four industrial fixtures recessed into the ceiling, sealed behind thick glass lenses rimed with frost, casting a flat and thorough illumination that renders everything in the same even register. There are no shadows to speak of — the light comes from enough angles to erase them — and so the room has a peculiar quality of *flatness*, as though it existed not in three dimensions but in two and a half. Things have presence but not depth. The turbines, the pipes, the grating, the thin scatter of crystalline frost — everything is equally near. You cannot find the corner of this room. Not because it's large, but because the light and the sound together dissolve the edges of spatial reasoning.

On the far wall — if it is the far wall; orientation is generous here — someone has bolted a small plaque. It is not a warning. It is not instructional. It bears a six-digit number and the word **HOLDING** in a typeface that was chosen for function, not aesthetics. Below that, in smaller stamping: *No biological maintenance. Revisit on structural schedule only.*

That's the room's only poetry.

---

Nothing grows. Nothing lives. There was never an attempt to make this a place for either. The sparse traces of something almost biological — a film, a residue — on the underside of one pipe junction might be a colony of chemolithotrophic bacteria feeding on mineral condensate, or it might be a stain. It does not move. It does not spread. It is as frozen in its quiet metabolism as everything else.

The chamber does not change. The turbines spin at the same speed they have always spun. The frost accumulates at the same rate it is swept away. The temperature holds. The sound holds. The light holds. If you left and came back in a year, or ten, or a thousand, you would find it exactly as it was — the same dust in the same patterns on the same grating — because the equilibrium here is not something achieved. It is something *installed*. This place was built to do one thing at one setting forever, and it is doing it, and it will not stop, and it will not vary, and the sound of that constancy is so loud it has become indistinguishable from silence.

You press your hand to the wall one more time. The steel is sharp-edged, factory-cold, and exactly as old as it was when you first touched it. You pull your hand away. The frost reforms on the surface in a pattern that looks like the outline of your palm for exactly one second before the next sweep of crystalline dust buries it.

There is nothing here that needs you. There is nothing here that knows you came.

The room holds.
