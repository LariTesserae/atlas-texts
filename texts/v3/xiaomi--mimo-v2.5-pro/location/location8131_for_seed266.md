# The Basalt Plain of Urrath

---

You are standing on the roof of the world, and the roof goes on forever.

The surface beneath your feet is stone — not loose scree, not gravel, but a single continuous skin of dark basalt, warm from geothermal gradient, worn by incomprehensible ages into a texture like rough leather. It stretches in every direction: a plain so vast that its edges are hidden not by curvature but by atmospheric haze, by the slow accumulation of mineral dust in the air. The horizon is a soft brownish-white line, featureless except where the occasional mesa or ruin breaks it, dozens of kilometers distant and appearing to float above the heat shimmer.

You are at extreme elevation. The air is thin and dry — breathable, but only just, and only if you are something that breathes this air natively. It carries almost no moisture. It has not carried meaningful moisture in a very long time. There is no rain here. There are no clouds, only the occasional high-altitude haze of suspended silicate dust that diffuses the sunlight into a permanent warm amber cast. The sun is visible as a pale disk, shrunken slightly by altitude, and at midday the light on the stone is a color somewhere between cream and old bone.

This is not a dead world. It is an *old* world — a world that has finished most of its work.

---

## The Shape of the Land

The erosion here is ancient beyond easy reckoning. Whatever mountains once existed have been planed down over geological epochs into something more patient: broad mesa formations, their flat tops at slightly different elevations, creating a kind of vast stepped landscape. Some mesas rise fifty meters above the plain, some five hundred. Their flanks are sheer on the windward side — carved vertical by billions of years of abrasive mineral wind — and gently sloped on the lee side, where dust has accumulated in slow fans. The edges of these formations are rounded and soft, the sharpness long gone.

Between the mesas, the plain is not truly flat. It undulates in very long, very slow waves — ridges and swells with wavelengths measured in kilometers, the surface expression of deep tectonic forces still working at their own impossibly patient pace. The stone here is scored with channels, not from water but from thermal expansion and contraction: long, shallow grooves running roughly north-south, each one the product of a million hot days and cool nights cracking the basalt along its grain. Over time, these grooves widen into small corridors, and the corridors collect dust, and the dust compacts into a secondary mineral — a pale, chalkyite substance that contrasts with the dark basalt.

Where the erosion is deepest, you find the salt pans: vast, perfectly flat basins, sometimes kilometers across, where ancient mineral deposits have concentrated into a pale, almost luminous white surface. They glow faintly in the amber light. The surface of a salt pan is hard enough to walk on — a crust of evaporite minerals, primarily sulfates and chlorides — but beneath the crust, in some pans, there are still slow brines, relic moisture from an epoch when water existed here in quantity. These brines are not water as you might recognize it. They are thick, mineral-saturated, nearly solid at ambient temperature. They move on timescales of centuries.

## The Fauna

This is the strangest part of Urrath, and the part that tells you this world is not merely barren but *otherwise alive*.

There are no plants. There is nothing photosynthetic in any macroscopic sense. The surface is naked stone and mineral crust. And yet the plain moves.

They are called — by the few outside observers who have catalogued this world — the **lithomorphs**, though the inhabitants of Urrath have their own names, dozens of names, because they recognize distinctions invisible to outsiders. Lithomorphs are organisms, if that word applies, that metabolize geology itself. They are warm-blooded in a sense — their internal heat comes not from chemical combustion alone but from controlled piezoelectric processes, minerals under pressure generating the tiny voltages that drive their slow biochemistry. They are abundant. In a good stretch of open basalt plain, you will see dozens within any kilometer, and in the denser feeding grounds near active thermal vents, hundreds.

They are *quiet*. This is the first thing you notice. They move without sound — or rather, the sounds they make are subsonic, below your hearing, felt more than perceived: a vibration in the stone beneath your feet as a herd passes. They have bodies of calcium-silicate composite, flexible in the way that certain ceramic laminates are flexible, and they move with a gliding, deliberate gait that covers ground steadily without urgency. The largest are the size of buildings — low, broad, multi-limbed forms that scrape the mineral crust with rasping mouthparts and convert it slowly into energy and body mass. The smallest are the fast ones, no bigger than a hand, that skitter across the open stone and feed on the waste of the larger.

Their colors are the colors of the land: dark basalt grey, chalky white, the amber-brown of ferric dust. They are nearly invisible when still. And they are *often* still. A lithomorph may stand motionless for days, processing a meal, its only sign of life the faint heat shimmer above its dorsal surface. They reproduce by a process no one has satisfactorily explained — something involving mineral nucleation, a splitting or budding that leaves both parent and offspring apparently unchanged in mass. Perhaps they are drawing substance from the stone itself. Perhaps they are, in some sense, *part of the geology*, organized mineral systems that learned to move.

Near the thermal vents — where the basalt is thinnest and geothermal heat breaks the surface in slow, shimmering exhalations — the lithomorphs congregate, and here the ecosystem reveals its full complexity. There are predator analogues: lean, angular forms with crystalline jaw-structures that strike and crack the shells of the grazers. There are scavenger-particles, microscopic, that form visible clouds above a carcass — a grey dust that is not dust. There are symbiotes that ride the backs of the great grazers, cleaning mineral deposits from their surfaces. None of this makes noise. The vents hiss very softly. The lithomorphs are silent. The predator-strike, when it comes, is a sharp *crack* of ceramic-on-ceramic, the loudest sound in this biome, and then silence again.

## The Settlements

The cities of Urrath are old. They are built on and into the mesa formations — carved from the stone, in many cases, so that the settlement and the geology are difficult to distinguish at a distance. The architecture is heavy, thick-walled, suited to the thin air and the slow thermal cycling. You see long terraces cut into mesa flanks, their surfaces glazed with a dark ceramic that collects and stores heat. You see towers, but squat ones — the wind at this elevation, even the thin wind, exerts real force over time, and the structures are shaped to shed it.

The population is sparse relative to the space. A city here might house tens of thousands in a footprint that sprawls across a mesa top and down its flanks, but between cities there are hundreds of kilometers of empty plain. The roads between them are not roads in the conventional sense but rather marked channels in the basalt — grooves deepened and straightened by hands and tools, navigable by the industrial vehicles that move between settlements. These vehicles are large, low-slung, riding on broad ceramic treads that distribute their weight across the stone. They move slowly. Everything here moves slowly.

The industry is geothermal and mineral-processing. Great heat-exchangers are sunk into the stone near the thermal vents, drawing energy from the planet's interior. Factories on the mesa edges refine the mineral wealth of the plain — sulfates, silicates, rare elements concentrated by aeons of geological sorting. The smoke stacks are short and wide, releasing mineral vapor that drifts in the thin air and eventually settles into the dust. The air quality, by outside standards, is poor — a permanent fine particulate haze — but the inhabitants of Urrath are adapted to it, as they are adapted to the thin atmosphere, the dryness, the altitude, the quiet.

There is electricity, though it moves through conductive mineral-ceramic cables rather than metal wire — metal is rare on Urrath, one of the things the industry seeks and refines from trace deposits. The lights in the cities are dim and warm-toned, matching the ambient light outside, as if the inhabitants see no reason to fight the color of their world. At night, the settlements glow faintly against the dark mesa silhouettes, and the only sounds are the deep subsonic hum of the heat-exchangers and the occasional distant crack of a lithomorph predator on the plain below.

---

## The Atmosphere of the Place

What strikes you, if you come from elsewhere, is the *patience* of Urrath. The landscape does not hurry. The weather — and there is weather, of a kind: slow mineral-dust storms that take days to cross the plain, thermal winds that reverse on cycles of weeks, the rare and spectacular phenomenon of salt-pan deliquescence when humidity briefly, briefly rises — exists on timescales that feel geological. Change here is measured in centuries. The mesas are lower by a few millimeters each millennium. The salt pans shift their boundaries by meters each century. The lithomorphs' herds migrate in vast, continent-spanning circuits that take years to complete.

The quiet is not empty. It is *full* — full of subsonic vibration, of the faint hiss of geothermal vents, of the distant ceramic crack of predation, of the low industrial hum from the mesa-top cities. But these sounds are low, and slow, and they blend into a silence that is more like the silence of a sleeping body than the silence of absence. You are standing in a world that is alive, and working, and vast, and it simply does not see any reason to be loud about it.

The stone is warm beneath your feet. The air smells of sulfate and hot dust. A herd of lithomorphs passes on the plain below, silent and grey and enormous, moving with the unhurried certainty of something that has been walking this route since before the first city was carved from the mesa wall. Above them, a predator watches from a ridge, patient as the land. Above everything, the pale sun holds its position in the amber sky, and the light is the color of old bone, and it does not change.
