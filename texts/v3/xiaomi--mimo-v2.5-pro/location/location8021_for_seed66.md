# The Weeping Downs

## The Stone

The bedrock is the oldest thing here, and it knows it. A highland basin the size of an inland sea, tilted and uplifted so long ago that the memory has settled into its bones the way water settles into clay. The surface has been worn past sharpness into something more like weather itself — broad domes of dark basalt, smoothed shoulders of granite that rise from the wet ground like the backs of sleeping animals, gullies cut so deep and so slow that their walls have become concave, cupped inward like the palms of enormous hands. The stone is dark — grey-black, dark umber, the occasional vein of rust-red where iron has wept outward and stained everything for hundreds of meters.

Everything weeps. That is the first thing anyone notices and the last thing they ever stop noticing.

Water seeps from every surface. Not dramatically — not in waterfalls or rushing springs — but in a constant, patient, sweating exhalation. The rock faces glisten. Pebble-strewn slopes have a sheen to them that never dries. Where the ground is flat, water gathers in shallow pans that stretch for kilometers, no more than ankle-deep, their surfaces trembling with the constant upwelling of moisture from below. The water table is not *below* the surface so much as *at* the surface, or slightly above it, always looking for the lowest wrinkle to settle into before overflowing and finding the next one. In the deeper gullies, streams move with a slow, milky deliberation, carrying dissolved minerals that give the water a faint opalescent quality — pale blue, pale green, shifting with the angle of light.

There are no rivers here in any grand sense. There is only the seep, the sheet, the endless lateral movement of water across stone, finding its way downslope in the least dramatic fashion possible. The air itself is wet. Not fog exactly — visibility remains good for kilometers across the open downs — but the air has *weight* to it, a warmth and a closeness that settles on skin and stays. Moisture condenses on every surface that is even slightly cooler than the air, which means everything metal, everything stone in shadow, everything tall enough to catch a breeze. The world beads and drips.

## What Grows (Almost Nothing)

There are no plants. This is the second thing that strikes the eye, and it strikes hard, because everything else about this place — the warmth, the water, the light — suggests green abundance. But there is no green. Not a blade of grass. Not a moss. Not a fern frond unfurling from a crevice. The ecology of the Weeping Downs made a choice long ago, or had a choice made for it, and the result is a world of stone and water and animals with almost nothing in between.

What *does* exist at the base of the food web are microbial mats — thick, rubbery crusts of chemosynthetic organisms that coat the shallowest pools and the wettest stone faces. They are not green. They range from charcoal grey to a dull rust-orange to, in some pools, a startling pale violet. They smell faintly of sulfur and something older, something like iron filings dissolved in warm broth. They are the foundation. Everything else eats them, or eats something that eats them. Fruiting bodies occasionally emerge from the thicker mats — not mushrooms exactly, but columnar protrusions the size of fingers, pale and waxy, that release spore-clouds into the humid air before dissolving back into the general slick. This is the closest thing to vegetation, and it is not close at all.

## What Lives (Everything)

The fauna is overwhelming. This is the word for it and no other word will do.

The downs are *crawling*. The mats are grazed upon by swarms of small, segmented creatures — pale, soft-bodied, the length of a hand, with rasping mouthparts that leave visible tracks in the microbial crust like the grooves of a plow in miniature. They move in herds of millions, and the sound of their feeding is a continuous wet whisper, like silk being drawn across glass, audible from a hundred meters away. Where they have grazed, the mats regenerate within days, and the cycle continues.

Above them in the food chain: everything. Armored things with too many legs that stalk the shallow pools, their carapaces slick and dark, ranging from the size of a dinner plate to the size of a tabletop. These are ambush predators — they bury themselves in the microbial mats and wait, and the grazers walk over them, and then the mats heave upward and the grazers are gone. There are flying things — not insects as such, but six-winged, buzzing creatures with bodies like elongated seeds, dark-bodied and numerous enough to form visible clouds above the open water-pans at midday when the warmth peaks. Their sound is a low, pervasive thrumming that harmonizes with the drip of condensation from every overhang and ledge. There are things in the deeper water — sinuous, eel-like, with lateral fins and smooth grey skin, that surface to breathe with a soft popping gasp before submerging again. Their numbers are such that the surface of every deeper pool is in constant motion, dimpled and ringed by their surfacings.

And then there are the large ones. They are rare individually but present in every vista — long-necked, heavy-bodied, six-limbed grazers that stand three meters at the shoulder and move across the open downs in loose aggregations of dozens, their broad feet leaving deep impressions in the wet ground that fill with water behind them as they walk. They are slow, unhurried, almost geological in their pace. Their hides are the same dark grey as the basalt, streaked with mineral stains from the water that runs constantly down their flanks. They make a low, resonant vocalization — not a call exactly, more a vibration, felt in the chest before it is heard by the ear — that carries for kilometers across the open basin. They have been here as long as the stone. They are as weathered as the stone. When they die, their bones are scavenged so quickly and so thoroughly that the skeletons are never visible; within days, only the largest bones remain, and within seasons, even those are broken down, carried off, dissolved.

The sheer *density* of animal life is what makes the Downs what they are. Every square meter of wet stone has grazers on it. Every pool has predators. The air is thick with fliers. The ground trembles faintly, constantly, with the movement of burrowing things beneath the surface — pale, blind, muscular creatures that churn the saturated soil and keep the water flowing upward. The place is not quiet. It is not silent. It is loud with the business of being alive — the buzzing, the grazing-whisper, the splashing, the low booming calls of the large grazers, the clicking and scraping of carapace on stone. This is an old and stable ecology, in balance with itself, and it has filled every available niche with a patience that borders on inevitability.

## What Was Built

The city is called — and the name is old, as old as the stone, as weathered as the gullies — something that translates roughly as *Threshold*. It is built on the ridgeline of a long, broad basalt dome that rises perhaps two hundred meters above the general level of the downs, one of the higher points in the basin. The elevation keeps it above the worst of the standing water, though not above the damp. Water still seeps from the foundations. Condensation still beads on every wall. The city has simply been built to expect this.

Threshold is old and industrial and it does not apologize for either quality. The architecture is heavy — thick-walled, low-slung, built from the same dark basalt as the bedrock it sits on, cut and dressed and fitted with the kind of precision that comes from centuries of practice with the same material. The buildings are rarely more than four or five stories, but they are *dense* — packed closely together along narrow streets that channel the warm, wet air into consistent breezes. The rooftops are covered in systems of gutters and channels that collect the endless condensation and route it downward into cisterns and processing tanks. From above, the city looks like a circuit board made of stone — every surface grooved, channeled, utilized.

The industry is centered around the fauna. This is the city's economy, its reason for existing on this wet, crawling, plantless highland. The grazers — the small segmented ones — are harvested in vast numbers. Processing facilities line the southern slope of the dome, their smokestacks venting warm, mineral-smelling steam into the already-humid air. The bodies are rendered for oils, for chitin, for the microbial-rich gut contents that serve as a base for chemical processing. The larger predators are hunted more selectively — their carapaces are worked into materials, their tissues processed for compounds that the microbial mats concentrate and the predators accumulate further. The eel-things in the pools are farmed in managed waterways that thread through the city's lower districts.

The sound of the city is layered. Beneath everything: the drip, the seep, the constant percussion of water finding its way downward. Above that: the hum and clatter of machinery — not the shriek of heavy industry but the deeper, more rhythmic sound of well-maintained engines, hydraulic presses, the thud of mechanical looms weaving chitin-fiber into textiles. And above that: the fauna. The buzzing of the fliers, which are thicker near the city because the warmth of the processing facilities draws them. The low booming of the large grazers, which pass by the base of the dome in their slow circuits of the basin, indifferent to the city built above them. The clicking of the smaller predators that have colonized the lower levels of the city itself, living in the drainage channels and the cisterns, feeding on the grazers that are drawn to the microbial mats that grow on the perpetually wet walls.

Because the animals have colonized the city. This is not a fight the city is winning or trying to win. Threshold has been built *around* the fauna, *with* the fauna, in the same way that a reef is built around the creatures that inhabit it. The lower stories of buildings near the base of the dome are given over to the larger burrowing creatures — openings have been cut in the foundations to allow them passage, and in return their churning keeps the ground-drainage functional. The fliers nest in the upper stories, in purpose-built alcoves, and their droppings are collected and processed for fertilizer for the microbial farms in the managed waterways. The entire city is a machine for coexisting with an ecology that does not notice its presence and would not care if it did.

## The Light and the Hours

The light is normal. There is a sun. It rises in a sky that is pale and hazy with moisture, burns through the damp for a long, slow arc — the days here are long, the seasons gentle — and sets in a haze of amber and grey. The clouds are low, constant, a ceiling of white-grey that rarely breaks to show the deeper sky. When it rains — and it rains often, warm and soft, more like the air deciding to become water than any dramatic precipitation — the downs take on a uniform silver sheen, and the sound of the fauna dims briefly before surging back, the creatures apparently unbothered.

At night, the city glows faintly — not with electric light, though there is some of that, but with the bioluminescence of the managed microbial cultures in the lower waterways, which have been cultivated to produce a soft, steady, blue-white radiance. The glow reflects off every wet surface. The city at night looks like it is lit from below, from within, a lattice of pale light laid over the dark stone. The fauna is quieter at night, but not silent. Nothing here is ever silent.

## The Pace

Everything is slow. This is the final and perhaps most defining quality of the Weeping Downs. The stone erodes slowly. The water seeps slowly. The fauna moves slowly — even the fliers have a heavy, deliberate flight pattern, their buzzing more like a vibration than a whir. The city changes slowly; buildings are modified over generations, not years, and the infrastructure has a layered quality that speaks to centuries of incremental addition. The industrial processes are steady-state, optimized long ago, running at the same pace they have run at for as long as anyone can remember. The gradients are weak. The cycles are long. The large grazers circle the basin once per season, and their route has not changed in living memory. The microbial mats grow and are grazed and regrow in the same places, at the same rates, with the same colors.

This is a place that has found its equilibrium and sees no reason to disturb it. The stone weeps. The fauna crawls and buzzes and grazes. The city hums and renders and builds. The water finds its way downslope. Everything is patient. Everything is present. The air is warm and wet and heavy with the sound of ten million small lives, and the occasional deep, resonant boom of something enormous shifting its weight on the far side of the basin, barely visible through the haze, moving north as it has always moved north, at the pace of geology, at the pace of a creature that has nowhere to be and all the time in the world to get there.
