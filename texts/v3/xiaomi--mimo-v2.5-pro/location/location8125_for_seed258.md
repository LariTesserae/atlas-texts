# The Standing Choir

---

You come up out of the treeline and the plateau opens before you without warning.

The first thing you notice is the sound.

It is not one sound. It is the sound of a place that has always been making this sound. A deep, arrhythmic groaning — like ship timber under load — rises from everywhere at once. Beneath it, a constant liquid whisper: water running down surfaces, condensing in mid-air, catching in cupped leaves the size of threshing floors and overflowing in thin continuous sheets. The groaning and the water-glide together make something almost tonal, almost melodic, but never resolving. It does not build. It does not shift. You realize after a while that you are hearing the same phrase repeat — not because it is looping, but because this is simply what this place sounds like and has always sounded like, the way a heartbeat is not a repetition but a *continuation*.

The structures stand across the high plain at wide intervals — maybe two hundred meters apart, maybe more in places. There is no grid. No pattern you can discern. They simply *are*, spaced the way a forest might be spaced if a forest decided to become a city and then thought better of the crowds.

They are alive. This is immediately, viscerally obvious. Each one is a single organism — or what began as one. They rise forty, sixty, eighty meters from the ground, and they are not trees. They are not fungi. They are something for which the language has not yet been forced to make a word, because the language was built by people who had not yet stood here.

Their surfaces are pale — the color of new wood beneath bark, the color of something not yet finished becoming itself. Ribs of structural tissue curve upward in long arcs, meeting and branching and meeting again, forming vaults and hollows and cathedral-openings that let the gray-white sky through in geometric fragments. The ribs are wet. Everything is wet. Water wells from every surface in slow, persistent beads that gather and run in thin rivers down the vertical planes, pooling at the base of each structure in wide, shallow moats that have never eroded the ground beneath them because the ground beneath them is too new — raw mineral clay and volcanic ash, barely consolidated, still holding the chemical smell of deep-earth processes. The moats do not drain. There is nowhere for them to drain *to*. The plateau is flat and young and the water simply stays, re-evaporating, re-condensing, cycling in a local weather system that belongs entirely to each structure and its immediate halo of wetness.

You walk closer to one.

Inside — if inside is the right word, because the walls are not walls but the organism's own body curving around you — the space is enormous. The ribs that looked like structure from outside are, closer, covered in textures that defy easy reading: ridges that might be vascular, pores that might be respiratory, surfaces that glisten with a mucus-thin secretion that smells vegetal and faintly sweet and also faintly of something metallic, like blood. The floor of the interior — the base where all the ribs converge — is a shallow pool of perfectly still water, maybe ten centimeters deep, and beneath the water the ground is a mat of roots so dense and fine that it looks like fabric. The roots are alive. You can feel the faintest vibration through the soles of your feet, a pulse that is too slow to be a heartbeat and too regular to be random.

The groaning is louder in here. It reverberates off the internal surfaces. You realize the structures are not producing the sound so much as *channeling* it — the wind, what little wind there is on this high flat place, moves through their hollow bodies and turns them into instruments. But the wind never changes. The instruments never change. The song is permanent.

---

Outside again. The sky is a uniform gray-white, bright but sourceless-feeling — the sun is up there somewhere behind a permanent veil of moisture, and the light that comes through is diffuse and shadowless, giving everything a slightly unreal flatness. The temperature is warm but not hot. Humid in the way of a greenhouse, of a bathroom after a long shower, of a place where water is always trying to become air and air is always trying to become water.

Between the structures, the ground is empty.

Not barren — empty. The clay-and-ash surface is wet, glistening faintly, and here and there small pools have gathered in depressions, and on the margins of those pools there are low, simple mosses and liverworts, the most embryonic possible colonizers of a landscape that does not yet know what it wants to be. But these are few. The space between the great living structures is defined by absence — by the fact that nothing has yet arrived to fill it. The erosion channels that should, over millennia, be cutting this plateau into ridges and valleys do not exist. The rivers that should be gathering the water into tributaries do not exist. This is a landscape in the instant before geography begins. The water falls, condenses, pools, evaporates, condenses again — and never *goes anywhere*. The ground holds it. The sky takes it back. The cycle is closed and has always been closed.

There are animals. You see one — or you think you do. A dark shape moving slowly through the interior of a distant structure, something low and heavy-bodied, unhurried. Then it is gone behind a rib and you are not sure you saw it. Later, a sound that might be an animal sound — a low, resonant call from far across the plain, almost indistinguishable from the groaning of the structures themselves. But fauna here is vestigial, incidental. The structures do not need pollinators. They do not need seed-dispersers. They reproduce — if they reproduce — by means you cannot observe. The animals that move among them seem to do so without purpose or consequence, the way passengers might walk through a cathedral that was not built for them.

---

You stand on the open ground between two of the structures and turn in a slow circle.

The scale of the place asserts itself now. This is not a forest. It is not a city. It is a region — a valley-scale expanse of high plateau, and the structures dot it for kilometers in every direction, thinning at the edges where the plateau meets escarpments you can just barely make out through the haze. Hundreds of them, perhaps. Each one a self-contained world of wet, resonant, living architecture. Each one standing in its small sea of still water. Between them, the raw, flat, empty, waiting ground.

Nothing here is growing. Nothing here is dying. The structures were not *built* — no hands, no tools, no technology of any kind — but they are *made*, in the way that a wasp's nest is made, in the way that a coral reef is made: the product of biological imperatives so ancient and so complete that the distinction between organism and architecture dissolves entirely. These are buildings that breathe. These are bodies that house.

And they are loud. The groaning never stops. The water never stops. The sound is enormous and ambient and without direction — it comes from above and below and every side, and after a while you stop hearing it the way you stop hearing your own pulse, and when you do stop hearing it you realize that the silence beneath the sound is even stranger than the sound itself, because it is the silence of a place that has been this way for a very long time and will be this way for a very long time and in which *you* are the only thing that is changing.

You walk. Your footsteps make soft sounds in the wet clay. Behind you, the impressions fill with water almost immediately.

The structures groan.

The water falls.

Nothing changes.
