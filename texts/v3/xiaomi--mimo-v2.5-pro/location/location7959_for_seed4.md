# The Fenmouth

## What it is

A pocket. That's what the locals call it, if you could call them locals — they arrived sometime after the pocket opened, which was not long ago. Perhaps a single human lifetime, perhaps less. The land here was low already, a coastal plain barely above tideline in a northern latitude, and something beneath it collapsed. Or perhaps something beneath it *exhaled*. A depression opened in the earth roughly a kilometer across but far deeper than it should be — the floor sits maybe thirty meters below the surrounding plain, and the walls are sheer in places, cracked in others, still raw and unweathered, still weeping mineral-stained water from faces of stone that have not known air in a very long time.

The walls are dark basalt, veined with pale calcite, and they glisten constantly. Water condenses on every surface and runs in slow threads toward the bottom, where it collects in a shallow, black, ankle-deep lagoon that covers perhaps a third of the floor. The rest is not dry. Nothing here is dry. The ground is saturated — you step and the surface dimples beneath you, and water rises in your footprint, and you feel the living mat beneath your boot shift and compress and spring back. It is always cold here. Not freezing — the water moves, and the depression holds what little heat the land offers — but the kind of cold that finds every seam in your clothing, that settles into joints, that makes the breath visible in slow pale ribbons that rise and vanish in the dark.

And it is dark.

---

## The dark

The pocket faces roughly north, and the sun — in those months it appears at all — clears the southern horizon at such an angle that the depression receives almost no direct light. In winter, which is most of the year, the pocket exists in a state of near-permanent twilight. Not absolute blackness: the sky above is a pale disk, sometimes grey, sometimes the colour of old bone, occasionally a faint and distant blue. That light reaches the upper walls. By the time it descends to the floor, it has been absorbed and scattered and spent. The lower Fenmouth lives in a dusk that never fully resolves.

But it is not unlit. The plants here — and there is almost nothing here that is not plant — have their own light. Faint, variable, unreliable. Bioluminescence runs through the moss and the creeping vegetation like slow green fire, and the fungi that colonize the older growth produce a cooler blue-white glow, localized, patchy, like foxfire on a forest log magnified a thousandfold. On the lagoon's surface, certain algae bloom in swirling constellations that shift with the water's movement. The light is never enough to read by. It is enough to see the shape of your hand, the outline of a face, the slow movement of something at the edge of vision. The settlement at the pocket's mid-level has learned to supplement this with tallow lamps and slow-burning peat, but even those are dim, reluctant, hooded against the damp. Light here is not a resource. It is a mood. A thing that comes and goes. You learn to see in the dark, or you learn to stop seeing.

## The vegetation

It fills the space. This is the defining fact of the Fenmouth, and it is impossible to overstate.

The pocket's walls, from the raw basalt at the rim down through the wet middle reaches to the lapped edge of the lagoon, are covered — not coated, *covered*, as in *replaced* — with plant matter. Cold-adapted mosses form a living shag that is sometimes a hand's width thick and sometimes a full arm's length, hanging in heavy curtains from ledges and overhangs, absorbing water until it drips from their tips in slow metronome beats. Liverworts spread in flat rosettes across the upper walls, their surfaces slick and dark. Lichens crust the highest, driest surfaces in patterns that look like maps of river deltas.

Below the moss line, where the water is more constant and the air more saturated, the vegetation shifts. There are plants here that should not exist — not in these temperatures, not in this light. Pale, leggy stems rise from the saturated mat, leafless or bearing leaves that are little more than translucent membranes stretched over veins. They grow upward toward whatever light the sky offers, and they grow fast — fast enough that you can mark a stem's height in the morning and perceive, by evening, that it has changed. They do not photosynthesize in any conventional sense. Their membranes are for condensation: they harvest moisture from the air, and the water they collect runs down their stems to the root mat below. They are *infrastructure*. Plumbing disguised as botany.

On the floor, especially around the lagoon's margin, the vegetation becomes stranger still. There are clusters of bulbous, soft-bodied growths — not quite mushrooms, not quite anything — that sit on the surface like pale fists and pulse, very slowly, with a rhythm that might be hydraulic, might be biological, might be both. They are warm to the touch, faintly. The fauna interacts with them constantly (more on this below), and they seem to exist in some ancient, obligate relationship with the animal life that no one has yet understood. They are not eaten. They are not avoided. They are *tended*.

The overall effect is that the Fenmouth is less a place with plants in it than a place made *of* plants. The settlement's structures are built not from imported material but from the living vegetation itself — walls of woven moss-mat, dried and layered, roofs of the translucent leaf-membranes stretched over frames of the pale leggy stems. The architecture is, in a sense, the landscape continuing itself through the hands of its inhabitants. Nothing is inert here. Everything is growing. The walls of your shelter are growing. The floor beneath you is growing. Given enough time — and not very much time — the Fenmouth would grow over anything you built and swallow it back into itself.

## The fauna

This is the overwhelming thing. This is the thing you remember.

The animal life of the Fenmouth is not abundant the way a rainforest is abundant — it is not a matter of diversity so much as *density*. There are not many species. There are a horrifying number of individuals.

The most visible, because they are everywhere and because they do not stop moving, are the arthropods. Something like amphipods, something like isopods, but neither — pale, segmented, many-legged, ranging from the size of a grain of rice to the length of a finger. They occupy every surface. They move in constant, rustling, whispering streams across the moss, up the walls, along the undersides of ledges, through the water. They are the primary processors of dead plant matter, and there is so much plant matter, and so much of it is dying at any given moment to make room for new growth, that their numbers seem to require no explanation and yet still shock the senses. You put your hand on a wall and the wall *moves*. Not metaphorically. The surface ripples and parts and reforms behind your fingers, because the surface is not moss — the surface is arthropods, walking over moss, eating what the moss has shed.

Above them, in the middle darkness, there are the amphibians. Pale, large-eyed, smooth-skinned, some the size of your thumb and some the size of your forearm. They sit motionless on ledges and stems for hours, barely distinguishable from the plant matter, and then they move — a snap of a long tongue, a quick scramble, a silent plunge into the lagoon. They are almost completely silent. A rare croak, a rarer splash. But they are *everywhere* you look once you learn to look. Hundreds. Thousands. The Fenmouth is a bowl full of frogs and salamanders and things in between, all watching, all patient, all feeding on the arthropods that swarm beneath them.

In the lagoon itself, something larger moves. Not much larger — nothing here is large; the scale is intimate, pocket, contained — but larger than amphibian. Eel-like, pale, eyeless or nearly so, they cruise the shallow water in slow sinuous passes, and when they surface to breathe (if they breathe; it is unclear) they create ripples that the bioluminescent algae follow, leaving brief glowing trails on the black water. There are perhaps a few dozen of them. They are the top predators in the system, and their presence seems to regulate everything below, because when one surfaces, the amphibians go still, and when the amphibians go still, the arthropod streams slow and consolidate, and for a moment the whole Fenmouth holds its breath.

There are also birds — a small, dark, wren-like species that nests in the upper walls and descends to the middle reaches to feed. There are perhaps thirty or forty of them. They are the loudest animals here, and even they are quiet: a soft churring call, a brief flitter of wings, gone. They seem to time their descents to coincide with periods when the eels are deep. The system is that precise. Everything watches everything else.

And there are the symbioses. The bulbous pale growths on the lagoon margins are — you can observe this if you sit still long enough — serviced by specific arthropod species that prune their surfaces, remove parasites, and in return feed on secretions the growths produce. The translucent leaf-membranes host micro-arthropod communities on their undersides that somehow protect the membranes from fungal infection. The moss mat is threaded with fungal networks that connect it into a single organism, and the arthropods' burrowing aerates these networks and helps them spread. The fauna is not living *in* the vegetation. The fauna and the vegetation are living *as* each other. A single system. One organism with many bodies.

## The settlement

It is called, by its inhabitants, something that translates roughly as "the dry place," which is both aspirational and inaccurate.

Perhaps forty structures, built into the mid-level walls of the pocket where the basalt offers ledges and shallow caves. The buildings are half-carved, half-grown — stone foundations hacked into the rock with simple tools (bone picks, sharpened basalt, antler hammers), with walls and roofs built up from dried moss-mat and woven stem-material. The construction is layered: new growth is laid over old, compressed, allowed to knit, and then new growth is added again, so that the buildings are, in effect, gardens as much as architecture. They glow faintly at night from the bioluminescent organisms that have colonized their surfaces. The settlement, seen from the lagoon floor, looks like a cluster of dim green lanterns floating on the dark wall.

The people — perhaps one hundred and fifty of them — are short, broad, adapted to the cold in the way of populations that have lived in cold for a very long time, though how long this particular group has been here is unclear even to them. Their oral history speaks of arrival, but the details have been eaten by generations of retelling until the arrival is more myth than memory. They fish the lagoon with hand-woven nets. They harvest the arthropods — a specific, slightly larger species — for food, drying them in racks over peat fires. They cultivate the translucent leaf-membranes as material for clothing, wrapping, light-transmission. Their tools are simple and constantly maintained: everything here rots, everything here grows, and a tool left unattended for a week will be moss-covered and a tool left for a month will be moss-eaten.

They do not venture to the rim often. The open world above — the flat grey plain, the sky, the wind — is not hostile to them, exactly, but it is *empty* in a way they find distressing. The Fenmouth is full. Every surface, every moment, every square inch of air holds something alive. Above, there is nothing. The emptiness is the threat, not the cold.

They are aware that the pocket is changing. The walls are still weeping, still raw; new cracks appear; small collapses send fresh debris to the floor, and within days the moss has claimed it, and within weeks the arthropods have colonized the moss, and within months it is indistinguishable from everything else. The pocket is growing. Slowly, but perceptibly within a single lifetime. It is deepening, or widening, or both. The lagoon rises. The settlement must periodically adjust its lower structures upward. No one knows what the pocket is growing toward, or what it will become, or whether it will stop. The general feeling is that it will not.

## The sound and the silence

It is mostly quiet. The dominant sound is water — dripping, condensing, running in thin threads, pooling with a sound so faint it is more felt than heard. The arthropods produce a constant, barely perceptible rustle, like dry leaves shifting in a breeze that isn't there, and this sound is so omnipresent that it becomes silence — the baseline of the Fenmouth, the thing your brain learns to edit out. Occasional sounds punctuate this: the churring of a wren, the brief wet sound of an amphibian's tongue, the deeper splash of an eel surfacing, the slow groan of the living wall adjusting its weight. A building creaks as its moss-mat contracts in the cold. A lamp gutter.

But between these sounds, there is the silence, and in the silence there is the *presence* — the awareness that you are inside something alive, that the walls are watching (the amphibians, the arthropods, the slow pulsing of the growths at the lagoon's edge), that the dark is not empty but full, and that the fullness is indifferent to you but not unaware of you. The strange thing — the 1.6 on whatever scale you use to measure strangeness — is not that the place is alien. It isn't. Everything in it is recognizably biological, recognizably of this world. The strangeness is the *concentration*. The density of life in the absence of light. The insistence of growth in the absence of warmth. The way the pocket seems to be not just a place where life has taken hold, but a place where life is *doing something* — pursuing some project, following some logic, building toward some state that the inhabitants can feel but not articulate.

The Fenmouth does not explain itself. It grows. It teems. It is wet and dark and cold and full of creatures that do not care that you are there, and it is, against all reason, the dry place, the home, the pocket where the world is alive and close and breathing.
