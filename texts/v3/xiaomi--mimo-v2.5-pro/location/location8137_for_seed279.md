# The Rumbling Shallows

The land has only just arrived.

That is how it feels to stand here — and to stand here you must accept that you are standing on something that does not yet know it is land. The ground is soft in some places, crunching and gritty in others, and in between it has the strange yielding firmness of something that was, until very recently, underwater. The surface is a mosaic of silt plains and sudden mineral crusts: pale grey flats where nothing has settled yet, interrupted by low ridges of calcium deposit and iron-stained clay that push up in lobes and fingers, each one sharp-edged, barely a meter high, running in parallel rows as far as you can see. These ridges are the oldest things here — older than the water table that still sits less than a hand's width below your feet, older than the flatness itself — and they are the reason the landscape is so dense with *presence.* Every few paces, the texture underfoot changes. Every few dozen paces, the color shifts: ochre to rust to ash-white to a black that shines with moisture.

There are no plants. There has not been time for them, or perhaps there is something in the chemistry of the soil — mineral-heavy, faintly sulfurous, cold to the touch — that forbids them. The ground is not sterile, though. It is layered. Peel back the top centimeter and you find a darker substrate, almostite-like, grainy with tiny crystalline nodules. Beneath that, clay. Beneath that, water. You can feel the water moving if you press your palm flat — a slow, lateral seepage, as though the land is draining from somewhere uphill, though there is no uphill, only the imperceptible rise to the north where the ridges grow slightly taller and the mineral crusts thicken into something that almost resembles stone.

---

## Sound

This is the first thing that strikes you, and it does not stop striking.

The landscape is **loud**. Not in the way of storms or traffic — there is nothing mechanical here, nothing powered — but in the way of a world that is still *settling.* The ridges hum. The silt plains crack. There is a low, pervasive rumble that seems to come from the ground itself, a tectonic murmur that you feel in your molars before you identify it with your ears. It rises and falls on a cycle that seems to take hours — a slow respiration, as if the crust is breathing — and when it swells, the mineral ridges vibrate in sympathy, producing a faint, high overtone that layers over the bass like a glass harmonica played through soil.

Water contributes. It hisses where it touches the hotter clay deposits (the ground is not uniformly cool; there are warm patches, and near them, steam whispers upward in columns so thin they are almost invisible). It gurgles in the shallow subsurface channels. And occasionally — unpredictably — a pocket of trapped gas will release somewhere beneath the surface, producing a sudden, startling *burp* of sound, wet and resonant, that echoes across the flats with no structure to catch it and so carries far, rolling outward and attenuating slowly, like a word spoken into an empty cathedral.

The few animals that live here — and they are rare, you might see one in an hour of walking — are small, burrowing things, pale and legless, that move through the upper clay layer and produce a faint scratching that you cannot locate. They seem to navigate by the rumble, pausing whenever the ground's breathing swells, resuming when it quiets.

## Structure

Scattered across the flats, at wide and irregular intervals, there are **marks of habitation.** Not buildings exactly. More like foundations — low rectangles of worked stone, barely distinguishable from the natural ridges unless you look closely and notice the angles are too precise, the joints too deliberate. Some of them still hold water in shallow basins, and in those basins the mineral deposits have formed thin, iridescent films — oily rainbows on the surface that shift slowly as the light changes.

There are not many. Perhaps one every few hundred meters, and most are half-buried in the newer silt. Whatever lived here once — and the age of the stone, its weathering, its style (if you can call it style: simple, load-bearing, without ornament) suggests it was a long time ago — it lived here before the water rose, before the land was drowned, and now the land has emerged again and the structures have come up with it, waterlogged and patient, their upper surfaces colonized not by moss (nothing grows here) but by thin mineral films and the faint etchings of small aquatic organisms that dried out and died when the water receded, leaving pale, geometric traces on the stone like frost patterns.

One structure is larger than the others. It sits slightly north of center in the visible landscape, on a rise that is barely a rise — perhaps two meters above the surrounding flats — and it is more intact: walls to waist height, a floor of fitted stone, and in one corner, a basin or channel that seems to have been designed to collect and direct water. The water still follows the channel, pooling at one end and overflowing in a thin sheet that runs across the floor and out through a gap in the wall, feeding a shallow rivulet that cuts across the silt plain for fifty meters before the ground absorbs it. The sound of this water is the loudest thing in the structure — a constant, trickling whisper against the stone, louder than you would expect from such a thin flow, amplified by the hard surfaces.

## Atmosphere

The light is **dim.** Not darkness — there is a sky, and it is a uniform pale grey, the color of wet plaster, featureless and low. The sun, if it is behind the clouds, does not locate itself. Shadows are faint, diffuse, almost absent, which gives the landscape a strange quality of *flatness* — the ridges, the ruins, the rivulets, all seem pressed into the same plane, like a photograph with no depth of field. Your eyes adjust. They find detail in texture rather than contrast: the grain of the clay, the crystalline sparkle of mineral nodules, the dark water in the channels.

The air is cool and damp, and it smells of wet stone and something faintly metallic — iron, maybe, or copper — and beneath that, a sweetness that seems wrong, too organic for a landscape with no life, and you realize after a while that it is coming from the ground, from the slow decomposition of microscopic organisms in the clay, a posthumous sweetness, the last chemical sigh of a biome that died when the water came.

There is a mist that forms and dissipates in patches, driven not by wind (there is almost none) but by the temperature variations in the ground. Near the warm clay deposits, the mist lifts in curtains that move slowly across the flats, thin and translucent, and when you walk through them, the sound of your footsteps changes — becomes closer, more intimate — and then you emerge on the other side and the rumbling opens up again.

---

## Time

The landscape is **slow.** This is what makes it strange: it is loud and dense and wet and newly born, and yet almost nothing is happening. The water seeps at the rate of centimeters per hour. The ridges grow by millimeters per year. The ruins do not erode further — the stone is too hard, the climate too gentle — and they do not sink. The mist forms and dissolves but always in the same places, as though the atmosphere has learned its habits and keeps them faithfully.

What you are witnessing is a place in the early stage of a geological pause. The land emerged, the water retreated to just below the surface, and now everything is waiting — for vegetation to find it (it has not), for erosion to shape it (it is too young), for something to happen (nothing does). The loudness is not activity; it is *potential.* The ground rumbles because it is still adjusting to being ground. The water hisses because it is still finding its level. The ruins sit in their own silence because they have already done everything they are going to do.

And yet: it is not sad. There is something almost generous about a landscape this patient. It holds its breath. It hums. It lets you see the bones of it — mineral, water, stone, the faint traces of what lived and built here before — without hiding them behind growth or weather or the slow violence of change. It is a world that has just arrived and is introducing itself, one rumble at a time.
