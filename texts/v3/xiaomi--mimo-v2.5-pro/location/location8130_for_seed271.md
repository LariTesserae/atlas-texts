# The Cistern at Low Tide

You step down.

The stairs were carved once, long ago, from the bedrock itself — or perhaps from stone blocks so old that they have fused with what lies beneath. Each step is worn into a shallow basin, a concavity that holds a film of water no deeper than a thumbnail. The surface reflects nothing; it is too shallow and too still for that, just a dark sheen against pale stone that has been smoothed by centuries of feet that no longer come.

The stair descends for perhaps ten meters, maybe fewer, and then opens into a low vault. You must duck your head. The ceiling is only two meters high at its peak, domed, and it sweats. Condensation gathers along every surface in slow, nearly imperceptible beads that occasionally break free and fall — *plip* — into the pools below. The sound arrives once every few seconds, irregular, from different directions, as though the room itself is slowly exhaling droplets.

---

The cistern — or whatever it was, there is no one left to call it by its proper name — is roughly circular, perhaps fifteen meters across. The floor is a mosaic of stone slabs, most of them intact, some shifted slightly out of alignment by time and the patient work of root or settling earth. In the low places between slabs, water has gathered. Not flowing. Not deep. The deepest pool might reach your ankle. It is clean and clear and perfectly still, and it smells of mineral and old stone and nothing else.

The walls curve inward. They were plastered once — you can see remnants, patches ofite-gray render that have calcified into something almost indistinguishable from the stone beneath. Here and there, faint lines are still visible: the marks of tools, the edges where blocks were fitted together. Someone built this with care. The joints are tight even now. But the plaster is mostly gone, surrendered to the damp over centuries, and what remains beneath is rough-cut limestone, pale as bone, veined with darker mineral in threads that run like the meridians of a map no one reads anymore.

---

Light enters from above, through a ragged opening in the dome where the ceiling has partially collapsed. The breach is narrow — perhaps a meter across — and it admits a column of gray, diffuse daylight that slants at an angle, touching the far wall and part of the water's surface. Outside, the sky must be overcast. The light is even and shadowless, the kind of light that seems to come from nowhere in particular. It illuminates enough to see by, but only just. The far reaches of the chamber are dim, the corners lost in a soft gradation toward dark.

The warmth is immediate and close. The stone holds heat from the day — or perhaps from the earth itself, this being low ground, not far above the water table, in a climate that never quite cools. The air is damp and warm and motionless. It sits against your skin like cloth.

---

Vegetation here is sparse and patient. Moss grows in a thin, almost dusty layer on the northern wall, where the moisture is most constant and the light most indirect. It is not lush — just a pale green haze over the stone, barely raised, more like a stain than a living thing. In one of the shallower pools, a few blades of some aquatic grass have found root in a crack between slabs. They are pale, almost translucent, reaching perhaps ten centimeters above the water's surface. They do not move. There is no breeze.

A single small fern clings to a ledge near the ceiling breach, where it catches the faintest suggestion of open sky. Its fronds are stunted, tightly curled, adapted to low light. It has likely been there for decades. It is not growing so much as persisting.

---

There is no animal life. No insects, no spiders — the damp and the dark and the stillness have apparently not attracted them, or perhaps the stone offers nothing to eat. The only sound, besides the intermittent dripping, is the faintest hum — not mechanical, not from any device, but the resonance of the chamber itself, the way enclosed stone vibrates almost imperceptibly with shifts in air pressure or the deep geology below. If you hold your breath, you can hear it: a tone so low and so quiet it might be imagined, the frequency of a room that has existed for a very long time and has settled into its own acoustics like a held note.

---

Near the base of the far wall, there is a low shelf carved into the stone. Not a bench — too narrow, too shallow. A ledge, perhaps half a meter wide, running three meters along the wall at knee height. On it sit the only signs that anyone was ever here: a shallow stone basin, roughly hewn, empty now but bearing the faint white residue of mineral deposits left by evaporated water. Beside it, a fragment of ceramic — the curved side of a vessel, unglazed, its surface rough and reddish, matching the clay of this region. Nothing else. No inscription, no ornament. The artifacts of someone who needed to hold water, or pour it, or store it, and who did so with simple, functional objects that did not survive the centuries intact.

Someone tended this place once. The plaster, the fitted stone, the carved ledge — these speak of intention, of maintenance, of a relationship between people and water that was practical and perhaps sacred. But whatever community this served has been gone long enough that their traces have been reduced to near-nothing, to a bowl and a shard and the faint geometries of stonework that still holds true after all this time.

---

The place does not change. The water level rises and falls with some unimaginably slow rhythm — the water table, the seasons, perhaps the tides of some distant sea filtered through layers of earth. But the change is too slow to observe. You could sit here for an hour, or a day, and the room would look the same. The moss would not visibly grow. The pools would not visibly shrink or swell. The light from above would shift by degrees so small they do not register as movement.

It is a place that has reached its own equilibrium — not abandoned, exactly, but finished. It has completed whatever process of erosion and settling and slow surrender that stone undergoes, and now it simply is. The damp will continue. The dripping will continue. The silence will continue. Nothing will come to disturb it and nothing will come to restore it. It is as close to permanent as anything built by hands can be, and it asks nothing of you except that you stand in it, for a moment, with your head bowed under the low dome, and listen to the water find its way down.
