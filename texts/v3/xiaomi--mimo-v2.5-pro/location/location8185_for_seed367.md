# The Terraces of the Maw

---

There is a place on the plateau where the stone remembers how to fall.

The land up top is ancient beyond reckoning — a high, wind-scoured table of fused calcium and silicate, the remnant of a range that once rose twice this height, now worn across hundreds of millions of years into something that barely qualifies as relief. Flat. Broad. Salted with crusted mineral deposits that catch the eye as pale lavender and bone-white in the unobstructed sky. The wind is the only thing that still moves with urgency up here, and even it seems tired, skimming low across the surface, carrying nothing but dust. There is almost no water. The rivers died long ago, and even the groundwater has sunk to depths that no longer concern the surface. What moisture exists is only fog — thin, ghostly, rolling across the plateau at dawn from the direction of some distant, forgotten sea, lingering an hour, then gone.

But the plateau is not as solid as it looks. Beneath the ancient flatness, the stone is riddled with dissolution chambers, collapse pockets, voids left by salt deposits that bled out over epochs. And in one such place — you would not find it unless you nearly walked into it — the ground simply opens.

---

The locals call it the Maw.

It is a sinkhole, but that word does not capture its age. This is not a recent collapse. The walls have been eroding for so long that they slope gently, almost gently enough to walk down without using your hands, worn to the smoothness of river-stone by ten million rains that no longer fall. The opening at the top is perhaps forty meters across — pocket-scale, hidden in the undulation of the plateau, invisible from more than a hundred meters away in any direction. But the cavity beneath is deeper than it is wide: sixty meters down to the floor, the walls curving inward slightly at the lip and then bowing outward below, like the inside of a skull turned upside down.

The light that reaches the floor is dim. It is always dim. The sun passes overhead and what slants in is reflected, refracted, scattered through the haze of mist that perpetually hangs in the upper third of the chamber — fog residue, trapped and slow-cycling, catching the light and turning it diffuse and pearlescent. Down below, you live in a permanent dusk, a warm amber-gray, the kind of light that makes everything look slightly older than it is.

And yet.

---

The walls are alive.

Not metaphorically. Every surface that isn't bare tool-marked stone is covered, layered, colonized, intertwined with growth. The Maw is a terrarium, a vertical garden sustained by almost nothing — fog condensation, mineral seepage, the slow chemistry of wet stone. Ferns uncurl from cracks in their thousands, their fronds overlapping so densely that the rock beneath is invisible. Mosses carpet every ledge in pads so thick they hold their shape when you press your hand in. Air plants cluster on the upper walls where the light is strongest, their roots gripping nothing, their pale leaves cupped to catch droplets. Epiphytic orchids — small, waxy, almost colorless in the dim light — grow in dense colonies on the larger fern stems. Succulent ground-cover with rubbery, dark-green leaves fills the floor in drifts and mounds, broken only by paths.

The vegetation is lush in the way that cave-adjacent ecosystems are lush — not sun-fed and exuberant, but deep, layered, patient. Everything here grows slowly. Everything is packed close. The density is striking: you cannot see the stone. Where the terraces have been carved by human hands, the plants have reclaimed the edges within a season. Where a shelf was cut to hold a water vessel, moss has grown over the lip and ferns have sprouted from the mortar. The air smells of green decay, of limestone dust, of wet mineral, of the sharp spice of the orchid-like things that bloom once a year with flowers the color of bruises.

---

And then there are the animals.

There are too many. This is the overwhelming truth of the Maw. The fauna is not diverse in any taxonomist's sense — there are perhaps forty species — but the sheer *number of bodies* in this space is staggering. Insects dominate. Beetles in the thousands crawl the moss walls, their carapaces winking with iridescence in the dim light. A species of wingless moth — pale, soft-bodied, the size of a thumbnail — drifts through the fern canopy in slow clouds, landing on everything. Crickets of a kind found nowhere else on the plateau chirp ceaselessly from every crevice, their sound layering into a continuous crackling hum that becomes the background of thought itself. Spiders have built webs so extensively between the wall ferns that entire sections of the Maw's interior are veiled in gossamer, shimmering with trapped moisture.

Larger things move too. Small lizards — slate-gray, pink-throated, quick — are everywhere, skittering across the terraced ledges, hunting the moths. A species of gecko with enormous dark eyes clings to the ceiling near the opening, where it can see in the slightly better light, waiting for the flying insects that spiral upward on thermal drafts. Bats roost in the upper third, perhaps three hundred of them, their guano feeding a secondary ecosystem of beetles and fungi on a high ledge. Small birds — finch-like, brown, loud — nest in the fern tangles and make sharp territorial calls that carry well in the enclosed space. Their droppings seed new growth on every horizontal surface. There is a faint, omnipresent smell of animal — musk, guano, shed skin — underneath the green and the stone.

You cannot sit still without something landing on you. You cannot put your hand down without disturbing something. The Maw is not dangerous — nothing here is large enough to threaten a person — but it is *full*, saturated with living bodies, and the feeling is one of being embedded in a hive that happens to be made of stone and ferns.

---

And in this, a town.

It is small — sixty people, perhaps seventy when the seasonal gatherers come down from the plateau. But it is organized, deliberate, layered with intention. The settlement is built into the upper terraces of the Maw, where the walls begin to slope more gently and the light is fractionally better. Shelters are carved into the softened stone — niches, really, doorways cut into the wall with hand-tools, their interiors hollowed into small rooms just large enough to sleep in, to store things in, to sit and work in. The stone is soft enough to shape with antler picks and sharpened basalt — it has been eroding so long that it is more calciumite deposit than hard rock, and it cuts like dense cheese. The walls of these rooms are covered in patterns: notches, hatched lines, repeated spirals — marks that might be counting, might be naming, might be something else entirely.

Between the shelters, terraces have been carved and reinforced with stacked stone — flat shelves where people work, sleep, cook, socialize. Some are connected by crude steps cut into the wall. Others require climbing, using hand-holds worn smooth by generations of use. At two points, bridges of lashed wood and woven fiber span gaps between wall sections, swaying slightly under weight, always damp with condensation.

The people are small, dark-haired, lean. They dress in woven plant-fiber — the succulent ground-cover yields long, strong fibers when stripped and dried, and the women of the Maw weave it into a rough, durable cloth that smells faintly of the plant. They keep no fire — the air circulation is poor and the smoke would be unbearable — but they heat food on heated stones warmed in a single communal pit near the top of the chamber, where an opening in the wall catches a draft and draws smoke upward through a narrow chimney-chute toward the sky. Their tools are simple: basalt scrapers, bone awls, antler picks, ceramic vessels fired in that same heat-pit. They are crafters of extraordinary patience — their baskets are tight enough to hold water, their cordage strong enough to support a grown man's weight across a bridge, their knowledge of the Maw's ecology encyclopedic and passed down through careful, redundant oral instruction.

They eat the lizards. They eat the moths — gathered in nets at dusk, when the clouds of them thicken near the fern tips, then pressed into cakes and dried. They cultivate a single fungal species on the bat-guano ledge, tending it with the precision of farmers. They drink water collected in ceramic cisterns from the fog-drip — the walls weep slowly, especially in the early morning, and channels carved into the stone direct the trickle into storage. Water is the currency, the worry, the rhythm. Every person knows the cistern levels. Every person knows the fog-patterns of the season.

---

It is loud here. Louder than you expect from a place so small and so hidden. The crickets are constant — a wash of dry, electric clicking that never stops, that changes pitch with the temperature and the time of day. The birds punctuate it with sharp, territorial staccato. The beetles make a faint, collective ticking as they move across dry fronds. The bats, at dusk, erupt into a storm of chittering and wing-rustle that fills the chamber for twenty minutes before they stream out through the top of the Maw to hunt. The water drips — a rhythmic, echoing percussion from a dozen points on the wall, each with its own pitch depending on the depth of the pool it strikes. The people's voices carry well in the acoustics of the space, and there is always someone talking, someone singing in a low, repetitive drone that seems to blend with the cricket-hum, someone calling across a gap to a neighbor on another terrace.

Above all of this, the wind across the top of the Maw produces a low, intermittent moan — air pressure differentials between the plateau surface and the chamber below creating a breathing sound, as if the earth itself were inhaling through a narrow throat.

---

The Maw is dynamic. It is not a still place. The fog moves in cycles — thickening at dawn, thinning by midday, sometimes returning at dusk if conditions on the plateau shift. The fauna cycles with it: the moths are active in the dim hours, the lizards hunt at the transitions, the birds are loudest in the false-dawn when the first light diffuses through the fog layer. The bat colony arrives and departs each night in a spectacle of compressed bodies and frantic wings. The ferns grow perceptibly — not fast, but if you mark a frond with a scratch, you can see the movement across weeks, the slow unfurling into the dim light. The mosses migrate, creeping across the stone at the pace of seasons. The spiders rebuild their webs every night, and by morning the gossamer veils are fresh and jeweled with moisture.

The people's lives cycle too. The fog-season — three months of thicker, more reliable moisture — is the time of abundance: more moths, more lizards, more fungal growth, more drinking water, more babies born. The dry season stretches long, and the cisterns are watched with a quiet, collective anxiety that never quite surfaces as words but shapes everything — who works, who rests, who is permitted to take a second portion, who climbs to the upper ledge to check the highest, slowest-dripping seep. When the fog returns, there is no celebration, exactly, but something like relief that settles into the body like warmth.

---

It is, in the end, a normal place. That is its most striking quality. The Maw is not fantastical. It is not the product of magic or impossible biology. It is simply what happens when a pocket of sheltered space persists for long enough on a dry, ancient land — life finds the margin, fills it, packs it dense, and stays. The people who live here are not mystical. They are practical, careful, communal, bound by shared dependence on a fragile resource. They know exactly where they are: at the bottom of a hole, in the dark, surrounded by animals, far from anywhere that matters to anyone but them.

And they do not leave. The plateau above is flat and barren and offers nothing. The Maw, for all its dampness and crowding and the constant feel of small legs across your skin, is the only place in this region where the world still bothers to be alive. They tend it. They listen to it. They have shaped themselves to fit its contours as precisely as the ferns have shaped themselves to its light.

It has been this way for a very long time.
