# The Subsidence

You stand on the edge of what appears to be a plain. It takes a long time to understand that it is not a plain.

The land stretches forward in a gentle, almost imperceptible decline — an old, old slope that has been eroding for so long it has nearly forgotten it was ever steep. What must once have been mountains are now low, wide swells in the earth, like the breathing of something enormous and asleep. They rise perhaps a hundred meters above the surrounding floor, but they are so broad, so *spread*, that you can walk across one without realizing you've gained or lost altitude. The stone beneath them is the same grey-brown tufa as everything else, pocked with tiny cavities where softer minerals dissolved long ago. It feels like standing on bone that has been left in weather for a million years.

The air is thin and bites at the inside of your nose. Not killing cold — not the white, annihilating cold of the deep arctic — but the persistent, leaching cold of elevation, the kind that settles into your joints and never quite leaves. Moisture comes not as rain usually but as a slow, nightly condensation that beads on every surface and collects in the hollows of stone. By morning, the low swells are dark with damp, and shallow pools sit in every depression, perfectly still, reflecting a sky the color of brushed tin. By afternoon, the moisture has retreated into the ground, the pools have shrunk to wet mud, and the tufa has gone pale and dry again. A rhythm. A breathing. The land inhales water at night and exhales it by day.

---

And yet — there are the gardens.

This is the part that does not make sense until you walk among them, and then it makes a different kind of sense, the kind that sits uncomfortably in the back of your throat.

Vegetation here is lush but *spaced*. Individual plants — or what you must call plants — grow in profuse, almost violent abundance, but between them are wide stretches of bare tufa and wet mud. They do not form a carpet. They form clusters, colonies, each one a small riot of life in the middle of enormous quiet. The tallest are thick-stalked things, maybe two meters high, with broad, fleshy leaves that are dark blue-green on top and a pale, almost luminous white on their undersides. They move slowly, tracking not the sun — the sun here seems incidental, a pale disc that the plants mostly ignore — but something else. You cannot tell what. Their stalks are rubbery and cold to the touch, and when you press your thumb into one, the indentation holds for a long time before slowly filling back.

Lower down, in the damper hollows between swells, there are mats of something like moss but thicker, almost cartilaginous, forming raised cushions that squelch underfoot. They are vivid green, the most saturated color in this landscape, and they exude a faint smell — not quite sweet, not quite mineral, like wet copper and crushed leaves. In the shallows of the condensation pools, you find fronded things anchored to the tufa, swaying gently in a wind you cannot feel. Their fronds are translucent. You can see the structure inside them, the veins, the slow movement of fluid — but the fluid is wrong. It does not move in one direction. It eddies. It stops. It reverses. You watch this for a while and realize you are not sure whether you are watching a biological process or simply *forgetting what direction looks like*.

There are no animals. No insects. No birds. The plants reproduce without them — spores, runners, the slow vegetative creep of rhizome networks through the shallow soil. But even this process seems uncertain here. You find places where a moss cushion appears to be growing *backwards*, its newest growth at the center withering while its oldest edges are tender and green. You find a fronded water-plant that appears to be two plants occupying the same space, one slightly out of phase with the other, so that the fronds shimmer faintly where they overlap. You are not sure the plant is aware of this. You are not sure *you* are aware of this.

---

The settlements are on the swells.

They are low and blocky, built from the same tufa as the ground beneath them, so that at first you mistake them for natural formations — another gentle rise in the terrain. But as you approach, the shapes resolve: walls, straight and deliberate; doorways, linteled with slabs of stone stacked without mortar, held by weight and patience; courtyards where the moss has been scraped away to expose bare rock, swept clean of condensation each morning by someone you will not meet for a while. The buildings are old. Not as old as the land, but old enough that their edges have softened, that lichens — or what passes for lichen here, a slow crust of grey-green that moves over decades — have claimed their northern faces.

There are perhaps a few hundred people in a settlement like this. They are sparse, like everything else. You see them at a distance, moving between the buildings or out among the gardens, tending the plants, harvesting the thick stalks at their bases. They wear layered garments of something woven from the rubbery plant fibers, stitched with bone needles, and their tools are carved from tufa or shaped from the dense, resinous heartwood of the oldest stalk-plants, which when dried becomes almost stone-hard. They do not speak much. You will later learn this is not cultural — it is practical. Sound behaves strangely on the subsidence. Words travel farther than they should, or not as far. A sentence spoken at normal volume in a courtyard may arrive at the next building garbled, re-ordered, the final syllables arriving before the first. The people have adapted. They speak in short utterances. They use hand signals. They have developed a sign language of extraordinary nuance, and their silence is not emptiness but a dense, active, gestural conversation that you simply cannot read.

They do not seem surprised to see you. This is unsettling. You are later told — through a combination of gestures and carefully spaced, single-syllable spoken words — that strangers arrive. Not often. But the subsidence is vast, and people walk in from its edges, having crossed distances that should have killed them, or they simply *appear* in the courtyards some mornings, blinking, wearing clothes from no place anyone recognizes. One woman signs to you that her grandmother arrived this way. The gesture for *arrived* is the same as the gesture for *came apart and reassembled*.

---

The reality problems are not dramatic. That is the worst part.

On the subsidence, nothing explodes. Nothing tears open. The sky does not bleed. Instead, things become *uncertain*. You wake in your borrowed room and the wall is in a slightly different place than it was. Not moved — you can see no gap, no disturbance — just *differently located*. The doorway is the same width but seems to be a different shape, a trapezoid now where it was a rectangle, and when you measure it with your hands, it is a rectangle again. You walk out into the courtyard and the distance to the nearest swell seems longer than yesterday. The settlement seems to have rotated a few degrees. The people do not react to this. They have lived with it for generations, and they have developed practices — rituals, habits, a kind of architectural theology — for coping with a world that does not hold still. They rebuild constantly. Not because the buildings fall, but because the buildings *shift*, and a shifted building must be un-shifted, corrected, brought back into alignment with the measurements that are carved into a tufa tablet in the center of town. The tablet is the most important object in the settlement. It is the only thing no one has ever seen change.

Or — no. That is not true. One man, very old, signs to you that the tablet changed once, when he was a child. One of the measurements was different by the width of a fingernail. The entire settlement spent a month deciding what to do. They eventually agreed that the tablet was right and that *the world* had changed. They adjusted everything else to match. The old man finds this very funny. His laughter is silent — just his shoulders, just the shaking of his head.

---

At night, the condensation comes, and the gardens glow.

Not brightly. Not all of them. But certain plants — the fronded ones, and some of the tall stalk-plants — emit a faint, cold luminescence as the moisture settles on them, as though the water itself is phosphorescent, or as though the plants are briefly remembering a time when there was light and are producing the memory of it rather than the thing itself. The glow is blue-white and intermittent, pulsing slowly, and it does not cast shadows. You can stand in the middle of a glowing garden and look down and see no shadow beneath you. You check again. You check your hands. They are there. But the light does not acknowledge them.

The silence at night is profound. No insects. No wind. The condensation itself makes no sound that you can hear — only a faint, felt vibration in the soles of your feet, as though the ground is very quietly absorbing something. The settlements darken. The people sleep, or do not sleep. You hear — or think you hear — the sound of a distant stone settling, a deep, slow, almost subsonic creak, as though the ancient plateau beneath you is turning over in its sleep. This sound may be real. It may be the land itself, still slowly subsiding, still settling into a final form that it has not quite reached, after all these eons.

You lie in your borrowed room and feel the wall with your hand. It is where you left it. For now.

The silence continues. The condensation gathers. Somewhere in the gardens, a fronded plant reverses the flow of its fluid, and the movement, if you could see it, looks exactly like time going backward — except that nothing else goes with it, and so it is only a plant, doing a small impossible thing in the dark, in the cold, in the vast and quiet ruin of a world that has been ending for longer than worlds are supposed to last.
