# The Mat

---

You are standing on the oldest living surface you have ever known.

It is a sphagnum carpet. Not a field of moss — the *carpet* — singular, unbroken, the one thing, continuous from edge to edge of this low basin. The basin is roughly room-shaped. Perhaps forty meters across, perhaps fifty, with shallow rim-like rises where older peat has compressed into something dark and almost stony, but even those rises are covered, even those are carpet. The ceiling — if you can call it that — is open air, a grey-lit sky that admits a flat, even daylight, the kind of light that comes through thick cloud that has held for a week. There is no sun to locate. The light simply is.

The carpet is not flat. It undulates gently in rolling swells, each one the memory of some ancient subsurface event — a boulder that sank, a trunk that finally compressed — but those events are geological now, smoothed into softness over what feels like an impossible duration. The surface is spongy and slightly uneven underfoot, pale green to gold to ochre, alive with a texture that shifts as you look at it: hair-fine filaments, tiny rosettes of leaves no bigger than a fingernail, capsules on pale stalks that catch condensation and bead it into globes that hold their shape a moment too long before sliding down into the weave. Your boots leave impressions that fill with dark water as you lift your feet. The water is not pooled anywhere in particular — there are no puddles, no streams, no visible sources — it is simply *present*, held in the body of the moss like memory in the body of a person, dispersed, ubiquitous, impossible to extract without also destroying the thing that holds it.

The air is cool. Not cold — but it finds your wrists and the backs of your ears. You are aware of your skin. The humidity is absolute. Your breath doesn't fog exactly, but it thickens the air briefly each time you exhale, a micro-weather event that dissipates inches from your lips.

Below the living surface — within reach of your arms if you were to dig — the color darkens rapidly. Green becomes olive, olive becomes umber, umber becomes a black-brown that resists your hands like compressed wood. This is peat. The lower layers are peat. Miles of accumulated generations, each year adding a few millimeters of living moss that dies and compresses beneath the next year's growth. Scientists — if any had ever been here — might call the deepest layers hundreds of thousands of years old. The mat does not know this. The mat is not aware of time in that way. It is aware of the damp, and of the light, and of the faint, distant vibration.

The vibration.

You feel it before you recognize it as sound. It enters through your feet, rises through your calves, arrives in your chest as a pressure rather than a frequency. Then, once you identify it, you hear it — a low, steady mechanical drone, industrial in character, a resonance like a generator running at the bottom of a well. It is the oldest sound in this place, older than the fauna, older possibly than the surface-level mat. Something beneath the peat, something in the substrate layers, in the rock or beneath the rock, has been running for a very, very long time. It does not change. It does not pulse or cycle. It hums. The moss has grown into harmonics with it. The fauna has adapted to it.

And the fauna.

They are everywhere. Not visible all at once — the density of the air is negligible, the space above the carpet is nearly empty, cathedral-height and hollow — but at the surface itself, *in* the weave, the place is teeming. You begin to see them as your eyes adjust: springtails, hundreds of thousands of springtails, tiny hexapod bodies hopping in silent arcs across the moss, each jump a few millimeters, the collective effect like static across a screen. Mites in the axils of the leaves, orange and red and translucent, no bigger than punctuation marks. Moths at rest during the day, their wings pressed flat to the surface, cryptically patterned in ochre and grey, nearly invisible until one shifts and you realize the carpet has an eye. Spiders — long-legged, patient — at the margins of the darker patches. Beetles with iridescent wing-cases moving purposefully between the capsules. Centipedes in the upper peat layers, glimpsed as pale, rapid shapes when you disturb something. There is no predation you can observe, but there must be. The equilibrium is too stable for there to not be predation — it is simply quiet, hidden, happening in the margins, resolved into the same unchanging pattern everything here has settled into.

The sound builds. Not the drone beneath — that is constant, is the ground state — but the collective sound of the fauna. A clicking. A ticking. A faint, dry, multiplicitous sound like seeds rattling in a paper bag, amplified by the resonance of the carpet itself, which turns out to be surprisingly good at transmitting vibration. The invertebrates are communicating. Stridulating. Drumming on leaf-surfaces. The mat listens and transmits. The result is a texture of sound at the edge of audibility that, multiplied by the hundreds of thousands, becomes the loudest thing in the room — a grainy, persistent, ambient roar that fills the hollow air above the surface like the sound of a beach heard from inside a shell. It is not unpleasant. It is not pleasant. It is the sound of a place that has arrived at its final configuration and has no reason to alter it.

There is no wind. There is no rain today, though the moss is wet with yesterday's rain, or the day before's — the moisture has no urgency to leave. Water here moves only by capillarity, by slow exchange between living cells and air, a migration so gradual it has no visible expression. If you press your palm flat to the surface and hold it there for a minute, your hand will be wet when you lift it. That is the speed of water here.

There are no trees. There is no vertical structure. The rim of the basin is just high enough to suggest enclosure, to give the impression that you are in a room whose walls are made of compressed earth and whose ceiling is grey sky. The sense of containment is gentle — you could walk out in any direction — but the mat, and the drone, and the clicking, and the cool damp air are persuasive. They suggest you have arrived at a place that does not change, that has not changed in longer than you can meaningfully conceive, and that your arrival is the most disruptive event in its recent history, and even that disruption is already being absorbed, your footsteps filling with water, the springtails closing around the edges of your prints, the moss already beginning the slow work of growing over your absence after you leave.
