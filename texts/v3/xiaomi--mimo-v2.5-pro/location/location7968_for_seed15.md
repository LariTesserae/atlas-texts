# The Grotto of Steaming Channels

The space is small — no larger than a village longhouse — and nearly all of it is water. Warm, mineral-laden water that laps against walls someone once cut from soft volcanic tufa with adzes and chisels and patience.

You would enter from below the surface. The water is milky, not clear — pale with dissolved calcium and sulfur, faintly yellowed where the light catches it. It is always warm. A natural geothermal vent somewhere beneath the carved floor sends up a slow, steady column of hotter water that blooms at the center of the chamber like a fountain too tired to reach the ceiling. The vent was not made, but everything around it was. Someone found this pocket of heat beneath the hillside and *worked* it.

The walls tell you everything. They are rough-hewn but deliberate — scalloped with tool marks still visible, still sharp, barely weathered by the mineral water that coats them. Channels have been cut into the stone at deliberate angles, directing overflow from the central spring toward the chamber's edges, where it drains through gaps in the stacked-stone foundation. These channels gurgle and hiss as water moves through them. The sound is immense in the enclosed space: water against stone, water against water, the low rumble of the vent itself, and everywhere the soft percussion of drips falling from the shallow ceiling back into the pool. Everything echoes. Everything is louder than it should be.

The light is extraordinary. The tufa is pale — cream and white and faintly golden — and it is studded, naturally and through human selection, with veins of crystallized calcite that catch and scatter any light that reaches them. A single shaft, roughly the width of two men standing shoulder to shoulder, has been cut vertically through the rock above. It rises perhaps four meters to the surface, where the hillside grasses part and the open sky meets its mouth. Sunlight pours down the shaft in a column — direct, strong, almost white — and strikes the water's surface, which shatters it into the calcite veins and the mineral haze of the water itself until the entire chamber glows. On clear days, the light is almost painful. On overcast days, it is softer but never absent. The stone seems to hold and redistribute it. There are no shadows that last.

The basin itself is not uniform. Someone carved broad, shallow steps along the near wall — a place to sit in water up to the waist, up to the chest, up to the neck. The treads are worn smooth where bodies once rested, but the edges are still crisp. This place was used, but not for long. Not long enough to wear away the ambition of its making. Deeper toward the center, the carved floor drops away and the natural vent dominates — a rough depression where hotter water wells up and mineral deposits have begun to build a low, crumbling cone of paleite crust. The crust grows slowly. In a century, it will be twice its current height. Now it is barely a knuckle above the waterline, soft and chalky, steaming faintly.

There is no animal life here. The water is too mineral-heavy, too sulfurous, too enclosed for fish or amphibians. There are no insects. The only things alive are the thin mats of thermophilic algae that cling to the walls near the waterline — pale green, almost white, barely distinguishable from the mineral deposits beside them. They photosynthesize in the shaft-light. They grow slowly. They are the only vegetation, and they are easy to miss.

The air above the waterline is warm and wet and thick. Steam rises from the vent and collects against the ceiling, where it condenses and drips back down in a constant, soft rain that feeds the pool from above. The ceiling itself is rough — untouched, left natural where the builders decided the chamber was tall enough. Water beads on its irregular surface and falls in uneven intervals, each drop catching the shaft-light for an instant before it strikes the water and adds its small sound to the larger noise.

The builders were not sophisticated. The channels are straight where they could have been curved, functional where they could have been ornamental. The stone is dressed but not polished. The foundation — where the carved chamber meets the natural hillside — is stacked dry stone sealed with clay, and some of the clay has begun to soften in the perpetual moisture. The place was made to hold and manage geothermal water, perhaps for bathing, perhaps for the ritual significance of a place where heat rises unbidden from the earth. The tools used were stone, perhaps copper. The work was skilled but not complex. Someone understood water's tendency to seek its own level and built accordingly.

The chamber is active. The vent does not stop. The water level rises and falls by a few inches over the course of a day as the outflow channels occasionally clog with mineral buildup and someone — or no one, now — clears them. The condensation drips. The algae photosynthesize and grow. The calcite veins in the walls slowly, slowly thicken. The paleite cone at the center of the vent builds upward grain by grain. In a thousand years, this chamber will be half-filled with mineral deposits and the channels will be choked and the water will be a shallow, warm puddle in a tomb of white stone.

But that is not yet. Now it is still a room. Now it still glows.
