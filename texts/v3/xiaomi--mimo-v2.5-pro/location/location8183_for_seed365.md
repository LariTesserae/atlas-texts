# The Lichenfold

---

You arrive, and the first thing you notice is that the ground breathes.

Not metaphorically. The basalt underfoot — raw, dark, still sharp enough to bite through a boot sole if you scuff wrong — pulses with a slow systole. Warmth wells up through cracks in rhythmic surges, and the mist that fills this caldera shifts with each exhalation, thinning for a moment so you can see farther across the bowl, then thickening again until visibility collapses to twenty meters, ten, the length of an outstretched arm.

The caldera is not large. Perhaps four hundred meters across, rim to rim, and the rim itself is not old — the walls are jagged spires of unweathered basalt, black and glassy where fractures split cleanly, rust-red where sulfur has begun its slow work. Some of the spires are taller than others, creating a ragged crown around the depression. Above them, there is only the grey-white lid of the cloud layer, seamless, featureless. You cannot tell where the sun is. You cannot tell if it is day or night. The light here is a flat, pearlescent dimness that comes from everywhere and nowhere — the mist itself seems to hold and redistribute whatever illumination penetrates from above, and the result is a perpetual grey-violet twilight with no shadows, or rather, with shadows that fall in all directions at once from every protruding thing.

You have been here for eleven minutes. You have not heard a single sound.

---

The forest begins at the caldera floor and climbs the interior slopes in tiers, following the terraces that the builders have laid into the hillside. But first — the builders, and then the forest, because the forest is what makes the builders make sense.

The settlement is small. Twenty-two structures, if you count generously — stone-walled, stone-roofed, built from the same basalt that forms the caldera walls, cut by hand with tools you can see stacked neatly near the largest building: chisels of harder volcanic glass, mauls of river cobble lashed to wooden hafts, measuring cords of braided plant fiber. The construction is organized but not dense. Buildings are spaced well apart, connected by paved paths of flat basalt slab, each path slightly sunken to channel the ever-present moisture into runnels that feed into a central cistern. The buildings are low — none taller than a person's shoulder — and their roofs are shallow domes, each one covered in a thick living cap of something that is not moss, not lichen, not quite plant.

This is where you begin to understand.

---

The dominant life form of the Lichenfold is a sessile animal.

It has no common name among the builders, who refer to it by a dozen specific terms depending on its age, color, and position. In the old taxonomy of the lowlands, it would not be classified as fauna at all — it has no visible locomotion, no apparent nervous system, no mouth in any recognizable sense. It grows in thick, ruffled sheets across every surface: basalt, wood, bone, leather, skin. It is the thing that covers the rooftops. It is the thing that makes the forest. It is the thing that, when you crouch and look closely at the caldera floor, has turned every square centimeter of exposed stone into a soft, breathing carpet.

It is a colonial organism, possibly — or possibly it is a single organism, the entire caldera-spanning body of it a single individual of unfathomable age. It photosynthesizes, but dimly, barely enough. Its primary energy source is the geothermal venting that pulses through the basalt: it is chemosynthetic, drawing heat and dissolved minerals from the deep rock through the cracks, metabolizing sulfides and iron compounds the way a plant metabolizes light. This is why it thrives in the dimness. This is why it thrives at altitude, in cold, in a place where nothing green should grow so riotously.

It is everywhere. And it is not alone.

The fauna of the caldera is overwhelming in the truest sense — not because you are swarmed, not because creatures crowd and press against you, but because *nearly everything you are looking at is animal.* The forest is a forest of animals. What you took for trees are columnar structures of aggregated lichen-animals, built up over decades, branching where the colonies have differentiated into specialized forms: broad, flat panels that maximize surface area for chemosynthesis; tall vertical ridges that catch the faint updrafts from thermal vents and disperse spore-analogues; dense mats at the base that house the reproductive forms. Each "tree" is a city. Each surface teems with smaller differentiated organisms — tiny motile forms that live *within* the sessile sheets, emerging at intervals you cannot predict to crawl across the surface in silence, performing some function you cannot guess at. Grooming, perhaps. Transport. Reproduction.

You press your palm against a trunk-structure. It is warm — warmer than the air, warmer than the stone beneath your feet. The surface gives slightly, like firm cartilage. When you pull your hand away, the impression you left fills in within seconds, the surface knitting itself shut. You cannot tell if the trunk noticed you.

The builders' settlement sits in the center of this, on a slightly raised platform of naturally smooth basalt where the lichen-animals grow more thinly — not absent, but restrained, as if the builders have negotiated a boundary. And in fact, when you look at the edges of the platform, you can see that the stone has been *cleaned* — scraped back to bare rock, regularly, by hand. The lichen-animals creep back, slowly, and are scraped again. This is the central labor of the settlement: an ongoing, silent negotiation with the dominant life form, a daily act of refusal and accommodation that defines the rhythm of life here.

---

There are people in the buildings. You know this because you can see a few of them — five, six, visible through the mist at various distances, each one engaged in one of the caldera's characteristic tasks. One is scraping the platform edge with a flat basalt blade, kneeling, moving in a slow sideways shuffle. Another is harvesting: cutting away a carefully selected section of lichen-animal from a tree-structure, working with obvious precision, taking only what is needed and leaving the underlying structure intact. The lichen-animal does not bleed. It does not react. But the harvester works with the focused attention of someone performing surgery.

The population of the settlement is small — perhaps forty people at most, though you have seen only a fraction. They do not call to each other. You have not heard them speak. This may be cultural, or it may be practical: the caldera is so quiet that even a normal speaking voice would carry across the entire basin, and there is something about this place that resists noise. Not aggressively. The walls do not echo. The mist swallows vibration. You try clapping your hands once, experimentally, and the sound dies so completely that you are left uncertain whether you actually made it. The silence is not absence. It is *presence* — a thick, weighted thing that fills the spaces between you and everything else.

And within that silence, the caldera is ferociously active.

The lichen-animals are in constant low-level motion — not the motile forms, but the sessile sheets themselves, rippling in slow peristaltic waves that you can only see if you stare at one spot for a long time. The thermal pulses come every few minutes, each one triggering a cascade of metabolic activity: color shifts across the sheets (muted blues and purples blooming into warmer ochres as the heat arrives, then fading back), spore-dispersal events from the vertical ridges (tiny particles, barely visible, rising into the mist like inverse snow), and a brief acceleration in the movement of the motile forms, who surface in greater numbers during the warm pulse and then submerge again as the stone cools. The mist itself is in slow, constant rotation — the thermal upwelling drives a convection cell within the caldera, and the fog turns like a vast, invisible millwheel, carrying moisture up the inner slopes and letting it condense and run back down through the runnels.

There are predators, of a sort. Certain motile forms specialize in consuming others — you spot one eventually, after sitting still for what feels like an hour: a creature the size of a thumbnail, six-legged, with a body like polished obsidian, threading its way through the surface of a lichen-sheet toward a smaller form that is busy doing whatever the smaller forms do. The approach is achingly slow. The smaller form does not flee. The predation, when it comes, is silent, brief, and almost gentle — the smaller form is simply *incorporated*, absorbed into the body of the larger in a process that looks more like digestion by contact than by consumption. When it is over, the predator is imperceptibly larger, and the surface of the lichen-sheet is imperceptibly thinner at that spot.

This is happening everywhere. Right now. Across every surface. You cannot see most of it because you are too large, and because the mist hides the finer details, and because the silence makes the violence — if it is violence — feel like something else entirely. Something closer to weather.

---

The buildings have interiors, and the interiors are the most human space in the caldera. Here, the lichen-animals have been kept out — scraped away, maintained, held at bay by daily labor. The walls are bare basalt, dark and cool. Sleeping platforms are raised on stone legs, above the damp. Fires are burned in central pits, vented through small chimneys — the smoke contributes to the dimness, perhaps, though it is a small contribution against the vast grey of the mist. There are tools: blades, awls, cord, woven-fiber containers for harvested lichen-animal (which is, you eventually learn, the primary food source, prepared in ways you are not privy to). There are also objects of no clear purpose — smooth basalt spheres, carefully polished, arranged in patterns on flat shelf-stones. Counters of some kind. Calendar-stones, perhaps. Mnemonic devices. The builders have a system, and it is a system made entirely of stone, fiber, and patience.

No metal. No fired ceramic that you can see. The basalt is worked by percussion and abrasion alone — painstaking, generational labor, each tool a small inheritance. And the tools are good. The scrapers hold an edge. The chisels split true. Primitive, by the standards of lowland cities, but precise, and perfectly adapted to the one task that matters most here: maintaining a livable space within a world that is, overwhelmingly, *not theirs.*

Because the lichen-animals will take the settlement back. You can see it happening in real-time, if you watch the platform edges: the slow creep of blue-violet sheets across bare stone, the emergence of motile forms exploring the new territory, the tentative growth of a new ridge-structure forming where a runnel of mineral-rich water has pooled. The builders scrape it back. It grows again. They scrape it back. This is the rhythm of the place — not domination, not symbiosis exactly, but something more like *coexistence under perpetual renegotiation.* The caldera belongs to the lichen-animal. The builders live in it by permission, and the price of that permission is constant, silent, careful labor.

---

You sit on the rim of the caldera, on a spire of raw basalt, legs dangling over the edge. Below you, the mist turns slowly, and through its thinner patches you can see the whole of the small world: the dark trees, the pale carpets, the grey buildings, the tiny figures of the builders moving with the deliberate slowness of people who have learned that haste is wasted here. The air is cool and damp and smells of sulfur and something else — a living sweetness, like the breath of a sleeping animal, which is in fact exactly what it is.

The silence presses against you like water. Beneath your hands, the stone is warm.

Something very small and very old is crawling across the back of your wrist. You do not brush it off.
