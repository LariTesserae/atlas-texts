# The Becoming Spine

---

The mountains are not old. They do not remember ice ages or river-carving or the patient work of lichen. They are *hours* old in some places — weeks at most — and they are still moving.

You stand on a ridge that did not exist yesterday. The rock beneath your feet is warm to the touch, not from any sun (there is no sun you can see, only a pale diffusion behind miles of particulate haze), but from below, from the heaving mechanism of the place itself. The stone is dark basalt threaded with intrusions of something lighter — a cream-colored mineral you cannot identify, shot through in veins like lightning frozen mid-branch. Where the two lithologies meet, the boundary is sharp and weirdly glassy, as though the contact happened too quickly for proper crystallization.

Because it did.

---

## The Shape of the Ground

The elevation is staggering and recent. Imagine a mountain range caught in the act of being born — not the slow tectonic argument of continental plates, but something more violent and compressed in time. Ridges erupt in parallel rows like the dorsal spines of something enormous and sleeping. They run roughly north-south (though direction feels meaningless here; there is no pole star, no magnetic field your instruments recognize). The ridges are sharp-angled and barely weathered. Corners of exposed rock still have the fracture surfaces fresh from whatever cataclysm split them free. In places, entire faces of cliff are mirror-smooth — glinting faintly with mineral sheen — where the stone simply *parted* along a plane no erosion ever cut.

Between the ridges: narrow defiles choked with rubble. The rubble is angular, unsorted, still warm. Some blocks are the size of buildings. Some are the size of fists. They lie where they fell, or where they were thrown, or where the ground simply opened and deposited them in chaos. There is no talus slope here, no orderly progression from cliff to scree to foothill. The debris fields are random, toppling, full of unstable voids and pockets where one wrong step would send blocks cascading in a roar that would take minutes to stop.

The scale is open. You can see for miles across this jagged terrain — ridge after ridge after ridge, diminishing into the haze, each one slightly lower or slightly higher than the last in no discernible pattern. The view is vast but featureless in its repetition, the way a sea is featureless: the individual waves matter less than the fact of them, everywhere, always.

---

## The Haze and the Dark

Light is scarce, though not absent. The sky (if it is a sky — the distinction between atmosphere and ground is blurred in places where particulate density increases near the surface) glows with a diffuse, sourceless amber. It is the color of thick honey held in front of a dying fire. There is no direct illumination. Shadows are soft and directionless, which makes depth perception unreliable — distances deceive, heights seem lesser than they are, and the ground ahead can hide drop-offs that do not register until you are upon them.

The haze is not smoke, exactly. It is mineral dust, suspended in air that is warm and dry and faintly metallic on the tongue. It smells of hot stone and something sulfurous, though not aggressively so — more like the memory of sulfur, an afterthought. The particles are fine enough to hang for what seems like days without settling, kept aloft by constant thermal updrafts from the restless ground. In places where the terrain opens into wider basins, the haze thickens into a murk that swallows the far ridges entirely, leaving you in a pocket of visibility no wider than a few hundred meters — an island of clarity surrounded by amber fog.

At what might be night (the light varies slightly, pulsing, as though something above the haze brightens and dims in cycles you cannot time), the darkness is profound. The warm glow dies, and what remains is a red-black murk lit only by the faintest thermal radiance from the rock itself. In that darkness, you can *feel* the ridges — not through any sense you can name, but because the air moves differently near them, channeling through the defiles in faint warm currents, and because the ground hums.

---

## The Hum

Sound is mostly quiet. But *mostly* does a lot of work in this sentence.

There is a subsonic vibration in the rock — a thrum that lives below hearing, felt in the bones of the feet and the walls of the chest. It is constant but irregular, rising and falling in slow swells, the way a fevered body pulses. Occasionally it intensifies. When it does, you can hear it: a low grinding, tectonic and vast, like the world clearing its throat. This is the sound of new rock being made somewhere beneath you. Somewhere close.

And then, intermittently — unpredictably — the quiet breaks entirely. A crack splits the air like a cannon shot as a ridge somewhere to your east (or west; direction is a guess) fractures and shifts. The sound arrives a half-second after the visible event: a wall of rock leaning, buckling, sliding down into a defile in a cascade of falling stone that roars and clatters and sends a plume of dust billowing upward into the haze. The plume rises, flattens against a thermal ceiling, and spreads into the amber murk, slowly feeding it. Then: quiet again. The hum continues. The ground settles. New fracture surfaces catch what little light there is, gleaming.

This happens often enough that you learn to expect it, but never predict it. The landscape is *turbulent*. Not chaotic in a windstorm sense — the air is relatively calm — but structurally chaotic. The geometry of the place rearranges itself. Ridges that were there this morning are rubble by evening. New spines push upward in the basins, sometimes visibly over the course of a day: the ground humps, cracks, and a seam of lighter mineral extrudes like a slow-motion wound opening in reverse, building itself upward, cooling at the edges even as it rises.

---

## What Lives Here

Almost nothing. The air carries no pollen, no spores you can identify. The rock hosts no lichen, no moss. There is no water — not in pools, not in vapor, not in the mineral chemistry you can test. The stone is anhydrous. Whatever fluids drive the volcanism below, they are not aqueous. They are something else — denser, hotter, mineral-rich, more akin to molten glass than magma in some of the veins you can see exposed in the freshest fracture faces, where the interior of the rock glows dull red before cooling to black.

And yet.

In the deepest defiles, where rubble has stabilized for perhaps a week and the haze is thinnest because the warm updrafts carry particles upward and away — in those sheltered pockets, there is *something* on the stone. A dark film. Thin as varnish, almost invisible, distributed in patches no wider than a hand-span. It has a faintly iridescent quality when the light catches it at the right angle: not green, not any organic color, but a deep bronze shift, like oil on warm metal. It does not grow. It does not spread, as far as you can tell. But it persists. It may be mineral — a precipitate from whatever gaseous effluent vents through the cracks. It may be something else. You cannot collect a sample; when you touch it, it flakes to powder and blows away. It is the only thing in this landscape that might be called *settled*.

## What Was Built Here

There are structures. Faint ones. Scattered and strange.

On the broadest ridge you can find — a spine of unusually stable rock that has not fractured in what might be weeks, its crest wide enough to walk without scrambling — someone has placed cairns. Not many. Perhaps a dozen. They are small, knee-high at most, built from selected stones chosen for their flatness and their color: the cream-colored intrusions, the lighter mineral. Against the dark basalt, they stand out like pale punctuation marks on a black page. They are arranged in a line along the ridge's crest, roughly equidistant, pointing in the direction of the ridge's run. They serve no structural purpose. They are not walls, not shelters, not markers for anything visible. They are simply *placed*. Intentionally. Patiently.

Whoever built them is not here. There is no camp, no refuse, no tool marks on the stones (though the stones show signs of careful selection rather than cutting — they were found, not shaped). The cairns are old enough to have settled slightly, their bases fused to the ridge stone by heat. They have survived at least one episode of the ridge fracturing beneath them — you can see where the rock split and re-sealed around the base of one cairn without toppling it, as though the mountain cooperated.

Elsewhere, lower down in a basin that is currently stable, there is a single wall. Just a wall — no roof, no floor, no second wall. It is made of stacked basalt blocks, mortared with nothing, standing perhaps two meters high and four meters long. It faces northeast (or what your compass calls northeast; the compass spins erratically here, but you have assigned a provisional north). Its purpose is unclear. It does not shelter; the wind is not strong enough to require shelter. It does not demarcate; there is nothing on either side of it that differs. It is simply a wall, standing alone in the amber haze, casting a soft directionless shadow, built by someone who understood the idea of walls but not, perhaps, the reason for them.

Or perhaps the reason is simply this: to place something here that did not grow from the ground. To say *I was here, in this place that is still becoming itself, and I made a mark*. The wall is that mark. It is already slightly out of true, its base shifted by the constant micro-tremors. In a month — if a month is a meaningful unit of time here — it will fall. The landscape will consume it. It will become rubble indistinguishable from the rest. But it is standing now.

---

## The Rules

This is a place in the act of formation. The rules are geological, and they are happening *fast*. Heat drives everything: the upwelling thermal energy that pushes ridges upward, that keeps the rock warm, that suspends the haze, that makes the air shimmer. Stability is temporary and local. A ridge that stands today is a basin tomorrow. The only constant is the motion — the heaving, cracking, extruding, collapsing rhythm of a world still cooling, still building its first skin of solid rock over whatever fire lives beneath.

Water will come eventually. Erosion will follow. Life, maybe. But not yet. This is the *before*. This is the raw draft, the unedited version, the world as first sketch — all energy and no patience, all form and no memory.

The cairns on the ridge are the only memory this place has. And they are already beginning to lean.
