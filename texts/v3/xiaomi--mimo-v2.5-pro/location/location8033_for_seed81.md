# The Wrought

## Basalt and Steam, In Absentia

The city has no name that anyone living uses, because no one living is here. But the city is not dead. That is the first wrong assumption to correct.

It rises from a high basalt plateau — three thousand meters of elevation where the air should thin and cool, but doesn't. The atmosphere is thick, close, and carries a permanent thermal haze that turns the sky into a diffuse white-gray ceiling. Not cloud. Not fog. Something between the two, a suspended particulate that catches what light the pale sun offers and diffuses it into a generalized glow that casts no shadows. Midday here looks like deep twilight elsewhere. Everything is rendered in slate and umber and the dull bronze of oxidized metal.

The heat is extraordinary. Not the dry blasting heat of open desert — this is a wet heat, a heat that *persists in the body*, that presses against the skin with the weight of humidity. The air carries water content that would be lethal to unprotected lungs over long exposure. Every surface sweats. The basalt itself weeps: dark rivulets trail down every cliff face, every exposed stone, mineral-laden water that evaporates almost as fast as it emerges, leaving white and orange crusts in layered patterns — years of slow secretion, geological calligraphy written by stone that cannot stop crying.

There are no plants. None. The soil chemistry is wrong — too much sulfur, too much dissolved metal, pH that would digest root tissue in hours. But this is not a sterile world. Crusts of extremophile organisms coat the rocks in dull films: black, rust-red, a strange iridescent green in places where copper seeps concentrate. These are not plants. They do not photosynthesize in any way that would be recognizable. They metabolize the mineral exhalations of the plateau itself — the sulfur dioxide, the dissolved iron, the trace heavy metals that percolate up from the magma chamber kilometers below. Life here eats rock-juice and breathes heat. It has no interest in sunlight.

## The Architecture

The city extends across the plateau in a rough radial pattern, organized but not rigid — more like a living system that grew according to some internal logic than like a plan imposed from above. The structures are industrial in character: tall, narrow buildings with heavily buttressed walls, their surfaces perforated with ventilation slots and water-channeling grooves. Pipelines of blackened iron and ceramic run between them at every level, some as thick as tree trunks, others hair-thin, creating a three-dimensional web that connects every structure to every other. Smokestacks and cooling towers punctuate the skyline at intervals, though what they vent now is mostly steam and a faint sulfurous haze.

The construction materials are local basalt, cut and fitted with extraordinary precision, joined with a dark hydraulic cement that has weathered to the same color as the stone — you cannot always tell where the natural rock ends and the masonry begins. Where the plateau has natural fissures and gorges, the city bridges them with arched viaducts, and these have become channels for water: hot springs and condensation runoff are funneled through the upper tiers of the city in open aqueducts, cascading down through a series of catchments and filtration pools before disappearing into subterranean infrastructure. The sound of water moving through the city is the loudest thing here — a constant, layered trickling and gushing that echoes off stone walls, muffled by the humid air into something soft and almost organic.

The buildings are empty. This is the fact that redefines everything else. Every window is dark. Every doorway opens onto interiors that are intact, furnished in some cases, but holding no one. Workbenches with tools laid down. Control rooms with switches in mid-position. Beds made with geometric precision and never slept in. The city was *completed* — fully built, fully equipped, infrastructure connected and running — and then either never populated or abandoned so thoroughly that no trace of habitation remains. No signs of violence. No decay beyond what the climate imposes. It is as though everyone simply chose to not arrive.

## The Machines

But the city is not still.

In the industrial quarter — a dense cluster of larger buildings on the plateau's northern edge, where the basalt is thinnest over the magma body — the machines still run. Engines. Turbines. Something that sounds like a heartbeat, if a heartbeat were made of iron and could be felt through the soles of your feet from half a kilometer away. The factories process... something. Raw materials are drawn up from bore-holes in the plateau floor — superheated mineral-laden water, volcanic gases, semi-molten silicate — and transformed through stages of cooling, chemical separation, and mechanical reforming into building materials. Basalt fiber. Hydraulic cement. Ceramic pipe. Iron drawn from the magmatic effluent in a process that involves enormous magnetic fields and temperatures that would kill you from across the room.

The factories are building the city. That is what they have always done. The city built itself, expanded itself, maintains itself. The infrastructure is not *for* anyone. It is its own purpose. Somewhere in the system's logic, there was perhaps once a plan for inhabitants, a planned population that these structures were meant to shelter and organize. But the machines received no stop signal, and so they continue: drawing stone from below, cutting it, fitting it, laying pipe, running wire, expanding the grid one structure at a time across the plateau's slow geological margins.

The rate is glacial. One new building every few years. A pipeline extension once a decade. But the city is very old, and so it is very large — kilometers across, dense with structures, a metropolitan grid that could hold tens of thousands. It holds none.

## Sound and Absence

The quiet is the most unsettling quality. Not silence — the water is always moving, the machines always humming at the threshold of hearing, the steam always hissing from a thousand open vents. But the *quality* of the quiet. Human spaces are made for human noise: echoes that expect voices, acoustics shaped for footsteps and conversation. Here, the sound that returns to you is always wrong. Your footsteps on the basalt streets are too sharp, too singular. When you speak, your voice finds the walls and comes back to you with nothing layered on it — no ambient chatter, no distant radio, no door slamming three blocks away. The city is an acoustic architecture of one, and the effect is of being the only living note in a cathedral designed for full orchestration.

Occasionally, something moves. The fauna is sparse but not absent. Pale, eyeless arthropods — related to the mineral crusts, perhaps, scaled up through some divergent evolutionary path — inhabit the ventilation systems and pipe-chases, feeding on the extremophile films. They are slow, deliberate, and utterly indifferent to human presence. When they move, their legs click faintly on the stone, a sound like a single drop of water striking a tile. There are larger things in the deep infrastructure, in the bore-holes and the lower factory levels — shapes heard more than seen, presences inferred from the occasional displacement of warm air or the sound of something heavy shifting in darkness. The fauna does not leave the infrastructure. It belongs to the city in a way that you do not.

## Water System

The hydrology is the city's circulatory system and its most alien feature. Water enters from below — superheated, mineral-saturated, drawn up through the bore-holes — and is cooled through a cascading series of open channels, catchment pools, and misting towers distributed throughout the city. By the time it reaches the surface streets, it is warm but survivable, and it flows through open gutters carved into the basalt roadways in precise geometric patterns. The gutters feed into filtration systems beneath each building, where further chemical processing occurs, before the water is either recycled into the industrial processes or vented as steam through the cooling towers.

But the system leaks. It has always leaked. Water seeps from joints, weeps from walls, springs from cracks in the road surface where the pressure below is too great. The entire city is damp. Condensation coats every interior surface, running in rivulets down interior walls, pooling in rooms where the drainage is imperfect. In the dim light, with no one to maintain them, some of the lower buildings have become shallow indoor pools — warm, mineral-rich, faintly luminous with the bioluminescence of extremophile blooms that thrive in the standing water. These pools glow a faint blue-green in the darkness, the only light sources that are truly *here* rather than filtered through the haze above.

The water is not safe. The mineral content alone would sicken you. The heat would burn. And there are things in the water — the same extremophile organisms that coat the rocks, but evolved for the liquid medium, forming filamentous structures that look almost like submerged roots, almost like hair, almost like something reaching.

## The Edge

At the plateau's margins, the city simply stops — its outermost buildings unfinished, walls rising without roofs, pipe networks terminating in raw open ends that vent steam into the air. Beyond, the land falls away in steep basalt cliffs into a lower landscape that is invisible from above, lost in the thermal haze. The edge is where the water is most present: springs burst from the cliff face in dozens of places, waterfalls of hot mineral water cascading down the rock in white and orange curtains, feeding a marshland at the cliff's base that is more chemistry experiment than ecosystem — pools of vivid green, ochre, and rust where different mineral concentrations create a patchwork of reactive environments.

From the unfinished outer buildings, looking out and down, you can see the marshland steaming. You can hear the waterfalls. You can feel the heat rising from below in waves that shimmer even through the already-shimmering air. And behind you, the city hums and drips and builds itself, one stone at a time, into the waiting void of its own emptiness.

It is not hostile. It is not welcoming. It is a place that was made for a purpose it never received, and it has been settling into the weathering of that absence for longer than you can measure. The basalt is smooth where hands were expected to touch it. The doorframes are worn — not by passage, but by the slow abrasion of wind and water in a place where wind and water never stop.

The city is patient. It is not waiting for you. But it would not turn you away.
