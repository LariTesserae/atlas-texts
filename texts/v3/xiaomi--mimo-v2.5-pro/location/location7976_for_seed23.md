# The Luminous Divulgence

---

The glacier never finished what it started here, and what it left behind is still not finished.

You come upon it from a saddle ridge, wind-scoured and bright — the light is *aggressive*, the kind that makes you see the red inside your own eyelids, that turns the snowfields into something almost liquid with glare. The air is so cold and so clear that distances become meaningless. A thing ten meters away reads as both arm's-length and impossibly far. You breathe and the air doesn't want to enter your lungs, as though it has forgotten it's a gas.

But then the terrain drops.

Not sharply — the stone has been here too long for sharpness. Everything is rounded, shaled, softened by ten thousand years of freeze-thaw that turned what was once a granite tor into something that looks like a sleeping animal, or a dissolving cathedral, or a melting candelabra. The rock is pocked with basins and channels, and in every one of them: water.

Not ice. Water.

The cold should have it. The temperature is wrong for liquid water — it should be locked, sealed, silent. But it moves. It *persists*. Thin sheets of it slide over rock faces that have been worn to a polish so fine they look like bone. It wells up from cracks in the bedrock, dark and impossibly clear, beading and channeling and joining and splitting without any apparent source or destination. The stones *weep*. You can see the wet darkening creeping down every surface in real time, the way a stain spreads through linen. Condensation fogs the air in places where it shouldn't, microclimates of moisture that cling to you and freeze instantly on your eyelashes, your knuckles, the fine hair at your temples — and then melt again, and then freeze.

The sound is —

Almost none.

This is the part that stays with you. The water moves without noise. Or rather: it makes noise the way a sleeping creature makes noise — breath, shift, the faintest creak — but it is so close to silence that the silence becomes the point. You hear your own heartbeat and it feels intrusive. You hear the tiny ticking of ice crystals forming on your coat and it sounds like a language. Once, a slab of mineral-stained ice the size of a dining table cracks and tilts downward into a basin with a slow, deliberate grace, and the sound it makes is a single low tone, almost musical, that hangs in the air for eleven seconds before the silence swallows it again.

The basin it falls into is the size of a small room, and it is *full of life*.

---

This is what the old walls are for.

They are low — waist-high, sometimes knee-high — and made of a darkite rock that someone, long ago, shaped with patience and simple tools. Notched and fitted without mortar. Some have fallen, splayed outward like toppled teeth. Others still hold their curves, defining semi-circular enclosures, passageways, roofless rooms that open to the blazing sky. They follow the contours of the stone basins, built to partition water, to create little bordered worlds within the greater basin. Settlement infrastructure. Primitive — stone-on-stone, bone-handles, the occasional rust-eaten iron tool half-melted into a crack — but *organized*. Someone built this with intention. Someone lived here and arranged their living.

Now the fauna lives here instead.

Or alongside. It's hard to tell what's wild and what's domesticated when everything is this strange.

The basin-pools are *crowded*. Small creatures — six-limbed, pale, furred with something that looks almost like mineral deposit — huddle in colonies along the water's edge. Dozens of them per meter of shoreline. They press together, overlapping, breathing in the same slow rhythm so that the whole colony seems to pulse. They don't move much. They don't need to. The water brings things to them: microorganism blooms, mineral films, tiny translucent creatures that surface momentarily and are seized with sudden, precise violence by a single pale limb before the stillness returns. The sound of feeding is inaudible.

Above the waterlines, along the eroded rock shelves: birds. Or something like birds. Long-bodied, short-winged, with reflective eyes that catch the relentless light and throw it back in shards. They perch so still they seem carved. They are packed shoulder-to-shoulder along every ledge and outcrop, hundreds of them, a density that seems to defy the sparse vegetation that should support this much life. But nothing here is eating plants. The food chain is *aquatic*, water-borne, based on chemistry and microbial abundance rather than photosynthesis. The vegetation — lichens, mostly, a few stunted grasses clinging to crevices — is almost decorative. The real ecology runs through the water, underground, through channels we cannot see.

Larger things move in the deeper basins. You catch their shapes through the water: long, flat, segmented, moving with a slow undulation that suggests enormous patience. They are not fish. They are not anything you have a word for. The old walls seem to have been built, in part, to *contain* them, or to define the spaces they move through, or to keep them away from the smaller pools where the pale furred colonies live. The walls are infrastructure in the oldest sense: management of dangerous neighbors.

---

And the place changes.

This is the turbulence. Not loud — almost never loud — but constant. The water shifts its paths. A channel that was flowing this morning is dry now, and a new rivulet has appeared twenty meters away, following a crack that wasn't there before. A rock shelf that held three hundred of the long-winged birds at dawn has calved off a piece — silently, slowly, the stone just *deciding* to be somewhere else — and the birds have redistributed themselves across the remaining space without alarm, without apparent communication, just a smooth redistribution like poured liquid. The larger basin-creatures surface, change pools, submerge again. A wall section, ancient and settled, cracks along a frost line and settles an inch lower, and the water immediately claims the new geometry, flowing into the gap, refilling, redefining what is wall and what is pool.

It is a place perpetually renegotiating itself. The brightness is constant — the light seems to come from everywhere, reflected off water, off ice, off the polished rock, off the mineral films on every surface, so that there are no shadows longer than a hand's width, so that everything is seen, everything is exposed, nothing hides. And yet the quiet persists. The dynamism is *silent*. Things are falling and rising and breaking and forming and the loudest sound is the wet creak of stone settling, the tiny crystalline ticking of ice, the almost-infrasonic pulse of the colony creatures breathing in unison.

You are standing in a room-sized place that contains ten thousand presences, and none of them are making any noise, and all of them are moving.

The air tastes of iron and old snow. A wall weeps. A creature adjusts its grip on wet stone. The light is so bright it feels like a sound you can't hear.

You are very small here, and very visible, and everything around you is quietly, furiously alive.
