# The Loom-Room

There is no soil. There never was.

You are inside something. That much is clear — there is a ceiling, or what functions as one: a canopy of woven fiber so dense and layered it holds its own geometry, dark at its highest reaches, pale where it swoops low enough to touch. The floor is the same substance, compressed underfoot into something that resists like packed earth but yields like felt. The walls are not separate from ceiling or floor. Everything is the same material — a fiber, a sinew, a dried tendon of plant-matter — woven, knotted, braided, coiled, layered upon itself in a construction so total and so ancient-looking yet so *recent* that you can still see where the fingers stopped. Where the last twist was made. Where the last tuck was pressed in.

This is a room. Perhaps thirty paces across. Perhaps forty. It is difficult to know, because the geometry here does not hold still in the mind.

---

The fiber is alive.

Not metaphorically. Where you look closely — and you will, because the quiet presses you to look closely — you can see that the weave is not inert. The strands are green, yes, though a green that leans toward ochre, toward amber, a green that has never drunk water but is not dying. They photosynthesize faintly. The light comes not from a sun but from the fiber itself, which at certain intersections gives off a slow, warm, almost reluctant luminescence — like embers in ash, except the ash is woven and the embers are chlorophyll. You could call it bioluminescence, but it's more accurate to say the structure *remembers* light, remembers a sun that may not exist, and the memory is enough.

The warmth is everywhere. It radiates from the walls, from the floor, from the compressed layers overhead. Not hot — not oppressive — but warm the way a body is warm, the way a nest is warm after the animal has left it an hour ago. The fiber breathes. Not rhythmically, not with any cycle you can detect, but it exhales in slow, almost imperceptible pulses of heat and a smell like dried lavender pressed between the pages of a very old book.

No water. None. You would know — your throat is beginning to feel it, the thirst that has no source, because there is nothing here that is wet, nothing that drips, nothing that condenses. The fiber is dry. The air is dry. The warmth is dry. Everything is dry the way a kiln is dry, the way the inside of a drum is dry. And yet it lives. The vegetation — if you can call it that, if this mass of woven living filament can be called *vegetation* — is lush in the way a tapestry is lush, thick with presence, thick with itself, layered so deeply that you cannot see the back of any wall.

---

There are things living in it.

They are quiet things, mostly. This is a quiet place. The dominant sound is a faint, arrhythmic creaking — the structure settling against itself, fibers tightening and releasing in micro-tensions that take hours to resolve. Below that, almost below hearing: a soft dry rustle, like paper being smoothed by a palm. That is the fauna.

They live in the weave. You see them at the edges of attention — a movement in the wall that was not the wall moving. A shape the size of a thumb, segmented, pale, withdrawing into a crevice between braids. A wing-case, iridescent, catching the amber light, attached to something you cannot fully see because it has folded itself into a knot in the ceiling. There are many of them. Dozens of species, perhaps more, all adapted to this interior, all dry-bodied, all quiet. Some seem to be made of the same fiber as the structure — as if the weave budded them, or as if they *are* the weave, given local agency. A beetle that is also a knot. A moth whose wings are frayed ends of braid. A long, pale, many-legged thing that moves through the walls the way a worm moves through soil, except the soil is architecture and the worm is part of the architecture and the architecture was built by hands and the hands —

You haven't seen any hands.

---

Here is where the geometry fails.

The room is small. You know this — you can see its edges, its walls, its ceiling. And yet: when you walk toward a wall, it takes longer than it should. Not much longer. You might not notice the first time. But your stride is normal and the wall is thirty paces away and it takes forty-five. And the corner you saw — sharp, the weave terminating in a crisp, recently-made edge (this place is *young*, this place was *just built*, the fibers haven't even begun to darken with age) — is not where it was. It moved. Or you moved. Or the room moved. Or the concept of "corner" does not apply here the way you want it to.

There are places where the weave opens into passages too small to enter but large enough to see through, and what you see on the other side is this room. This same room. From an angle that should not exist. From above, perhaps, but there is no above — the ceiling is solid. From inside the wall. You are looking at yourself from inside the wall. The fauna are watching you through these passages. They are not hostile. They are *patient* in a way that suggests they have been here much longer than the structure, or that the structure has been here much longer than it appears, or that *longer* is not a concept that applies.

The rooms shifts — but slowly. So slowly. The dynamic here is geological, almost. A wall that was twenty paces away yesterday is twenty-one paces away today. Or eighteen. The change is too slow to watch but too real to ignore if you stay long enough. And you will stay. The quiet holds you. The warmth holds you. The density of life, woven so thick around you that you are essentially *inside* a living thing, holds you.

---

And this is the final thing, the thing you understand without being told:

Someone made this. Many someones. With their hands. With no tools more complex than a bone awl and a stone blade and their own ten fingers. Every knot was tied by a person. Every braid was braided by a person. Every layer was laid down by a person pressing their palms flat against the living fiber and *pushing* it into the shape of a wall, a ceiling, a floor, a room. There are finger-marks in the weave — thousands of them, fossilized in the fiber, pressed in by hands that must have worked for years, for generations, to construct a space this dense, this layered, this *total*.

But there are no people now.

Only the room. Only the warm, dry, quiet, living, *wrong* room — growing slowly into its own impossible geometry, its fauna moving through its walls like thoughts through a sleeping mind, its light burning amber from the memory of a sun, its silence so deep and so complete that after a while you begin to hear the weave itself, the sound of living fiber holding itself together against every law it is quietly, patiently, breaking.
