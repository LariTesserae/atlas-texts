# The Terraces of the Incipient Caldera

---

You are standing on the lip of something that has not finished becoming.

The ground beneath you is basaltic andesite — dark, glassy where it cooled against air, dull and microcrystalline where it cooled slowly within the formation. It is sharp. Not weathered-into-roundedness sharp, but *newborn* sharp, as if the mountain range extruded itself from the planetary mantle within living memory and has not yet learned to accommodate visitors. Every ridge still carries the fracture patterns of its own emergence: conchoidal breaks, columnar joints that terminate abruptly in surfaces still slick with volcanic glass, edges that could draw blood from a careless hand. The rock has barely begun to know rain.

And yet there is moisture here — insistent, persistent, arriving from below rather than above.

---

**The heat rises from the ground.**

Not uniformly. Temperature is a landscape unto itself, a topography mapped in invisible contours. In some places — near the vents, the fumaroles, the black-shrouded fissures where the caldera's breath escapes — the rock surface reaches temperatures that blister. You would not stand there barefoot. You would not stand there for long at all. But move twenty meters laterally across the raw basalt shelf, and the heat diminishes to something merely oppressive, merely desert-like, merely *high-altitude-equatorial-noon-with-no-shade* hot, which is survivable, which is the temperature at which most of the city operates. The thermal gradient is one of the governing laws here. People orient their lives by it the way lowlanders orient theirs by rivers.

The vents produce steam — superheated, mineral-laden, smelling of sulfur and something older than sulfur, something with a metallic sweetness that coats the back of your throat. This steam rises in plumes and curtains and then encounters the high-altitude air, which is thin and startlingly dry in its upper reaches, and the steam collapses into condensation almost immediately. Droplets form on every cool surface. The undersides of ledges weep. Metal fixtures — pipes, railings, structural beams — are perpetually slick with a warm film of mineral-rich water that deposits its dissolved solids as it evaporates, leaving behind a slow accretion of white and ochre and pale green crusts. Some of these crusts are sulfates. Some are arsenates. Some are compounds for which the mineralogists on staff have had to invent provisional names.

This is the primary water cycle of the place: not sky-to-ground, but ground-to-surface-to-air-to-surface again, a local convective loop that never rises high enough to form clouds and never travels far enough to reach the lowlands. Water exists here in pockets, in films, in trickles that follow the microtopography of the rock for a few dozen meters before evaporating into nothing. There are pools — hot, shallow, mineral-saturated — in depressions on the caldera rim and along the inner terraces. Some of them are vividly colored: emerald green from dissolved copper, rust-red from iron, a milky, almost luminous blue-white from silica in colloidal suspension. None of them are potable without extensive treatment. Most of them are beautiful in a way that makes you uneasy.

---

**The city is built into the caldera wall itself.**

Not on top of it. *Into* it. The rim of this caldera — which geologists believe is still in its formative phase, still settling, still adjusting, possibly still accumulating magma in a chamber far below — rises to an altitude that would be extreme on any terrestrial world. The air at the rim is thin enough that exertion becomes difficult and cognition dulls slightly if you are not acclimated. The sky above is a pale, washed-out blue-white, almost colorless, the way high-altitude skies always are — stripped of the moisture and particulate matter that give lower skies their depth.

Into this thin-air world, the settlement descends in terraces. The terraces are not carved so much as *fitted* — the builders found the natural ledges, the columnar-joint platforms, the broad shelves where the basalt fractured horizontally during formation, and they built upon them, and then they cut connecting passages between them, and over generations — slow generations, measured in decades per level — the construction accumulated. From a distance, the city looks like a stratigraphic column. Each terrace is a stratum of human habitation layered over a stratum of geological youth.

The construction materials are overwhelmingly mineral. Stone cut from the caldera rim itself. Metal smelted from the rich mineral deposits that condense around the geothermal vents — iron, copper, trace amounts of heavier metals that the volcanic chemistry concentrates in peculiar ways. There is almost no wood, no fiber, no organic material of any kind. The buildings are dense and heavy: thick-walled, flat-roofed, with deep-set openings that function as both windows and thermal regulators, allowing the occupant to modulate how much caldera-heat enters the space. The walls themselves accumulate mineral crust over time, so that older structures look as if they are slowly being absorbed back into the geology, their artificial geometries softening under layers of deposited stone.

The density of the built environment is high. Terraces are narrow — the usable width of each natural shelf is limited — and so the construction is vertical where it can be and tightly packed where it cannot. Buildings share walls. Passages between terraces are sometimes no wider than a body. Infrastructure runs through every gap: pipes carrying heated fluid from the geothermal wells to the industrial sectors, pipes carrying condensed water from collection surfaces to treatment facilities, conduits of indeterminate purpose that hum with a vibration too low to hear but that you can feel in your molars if you press your face against them.

---

**The industry is geothermal, and it is everywhere, and it is quiet.**

This is the strangeness of the sound-scape, or rather the absence of it. A place this dense with industrial infrastructure should roar. It should clatter and hiss and bang. But the primary energy source is heat — steady, reliable, endlessly rising from the chamber below — and the conversion processes are largely passive. Fluid circulates through closed loops by convection. Turbines turn slowly, geared for torque rather than speed. The factories — and there are factories, producing refined metals, processed minerals, construction materials, chemical compounds — operate on thermodynamic gradients that barely fluctuate. The heat source does not vary. The atmospheric temperature does not vary. There are no seasons here, no weather to speak of (a high-pressure system that occasionally slides across the caldera rim brings a thin overcast and a marginal increase in surface moisture, but this is the extent of meteorological drama). The machines turn at the speed of geology, and they do so in near-silence.

What you hear, instead, is the earth. A sub-bass drone that is not so much heard as felt — the resonance of the magma chamber, transmitted through kilometers of rock, expressed as a vibration in the ground itself. It is always there. You stop noticing it after a few days, or you are told you stop noticing it, and perhaps that is the same thing. You hear the occasional hiss of a vent releasing pressure through a controlled aperture. You hear the trickle of condensate down a wall. You hear, very faintly, the voices of the inhabitants — but there are not as many of them as the city's density would suggest, and they tend toward quietness, toward a kind of hushed economy of speech that might be cultural or might be an adaptation to the thin air, in which speaking loudly costs more breath than it is worth.

---

**The inhabitants are rare, and the fauna is rarer.**

There is no life native to this altitude and heat that the inhabitants recognize as fauna. There are microbial communities — thermophilic, chemolithotrophic — that form thin biofilms around the cooler hot springs and in the condensation zones. These films are sometimes visible as faint iridescences on wet rock: a sheen of rose, a slick of amber, a grey-green bloom that vanishes when the surface dries. Whether these organisms are truly alive in the way the inhabitants understand life is a subject of quiet, unresolved debate. They metabolize. They reproduce, or at least they spread. But they have no structure that anyone has been able to classify as cellular. They seem more like *patterns in the chemistry* than organisms. The strangeness here is subtle and persistent: the feeling that you are looking at something that has not yet decided whether to be alive.

Among the inhabitants themselves, there is a similar ambiguity, though they would not thank you for saying so. They are human — baseline human, or close enough that the differences might be epigenetic, might be developmental, might be nothing more than the accumulated adaptations of a population that has lived at extreme altitude in a mineral world for long enough that the body has begun to accommodate. They are lean. Their hands are rough with mineral dust that has ground into the skin over years and become almost dermatological, almost structural. Their eyesight is excellent at close range — the fine discrimination needed to assess mineral specimens, to read the texture of rock, to work intricate metal fittings — and slightly poor at distance, as if the thin air and the washed-out sky have taught their eyes not to bother with the long view.

They number in the low tens of thousands, spread across perhaps thirty vertical levels of the caldera wall and a rim-length of several kilometers. This is enough to fill the terraces densely but not enough to fill them *fully* — there are sections of the city that are quiet and partially abandoned, older terraces where the mineral crust has thickened to the point that doorways have narrowed to half their original width, where the walls have begun to look less like architecture and more like geological features. These older sections are not derelict exactly. They are simply... *slow*. Left to accrete. Left to become part of the rim again, at the pace at which the rim itself is becoming.

---

**The pace is the thing.**

Nothing here happens quickly. The caldera is rising — measurably, instrumentally, but not perceptibly within a human lifetime. The city is growing — a new terrace is under construction on the northeastern rim, has been under construction for eleven years and will be under construction for perhaps another five. The mineral deposits are accumulating — the pipes need to be descaled of their internal crusts on a regular schedule, and the material removed is valuable enough to justify the labor, and this cycle has been running for longer than anyone alive can remember. The inhabitants measure their lives in geological terms. They speak of *this formation* and *the previous formation* and *the next formation* with the easy familiarity that lowland cultures use for seasons.

The sky above does not change. The heat does not relent. The ground hums its sub-bass note, always. The steam rises and condenses and rises and condenses. The metal weeps its mineral tears. The buildings sink slowly into the stone they were built from, and the stone slowly rises to meet them, and in the middle of this immense and patient process, the inhabitants move through their terraces with the unhurried precision of people who know that the ground beneath them is still becoming, and will be becoming for a long time yet, and that there is no particular reason to rush any of it.
