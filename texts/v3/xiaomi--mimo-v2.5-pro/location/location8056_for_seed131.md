# The Overflow

You are standing in a place that is not much larger than a closet.

The walls are concrete, or were concrete once — now they have the texture and weight of something geological, something that accumulated rather than was poured. You could mistake them for tufa, for the calcified deposits of a hot spring, if you did not notice the faint rectangular regularity still ghosting through the grain. A rebar spine shows through the north wall where the surface has finally sloughed, and the exposed iron has become something else entirely: a seam of ochre mineral, rusted so completely past corrosion that it has stabilized into a kind of ore. It will never rust further. It has finished becoming what it was becoming.

The floor is a shallow, warm puddle — never more than two inches deep, never less than one. It does not drain. The water arrives from somewhere behind the west wall through a pipe whose mouth has been eaten smooth by time until it resembles a natural spring or the open beak of a limestone cave formation. The pipe has been here so long that calcite has grown over its flange in scalloped rings, and the water that emerges carries dissolved calcium that deposits itself in slow terraces across the floor, building tiny shelf-dams that the water sheet-lips over in miniature cascades no wider than a hand.

The sound is extraordinary. The sound is *total*. That pipe — or whatever system feeds it, whatever ancient reservoir and gravity-head still pushes water through after all this time — delivers a roaring, thrumming, unchanging torrent. It is not loud the way a factory is loud, with rhythm and variation. It is loud the way a waterfall is loud: a single white body of noise that fills every molecular space. You cannot hear yourself breathe. You feel the vibration in the water of the puddle, in your molars, in the thin cartilage of your ears. It is the kind of sound that, after long enough, you stop hearing and begin to *be inside of*, the way a fish is inside water. If you lived here, you would go deaf and not notice.

Every surface that the water touches — and the water touches everything, because the humidity is absolute, because mist hangs in the air and condenses on the ceiling and drips downward in a second rain competing with the first — every surface is covered in life. Not much life. Not diverse life. But *lush* life: a single thick quilt of moss so deep green it is nearly black, feathered with the pale fronds of a handful of fern species that have found their niches and never left. One species grows only on the east wall where light falls from the grate above. Another grows only on the horizontal pipes near the ceiling where mist collects into droplets. A third grows in the calcium terraces of the floor itself, threading its roots into the mineral shelves. They have divided this room among themselves absolutely, and the divisions have not shifted in a long time.

Above — the ceiling is low, barely eight feet — a grate of cast iron bars admits the only light. Daylight, warm and ordinary, falling in slatted bars across the moss and the water. The grate is old enough that a vine of some terrestrial origin has grown down through it and colonized the upper pipe-work, its roots gripping the corroded metal with the slow hydraulic patience of tree-roots splitting stone. It gets enough mist to survive. It has never produced a flower. It has no pollinator to aim for.

There are no animals here. There are no animals *near* here. The sound alone would drive away anything with ears to hear. Once, perhaps, something small and amphibian sheltered in the moss — but that would have been at the very beginning, before the water found its full voice, before the calcium terraces sealed the floor. Now there is nothing that moves on its own except the water.

The temperature is steady and warm, warmed by the water, warmed by the compression of sound into such a small space, warmed by the slow chemical patience of the calcium dissolving and precipitating and dissolving again. It does not change with the season. Whatever feeds this pipe is deep enough or pressurized enough to deliver water at the same temperature, the same flow rate, year after year after year. The moss grows the same thickness in winter as in summer. The fern fronds emerge at the same rate. The mist hangs at the same height.

Nothing about this place has changed in a very long time. It is not *old* the way ruins are old, with collapse and loss and the drama of entropy. It is old the way a stalactite is old: it finished its essential work long ago and now simply persists, each moment a repetition of the last, the system closed and complete and running on the momentum of geology. Someone built this room — someone poured these walls, hung these pipes, set this grate — and what they built was so simple and so sturdy and so perfectly located over whatever water table or artesian seam feeds the pipe that the construction outlived its makers, outlived their language, outlived any memory of why it was built or what it was supposed to do. Now it is just this: a wet, roaring, green, bright pocket of nothing, holding its shape against time by having no reason to change.
