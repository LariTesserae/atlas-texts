# The Woven Lowlands

---

You stand at the edge of what appears to be a flooded garden, but there is no earth beneath your feet. There never was.

The ground — if it can be called that — is layers upon layers of packed fiber, reed-mat, daubed clay, woven palm-frond, pressed pulp, dried and wet and dried again over a span of time no one here counts. It is perhaps four or five meters deep, this accumulated substrate, and below it: more of the same, older, compressed into something approaching stone but never quite arriving. The surface is damp. It is always damp. Water wells up through the weave in slow seeps that darken the fiber in spreading stains, pooling in shallow depressions that someone scooped out generations ago with hands and sticks and broken shells. The water is warm. The air is warmer. Everything is bright — a saturated, white-gold brightness that seems to come from the sky but also from the surface of things, as though the woven material itself is remembering light and giving it back.

There are no natural features. No rock. No soil. No slope that wasn't shaped by pressing and piling. The low mounds rising from the wet matting are structures — or were structures — or are in the slow process of becoming something that language struggles to pin down. Some are huts, clearly: walls of lashed vertical reeds plastered with a grey-white paste that has hardened into something with the texture of soft limestone but the weight of cork. Others are terraces, raised platforms of layered fiber and clay where plants grow in thick profusion — not in soil, but in the composted upper layers of construction itself, which have become indistinguishable from soil if you do not look too closely.

And the plants. They are everywhere, and they are enormous for this flat, wet, low-lying country. Broad-leafed things with waxy surfaces that bead with condensation even in this heat. Climbing vines that have been trained along frameworks of bent sapling-ribs — but the training happened so long ago that the frameworks are now entirely inside the living wood, invisible, the vines holding their shapes through pure botanical memory. There are groves of something like bamboo but thicker, their hollow stems resonating faintly when the air shifts, though the air rarely shifts. Cabbage-analogues the size of tables, their inner leaves a startling, almost luminous chartreuse. Water-growing plants that fill the seep-pools with rosettes of succulent foliage, edged in a colour that is not quite red, not quite copper — a colour that seems to exist in a frequency your eyes weren't built for, and which leaves a faint ache behind your optic nerve when you stare too long.

This is one of the ways the world is breaking down. The colours. There are too many of them, and some of them are wrong.

The scale of the place reveals itself slowly. From any single point, the landscape appears intimate — a cluster of mounds, a copse of shaped vegetation, a pool, a corridor of woven walls chest-high. But it extends. It extends in every direction with a flatness that defeats perspective. The raised structures and planted groves are spaced irregularly, sometimes with fifty meters of open, wet matting between them, sometimes with barely room to walk. The sparseness is deliberate, you think, or at least it has become customary. These people — and there are people, or were people, or are something that remembers being people — did not build densely. They built broadly, patiently, with the unhurried accumulation of generations, each one adding a mound, extending a terrace, weaving a new section of wall into the old, training a vine to grow toward a gap where light was needed.

The fauna moves through this constructed world as though it were forest. There are creatures in the upper layers of the taller structures — something with a long, flattened body that slides between woven panels with a dry, papery sound. Birds, or bird-analogues, with enormous wingspans relative to their small bodies, riding thermals that rise from the hot matting in slow, visible columns of shimmer. In the pools: amphibious things, not quite frogs, not quite salamanders, their skin the same not-quite-red as the water-plants, their calls a single sustained tone that blends so completely into the ambient quiet that you only notice it when it stops. Insects are everywhere but understated — small, metallic-green, hovering in loose clouds above the vegetation, making almost no sound at all. Larger things move at the edges of perception. Something that might be a mammal-analog, low-slung and slow, browses on the outer leaves of the cabbage-plants. It is the colour of the matting. It is shaped like the matting. When it stops moving, it becomes part of the floor.

The quiet is profound. For a place so alive — and it is alive, seething with creatures and dripping with water and burning with plant-growth — the absence of noise is almost aggressive. The water seeps rather than flows. The vegetation doesn't rustle; the air doesn't move enough. The creatures have evolved, or been shaped, or have simply settled into a collective discretion. Occasionally a stem of bamboo-analog will emit a low, resonant hum as internal temperatures shift — a sound like a distant foghorn played through wood — but even this fades quickly, absorbed by the wet matting, which drinks sound the way it drinks water.

The heat is tremendous but oddly uniform. There is no gradient, no breeze, no shifting of cloud. The sky is a flat, brilliant, unblinking white — not overcast, not clear, simply *bright*, as though the atmosphere itself is a lamp rather than a medium through which light passes. The heat does not build toward storms. The moisture does not build toward rain. Everything is already at the level it has always been at, or seems to be. The world is in equilibrium. A strange, suspicious equilibrium — the kind that suggests not balance but *arrest*, as though something that should be changing has been frozen mid-process. The plants are lush but do not appear to be growing. The water seeps but the pools never rise. The creatures move but the populations do not increase or decrease. Decay happens at exactly the rate of renewal. Nothing accumulates. Nothing diminishes.

And yet the construction persists. The woven walls do not rot. The clay daub does not crumble. The fiber matting does not compress further. The sharp edges of recently-formed structures remain sharp — the clean cuts of shell-knife and stone-blade still visible in cross-sections of reed and bamboo, unsoftened by time or weather. Erosion should be tearing this place apart. A low-lying, wet, hot, biologically rich environment should be composting these hand-made things back into raw material within years, decades at most. But the constructions endure with an almost defiant permanence, as though they have more right to exist than the forces that would unmake them.

This is the other way the world is breaking down. The rules. The laws that govern what happens to things over time are present but *slack*, here, like ropes that have been cut and retied too many times, each knot adding a little slack, until the whole system hangs loose and the load it was meant to hold drifts without tension.

The people who built this are gone, or changed, or sleeping inside the walls. There are marks everywhere — tool-marks on the hardened clay, finger-pressions in the daub, the deliberate geometry of lashed joints, the clear intentionality of vine-training and pool-shaping and path-making. Someone built all of this. Many someones, over a long time, with their hands and their simple tools and their accumulated craft. But they are not here now, and the structures they left behind seem less like abandoned buildings and more like shed skins — hollow forms that once held a living shape and now persist on their own, animated by nothing, decaying into nothing, frozen in the moment of being-left.

Or perhaps they are the walls. Perhaps the builders are still here, but have become the thing they built — their craft so total, so encompassing, that it consumed the distinction between maker and made, and now the landscape itself is a single, sprawling, barely-conscious organism that dreams in woven fiber and wakes in seeping water and has no memory of ever being anything else.

The light holds. The heat holds. The water seeps. The vines do not grow. The creatures move in their orbits. Nothing changes. Nothing has changed for a very long time.

It is bright, and wet, and quiet, and still, and it goes on in every direction, and it does not end, and it does not begin.
