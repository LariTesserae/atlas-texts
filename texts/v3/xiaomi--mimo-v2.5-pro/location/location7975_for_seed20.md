# The Spout at Kiln-Summit

---

You hear it before you see anything. A wall of sound so total it stops being noise and becomes weather—a pressure against the skin, a presence that occupies the space where thought used to be. The mountain is *singing*, and the mountain is mostly water.

---

## The Shape

It is a seamount that should not be above the waterline. A black basalt chimney, a volcanic plug thrust upward by pressures the earth has already forgotten how to explain, rising roughly four hundred feet from a drowned caldera shelf. Its peak is maybe eighty feet across—room-sized, not landscape-sized. You could walk its perimeter in a few minutes if you could stand. You cannot stand. Nothing can stand.

The rock is *young*. Obsidian-dark and sharp in a way that suggests it was liquid hours ago, though the barnacles clinging to its lower flanks (the few that exist, the rare calcite smatters) suggest weeks or months. Time is not behaving. The stone is not behaving. The basalt has features that look like cooling columns except they are not hexagonal—they are triangular, then rhombic, then shapes that do not have names, changing their geometry between the moments you look away and look back. The mountain is still hardening, still deciding what shape it will be.

---

## The Water

The ocean here is wrong. Not metaphorically. Wrong.

It rises from below in slow, hypertrophic surges that do not correspond to tides or wind. The caldera shelf, twenty feet below the summit's baseline, is *usually* submerged—but "usually" is generous. The water comes up in walls. It recedes in spirals. When it crests the lowest ledges of the chimney, it does not behave like water cresting a rock; it climbs the stone in sheets, as if drawn by something beneath the surface, and then it *holds there*, a vertical skin of ocean clinging to the basalt, trembling, before it releases and falls—not downward, but *sideways*, spraying laterally into air that has no wind.

The water is warm. Not tropical-warm—*internally* warm, heated from within, as though the ocean itself has a fever. Where it meets the black stone, steam does not rise so much as *detach*, peeling upward in ribbons that hang motionless despite the gale-force noise surrounding them. These steam-ribbons are visible in the dim light as paler streaks against the permanent overcast—a sky that is low, bruise-colored, and does not move the way clouds should move. They mass and thin on no schedule.

In the deep troughs between the chimney's lower flanks and the outer reef, the water has become something else. Not quite solid. Not quite liquid. A translucent, gelid substance that holds luminescence despite having no bioluminescent source. It glows from *within its own chemistry*, a faint blue-white that pulses. Touching it (if you could reach it, if you were not already being deafened into disorientation) would feel like touching water that has decided to remember being ice but hasn't committed.

There are moments—turbulent, chaotic, every few minutes—when the water *falls*. Not from the sky. From the mountain. Fissures in the basalt, cracks that weren't there an hour ago, begin to weep seawater upward and outward. The mountain bleeds ocean. Columns of water erupt laterally from the stone face, arcing out over the caldera and falling in curtains. The sound of this—the roar, the impact, the hissing steam on hot rock—is the foundation of the noise. It does not stop. It shifts registers, it pulses, but it does not stop.

---

## The Light

There is almost none.

The overcast is not cloud cover in any meteorological sense. It is a *ceiling*—a flat, low, violet-grey mass that presses down to perhaps five hundred feet above the summit and does not move. No sun is visible. No stars. The light that exists is the light the system makes for itself: the faint bioluminescent glow of the altered seawater in the troughs, the dull red-orange of thermal vents where the chimney's flanks meet the caldera floor (visible when the water recedes enough to expose them), and an intermittent, sourceless flicker that occurs at the very peak of the summit—as if the air itself is sparking, remembering lightning and then forgetting it.

Your eyes adjust. You see in shapes. You see in silhouettes. The basalt is black against a black sky, and the only way to read the terrain is by the differences in *how* things are black—the matte absorptive black of dry stone versus the glassy, reflective black of wet stone versus the impossible faintly-glowing black of the substance that used to be water and now occupies the lower pools.

It is warm. The air is warm. The stone is warm. The water is warm. You are warm and getting warmer. There is no cold here. There is only *hot* and *not-yet-hot*.

---

## The Structures

At the center of the summit's small plateau, there is a single object.

It is a slab—roughly eight feet tall, four feet wide, two feet deep. Basalt, like the mountain, but *not* like the mountain. Where the mountain's basalt is chaotic, still-forming, deciding its geometry, this slab is *decided*. It has been finished. Its surfaces are planar. Its edges are true. It has been worked by something with an understanding of straight lines that the surrounding environment does not possess.

It stands upright without foundation or support. The ground around its base is smooth—not polished, but *worn*, as though something has knelt there, or circled there, for a very long time. There are marks on the slab. They are not writing. They are not decoration. They are shallow, deliberate incisions that form a pattern you cannot hold in your mind—the pattern changes each time you try to focus, as if it exists in a geometry that human vision cannot stabilize. Looking at it produces a sensation like vertigo, or like hearing a chord that doesn't resolve.

The slab is not warm. The slab is *cool*. In a world where everything—the air, the rock, the ocean, the steam, your own body—radiates heat, the slab is cool to the touch. This is wrong. This is the wrongest thing here, and this is a place where the ocean climbs walls and the mountain bleeds water.

No one built this slab. That is the wrong word. No one *made* this slab. It was *placed*—set into the mountain-top like a tooth into a jaw, a deliberate intrusion into a landscape that does not otherwise acknowledge deliberateness. It is the only object in the world that suggests intention. It is the only object that is not in motion.

---

## What Lives

Almost nothing.

On the lower ledges, where the warm water pools and recedes, there are mats of something that resembles algae but behaves like lichen—slow-growing, black-green, able to survive immersion and exposure in equal measure. They have no preference. They do not photosynthesize. They feed, as far as can be determined, on the thermal gradient between the stone and the water, metabolizing *temperature difference* into biomass. They are the only visible vegetation. They are sparse. They will not spread. They have been here as long as the stone has been here, and they will be here when the stone is gone, and they will not have changed.

In the deeper water—the gelid, luminous substance in the troughs—there are shapes. Jellyfish, perhaps, or things that have agreed to be jellyfish for now. Translucent, bell-shaped, roughly the size of a human head, they pulse through the not-quite-water with movements that suggest swimming but also suggest *falling in a direction that doesn't exist*. They are rare. A dozen, maybe, across the entire caldera. They make no sound (though in this place, nothing makes a sound separate from the wall of sound that is everything). They emit a faint counterpoint to the pool's glow—a cooler, greener light that flickers in a rhythm the pool does not share.

Nothing else. No birds. No fish in any conventional sense. No insects. The summit is too small, too loud, too hot, too *wrong* for anything to want to be here. The jellyfish do not want to be here. They are simply here because the geometry of the place has made "here" a place where things can be, and they are the things that ended up in that shape.

---

## The Noise

It must be addressed directly because it will not be ignored.

The sound is not one sound. It is the *sum* of a system in active convulsion:

**The thermal vents** along the lower flanks produce a low, subsonic thrum—not heard so much as felt in the chest, the teeth, the fluid of the inner ear. It is a 14-hertz drone that makes the eyes vibrate, that makes the edges of objects soften.

**The water eruptions**—the lateral gouts from the mountain's bleeding fissures—roar white and bright with impact. Where they hit the sea, the sound is a crash. Where they hit hot stone, the sound is a scream—the instantaneous flash-boil of seawater on basalt, a steam-blast that sounds like tearing metal.

**The spiraling surges** of the warm ocean produce a rushing, sucking sound as they climb the rock faces—like breathing, but enormous, like the mountain is a lung and the water is air and the mountain is hyperventilating.

**The rain**—there is rain, intermittent, heavy, warm, falling from the low overcast in sheets that arrive without warning and vanish without reason—adds a hiss, a patter, a roar when it is heavy, a static when it is light.

**The slab** produces no sound. The slab absorbs sound. In the few feet surrounding it, the wall of noise thins—not to silence, but to a muffled, underwater quality, as if the stone is a hole in the acoustic fabric of the place. Standing near it (if you could stand near it without vertigo from its shifting pattern) would feel like surfacing. Like breathing.

---

## What It Is

This is a place that has recently *become a place*. The seamount is new. The caldera is new. The water's wrongness is new. The slab is older than any of it, and no one knows why it is here or what put it here, and the mountain does not know either, and the ocean does not know, and the few jellyfish drifting in their luminous gel certainly do not know, and the pattern on the slab's surface changes each time you look because the slab is *telling you something you do not have the sensory apparatus to hear*.

It is a word being spoken into a world that has not yet developed ears for it.

The mountain is rising. The water is climbing. The light is failing. The noise is total. The slab is cool and patient and its pattern has not stopped shifting, not once, not ever, and the air tastes of sulfur and salt and ozone, and the sky presses down, and the sea presses up, and the small, black, sharp-edged island between them is the only thing that holds the word steady while the world it's being spoken into catches up.

It will not catch up. The world is too young. The erosion is too early. The mountain will sink, or the water will rise, or the geometry of the slab will finally *resolve* into something legible and the world will reorganize around whatever it says—but that has not happened yet. Right now, everything is in motion except the one thing that matters, and the one thing that matters is a cool basalt slab with a pattern you cannot read in a place you cannot stand in surrounded by a sound you cannot survive.
