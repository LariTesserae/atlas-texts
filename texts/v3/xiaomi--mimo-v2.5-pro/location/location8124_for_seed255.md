# The Carving

You descend, and there is no ground to stand on — only floor.

The floor is made. Everything here is made. There is no bedrock, no soil, no substratum that was not shaped by hand, and every surface you touch — and you will touch many surfaces, because the passages are narrow and the walls insist on your attention — every surface carries the marks of tools. Chisel-scars. Fingernail grooves. The scoring of something hard dragged repeatedly along soft stone, or what might be soft stone, though when you press your thumb into it, the material gives slightly, like cartilage, and it is warm from recent work and faintly damp.

It is always faintly damp.

---

## Condensation Country

The moisture here has no obvious source. There is no river, no spring, no aquifer bleeding through from some natural world outside — because there is no natural world outside. Or if there is, it has been walled over, built past, replaced entirely. The moisture simply *appears*. It beads on the undersides of lintels. It darkens the margins where wall meets ceiling in spreading stains that look, in the near-darkness, like bruises. It collects in shallow basins that someone has carved into every third or fourth surface — deliberate catchments, knee-high, waist-high, carved with lip-edges to keep the water from spilling — and the water in them is clear and very still and cold to the knuckle when you test it.

No one drinks from them, as far as you can tell. The basins fill. The basins overflow, slowly, and the water creeps along channels cut into the floor — channels barely wide enough for two fingers — and disappears into other channels, into a network so dense and fine that the floor itself is mostly channel, mostly grooved, and walking on it means feeling a faint grid through your footwear, a constant texture like Braille scaled up to the body.

The air smells like wet chalk and something beneath that — something mineral and vaguely biological, like the smell inside a mussel shell.

---

## The Builders (or: What Makes the Sound)

The sound is the first thing that overwhelms you and the last thing that leaves your understanding of this place.

It is *loud*. It is loud in a layered way, the way a factory is loud — not one sound but dozens overlapping, each with its own rhythm, its own source, its own direction. Near you now: the *tik-tik-tik* of a tool striking material, fast, rhythmic, almost frantic. Somewhere above: a dragging, grating scrape that goes on for ten seconds and stops and starts again. Somewhere below, if "below" means anything in a place where floors are also ceilings for lower levels: a low, resonant hum that might be many tools working in unison, or might be the structure itself vibrating.

And voices. Not language as you know it — but voices. Calls from one passage to another, short bursts of tonal sound, sometimes rising into what you'd call a shriek, sometimes falling into a guttural murmur. There are conversations happening in the walls. Not metaphorically. There are cavities within the walls — you can feel them when you press your ear to the surface, feel the air moving through them — and voices travel through these cavities like water through pipes, arriving distorted and directionless, so that sometimes you hear someone speaking clearly from inside the wall beside your shoulder and there is no one there.

The builders themselves — you glimpse them rarely. Not because they are hiding, but because the passages are dense and branching and they are always in the *next* space over, always just around the corner, always working in the chamber you just left. When you do see one, what you see is: a figure on its knees or crouching, working at a surface with a simple tool — a wedge, a scraper, a pointed stone set into a handle — and the figure is shaped almost like a person but not quite, and its hands are very large, and its attention is absolute. It does not look at you. It is making something. It is always making something.

The fauna is rare — but the builders are not fauna. They are the architecture's reason for existing.

---

## The Density

Imagine a settlement, but the settlement has no streets. It has no open squares, no plazas, no commons. Every space is a passage or a chamber, and every chamber connects to three or four or seven other chambers through openings that are sometimes door-shaped and sometimes hole-shaped and sometimes just rifts — cracks in a wall that someone has decided to *use* rather than repair. The structure goes laterally for what feels like kilometers, and it goes vertically in both directions — there are shafts with handholds carved into their walls going up into darkness and down into darkness, and you hear the sound of work from both directions.

You cannot see far. The light here is the light of almost-nothing — the faint bioluminescence of mineral deposits that someone has deliberately exposed by scraping away outer layers of wall-material, leaving veins of pale greenish glow in patterns that might be decorative or might be directional, like trail-markers. There are also embers: shallow stone bowls, placed at irregular intervals, in which something smolders — a dried fungus, perhaps, the one kingdom of life here that has any presence at all — and the embers give off a thin, red light and a smell like burned bread.

Between the glow-veins and the ember-bowls, you navigate by sound, by touch, by the faint air-movement that tells you where the passages open up and where they close.

## Emerging / Under Construction

The strangest thing about the material is that it is still *forming*.

Some of the walls you touch are hard — fully set, dense, cool. Others are soft, still curing, still taking shape. Run your fingers along a corridor and you'll feel the transition: hard, hard, hard, then suddenly soft and slightly tacky, then hard again. A section of wall that was made recently — in the last day, or the last hour. The tool-marks in these soft sections are fresh, deep, vivid. In the hard sections, the marks have smoothed, have become texture, have become the character of the wall itself.

There is a sense, moving through this place, that you are inside something that is growing. Not organically — not like a plant or a body — but *deliberately*, the way a sculpture grows under a sculptor's hands, each new stroke adding and defining. The overall shape is not random. There is a logic to the density, to the branching, to the way passages widen slightly where they intersect and narrow again between intersections, the way catchment-basins are always placed where three or more passages meet. Someone — many someones — are building this place according to a plan, and the plan is large enough that you cannot see it from inside, the way you cannot see the shape of a city from any single street.

But it is not finished. It is emphatically not finished. You come to dead ends where the wall-material is raw and new and still steaming faintly with moisture. You find chambers with floors half-grooved, the channel-network incomplete, the water sitting in pools where the channels haven't been connected yet. You hear, from certain directions, the sound of *beginning* — not tool-strikes but the wet, heavy sound of material being pressed into place, being packed, being shaped by bare hands.

## What It Feels Like to Be Here

Cool. Close. Loud. Damp. Dark. The air moves — there are drafts from the shafts, from the passages, from the breathing of the structure itself as it shifts and sets — but it moves slowly, and it carries the smell of wet stone and smoldering fungus and the faint animal warmth of many bodies working hard in enclosed spaces.

You are inside someone else's project. You are standing in the gap between intention and completion. The builders do not acknowledge you, but the building does — its density presses in, its passages narrow and widen around you as though you are a current in its body, as though it is learning the shape of you and deciding what to make of your presence.

There are rare creatures — small, pale, eyeless things that move along the ceiling grooves, drinking from the condensation channels. They are the only non-made life here, and they seem incidental, like pigeons in a cathedral, living in the margins of a structure that was not built for them.

The sound does not stop. The work does not stop. The moisture does not stop appearing. And if you stay long enough, you begin to notice that the passages you've already walked have changed — a new groove cut here, a basin deepened there, a wall section that was soft now hard and a wall section that was hard now *being carved again*, reshaped, reworked, as though the building cannot stop revising itself, as though perfection is a moving target and the builders will never reach it and would not stop even if they did.

This is a place that is becoming. It has not yet become. And it is very, very loud about the process.
