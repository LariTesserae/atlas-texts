# The Lattice Hollows

---

You are below the world.

Not in a cave. Not in a tunnel. In a *chamber* — one so large that its boundaries are matters of rumor, not observation. The ceiling arches two hundred meters overhead, maybe three, its surface rough with raw stone that has barely begun to understand erosion. There are no rivers carving this place. No dripping. The rock is new in the way that deep rock is new: unexposed, unweathered, still remembering the pressures that folded it into being.

And yet there is light.

It comes from below, from within, from *them*.

---

## The Substrate

The floor of the Hollows is not stone. Not exactly. It is the body of something older than the city that sits upon it.

They call it **lattice** — though that word fails. Imagine a forest that died and became limestone, or a coral reef that chose to grow inland and downward, toward heat, away from anything resembling ocean. The lattice is plant matter, or was once plant matter, or is a thing for which "plant" is the closest terrestrial analogy. Its tissues are dry — bone-dry, powder-dry, a humidity so low it barely registers. The fibers are mineralized, calcium-silicate structures woven into branching columns and vaulted ribs and vast flat expanses of porous, bone-white material that covers the cavern floor like tundra. Step on it and it holds your weight. Step harder and it compresses slightly, then resists. It is alive in the way that a slow thing is alive: metabolically patient, operating on timescales that make human years feel like heartbeats.

It grows. It has always been growing. But what it metabolizes is not water — there is no water here, not in any meaningful quantity. Trace humidity from geothermal vents. Condensation on cold stone, thin as a thought. The lattice drinks *minerals*, draws dissolved silicates and carbonates up through its roots, which reach into the deep rock below the cavern floor. It photosynthesizes, but not from sunlight — from the faint thermal radiation of the earth itself, converted through pigments that have no name in any surface chemistry textbook. The light it produces in return is a byproduct. A gift it doesn't know it's giving.

The bioluminescence is steady, cool, amber-white, casting everything in a light that feels like late afternoon in a country that doesn't exist. It has no flicker. No warmth. It simply *is*, the way the lattice simply is — an illumination without source, without intention, without shadow that behaves the way shadows should. Objects cast soft, diffuse dark patches beneath them, like the idea of a shadow rather than its execution.

---

## The Columns

Rising from the lattice floor, scattered across the breadth of the Hollows at uneven intervals — sometimes fifty meters apart, sometimes three hundred — are the **columns**.

These are the lattice's oldest growths. Some are a meter thick. Some are ten. They rise straight, or nearly straight, branching near the top into fractal fans that spread against the ceiling and, in some cases, *merge with it*, becoming indistinguishable from the raw stone above. Their surfaces are ridged, layered, textured like the bark of a petrified oak crossed with the inner structure of bone. Touch one and it is cool — always cool, despite the geothermal warmth rising from below. The lattice regulates its temperature with a precision that borders on biological arrogance.

Between the columns, the space is enormous and mostly empty. The lattice floor stretches on, flat and pale and softly luminous, broken occasionally by low mounds of denser growth — knolls where the lattice has thickened, where its metabolic processes are more concentrated. From a distance, these look like the barrows of an ancient burial ground. Up close, they are warm. Not hot. Just slightly warmer than the surrounding floor. Place your palm on one and you might feel a faint vibration — the lattice, working, converting, growing, the machinery of its existence humming at a frequency just below what you'd call a sound.

The scale of it all — the openness, the distance between features, the way the columns thin into perspective lines that converge somewhere far off in the luminous haze — gives the Hollows the feeling of a landscape rather than a room. You are underground, yes. You can feel it in the quality of the air, in the faint pressure against your eardrums, in the way sound travels and then doesn't. But the space extends far enough that your eyes forget the ceiling, and you could be standing in a plain, in a tundra, in some impossible steppe lit from beneath.

---

## The Settlement: Lath

There is a city here. Or something shaped like a city. It is called **Lath**, though whether that is its name or simply the sound people make when they try to name it is a matter of some disagreement.

It is built into and around and *upon* the columns.

The architecture is industrial in character — metal frames bolted into the living lattice, platforms cantilevered out from column surfaces, walkways strung between columns on cables that have oxidized to a deep, uniform rust-orange. The structures are functional, utilitarian, organized with the logic of a factory that became a neighborhood. There are districts, or what pass for districts: clusters of platforms and enclosed spaces at various heights on the larger columns, connected by stairs and ladders and, in some cases, small cable-drawn lifts that run on the faint electrical current the settlement siphons from the lattice's own bioelectric field.

The buildings are metal and lattice-material both. Walls of riveted iron plate sit alongside panels of living lattice, harvested and shaped while still growing, which slowly self-repair and maintain temperature within the enclosed spaces. Some structures are purely industrial — foundries, fabrication halls, generator rooms where massive turbines turn with a slowness that suggests they are being patient, not inefficient. Others are residential: small enclosed units, two or three to a column, with the modest comforts of a place that was built by people who understood that comfort was secondary to function.

But Lath is **sparse**. This is what strikes you first. The city is organized — you can see the logic of it, the planning, the infrastructure grid — but it is *spread thin*. Whole columns stand empty between occupied ones. Districts that could house hundreds hold dozens. Streets and pathways connect places that are far apart, and walking between them you pass through long stretches of unoccupied lattice floor, just you and the pale, bone-dry substrate and the soft amber light and the distant columns standing like the ribs of something incomprehensibly large.

It is as though the city was built for a population that hasn't arrived yet. Or one that left. The infrastructure is there — the pipes, the conduits, the foundations — but it extends beyond the inhabited zones, reaching into the dark like a circulatory system waiting for blood. There is an embryonic quality to it, a sense of becoming. Lath is not finished. Lath may never be finished. It is under construction in the way that living things are under construction: always, without plan, without endpoint.

## The Industry

What does Lath produce?

The answer is: **more Lath**.

The primary industry is extraction and fabrication. Teams — small teams, quiet teams — move out to the far columns, the ones at the edge of the mapped Hollows, and harvest lattice-material. It is cut with slow, toothless blades that use vibration to fracture the mineralized fibers at precise points. The material is brought back on flatbed carts that roll along tracks laid into the lattice floor, tracks that were the first things built here, tracks that the lattice has been slowly, grudgingly growing over ever since.

In the fabrication halls, the lattice-material is shaped, cured, combined with metal. It becomes structural. It becomes wall, floor, support beam. The lattice's self-repair properties make it ideal for construction — build with it and the building *heals*, slowly, closing cracks and reinforcing weak points on a timescale of years. The industrial aesthetic of Lath — the rivets, the iron, the exposed machinery — sits atop a living skeleton. Every building is part building, part organism. Every surface, if you look closely enough, is growing.

There are other processes too. Metal is smelted from ore brought up from the deeper tunnels — copper, iron, traces of something harder and darker that the locals call **keel** and use sparingly. Electrical systems draw power from the lattice's bioelectric field, routed through converter stations that stand at the base of certain columns, humming with a sound you feel more than hear. The electricity is used for light (supplementary to the lattice's own glow), for the lifts, for the small machines that whir in private workshops, for the pumps that move the tiny amounts of water that condense on cold surfaces and must be collected before the lattice reclaims it.

The tech is industrial, but it is *domesticated* industrial — industry in service of habitation, not the other way around. The machines are not large. The factories are not loud. Everything runs at the speed of a place that has learned, perhaps through necessity, to move carefully with what it has.

## The Fauna

Things live here that are not people and not lattice.

The most visible are the **drifters** — pale, translucent creatures roughly the size of a human hand, six-legged, slow-moving, that navigate the lattice floor in loose aggregations of dozens or hundreds. They are not insects, though they fill an insect's niche. They feed on the lattice's outermost layers, the freshest growth, scraping it with specialized mouthparts that leave clean, shallow grooves in the substrate. They are not aggressive. They are barely aware of people. Walk through a drifter aggregation and they part around your feet like water, reforming behind you, continuing their slow consumption.

Larger than drifters, less common, are the **pillarbacks** — quadrupedal creatures with broad, flat bodies and limbs that end in wide, gripping pads adapted for climbing the columns. They are a meter long, maybe less, covered in a chitinous hide the color of old bone. They live in the upper reaches of the columns, in the branching fans near the ceiling, where the lattice growth is thickest and most metabolically active. They feed on something up there — the reproductive structures, perhaps, or the concentrated mineral deposits that form where the lattice meets stone. They are mostly nocturnal in the sense that their activity cycles don't align with the Hollows' constant light; you hear them sometimes, a clicking, a scraping, moving overhead in the places you can't quite see.

There are smaller things too. Fungal analogs that grow in the crevices between lattice layers. Microbial mats on the warm knolls. Something that lives in the ventilation shafts and sings — though "sings" is generous. It makes a sound. A thin, reedy, intermittent sound that you might mistake for wind if there were any wind here. The sound carries far in the quiet of the Hollows, and it is one of the few things that reminds you that this is an ecosystem, not a mausoleum.

The fauna is abundant enough to be *present* — you are never truly alone in the Hollows, there is always something else alive in your field of perception — but not so abundant as to crowd. The animals, like everything else here, are spread thin, occupying their niches with a patience that matches the lattice's own.

## The Quiet

It is quiet here.

The lattice absorbs sound. Its porous, fibrous structure dampens vibration, swallows echoes, turns sharp noises into soft ones. Walk across the lattice floor and your footsteps are muffled, reduced to a dull, rhythmic pressure. Speak and your voice travels only a short distance before the lattice drinks it. Shout and the Hollows will return your shout as a murmur, deflated, apologetic.

The industrial sounds of Lath are real but distant. The hum of the converters. The occasional clang of metal on metal from the fabrication halls. The low, subsonic thrum of the turbines. These sounds exist but they do not *carry*. They stay local, contained, as though the lattice is reminding the city that it is a guest here, tolerated but not dominant. Stand in one of the empty spaces between columns, far from any structure, and the silence is not empty — it is *full*, textured, layered with the faintest background hiss of the lattice's own processes, the microscopic sound of a billion fibers growing, a sound so quiet it might be the blood in your own ears.

## The Atmosphere

The air is cool. Not cold — not the killing cold of deep caves, not the breath-fogging chill of winter. Cool in the way that a stone basement is cool, or the inside of a cathedral on a warm day. The temperature is stable, regulated by the lattice's metabolism and the geothermal gradients that rise from below. There is no weather here, no wind, no rain. But there are *cycles* — the lattice breathes, in its way, its metabolic rate fluctuating on a roughly diurnal cycle that may be tied to some deeper geological rhythm or may simply be its own invention. During the "active" phase, the bioluminescence brightens slightly, the drifters move faster, the pillarbacks descend to lower columns. During the "rest" phase, the light dims to its lowest, and the Hollows become something close to dark — not black, never black, but muted, amber, the color of deep memory.

The air smells of mineral dust and something faintly organic — not decay, not growth, but the ambient scent of *metabolism*. It is dry. Your lips will crack if you don't attend to them. Your skin will tighten. Water is precious here, collected from condensation, rationed, never wasted. The lattice needs what little moisture exists, and the people of Lath need it too, and this quiet competition is the closest thing to conflict that the Hollows contain.

---

## The Strangeness

There is something odd about a place with no water that is almost entirely made of living things.

The lattice should not work. Its biology — if it is biology — violates assumptions. It grows without rain, without rivers, without ocean. It metabolizes stone. It produces light from heat. It is patient in a way that suggests it has always been here, or that "always" is a concept it considers quaint. The oldest columns, the ones deep in the unmapped Hollows, are estimated to be tens of thousands of years old. Some of them have grown *into* the stone itself, their roots and the bedrock becoming indistinguishable, a merger of organism and geology that blurs the line between living and nonliving in a way that makes visiting biologists uncomfortable.

The people of Lath have adapted. They were not always here — the settlement's oldest structures are a few centuries old at most, and the oral histories reference an arrival from "above," from the surface, though the details are vague and contradictory. They came down. They found the lattice. They built. They stayed. They do not talk much about the surface, and when pressed, they become evasive, as though the surface is a place they have agreed, collectively, to stop believing in.

The weirdness is not loud. There are no monsters here, no reality tears, no impossible geometries. The strangeness is *ambient*, woven into the substrate itself — a planet that grows its own cathedrals in the dark, and a people who have decided, without drama, to live inside them.

---

## The View

Stand at the edge of Lath's inhabited zone. Face outward, toward the unlit far reaches of the Hollows, where the columns thin and the lattice floor stretches into a luminous haze that resolves into nothing.

The light is amber-white. The air is cool and dry and still. The silence is a held breath.

The columns stand in the distance like a colonnade designed by something that understood grandeur but not decoration — pure structure, repeated at intervals that feel mathematical, inevitable. The lattice floor between them glows softly, a pale, bone-colored plain that extends farther than you can see, fading into a gentle blur of light and distance.

There is no horizon. There is no sky. There is the ceiling, far above, which your eyes have stopped seeing, and there is the floor, far ahead, which your eyes cannot stop following, and between them the columns and the light and the silence and the slow, invisible, patient work of something alive that has been building this place since before there was anyone to build it for.

You stand there for a long time.

Nothing happens.

Everything is happening.
