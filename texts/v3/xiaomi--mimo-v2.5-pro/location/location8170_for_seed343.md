# The Thundering Highs

---

You are standing at the roof of the world, or what was once the roof of the world, and what remains is something older and stranger than any peak.

The land stretches in every direction — a rolling, slow, almost imperceptible undulation that extends to horizons so distant they blur not from haze but from sheer planetary curvature. This was once a mountain range of incomprehensible scale. You can feel it in the bones of the place: the bedrock, where it peeks through, is *dense*, banded in colors no current geological process could produce — deep violets shot through with veins of copper-green mineral, stone so old it predates the memory of erosion itself. But the mountains are gone. Eons of rain that no longer falls, wind that no longer scours with the same fury, tectonic forces that have long since quieted — all of it has ground these titans into what they are now: a high-altitude peneplain, a vast and gently rolling tableland that sits at an elevation where the sky is a slightly different shade of blue, where the air is thinner than it should be and yet still warm, somehow. The warmth comes from below. Geothermal seep — ancient and persistent, a last exhale from the world's cooling interior — bleeds through the oldest rock on the planet and keeps this plateau temperate at altitudes that should be frozen. Pockets of mist rise from fissures in the stone in the early hours, then dissolve. Dew forms on every surface overnight and vanishes by mid-morning. The water is not absent; it is merely *patient*, cycling slowly through condensation and seepage and the occasional deep basin of rainwater cupped in some ancient weathered hollow, tinted amber with dissolved minerals.

---

The vegetation. God, the vegetation.

It is not a forest. It is not a meadow. It is not any category you have a word for. The plant life here is the substrate itself — a living carpet so total and so ancient that where the plants end and the geology begins is a philosophical question more than a botanical one. The dominant organism is a creeping, matted, woody groundcover with leaves the color of dark jade, each plant radiating outward from a central root-stock that plunges deep — ten meters, twenty — through cracks in the primordial bedrock to reach the moisture that lingers in the deep stone. Single individuals spread across astonishing distances, their outermost fronds kilometers from the central tap-root, and where one plant's territory meets another's, the boundary is marked by a barely perceptible change in leaf shape or a slight shift in shade. From any vantage point, the ground appears to be a single unbroken organism stretching to the horizon.

But it is not flat. The plants build *up*. In the deeper swales and basins where moisture collects more generously, the groundcover thickens and heaves into soft, rounded mounds — some the size of houses, some the size of hills — dense tangles of woody stems and aerial roots and broad flat leaves that layer over one another like shingles. These are not trees. They are not shrubs. They are structures, self-organizing heaps of accumulated growth that have been building for thousands of years, slowly rising as lower layers die and compress and upper layers continue their patient upward creep. The oldest of them have cores of almost-peat, almost-wood, material so densely packed and chemically strange that it resists decomposition even in this living landscape. You could climb one — the outer surface is spongy and grippable, the leaves tough underfoot — and from the top, you would see them scattered across the plateau like the vertebrae of some colossal buried creature: dark green humps against the paler green of the flatland, receding into blue distance, dozens of them, hundreds, as far as you could see.

Occasionally, something taller. A single woody stalk — a flowering spike — erupts from the crown of one of these mounds, rising ten, fifteen, twenty meters into the air, rigid as a mast, topped with a heavy cluster of pale blossoms that open once in a century and release clouds of pollen that drift across the plateau on thermal updrafts. Most of the ones you can see are dead, gray, slowly toppling, their dried stalks leaning at angles against the wind. But one or two are fresh, green-white, trembling slightly with their own growth. They are louder than you expect. The wind across their hollow stems produces a low, resonant hum that you feel in your chest before you hear it with your ears.

---

And the noise.

The noise is *staggering*.

You did not expect this. You should have — the parameters of this place practically demand it — but nothing prepares you for the wall of sound that presses in from every direction the moment you stop moving and actually listen. It is not one sound. It is a *layering* of sounds, dense and polyphonic, the combined voice of a fauna so overwhelming in its presence that the landscape's apparent emptiness becomes almost ironic.

The insects first. They are the foundation of the sound, a high, thrumming, omnipresent drone that saturates the air like carrier-wave static. Vast-winged things — not flies, not exactly, something more ancient in design, with bodies like polished seeds and wings that move so fast they become translucent blurs — hover in loose clouds above the groundcover, millions of them, each producing a thin whine, and together they produce a roar. It is the sound you hear most constantly. It does not stop. Day or night, it does not stop.

Beneath the insect-drone, the larger fauna. The plateau supports an ecology that should not exist at this altitude — the geothermal warmth makes it possible, and the total vegetation makes it inevitable — and the animals that have evolved here are numerous, large, and *vocal*. The dominant herbivore is a broad-bodied, low-slung creature, something between a tortoise and a boar in build, with a wide flat head adapted for grazing the groundcover. They move in loose aggregations across the open flats, dozens or hundreds in sight at any time, each the size of a small car, and they *bellow* — deep, resonant, infrasonic calls that carry for kilometers across the thin air, used to maintain contact across the immense distances they keep between individuals. One alone is loud. A hundred, calling and responding across the breadth of the plateau, create a rolling, almost geological sound — like tectonic plates groaning, like thunder that never quite breaks. The ground vibrates with it.

Predators are present too, though sparser. Sleeker, taller, more upright in their posture — long-necked, sharp-eyed, moving in pairs or trios through the spaces between the herbivore herds. They are largely silent, but when they hunt, they produce a sound that cuts through everything else: a sharp, percussive, almost metallic *crack*, made by some specialized structure in their throats, loud enough to startle prey into motion. You hear these cracks intermittently, randomly, like gunshots in a vast empty field — each one the punctuation mark of some small drama unfolding somewhere out on the plain, invisible at this distance.

And the birds — if they are birds. Winged creatures with broad, flat wings optimized for soaring on the thermals that rise from the geothermal vents. They circle in wide arcs above the plateau, sometimes singly, sometimes in spiraling columns of hundreds, and they are not silent. Their calls are high and penetrating, almost painfully so at close range, and when a large flock wheels overhead, the combined shrieking is physical, pressing against your eardrums like a hand.

All of this together. The insect-drone. The herbivore-bellow. The predator-crack. The bird-shriek. The hum of the flowering stalks in the wind. It layers and layers and layers until the silence becomes a theoretical concept — something you can reason about but never experience. You adapt. Your hearing adjusts, finds ways to parse the cacophony into components, to locate individual sounds within the whole. But the whole never diminishes. It is the sound of an ecosystem in full, exuberant, ancient, unrestrained expression — life at the scale of a landscape, vocalizing constantly, as if the plateau itself has a voice and this is what it sounds like.

---

The built environment is faint. Almost apologetic.

Someone lives here — has lived here for a very long time — and their presence is marked by structures so sparse and so modest that you could walk for hours and never see one. They are low, rounded shelters built from the only available materials: the ancient stone of the bedrock, shaped by hand into rough blocks, and the woody stems of the groundcover, cut and layered into roofs that blend seamlessly with the living carpet. From a distance, they are indistinguishable from the natural mounds. Up close, you can see the hand of their making — the deliberate stacking, the fitted joints, the smoke-darkened interior where a fire-pit sits at the center and the walls are black with the residue of ten thousand meals. These people are few. They must be. The structures are scattered so widely — one here, another visible only as a distant irregularity on the horizon, maybe a third beyond that — that the population of this entire vast plateau might number in the hundreds. Perhaps fewer. They live among the fauna as something close to equals: hunting, yes, but also herding, in a loose and passive sense, following the great herbivore aggregations across the flats, taking what they need, leaving the rest to its own thunderous rhythms.

Their tools are simple. You find them occasionally, half-buried in the groundcover near an abandoned shelter: a hand-axe of knapped stone, its edge still sharp after what might be decades of disuse; a length of dried plant-stem, notched and bound with animal sinew, used for some purpose you cannot determine; a shallow bowl carved from the dense core-material of one of the ancient mounds, its interior polished smooth by long use. These people do not build monuments. They do not dig mines or raise walls. They exist in the landscape as the landscape exists on the plateau — lightly, persistently, with the minimum of disturbance that a living thing can manage while still being alive.

---

At midday, the light is strong but not harsh — the thin air does not scatter it the way denser atmospheres would, so shadows are crisp and the colors are vivid: the dark jade of the groundcover, the violet of exposed bedrock, the amber tint of standing water in the basins, the pale cream of the flowering stalks against the deep blue sky. The warmth is constant and pervasive, rising from below as much as from above, and in the shallow basins where moisture pools, you can sometimes see steam — faint, almost invisible, a faint rippling of the air that marks where geothermal heat meets water and the two negotiate their difference.

By late afternoon, the clouds come. Not storm clouds — this is not a place of heavy rain — but high, thin, layered formations that move slowly across the plateau from the west, casting moving shadows that roll across the landscape like dark rivers. In the shadow-zones, the temperature drops slightly, and the insects descend lower, and the sound shifts — becomes more muted, more bass-heavy, the insect-drone thinning and the herbivore-bells gaining prominence. In the light-zones between clouds, the insects surge upward again, and the birds wheel more aggressively, and the noise brightens, sharpens, becomes almost unbearably dense and high-pitched.

At dusk, the condensation begins. Moisture beads on every leaf, every stone, every surface, and the groundcover glistens in the last light as if sweating. The temperature holds — it does not drop sharply, the geothermal warmth preventing the frost that should form at this altitude — and the fauna does not quiet. The insects drone on. The herbivores bellow. The predators crack. The sound continues, unabated, all through the night. You would lie in one of those dark, smoke-stained shelters and listen to it through the walls — that vast, layered, thundering chorus — and you would understand that this is not a quiet place and never has been and was never meant to be.

This is a landscape of age and openness and overwhelming life, held in a warm palm of geothermal grace at the top of a world that has forgotten its own mountains. The stone is the oldest thing you have ever stood on. The plants are the most complete conquest of space you have ever witnessed. The animals are the loudest community of living things you have ever been embedded in. And the people, scattered and sparse and almost invisible in their small stone-and-wood shelters, are the quietest — the ones who have found a way to exist in all this noise and age and growth without adding more than the faintest whisper to it.

The plateau extends. The sound continues. The groundcover creeps another millimeter outward.
