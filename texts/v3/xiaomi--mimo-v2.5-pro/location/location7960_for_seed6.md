# The Dripping Cathedral

---

You ascend. That is the first and most persistent fact of this place. You ascend through something that was not designed to be ascended through — a tower of iron lattice and riveted copper plate, perhaps forty meters across at its widest, rising from a high alpine ridge into cloud. The structure is industrial in the oldest sense: bolted, welded, stamped with manufacturer's marks from a company whose name has already begun to corrode away. Girder ribs. Cross-bracing like the interior of a ship turned upright and made to climb. But every surface, every joint, every angle of manufactured steel — every single one — has been colonized.

---

## What has colonized it

Not animals. There are no animals here. No birds nest in the struts. No insects move through the canopy. No fish, no frogs, no copepods in the standing water. This absence is the second fact, and it is *felt* — not as emptiness but as a kind of topological smoothing, as though some entire dimension of the place has been removed. Things grow here. Nothing *moves* here. That is the rule.

What grows is vegetation so dense and so *present* that the word "lush" fails — it suggests something pastoral, something pastoral and optional. This is not optional. The vegetation has filled the structure to saturation. There is no gap between one leaf and the next. There is no gap between the stems. The plants have grown into and around and through the metal until you cannot always tell where the iron ends and the xylem begins. Roots have threaded through bolt-holes and split open weld-seams. Leaves overlap in shingle-patterns so tight they form a continuous surface — green, dark green, waxy green, a green so deep it reads as black in the lower light.

The plants are strange. They are not quite any species you could name. Broad-leafed things with the architecture of aroids but the texture of ferns. Epiphytic tangles that drip with a substance somewhere between water and gel. Tall segmented stalks — bamboo-like — but with joints that are translucent, almost glassy, and ringed with what appear to be small ceramic collars, as if they were grown around a manufactured scaffold. Flowers appear here and there: enormous, waxy, odorless, shaped like open hands or like the bells of trumpets, but their colors are muted — grey-green, blue-grey, a deep and rusty indigo. No fragrance. No nectar. They exist for some purpose that does not involve pollinators, because there are none.

These are engineered plants. Grown from stock that was designed, planted in substrate that was manufactured — nutrient-gel trays, hydroponic troughs, capillary-mat systems bolted to the walls. You can see the remnants of the infrastructure beneath the growth: copper drip-lines, polymer nutrient channels, automated dosing heads now choked with roots. The engineering was deliberate. But the growth has long since outpaced whatever program was feeding it. The plants have become self-sustaining. They pull water from the air itself now.

---

## The water

The air is water.

This is not metaphor. The atmosphere inside the structure is so saturated that visibility reduces to perhaps fifteen meters in the densest passages. It does not feel like fog. Fog implies droplets suspended in air. This feels like *air suspended in droplets* — a continuous medium of warm moisture so thick that breathing requires a slow, deliberate rhythm, as though you have submerged in a bath of warm cloud. Your skin beads with it instantly. Your clothes are heavy with it within minutes. Water runs down every surface in continuous sheets — not flowing but *seeping*, the way moisture runs down the inside of a greenhouse times ten thousand.

The water accumulates. The lower third of the structure — the lower fifteen meters or so — is submerged. Not in a pool or a reservoir. The water has no basin. It fills the structure itself, held in place by the architecture, by the density of the vegetation, by the sheer volume of moisture that condenses faster than it can drain. You wade through it. It is warm. It is the temperature of the air, which is the temperature of the plants, which is the temperature of the metal — all of it around thirty degrees, a uniform warmth that makes temperature itself disappear as a category. There is no boundary between wet and dry. There is only degrees of wetness. At the top of the structure, you are damp. At the bottom, you are submerged to your chest. Below that — below where you can easily go — the water is dark and the plants form a continuous underwater mass of stems and leaves, gently moving with currents too slow to feel.

The water does not drip loudly. It makes almost no sound at all. It moves too slowly. It is too warm. It has no urgency.

---

## The sound

There is almost none.

This is the third fact, and it is perhaps the strangest. An industrial structure this size, filled with this much living matter, in water this warm — it should be deafening. It should roar with insects, with frogs, with birds, with the creak of thermal expansion, with dripping, with wind. But there is no wind. The structure is sealed — or effectively sealed — at its upper reaches, open only at the base where the water meets the ridge, and even that interface is so overgrown that air barely moves through it. There are no animals. There is no wind. The water moves too slowly to make noise. The plants do not rustle because there is no air moving through them.

What you hear:

A hum. Very low. Very quiet. Somewhere in the structure — below the waterline, perhaps, or embedded in a wall — an engine turns. Not fast. Not with purpose. The kind of slow, patient rotation of a machine that has been running for a long time on minimal load, its bearings worn smooth, its fuel — whatever fuel — being drawn at a rate so low it will not run out in your lifetime. The hum is felt more than heard. It is a vibration in the metal under your hands, a resonance in the water against your ribs.

And dripping. Occasional. Not the staccato of water striking metal — more the soft, full sound of a drop of water striking a leaf and being absorbed without echo. *Tup.* Minutes of silence. *Tup.* More silence.

Your own breathing sounds obscenely loud.

---

## The structure from outside

From the ridge — if you could see it from the ridge, which you cannot easily, because the clouds come low here and the structure's exterior is already green with spillover growth — the tower would read as a lighthouse. Or a grain silo. Or a boiler house. Its form is cylindrical, tapered, its upper reaches slightly bulbous where a reservoir or observation deck was once enclosed in glass (the glass is still there, but you cannot see through it; the interior surface is completely coated in plant life, in condensation, in mineral deposits from the water). Riveted steel plates. The original color was probably a factory grey. Now it is a mottled patchwork of rust-patina and green — the oxide of iron and the chlorophyll of leaves producing, together, an almost geological texture, as though the structure were a cliff-face that had been there for millennia.

It has not been there for millennia. The erosion profile is young. The edges of the metal plates are still sharp where they are not rusted. The bolts still have visible heads. The manufacturer's stamp — partially legible — includes a date within the last few decades. The concrete footings at the base are crisp-edged, unweathered. Whatever this place is, it was built recently. The plants are older than the building's apparent age would suggest they should be. They have grown as if on a different clock.

---

## What it was

It was a factory. That much is evident from the interior: the industrial fittings, the hydroponic infrastructure, the automated systems now buried under growth. This was a facility for growing something — these strange plants, or their precursors — at altitude, in isolation, under controlled conditions. The sealed environment. The climate systems. The nutrient delivery. The absence of any fauna suggests it was built without them deliberately — a sterile growing environment, clean-room adjacent, where plants could be cultivated in isolation from any contaminating biology.

Why here? The elevation. The cold outside. The thin air. The isolation. All of it suggesting that what was being grown needed to be kept *away* from something — away from pollinators, away from soil microbes, away from wind-borne spores. These plants were meant to grow in total solitude. They were meant to be the only living things.

The project succeeded. The plants survived. The project also failed, in the way that such projects always eventually fail — through neglect, or budget, or collapse of the institution that built it, or simple entropy. The automated systems ran down. The climate controls defaulted to a warm, wet equilibrium. The sealed environment held. And the plants, which needed no pollinators, no soil, no animals of any kind — the plants, which were *designed* to need nothing but light, water, mineral nutrients, and time — the plants kept growing.

---

## What it is now

A room. A grove. A garden inside a machine, slowly becoming a machine inside a garden. The scale is intimate despite the height. You cannot see far. You cannot move fast. The vegetation is too dense, the water too deep, the air too thick. You are in a space the size of a cathedral nave, but experienced as a series of small enclosures — a passage between stems, a clearing the size of a bedroom, a low ceiling of overlapping leaves that you must duck under, a wall of roots that forces you to wade around it.

Everything is constructed. There is no soil that was not manufactured. There is no rock. The substrate is metal, concrete, polymer, ceramic — and over all of it, the manufactured nutrient media, now so old and so overgrown that it has become indistinguishable from soil in texture if not in composition. The roots have woven through it all. You are standing on a floor of iron plate under a meter of water under a carpet of fallen leaves under a canopy of living green, and not one of those layers is natural.

And yet it feels — not natural, exactly. *Inevitable.* As though this is what this place was always going to become. As though the engineers built the cage and the plants merely remembered how to fill it. The slowness helps this feeling. Nothing is happening fast enough to observe. The plants grow, but you would have to watch for days to notice. The water level rises, but by millimeters per season. The engine hums, but its RPM has not changed in years. The whole place is in a state of near-perfect equilibrium — warm, wet, dense, green, quiet, still — and it will remain in this state for a very long time.

You are the only animal here. Your movements are the fastest thing in the room. Your sounds are the loudest. Your warmth — which is the same temperature as everything else, which means it is no warmth at all — is the only metabolically generated heat that is not a plant's. You are, in every sense, an intrusion. The water does not mind you. The plants do not mind you. The engine does not mind you.

The silence does not mind you.
