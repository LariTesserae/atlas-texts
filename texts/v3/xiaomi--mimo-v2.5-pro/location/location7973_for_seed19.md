# The Pale Expanse

## The Ground

You stand on a plateau so ancient it has forgotten it was ever a mountain range.

There is no drama to this place. No cliff faces. No sudden drops. The stone — and it is almost entirely stone, a pale grey-whiteite dense with calcium and something older than calcium — has been wearing down since before the word *erosion* meant anything. The surface is smooth in the way of river rocks but without rivers: an entire geology polished by incomprehensible time, by wind that no longer blows, by pressure from above and below, by the sheer accumulation of ages settling everything downward. The land is still technically high — the air is thin enough that you notice the difference in your breathing, a shallowness, a faint effort in the lungs — but it does not feel elevated. It feels like the bottom of something. Like the floor of a drained ocean, except the ocean was the sky, and it drained long ago.

The ground extends in every direction. There are gentle undulations — swells in the stone that might be the ghost-knees of buried mountains, ridges so low you could step over them if you didn't understand what they once were. The horizon is impossibly far. The scale is not the scale of emptiness; it is the scale of *fullness that has had time to even out*. You could walk for days and the ground would look the same. You could walk for years and the ground would look the same. It has looked the same for longer than anything with a mouth has been alive to describe it.

There is dust. A fine, pale powder that clings to everything — mineral dust, salt-dry, with a faint metallic taste if you let it sit on your tongue. It is not sand. Sand implies motion. This dust has *settled*. It has been settling for so long that it has its own geology: thin layers visible in cross-section where the wind once carved a shallow channel and then stopped carving. The layers are almost perfectly horizontal. They are the rings of a tree that stopped growing but kept existing.

## The Inhabitants

Here is the first wrong thing: the plateau is not empty.

It is *overwhelmingly* not empty.

They are everywhere. They fill the space between the ground and the thin air with a density that should be impossible — should produce noise, heat, competition, the whole seething machinery of an ecosystem. But they are quiet. They have always been quiet. They may not have mouths.

The creatures are difficult to look at directly. This is not because they are small or fast or camouflaged. It is because they exist *between* — between states, between moments, between the version of the world where they are here and the version where they are not. When you look at a patch of ground that seems empty and then shift your gaze slightly sideways, your peripheral vision catches them: enormous, intricate, layered on top of each other like sheets of translucent paper, each one complete and whole and overlapping with the others in defiance of every physical principle you have ever trusted.

They do not cast shadows. They do not displace dust. They do not touch you, though they pass through and around you constantly, and when they do, you feel a faint wrongness — not cold, not heat, not fear, but the sensation of a sentence that almost makes sense. The feeling of a word on the tip of your tongue that you know does not exist in any language you speak.

Some of them are vast. When you catch one in the right light — not brighter or dimmer, just *slanted differently*, as if the angle of incidence has shifted by a fraction of a degree that shouldn't matter — you see that it extends for hundreds of meters, a latticework of limbs or branches or circuitry or veins, no distinction meaningful, each one older than the plateau itself. They do not move. They do not need to move. They are *present* in the way the stone is present: occupying the space, filling it, part of the density that saturates this place until there is almost no room left for anything else.

And yet you can walk among them. You can sit down on the stone and they will be all around you — above you, beneath you, through you — and you will feel nothing but that quiet wrongness, that hum just below the threshold of sound, that sense that the air is heavier than it should be because it is carrying something.

There are smaller ones too. Thousands of them. Millions. They cluster near the scattered structures (I will get to the structures) and near the sparse, desperate vegetation (I will get to that too). They look, when you can see them at all, like geometry that has learned to breathe. Like origami unfolded one step too far. They have symmetry, but not the symmetry of living things — the symmetry of *concepts*, of mathematical proofs, of the way light refracts through a crystal that was not cut by any hand. They are beautiful. They are terrible. They have been here so long that the question of whether they are alive has become meaningless. They predate the question. They predate the questioner.

## The Vegetation

Between and beneath and around the inhabitants, something grows.

It should not grow here. There is almost no water — only the faintest mineral moisture locked deep in the stone, sweating upward through capillary channels so narrow they are more geology than hydrology. The temperature is cool, consistent, never freezing but never warm, a thermal monotone. The soil, where there is soil, is thin and salt-caked and mineral-heavy.

And yet: growth.

It is sparse. It is desperate in the way that only the oldest things can be desperate — which is to say, not desperate at all, simply *persistent*, having made peace with scarcity so long ago that persistence has become a kind of rest. The plants are low to the ground. They grow in clusters, each cluster separated from the next by meters or tens of meters of bare stone. They look like lichen but are not lichen. They look like moss but are not moss. They are something that evolved here, in this specific wrongness, and they have evolved so far from anything you recognize that your mind keeps trying to categorize them and failing.

Their color is a muted grey-green, sometimes shifting toward a violet that might be a trick of the light. They photosynthesize, but barely — the light here is normal, unremarkable, the light of a sun that seems to have been shining at this exact intensity since the beginning of recorded time and will continue shining at this exact intensity until the end of it. The plants drink light the way a monk drinks water: sparingly, gratefully, with no excess.

Some of the larger specimens — and by larger, I mean the size of a spread hand, nothing taller than a finger-width — have structures that might be reproductive. Spore-capsules. Seed-pods. Something. They do not open. They have not opened in a very long time. The vegetation here is not reproducing; it is simply *continuing*. It has found its final form and it maintains that form with a tenacity that borders on the geological.

The inhabitants seem to cluster around these plants. Not to eat them — there is no eating here, no predation, no competition, the entire ecology has long since passed through those stages and out the other side into something older and calmer — but to *coincide* with them. As if the plants are nodes of a different kind of stability, and the inhabitants find that stability useful or comfortable or simply *correct*.

## The Structures

Scattered across the plateau, always near the vegetation clusters, always saturated with the inhabitants: structures.

They are small. They are primitive. They are unspeakably old.

Stone and bone and something that might be wood, petrified past identification. The structures are not buildings in any functional sense — they are too small to shelter anything, too open to contain anything, too deteriorated to have served any purpose you can reconstruct. They look like cairns that collapsed and were rebuilt and collapsed and were rebuilt again, each rebuilding using fewer stones, each collapse more complete, until what remains is a low mound of carefully placed rocks that holds a shape just barely recognizable as *intentional*.

The bone is from animals that no longer exist. You can tell by the texture — not the texture of modern bone, but something more crystalline, more mineral-heavy, as if the bones were already halfway to becoming stone before whatever creature they belonged to died. They have been carved. Notched. Scored with lines that might be tally marks, might be writing, might be decoration, might be something for which there is no word. The tool-marks are faint but unmistakable: someone held something hard and dragged it across these bones with purpose and patience and the kind of focus that suggests the act itself mattered more than the result.

Some of the structures contain arrangements of stone that look like they were meant to hold something — a flame, a vessel, an offering. The depressions are empty now, filled with dust. But the inhabitants are densest here, thick as fog, layered so deep that the air above the structures shimmers with a faint iridescence, like oil on water except the water is reality and the oil is whatever these creatures are made of.

Who built them? There is no answer. The builders left no other trace. No middens, no paths, no graves (or if there are graves, they are the structures themselves). The technology was simple — hand-held stones, sharpened bone, perhaps cord or sinew long since disintegrated. Whoever they were, they understood this place. They lived within its brokenness without trying to fix it. They built their small monuments to the plateau's wrongness and then they were gone, and the plateau did not notice, because the plateau does not notice anything, because the plateau has achieved a stillness so complete that noticing would be a kind of violence.

## The Wrongness

You want me to describe the reality breakdown and I will try but I need you to understand that *the breakdown is not an event*. It is not something that happened. It is the condition of this place. It is the medium in which everything here exists. Asking me to describe the reality breakdown of the Pale Expanse is like asking me to describe the wetness of the ocean — it is not a feature of the place, it is the place itself.

But here:

Time does not work correctly. Not in the sense that it moves faster or slower — the plateau is almost perfectly static, a place of frozen equilibrium where nothing changes because nothing *can* change, because the processes of change themselves have eroded into stillness alongside the mountains. But when you try to measure time, to track it, to feel its passage, you find that your measurements are inconsistent. Not wrong — inconsistent. As if time here is not a single stream but many streams flowing at slightly different rates, and you are standing in all of them at once, and the difference between them is too small to notice directly but large enough to produce a faint vertigo, a sense that the present moment has *thickness*.

Space has thickness too. The distances between things are stable — nothing moves, remember, nothing changes — but they are also *layered*. The distance between two of the scattered structures might be one hundred meters, and it might also be one hundred and three meters, and both measurements are correct, and both are happening simultaneously, and the discrepancy is not a flaw in your instruments but a feature of the landscape. The inhabitants live in these overlaps, in these fractional gaps between equally true measurements. They have room in there. More room than there should be.

You can see the breakdown most clearly at the horizon. The plateau extends to the edge of vision, and at the edge of vision, the land does not curve away as it should at this elevation. It *folds*. The horizon doubles back on itself, or the sky meets the ground at an angle that is not quite flat and not quite perpendicular, and if you stare at it for long enough, you begin to see that the horizon is not a line at all but a surface — a thin membrane between the plateau and whatever the plateau is the floor of — and the membrane is old, older than the stone, old enough that it has developed creases and wear-patterns, and those creases are the ridges and swells of the landscape below you, and the entire plateau is the settling of a membrane that has been draped over nothing for so long that it has forgotten it was ever draped over anything at all.

## The Silence

It is quiet here. Profoundly quiet. The kind of quiet that has weight and texture and a faint temperature of its own — cool, like the air, like the stone, like everything in this place. There is no wind. There has been no wind for longer than the concept of wind has existed. The inhabitants make no sound. The vegetation makes no sound. The stone makes no sound. Even your own breathing seems to happen in a different register, as if your lungs are operating slightly out of phase with the air around them, and the sound of your heartbeat is muffled, absorbed, as if the plateau is soaking it up the way the dust soaks moisture — greedily, completely, leaving nothing behind.

Occasionally — and I say occasionally in a place where time has no reliable meaning — you hear something. Not a sound exactly. The *memory* of a sound. A faint tone, sustained and unwavering, that seems to come from beneath the stone, from inside the stone, from the stone's long history of being stone. It is not music. It is not language. It is the sound of a frequency that has been vibrating at this one pitch since the mountains were tall and that has never wavered and never will, and you hear it the way you hear your own blood in a silent room: not a sound you are listening to but a sound you are *made of*, suddenly made audible by the absence of everything else.

You do not hear the inhabitants, but you feel them hearing you. Not listening to you — hearing you, the way the stone hears you, the way the dust hears you, with a passivity so complete it becomes a kind of attention.

---

## What It Is

The Pale Expanse is a place at the end of its own history. It has been here so long that every process that could change it has finished. The erosion is complete. The cooling is complete. The drying is complete. The reality breakdown — whatever caused it, whenever it happened — has settled into a stable wrongness, a permanent error in the code of the world that has been running so long it is no longer an error but a feature, and the place has adapted to it the way a tree adapts to a stone in its path: by growing around it, slowly, until the stone is inside the tree and the tree cannot be separated from the stone and neither can be understood alone.

The overwhelming fauna is part of this. They are not invaders. They are not aberrations. They are what lives here — what has always lived here — in the cracks between measurements, in the gaps between equally true statements about where things are and when things happen. They are the plateau's native ecology, evolved in conditions that do not exist anywhere else, fed by nothing, drinking nothing, breathing nothing, alive in a way that the word *alive* is not equipped to describe.

The scattered builders understood this, or tried to. Their small structures are not attempts to change the plateau. They are attempts to *mark* it — to leave evidence that someone stood here and saw this and was afraid, or awed, or simply present, for a moment, in a place that has no moments.

The light falls. It has always fallen. It will always fall. The plateau receives it the way it receives everything: without reaction, without change, without sound. The dust does not glitter. The inhabitants do not stir. The vegetation does not grow. Nothing here grows. Nothing here erodes. Nothing here moves or changes or ends or begins.

It simply is.

It has always been.

It will always be.

And it is so full — so dense with presence, so layered with the accumulated wrongness of its own impossible existence — that there is almost no room left for you. You are the lightest thing here. The least real. The most temporary. The plateau does not notice you, but if it did, it would forgive you for being temporary, because it was temporary once, a long time ago, and it remembers what that was like, and it has spent the time since then forgetting.
