# The Weeping Terrace

---

You arrive at a place that should not be so wet at this altitude.

The terrace is small — perhaps the size of a cathedral nave laid on its side, bordered on three sides by walls of raw stone so freshly uplifted they are still sharp enough to tear skin. The rock is a dark basaltic grey shot through with veins of something brighter, almost white, that catches the high-country light and holds it a beat too long. Water runs in thin sheets down these walls, weeping from fractures that are not quite random. If you watch long enough — and the heat makes you watch, because moving is unpleasant — you begin to notice that the water does not always obey the slope. Certain rivulets pause. Certain droplets cluster in corners where no geology explains a catchment. The stone is warm to the touch everywhere, and the water warmer still. Steam does not rise from it, quite, but the air above every wet surface has a faintly visible density, a wavering not unlike what you see above summer asphalt, though this is a place that has never seen asphalt.

The heat is extraordinary. Not dry heat — the humidity is fierce, soaking, a constant breath against your face — but the temperature itself pushes past anything comfortable. Your lungs labor gently. The air smells of minerals: sulfur, yes, but also something coppery and faintly sweet, like overripe fruit left on metal. You learn quickly not to touch the rock in direct sun for more than a few seconds.

The terrace floor is not flat. It shelves and dips in a series of shallow basins, some no wider than a tabletop, others broad enough to stand in. Water collects in these — clear, almost luminously clear, with a faint blue-green tint that comes from dissolved minerals you cannot name. The basins are rimmed with scalloped edges of deposited stone, built up in thin laminations that look like they were laid down in hours rather than centuries. This is a landscape in fast-forward. The mineral deposits grow visibly over days. Walk away from a basin and come back in a week and the rim will have crept forward, the pool will have narrowed, a new channel will have opened where water found a lower point. The terrace is actively building itself, and it does so with a strange deliberateness that does not quite feel geological.

---

Vegetation here is sparse but emphatic. There is no carpet of growth, no canopy. What grows does so in isolated stands, each one vivid and particular, separated from its neighbors by meters of bare, wet stone. Ferns — but not ferns as you know them. These have fronds of a deep, almost black green, and the undersides are rufous red, and the texture is leathery and faintly waxy in a way that suggests adaptation to heat that would kill most temperate plants. They cluster around the edges of the warm basins, rooting into the mineral crust with thick, pale rhizomes that look more like coral than like any root system you've seen. Between the ferns, mosses grow in patches of brilliant chartreuse, so vivid they seem to generate their own light, though they do not. They are merely saturated with chlorophyll in quantities that seem wasteful, extravagant, as though the light here is different in some way the plants have understood and you have not.

Here and there, small trees — if they are trees. They grow no taller than two or three meters, with smooth, pale bark that peels in long strips revealing a darker underlayer. Their leaves are thick, rounded, and semi-translucent; when the high-country sun passes behind thin cloud, you can see the veining inside them, complex and geometric in a way that borders on unsettling. They do not look engineered, exactly. They look like something that evolved in a place where engineering was part of the environment for a very long time and then stopped, and biology rushed in to fill the shaped spaces.

The trees hum. Not audibly — not in any frequency your ears report — but something in your sinuses or your sternum registers a vibration when you stand close to one. This may be the wind moving through their hollow stems. It may not be.

---

Fauna is rare but not absent. You see it in glimpses. A shape moving through one of the shallow pools that resolves, when you look directly, into a dark, elongated amphibian — or something amphibian — with too many limbs and eyes that reflect a faint, coppery sheen. It submerges and does not resurface. Later, something that might be an insect, large as your thumb, lands on one of the pale-barked trees and folds wings that are not chitinous but membranous, almost gelatinous, and briefly iridescent. It is gone before you can study it. You have the persistent sense of being the largest, loudest, most conspicuous thing in this place, and of everything else having long since learned to be small and quiet and elsewhere.

The soundscape confirms this. The place is mostly quiet. The quiet is not empty — it has texture. There is the constant, gentle hiss of water moving over mineral deposits. There is a deep, sub-bass thrum from the earth itself, the mountain's geothermal heart beating somewhere below. Occasionally a sharp, glassy crack as thermal stress fractures a piece of cooling mineral buildup. Birdsong, if it exists, exists at the margins, far below or far above the terrace, never within it. The silence has a held-breath quality, as though the place is listening to you with more attention than you are paying to it.

---

The built environment is barely there, and what remains is strange.

At the far end of the terrace, where the basalt wall rises to its highest, there is a structure embedded in the living rock. Not built against it — *into* it, as though the rock was poured around something rather than carved to receive it. The visible portion is a flat, smooth surface roughly two meters on a side, made of a material that is not metal, not stone, not ceramic, not anything you can name with confidence. It is dark, slightly warm, and when you lay your hand flat on it you feel a faint, irregular pulse — or think you do. The edges where it meets the basalt are seamless. No mortar, no gap, no visible joint. The transition from known rock to unknown material is so gradual that you cannot point to the exact line where one becomes the other. The surface is unmarked except for a shallow circular depression near one corner, smooth and round and just slightly too perfectly shaped to be natural. It might be a basin. It might be an eye.

Elsewhere on the terrace, half-buried in mineral deposits that have slowly crept over them, you find smaller artifacts: a thin rod of the same dark material, bent at an angle that does not correspond to any break or tool mark, as though it was manufactured curved. A flat disc, perhaps a handspan across, embedded in the floor of one of the shallow basins and visible through the startlingly clear water. When the light hits it at certain angles it shows patterns — not inscriptions, not images, but faint, organized variations in opacity, like interference fringes, that shift as you move. You cannot tell if they are encoding something or if they are an artifact of the material's structure. You cannot tell if the distinction matters.

There is no indication of who made these things, or when, or why. The mineral deposits that coat the terrace are building over them, slowly. In another century they will be buried entirely. Perhaps they have been buried before, in previous cycles of deposition and erosion, and this is not the first time the terrace has wept itself clean enough to show them. The rocks are young, yes — recently thrust upward by tectonic forces still ongoing — but what is embedded in them may not be young at all.

---

The strangeness of the place accumulates rather than announces itself. No single feature is inexplicable in isolation. Hot springs exist at elevation. Thermophilic plants exist. Rare fauna exists. Mineral deposits exist. Even the artifacts, viewed separately, might be explained as unknown but natural mineral formations, or as human relics from some forgotten era of sophisticated craft. But taken together, the place produces a sensation that builds in the back of your mind like pressure: *something happened here that is still happening*. The weeping water, the growing mineral crust, the geometric veining in the translucent leaves, the pulse in the embedded surface, the water that occasionally moves uphill — these are not separate phenomena. They are aspects of a single process that you are standing inside of, and you lack the categories to name it.

The terrace is active. Things are in motion. The mineral deposits grow. The ferns unfurl new fronds in a matter of days. The amphibian — or whatever it is — is hunting something in the warm water, or being hunted, in a slow, patient ecology that operates on timescales you can only partially perceive. The mountain hums below. The air thickens and thins with changes in the weeping rate of the rock walls, which appears to follow a cycle — not daily, not tidal, not anything you can correlate to the weather. The terrace breathes. That is the wrong word and the right one at the same time.

You could sit here for an hour and see the place change. You could sit here for a year and not understand it. The high-country sun moves across the sky and the shadows shift on the raw basalt walls and the water catches the light and throws it back in colors that are not quite the colors of refraction, and the quiet holds, and the heat presses down, and the terrace continues whatever it has been doing since before it was a terrace, since before the mountain rose, since before the rock that holds those dark embedded surfaces knew it would be rock.

You are welcome here, in the sense that nothing attacks you or drives you away. You are not needed, in the sense that nothing you observe requires your observation to continue. The place is complete. The place is in process. These are the same statement.

Eventually you will leave, following the sharp-edged ridge back down toward wherever you came from. The water will keep weeping. The mineral crust will keep growing. The ferns will keep reaching. The dark surface in the rock will keep its faint, irregular pulse, counting nothing you can count, keeping nothing you can name, but keeping it still.
