# The Delta Works

The city has no name that everyone agrees on. It sits in the lowlands where three rivers have forgotten which direction they flow, and the land is barely higher than the water table — in some districts, it isn't higher at all. The ground is dark mud over ancient sediment, packed and repacked over centuries, layered with iron plates and poured concrete that has long since cracked and surrendered to the damp. Water comes up. It seeps through the foundation slabs of every building, pools in the streets, beads on the underside of every horizontal surface. Condensation runs down the interior walls of factories that have been operating for a hundred years. There are springs in places where no springs should be — hot ones, mineral-laden, hissing through fissures in the bedrock and steaming in the open air. The rivers themselves are slow, braided, shallow, more like a condition of the land than features on it.

Nothing grows here. The water is too mineral-rich — iron-heavy, sulfurous in places, alkaline in others — and the heat kills anything that tries. The soil, where it's exposed, is gray-black and slick, sterile as slag. No moss on the stones. No algae on the water's surface, or almost none — just a faint orange bacterial film in some of the hotter pools that nobody thinks about. This is normal. The land has been like this for longer than the city has existed, longer than anyone's records. The erosion is ancient and settled: the soft stone has already been cut to its final profile, the riverbanks have slumped to their resting angle, the mud flats have found their shape. Nothing is being carved anymore. Everything has found its level.

And yet the city sprawls.

It is not dense. That's the first thing a visitor would notice — how much empty space there is between the structures. A factory complex here, then a hundred meters of open, wet ground — puddles, iron grating, mud — then a row of housing blocks. A processing plant, then a long gap of nothing, then a warehouse district. The city grew outward along the river margins, following the slightly higher ground where the mud is only ankle-deep instead of knee-deep, and it grew in patches, clusters, as industry demanded rather than as planning dictated. The result is a settlement the size of a county, with maybe a tenth of its area actually built upon. The rest is interval: empty ground, open water, standing pools, bare mud flats that shimmer faintly in the low light.

The light is dim. Not dark — there is no ceiling, no canopy, the sky is open — but the atmosphere holds moisture so consistently that everything is diffused, softened, overcast. On the clearest days, the sun is a white disk behind haze. Most days, the sky is a uniform gray-white, bright enough to see by, too flat to cast sharp shadows. The city's own emissions contribute: thin brown smoke from the processing plants, steam from the hot springs, the perpetual evaporation off every wet surface. It gives the place a twilight quality even at noon. Streetlamps run on a low burn around the clock, their light making yellow corona in the mist.

It is hot. Not the killing heat of open desert, but a heavy, wet, inescapable heat — the kind that sits on the skin and doesn't evaporate because the air is already saturated. Metal surfaces are warm to the touch. The mud is warm. The standing water is warm. Inside the factories, where the engines run and the furnaces process, it is hotter still — workers move slowly, methodically, with the economy of people who have learned that urgency wastes energy in a place like this. There is no breeze. The air moves only when thermal columns rise off the hot springs, creating brief, local updrafts that stir the mist and then die.

The sound is mostly quiet. This surprises people who expect an industrial city to roar. But the engines here are old, well-maintained, running at their settled RPM — they hum, a low vibration you feel in the floor of your chest more than hear. The processing plants emit a steady mechanical breath, valves cycling, presses dropping on slow intervals. Water drips constantly — from eaves, from pipe joints, from condensation on structural steel — and this is the dominant sound, a soft irregular percussion on every surface. Beyond that: quiet. The streets are not crowded. There is foot traffic, the occasional engine-driven cart on the iron-plated roads, but between these sounds there are long stretches of nothing but drip and hum and the faint wet hiss of steam from somewhere you can't see.

The fauna is overwhelming.

This is the other thing that surprises visitors, and it shouldn't, because this is what the city is *for*. The hot, mineral-rich, plant-free wetland supports an enormous and thriving animal population. Crabs — thousands of species, it seems, filling every niche from thumb-sized mud-burrowers to heavy-shelled things the size of dinner plates that clatter across the factory floors at night. Eels and eel-like fish in every channel and drainage ditch, slipping through culverts, coiled in the standing water beneath buildings. Insects in staggering variety: flying things with heavy wings that drone through the mist, crawling things in the mud, burrowing things in the foundations. Amphibians — sleek, pale, enormous-eyed — huddling in every cool shadow, every pipe junction, every hollow in the stonework. Snails the size of fists on the walls of buildings, leaving silver trails. Colony organisms in the warm pools — things like segmented worms, like mats of interlocking bodies — that no one has fully classified but everyone knows to step around.

They are everywhere. There is no structure in the city that is free of them. They nest in the walls. They breed in the drainage systems. They carpet the open ground at certain hours — early morning, when the heat hasn't fully set in — and then disperse as the temperature rises. The factories process them: crab oil, chitin for industrial polymers, protein meal, calcium compounds, the glandular secretions of the larger amphibians that have some pharmaceutical use. This is the economy. It has been the economy for generations. The city exists because the animals exist, and the animals exist because the land is what it is — hot, wet, mineral, barren of anything green, and absolutely saturated with animal life.

It is, by any standard, normal. This is a working city. People live in the housing blocks, eat the animals, work in the processing plants, maintain the engines. The mud is the mud. The heat is the heat. The crabs are in the walls again, and someone will deal with it. The water is coming up through the floor of warehouse six, same as last season, and someone will lay another layer of concrete. The light is gray and the air is wet and the engines hum and the eels are breeding again in the south canal and the snails have reached the roof of the customs house, which means they'll need to be cleared before the shells damage the drainage gutters.

The land is settled. The city is settled. Everything is in its place, including the damp.
