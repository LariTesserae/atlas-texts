# The Drowned Factory at Vennock Deep

---

You wouldn't find it if you weren't looking. You might not find it even then.

The city of Vennock sank forty years ago — or two hundred, depending on which surveyor's dates you trust, because the subsidence was gradual, a swallowing, and people kept living on certain streets even as the water crept past their ankles, their knees. Eventually the sea took the last rooftop and Vennock became a name on nautical charts marking a hazard: *submerged urban infrastructure, depth variable, do not anchor.* Fishermen avoid it. Sonar returns show the predictable signature of blocks and intersections rendered as echo-shapes. Someone has mapped it in the abstract. No one visits.

Almost no one.

Below the upper stories — past the waterlogged apartments where sand has drifted through broken windows and settled on ceilings that are now floors, past the old commercial district where signage still hangs in the dark water, painted letters slowly dissolving, past the cathedral whose spire is a reef now, encrusted with something that isn't quite coral — there is a descent. The streets slope. Vennock was built on a hill that became a shelf that became a trench. At the lowest elevation, a hundred and forty meters below the surface, the architecture changes: older, heavier, the buildings from Vennock's industrial foundation era, built with walls like fortifications because the machines they housed were enormous and the foundations had to match. One of these buildings is still dry inside.

This is the part that shouldn't work. Pressure at this depth should crush an unsealed structure. But the Vennock Deep Textile Mill — once the economic heart of the city, once employing six thousand people in shifts around the clock — was built by a chief engineer who was either paranoid or brilliant, probably both, and who constructed the mill's lower levels as a series of interlocking pressure vessels, each room capable of independent sealing, because he feared flood. His fear outlived him. The mill held. Water fills the outer shell, presses against the outer walls, but the interior rooms — and there are dozens of them, climbing and connecting through corridors and stairwells in a labyrinth designed for the passage of raw fiber and finished cloth — remain pressurized with air. Stale, warm, living air.

The sound begins before you enter and does not stop.

It is *cavernous.* The sound of water compressing. The sound of the building's own survival: metal walls groaning in micro-adjustments, bolts flexing, the deep tidal hum of a hundred and forty meters of ocean sitting on top of you and being resisted. And beneath that, the reason anyone comes here at all: the machines.

Someone — some group, some faction, some whispered network that operates in the drowned margins of the coastal states — has restarted portions of the mill. Not the textile machinery. The *engines.* The original coal-fired steam plant that once drove the looms has been retrofitted, crudely, with something else: diesel generators salvaged from shipwrecks, turbines pulled from offshore platforms, a patchwork grid of industrial power sources bolted into the old boiler housings and piped through the existing steam channels. It is not elegant. It is not safe. It is *loud.* The generators run constantly because the mill cannot afford to stop — stopping means losing pressure equilibrium in certain rooms, means the seals contract, means water finds new paths inward. So the engines run, and the sound they make is a roar that saturates every surface, a mechanical thunder that you feel in your sternum and your teeth and that makes your vision blur at the edges from the sheer vibration.

The main engine hall is enormous — it was built to house boilers three stories tall — and mostly empty now. The generators are large but few, arranged in a loose cluster near the center of the floor, each one surrounded by a fence of welded rebar and flanked by fuel tanks that look like they were made from cut sections of submarine hull. Cables run from the generators up the walls and through holes punched in the ceiling, feeding power upward to — you don't know what. The upper levels are sealed off. You've been told not to go up there. The people who work here, who you've seen only in glimpses, don't go up there either, or if they do, they don't talk about it.

What they do talk about, in the rare moments when someone shouts something into the noise and you catch it, is *the balance.* This place operates on a knife-edge. The water wants in. The walls want to buckle. The generators need fuel that arrives by a route you've never been shown, in containers you've never seen unloaded, at intervals that seem irregular. The air is warm — partly from the engines, partly from something geothermal, a warmth that comes up through the floor in certain rooms and has left mineral deposits on the lower walls, pale and crystalline, not quite calcite, not quite anything you can name. The air smells of diesel and hot metal and salt and something faintly organic, like kelp, like the memory of the sea pressing in from all sides.

Light comes from industrial fixtures bolted to the walls and ceiling, wired into the generator grid. Most work. Some flicker. Some are dead, leaving pockets of absolute darkness in the corners and corridors. The light is yellow-white, sodium-bulb harsh, and it doesn't reach far — the ceilings are too high, the rooms too vast, and the darkness beyond the light's reach is total, a blackness that has weight. You carry a headlamp when you move between rooms. The corridors are narrower, lower-ceilinged, and some of them slope downward into ankle-deep water that nobody has pumped out — just enough to remind you that the dryness of this place is an *achievement,* a constant negotiation with the ocean.

There are people here. Not many. Maybe twenty, maybe thirty. They move through the space with the efficiency of long practice, checking gauges, carrying tools, feeding the machines. They wear coveralls and rubber boots and hearing protection that makes them look like they're underwater even when they're not. They rarely acknowledge you. You are not the first outsider to find this place, and you will not be the last, and they have decided — collectively, silently, long before you arrived — that the best way to handle visitors is to let them see enough to understand and not one thing more. You are free to stand in the engine hall. You are free to walk the corridors. You are *not* free to open certain doors, and you will know which ones because they are the ones that have been welded shut, the bead of metal running along the frame like a scar.

The turbulence — and it is turbulent, make no mistake — is not only mechanical. The water outside is not still. Currents move through Vennock's streets, driven by tidal forces and the geometry of the submerged city itself, which channels flow through its grid of blocks and alleys like a maze. You can feel this from inside: the building shifts, not dangerously but *perceptibly,* a slow sway that has a rhythm but not a predictable one. Sometimes the sway quickens. Sometimes you hear, beneath the generators' roar, a sound like a freight train passing — that is a current hitting the building broadside, the water's mass expressing itself as pressure and noise. The walls hold. The walls always hold. But each time, there is a pause afterward, a half-second of someone checking a gauge, someone listening to a seal, before work resumes.

It is not a place that makes sense to exist. It is a pocket of air and fire and noise at the bottom of a drowned city, maintained by stubbornness and engineering and whatever motive drives the people who keep the generators fed. It will not last forever. Nothing down here will. The walls are weathered, the seals are aging, the water is patient. But it has lasted longer than anyone expected, and the engines are running, and in the deafening heartbeat of the mill, someone has strung a clothesline between two pipes and hung laundry to dry in the warm updraft from the generators, and the shirts sway gently in the vibration, and that is the most human thing you have ever seen.
