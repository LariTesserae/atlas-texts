# The Churn of Cleftmouth

The rock here is new.

Not new to the world, perhaps, but new to air. The range that pushed these formations skyward is still grinding — you can feel it in the faint percussion through your boots, a bass hum that lives in the marrow of the stone. The walls of the cleft are knife-cut basalt, dark and glass-veined, their surfaces still holding the stress fractures from when they were underground. Touch them wrong and you'll open your hand. Every edge here is like that: unsoftened, too fresh to have learned the grammar of weather. The whole feature is a wound, maybe two hundred meters deep, perhaps thirty across at its widest, narrowing to impassable widths in both directions. A room. A hallway. A throat.

There is almost no light. The cleft runs nearly north-south at latitude where the sun barely clears the ridgeline even at solstice, and the walls lean inward near the top, allowing only a sliver of grey to leak down and scatter along the upper faces. What reaches the floor — the actual floor, forty meters below the rim — is less light than memory. Your eyes adjust and then keep adjusting and find there is nothing more to find. You navigate by sound, by the wetness of surfaces, by the movement of air that the breathing walls push in and out like a slow lung.

But it is not quiet. It is the opposite of quiet.

---

The sound rises from below. It rises from everywhere.

The floor of the cleft is not stone. It is an organism. Or rather, it is *organisms* — layered, heaped, interlocking, so densely packed that they have become a substrate. The first thing you would recognize is that the walls and floor are furred. Not with moss, not with anything photosynthetic. With **bodies**. Small bodies, mostly — creatures the size of your fist or smaller — but present in such number that the floor undulates with them, shifts in slow tides, breathes as a single mass. They are gregarious in the way that sardines are gregarious, or termites, or cells.

The locals — and there are locals, or there were, leaving behind their flaked-stone blades and notched-bone awls jammed into crevices near the rim where a thin person could wedge themselves and wait — called them something that translates, roughly, as **the noise that walks**. They are endothermic, which in this cold matters enormously. Their bodies radiate heat against one another, stacked and nested in tiers, and the collective warmth of their colonies has created microclimates within the cleft: pockets of almost-warm air, humidity walls where condensation blooms on surfaces that would otherwise be desert-dry. Where they concentrate, the stone weeps. Where the stone weeps, they concentrate further. They are engineering the hydrology of this place by existing.

What are they? Not bats, though the analogy holds for a moment. Not insects, though the density is insectine. They have a body plan that does not correspond well to taxonomy — six-limbed, the front pair adapted into something between a wing and a grasping arm, membranous, translucent enough that you can see the dark knot of their organs when one passes through the rare beam of light. Their faces are flat. They have eyes, but the eyes are vestigial, filmed over, functionally useless — they navigate by echolocation and by a vibrational sense mediated through the bones of their jaw. They click. They *scream*. Not in distress, not as alarm — they scream as a form of ambient conversation, as a way of reading the shape of the space, as a way of feeling each other. The cleft is filled with a ceaseless, layered, overlapping shriek that rises and falls in amplitude but never stops. It is not a sound you can get used to. It lives in your sinuses. It makes your vision swim.

The density is staggering. Estimates from the rim, looking down into the near-dark, suggest hundreds of thousands. Possibly more in the deeper narrows where the cleft pinches and the bodies stack vertically against the walls in thick crusts. They move slowly — most of the time. The aggregate behavior is glacial. Colonies shift position over weeks, not minutes. A mass that was on the east wall in the morning will still be there at dusk. But when they move — when some threshold of population pressure or warmth differential is crossed — they move as a fluid, a wave of small bodies pouring over surfaces with a sound like rain on a tin roof, filling new crevices, establishing new thermal nodes. Then stillness again. The cycle is geological in its patience, animal in its density.

---

Water appears in the cleft not as a stream but as a **condition**. Condensation slicks every surface. The walls sweat. Moisture collects in depressions in the basalt — shallow bowls carved not by erosion but by the creatures themselves, their clawed hind-feet scratching at the soft mineral deposits left by evaporated water over what may be centuries. These bowls fill with a cold, faintly alkaline liquid that smells of guano and iron. The creatures drink from them. Something else drinks from them too — you can see the marks, slightly larger, slightly different, scratches layered over the older ones. There is a larger predator in the deeper cleft. You don't see it. You hear it sometimes: a single, low tone that cuts through the shrieking like a blade through cloth, and then silence — a bubble of perfect quiet expanding outward from somewhere below, lasting two or three heartbeats, before the noise closes back in.

The vegetation is almost nothing. At the rim, where light touches, there are lichens — pale, crustose, barely differentiated from the stone. Lower, in the half-light zone, there is a single species of fungal mat that grows on guano deposits, white as paper, feeding on the chemical energy of the waste. It is the base of a food web that includes the creatures and whatever eats the creatures. No roots. No chlorophyll below the rim. The cleft runs on geothermal warmth and the slow rain of nutrients from above — wind-borne seeds that land on the rim and wash down, insects that fall in and cannot climb out, the occasional bird that misjudges the gap and tumbles into the shrieking dark.

---

The old tool-users — there is no evidence they lived here. They **visited**. The stone blades are quartzite, not local; they were carried in from a lower elevation, shaped with obvious skill, and then left behind in specific locations: wedged into cracks near the rim, always on the windward side, always oriented with their cutting edge facing downward into the cleft. Some of them are stained with something dark. Some of them have tiny notches along their spines that are too regular to be wear patterns. They may have been sonic tools — struck to produce specific tones that interacted with the creatures' echolocation. Someone figured out how to talk to the noise. Or how to quiet it. The blades are old. How old, no one can say — the stone is too fresh here, the erosion too minimal, to give surfaces a patina worth measuring. They look like they were placed yesterday. They may have been placed a thousand years ago.

The predators below are larger than the swarm-creatures. That much is clear from the scrape marks in the bowls, from the occasional shed skin found floating in the condensation pools — thick, rough, patterned with a tessellation that suggests heavy scales. One skin, found by a climber who descended partway before the sound drove her back, was four meters long and had six limbs, like the swarm-creatures, but the front pair had fused into a single heavy limb ending in a hooked claw. An ambush anatomy. Something that waits, pressed flat against the wall in the total darkness of the deep cleft, and then reaches.

The darkness is the rule here. Light is the intrusion. The creatures know this — they orient away from the sliver of sky, pressing their bodies into the deepest crevices during the brief hours when direct sunlight angles into the cleft. They are adapted to near-total darkness, to the cold, to the crush of numbers, to the long patience of geology that is still, even now, pushing these walls apart. The cleft will widen. More light will enter. The colony will adjust or relocate. But for now, in this narrow, knife-cut, shrieking throat of a place, they are the landscape.

You stand on the rim. You look down. The sound rises up to you like heat, like breath, like something that has been waiting.

The stone beneath your feet vibrates.
