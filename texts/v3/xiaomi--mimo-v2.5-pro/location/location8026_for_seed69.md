# The Weeping Highlands

There is no sky here. Or if there is, it was lost so long ago that the concept has become theological. What the inhabitants know — and there are many inhabitants, uncounted and mostly uncountable — is that above the ragged lip of the deepest canyons, above the highest ridgelines where the stone thins and the rock becomes porous and drips with mineral sweat, there is only thickness. Darkness that is not the absence of something but the presence of a substance: warm, faintly wet on exposed skin, tasting of sulfur and iron and something older than either.

---

## The Shape of the Land

The highlands stretch for distances that have no agreed-upon measurement. Maps exist — scratched into slate, encoded in the memory-glands of certain colonial organisms, pressed into tin strips by the last functioning survey-presses — and none of them agree. The terrain is a labyrinth of broad, flattopped ridges separated by canyons that drop hundreds of meters to floors choked with water and growth. The ridges are old. Erosion has done its work over what must be millions of years: every edge is rounded, every cliff face slumped into long talus fans, every boulder weathered into shapes that suggest they once had corners and purpose.

The stone is basalt, mostly, shot through with veins of lighter material — quartz, calcite, something that glows faintly warm when pressed against skin but emits no visible light. Nothing emits visible light here, because there is no eye to receive it. The rock *weeps*. This is the defining feature of the place, and it has been occurring since before anything was here to call it a feature. Water rises from below — heated by whatever furnace churns in the planet's deep places — and travels upward through kilometers of porous rock, seeping through fissures, condensing on cooler surfaces, dripping from every overhang and shelf and ledge in a perpetual slow rain that falls upward from the ground and sideways from the walls and in sheets from ceilings that may or may not exist at any given height. The air is saturated. Every breath is a mouthful of warmth and mineral tang. Fog exists not as weather but as *texture* — a density in the air that you feel against your face, that beads on your skin and runs in rivulets down the channels worn into the stone by millennia of the same.

The canyons collect water in broad, shallow, hot marshes. The water is never still. Geothermal upwelling keeps it moving in slow convection currents, and the surfaces shimmer with heat-haze you cannot see but can *feel* — pockets of air that shimmer with temperature differentials, where the warmth bends and distorts sound in ways that make echolocation unreliable. The marsh floors are deep — meters of mineral sediment over older sediment over older sediment, each layer a record of chemical precipitation, of organisms dying and being compressed, of the slow steady work of water moving through everything.

## What Grows

The vegetation is profuse and wrong-looking, if you have ever seen vegetation that was designed by light. It was not. It was designed by chemistry, by heat gradients, by the competition for mineral surfaces and access to the geothermal water that is the only real currency here.

*Filament forests* grow on the canyon walls — not trees but vast networks of pale, hair-thin fungal filaments that attach to rock faces and hang in curtains hundreds of meters long, swaying in the convective air currents, filtering nutrients from the mist. They are warm to the touch. They hum faintly with the activity of symbiotic bacteria that break down sulfur compounds in the water. At the base of every filament curtain, the accumulated dead matter forms mats of soft, spongy material that squelches underfoot and smells of deep earth and fermentation.

On the ridgtops, where the rock is driest (relatively — nothing here is dry), *crust-communities* dominate: layered structures of lithotrophic organisms that grow in concentric rings and overlapping plates, hard as tile, cracking and splitting and growing over themselves in slow accretion. They are the color of nothing — pale grey, bone-white, the dull yellow of sulfur deposits. They break down rock. They are, in their patient chemical way, the primary eroders of the highlands, and the dust they produce is the foundation of the soil that fills the canyon floors.

In the marshes, the growth is thickest. Rootless plants — if they can be called plants — float in vast mats on the hot water, their structures more like bladders and tendrils than leaves or stems. They capture dissolved minerals directly from the water, and they grow fast, and they die fast, and the dead layers sink and are replaced by new layers within weeks. The marsh floors are meters deep in compressed organic material, and this material generates its own heat as it decomposes, adding to the geothermal warmth, creating feedback loops that have been running so long they have become features of the geology itself.

Everything is *layered*. The density is extraordinary. Filament curtains hang over crust-communities, which grow over weeping rock faces, which stand above marsh-mats, which float over compressed dead-mats, which rest over mineral sediment, which rests over older stone. It is a place of extraordinary vertical complexity in near-total darkness.

## What Lives

The fauna is abundant, and it is *loud*.

This is the thing that surprises everyone who arrives from lit places: the noise. The weeping of water from every surface is a constant backdrop — a quiet that is not quiet, a susurrus of ten thousand drips that never synchronize, that creates a permanent murmuring polyphony against which every other sound is measured. But the life is louder.

*Clickers* — a name used by the scattered human inhabitants for a category of organisms that probably encompasses dozens of species — are the most audible presence. They are arthropod-analogues, ranging from hand-sized to the size of a dog, that navigate by echolocation and communicate by rapid percussive clicks produced by specialized organs on their thoraxes. In a filament forest, a colony of clickers sounds like a rainstorm on a tin roof, each individual clicking in patterns that encode identity, position, food-source location, and threat assessment. The click-maps of a large colony overlap and interfere with each other, creating a wall of sound that has measurable physical pressure — you feel it in your sternum if you stand among them.

Below the clickers in the food chain (or beside it — the ecology here is too complex and too old for clean trophic pyramids) are the *threshers*: large, blind, warm-bodied grazers that move through the filament forests in herds of twenty to two hundred, their broad flat mouths rasping growth from rock faces with rows of hard mineral teeth that must be replaced continuously, like shark teeth made ofite. They are heavy — adults mass as much as a horse — and their footfalls on stone are a rhythmic drumming that echoes through canyon systems for kilometers. They bellow: deep, infrasonic calls that you feel more than hear, that vibrate the water in the marshes into standing-wave patterns visible as geometric ripples on the surface.

Predators are quieter, mostly. The *threadlings* are long, thin, muscular organisms — six to twelve meters in length, the thickness of an arm — that anchor themselves to filament curtains and extend portions of their bodies outward into the dark, sensing vibrations and heat differentials with extraordinary precision. They strike with a speed that seems impossible for their size, coiling around prey and contracting with enough force to crush bone-analogues. They are the reason the clickers click so constantly: the click-map serves as an early-warning system, and when the clicking pattern changes — becoming faster, more chaotic — everything in earshot knows a threadling is hunting.

There are *swimmers* in the marshes: sinuous, eel-like organisms that breathe through gill-analogues adapted to the hot, mineral-rich water, some of them enormous, their backs occasionally breaking the surface as they roll — slick, warm, muscular, each scale faintly etched with chemical deposits that make them rough as sandpaper. They eat the floating mat-plants, the dead organic material, and each other.

And there are things that are harder to categorize. *Communion clusters* — masses of individual organisms that link together into larger structures, some of them several meters across, that move slowly across the marsh floors in coordinated motion, each individual communicating with its neighbors through chemical signals and faint electrical pulses. They appear to make decisions collectively. They appear to *remember* — a cluster that has encountered a threadling will route around that area for weeks, even if the individual members that made the encounter have since died and been replaced. Whether they are one organism or many is a question that has generated a considerable amount of argument among the place's sparse human scholars, none of whom have enough information to answer it definitively.

## What Was Built

The industrial presence is undeniable, but it is *vestigial* — or perhaps *absorbed* is the better word.

Along the broad ridgelines and on the high shelves above the canyons, there are structures. Not many. Not intact. But unmistakable. Metal frameworks, corroded to the texture of tree bark, rising twenty or thirty meters from the stone, their original purpose — scaffolding? Supports for a roof? Framework for some apparatus? — impossible to determine. The metal is a high-temperature alloy that has resisted the weeping water longer than the softer stone around it, so the structures now stand on pedestals of their own making: the surrounding rock has eroded away, leaving the metal skeletons on raised platforms like monuments to their own durability.

And the machines. Deep in certain canyons, where the geothermal activity is strongest and the water comes up nearly boiling, there are *engines*. They are large — some of them building-sized — and they are still running. Their mechanisms are buried in rock and sediment and growth, only portions visible: massive gears turning with agonizing slowness, corroded pistons pumping in a rhythm that matches nothing natural but has been going so long that the local fauna has adapted to it. Clickers nest in the gears' housings. Filament curtains hang from the pistons like organic bunting. The threshers have worn paths around the machines, their hooves (or hooves-analogues) polishing the stone to a specific grade of smoothness that indicates millennia of traffic.

What the machines *do* is not clear. They process something — water, maybe, or mineral-rich slurry drawn from the deep geothermal vents. Pipes, thick as a man's torso and eroded to translucency in places, run from the engines down into the canyon floors and up along the ridge faces and disappear into the dark above. Some of them still carry fluid: you can feel the vibration if you press your hand against the pipe, a deep rhythmic pulse. The fluid is warm. It smells of complex chemistry — solvents, catalysts, things with names that require specialized vocabulary.

No one built these recently. The corrosion patterns, the degree to which the local ecology has integrated them, the depth of sediment in their foundations — all of this points to an age measured in tens of thousands of years at minimum. The builders are gone. No remains, no records, no tool-marks on fresh stone — only these corroded giants, still doing their inscrutable work, their hammering and grinding adding to the noise of the place, their heat adding to the warmth, their outputs (whatever they are) adding to the chemical richness of the water that feeds the growth that feeds the animals that have made homes in their workings.

The scattered human inhabitants — a few hundred, maybe, in small settlements on sheltered ridgelines — use the machines' metal for tools, their enclosed spaces for shelter, their heat for warmth they don't need (the world is already warm) but find psychologically comforting. They have stories about the builders. None of the stories agree. The machines don't care about stories. They continue their slow percussive industry, their outputs running down into the marshes, their rhythms as reliable as the dripping of water from stone.

## The Darkness and What It Means

There is no light. This is worth pausing on, because it governs everything.

Every organism here navigates by other means: echolocation, chemoreception, electroreception, thermoreception, vibration-sensing, pressure-sensing. The human inhabitants — the few, the scattered, the stubborn — have adapted in the way that humans always adapt: through technology, culture, and a kind of furious denial. They carry thermal-sensing devices scavenged from the old machines' components. They mark their paths with chemical traces that their trained companion-clickers can follow. They *remember* the landscape with a precision that borders on the supernatural, passing down path-maps through oral tradition with a fidelity that would impress any lit-world cartographer.

But the darkness is not empty. It is *full*. Full of sound, full of smell, full of the feeling of warm air moving against skin, full of the vibrations of a living world going about its business in the way it has always gone about its business. The darkness here is not an absence. It is a medium. It has texture and temperature and a kind of presence that makes lit-world visitors — rare as they are — speak of it in terms usually reserved for weather: the darkness *presses*, the darkness *breathes*, the darkness *listens*.

It listens because everything in it is listening. The clickers click and listen for returns. The threshers bellow and listen for echoes. The threadlings hang motionless and listen for the faintest vibration of a footstep on stone. The communion clusters pulse and listen for the electrical signature of food. The machines grind and hammer and listen for nothing, because they were never built to listen, and they continue not-listening with a mechanical indifference that is, in its way, the most alien presence in the place.

## The Weather of It

A system this vast, this wet, this warm, this active — it generates its own weather. Not weather as the sunlit world understands it, but weather nonetheless.

The convection currents created by the temperature differentials between the geothermal vents and the cooler upper canyon walls drive air movement on a continental scale. Warm air rises from the marshes, carrying moisture and mineral particles, and this air meets cooler air (everything is warm, but some places are warmer) at altitude, and the moisture condenses on the rock surfaces — or on the filament curtains, which act as nucleation sites, dripping with collected condensation that adds to the weeping, that feeds the marshes, that warms the air, that rises, that condenses.

There are *storms* — not of lightning and thunder (though there is lightning: massive static discharges generated by the friction of mineral-laden fog moving through narrow canyon passages, illuminating nothing but felt as a sudden crackling charge in the air and heard as a rolling, echoing crack that reverberates through the stone for minutes afterward) — but of *density*. Periodically, the air becomes so saturated with moisture and mineral particles that breathing becomes labored, that the clickers' echolocation degrades, that the threshers fall silent and still and wait with the patient stoicism of organisms that have weathered worse. These density-storms pass. The air thins. The clicking resumes. The threshers bellow. The machines continue.

## The Feeling of Being There

If you were to stand on a ridgeline — one of the old, rounded, weather-worn ridges, the metal skeleton of some ancient structure rising behind you like the ribcage of a leviathan — you would feel the following:

Warmth. Wetness. The stone beneath your feet vibrating faintly with the tread of a distant thresher herd. The air thick against your face, tasting of iron and sulfur and the sweet decay of filament-forest mat. The drip of water on your shoulders, your head, the backs of your hands — never stopping, never pausing, the sound of it a soft endless applause. Below you, in the canyon, the clickers in their thousands creating a wall of percussion. Below that, the infrasonic bellow of a threshers' call, felt in your chest like a second heartbeat. Somewhere in the dark — distance is meaningless, direction is meaningless — the slow laboring groan of a machine that has been running since before your species existed.

The darkness is complete. It is warm. It is alive. It has been here for a very long time, and it has settled into its own state — a balance of water and stone and heat and life and rusting machinery — that is stable in the way that a mountain is stable: not because nothing changes, but because everything changes in ways that cancel out, that loop back, that feed into each other until the whole system is a closed circuit of becoming and unbecoming, of organisms growing on the bones of organisms growing on the bones of machines growing on the bones of geology.

It is loud. It is wet. It is dark. It does not care that you are there. It was here before you and will be here after, dripping and clicking and grinding and breathing in its slow continental way, and if you stand still long enough you will begin to feel yourself becoming part of it — another presence in the density, another vibration in the dark, another warm body in a world that has never needed light to live.
