# The Hollow in the Green Engine

You are inside a cylinder. Not a room — a cylinder, perhaps three meters across, perhaps four tall, though it's hard to say because everything curves and nothing is square. The walls are steel, or were steel. Now they are wood. Or rather: they are both at once, inseparable, and the boundary between what was bolted and what grew has become a philosophical question. Corrugated iron sheeting forms the outer shell of this place — you can still see the wave-patterns in places where the bark hasn't yet claimed them — but the interior surface is entirely living: a crenellated mass of banyan roots, each one as thick as a wrist, webbing across the metal like veins across muscle. The roots have swollen around rivets. They have absorbed bolts. In one spot near the bottom, a root has grown directly *through* a hole where a pipe once connected, and on the other side it has continued growing as though the pipe had never existed, because to the root it didn't.

---

## What this place is

This is a single pocket — a small hidden volume — within a much larger structure that was once, perhaps a century ago, maybe two, a pumping station or water processing facility in a low tropical floodplain. The larger building is still there in outline. You could walk along the outside of it and read the shape: long horizontal cylinders, a corrugated roof now lying in pieces beneath the canopy of the tree that has grown from and through it. But *this* — the space you are in — is one of the smaller internal vessels, a holding tank or pressure chamber, now hollow, now breathing.

The tree that consumed it is a strangler fig, or something very like one. It germinated in the accumulated leaf-litter on the roof, sent roots down the exterior walls, found gaps where water had rusted the bolts, and entered. Over decades it filled the structural cavities, ate the pipes, drank from the condensation. Now the tank is more tree than metal. But the metal remains — it's *in* there, a skeleton within the root-mass, and in places the roots have grown so precisely along old pipe-runs that they look engineered. The place looks like a factory that is also a body. A machine that is also a forest.

---

## The water

The floor is wet. Not flooded — there's no standing water visible — but the surface of every root, every exposed patch of iron, every moss-pad is slick with a perpetual sheen of condensation. The air is warm and saturated, close to dew-point, and the metal walls radiate coolness from the water seeping through the exterior soil, so moisture gathers on every interior surface in slow, drooling beads. You can hear it: a constant soft patter, a ticking, the sound of droplets detaching from roots and falling a few centimeters into moss. This is the loudest quiet sound in the place, and it never stops.

Below the root-mass, there is water — shallow, dark, and still, perhaps only five or six centimeters deep, sitting in the old floor of the tank. It smells of iron and tannin. A root dips into it, and you cannot tell where the root ends and the water begins. The water is warm.

---

## The noise

But the dripping is not what makes this place loud.

This place is *loud*.

The walls ring with the sound of insects — not one species, not two, but a layered congress of them, each occupying a different band of the frequency spectrum. There is a deep, chest-resonant thrumming from beetles somewhere in the root-mass, a pulsing, almost mechanical drone that rises and falls on no cycle you can detect, just a slow oscillation like idling engines. Above that, a chorus of smaller insects — cicadas, perhaps, or something like them — produces a high, sawing, continuous shriek that fills the upper volume of the cylinder and reflects off the curved walls until it seems to come from everywhere. And woven through both of these, a third layer: the quick, irregular clicking of smaller things, mites and ants and termites going about their work inside the wood and inside the rust, audible as a kind of static when you hold still and listen.

It is not music. It is not even noise in the way we usually mean. It is *infrastructure* — the sound of a system operating, of a metabolism running. The fig-tree is a factory, and the insects are its machinery, and the noise they produce is so constant and so layered that after a few minutes it stops registering as sound and begins to feel like pressure, like a physical fact of the air. You feel it in your sternum. It is exactly the volume and texture of a running diesel engine heard from inside a building, and the resemblance is so precise it's unsettling — as though the industrial past of this place has been *replaced* by a living industrial present that does the same thing, makes the same sound, but for no human purpose.

---

## The inhabitants

The fauna is abundant, and it is everywhere, but it is small. Nothing larger than a rat lives here, and even the rats are rare — you see their droppings but not them. The visible animals are:

**Insects**, obviously, in profusion. Beetles the color of crude oil crawl along the root-bark in slow processional lines, carrying fragments of dead leaf and dead insect into crevices in the root-mass. They are not building nests. They are depositing material that the roots will eventually absorb. The beetles are gardeners. They don't know this.

**Geckos**, at least three species, plastered to the curved walls at various heights, their bellies flat against the wet bark, their throats pulsing. They are very still except when they are very fast. One of them has positioned itself directly below a cluster of small flies that orbit a patch of moss, and it has been in that position, you suspect, for a very long time. The flies seem to know it's there. They orbit anyway.

**A frog** — small, brown, the exact color of wet bark — sits in a depression where two large roots cross, cradled in a cup of collected water no wider than a palm. It is calling, a brief repeated note, barely audible above the insect-drone. It is calling because it has always been calling. The sound it makes is part of the place's permanent acoustic signature, like a single repeating note in an otherwise chordal mass. You get the sense it will call forever, and that it has been calling forever, and that these are the same thing.

**Spiders** — orb-weavers, mostly — have strung webs in the gaps between roots, and the webs are beaded with condensation until they look like constructions of glass beads, heavy, sagging, catching what little light enters from above. The spiders sit at the centers and do not move. They are eating well. The insects are in endless supply.

---

## The light

Light enters from above, where the roof of the tank has rusted through entirely in one spot and a gap in the canopy of the parent tree allows a single column of warm, diffuse daylight to fall through. It illuminates a roughly elliptical patch of root-mass and floor, perhaps a meter across, and in this patch the moss grows thicker, the green is brighter, the geckos are more numerous. Outside this column, the light is dim but not dark — a warm, amber, reflected glow, the color of light filtered through layers of green leaf and brown water and living wood. You can see well enough. You can see everything. But everything is the same color.

---

## What doesn't change

This is the thing you notice if you stay long enough: nothing here is changing.

The vegetation is not growing. Or rather, it is growing so slowly that the rate is imperceptible — the roots thicken by millimeters per year, the bark absorbs another fraction of the iron wall each season, the water level rises and falls by centimeters with the rains but always returns to the same level. The insects do not vary in number or kind. The frog does not change its call. The geckos do not change their positions.

This place has reached equilibrium. It is what it is, and it has been what it is for long enough that the concept of change has become irrelevant to its internal logic. The erosion is not ongoing — it is *complete*. The metal is not rusting; it has rusted to the degree it will rust, because the roots have sealed it, because the moisture is constant, because no new oxygen reaches the deeper layers. The tree is not growing; it has filled the available space, and what remains is maintenance, not expansion.

It is a pocket of absolute stability in a world that may be changing around it. But inside the green engine, nothing changes. The diesel-drone of the insects runs on. The frog repeats its note. The water beads on the root-bark and falls, falls, falls.

---

## The feeling

It feels like being inside a heart. Not a human heart — an industrial heart. A pump that has converted to living tissue but continues to pump. The rhythm is still there. The noise is still there. The function is still there. Only the purpose has changed, and even that is not quite true, because a pump that moves water through a living system is still a pump, and this place still moves water — condensation runs down the roots, collects in the floor, seeps through the old rust-holes in the base, and rejoins the floodplain below. It is still a pumping station. It always was. The fig-tree just made it better at it.
