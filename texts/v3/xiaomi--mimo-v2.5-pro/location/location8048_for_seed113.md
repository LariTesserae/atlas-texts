# The Cathedral Dripping

You descend.

Not far — a gentle slope, perhaps forty meters, into a concavity in the earth whose edges you can almost see from any point within it. The walls are limestone, but only just: they're young, still sharp in places where some process bored them out, still bearing the grooves of whatever cut them. Water sheets down their surfaces in thin, continuous veils, catching what little light there is — and there is little — in grey-gold shimmers. The air is warm. Damp. It tastes of calcium and something faintly metallic, like a coin on the tongue.

The space is not large. Perhaps three hundred meters across at its widest, shaped like a rough bowl or a very wide, very shallow well. And yet it feels enormous — not because of its dimensions but because of what fills, or rather *fails to fill*, it.

---

**The structures.**

They are there. You cannot miss them. They are also almost entirely alone.

Seven... no, eight major constructions, spaced with an almost mathematical emptiness between them, as though each requires a hemisphere of unoccupied air twice its own size to function. They are tall — the tallest is nearly the height of the space itself, tapering upward into the wet dark — and they are *not* built from the limestone that forms the walls. Their material is something darker, something with a subtle iridescence under moisture, a matte black that shifts to deep violet when water runs over its edges. The edges themselves are impossibly sharp. Not sharp in the way of new metal — sharp in the way of *grown*, as though a crystal decided to become architecture and never reconsidered.

The forms are hard to describe. They are not buildings in any familiar sense. There are no doors, no windows, no legible entrances. They are more like... monuments? Or organs? The largest has a shape that suggests a standing wave frozen in a single instant — concave surfaces sweeping into convex, the geometry somehow implying motion even in stillness. Another is a cluster of thin, blade-like walls arranged in a radial pattern, each wall tapering to a point at its top, each point at a slightly different height, so the whole structure reads like a gesture caught mid-completion. A third is nearly featureless: a tall, slightly tapered obelisk, except that its surface is covered in what appear to be channels — narrow grooves, too regular to be erosion, too small and numerous to be architectural ornament. Water runs through them. All of them.

Between these structures: nothing. Not empty floor — the floor itself is interesting, a mosaic of old limestone and newer composite materials, damp, occasionally broken by shallow channels where water flows in quiet rivulets toward a slightly lower point in the center of the bowl, where it pools in a basin perhaps ten meters across, still, black, reflective. But nothing *lives* between the structures. Nothing moves at human scale. The emptiness is not desolate; it is deliberate. It is the emptiness of a room after furniture has been removed, but here the room *is* the furniture, and the absence is the point.

---

**The water.**

It is everywhere and it is the loudest thing. Not roaring — not even rushing, for the most part — but *present* in a way that fills the air. A constant, multi-layered sound of dripping, sheeting, trickling, seeping. The limestone walls weep in continuous curtains. The structures themselves produce water: it condenses on their surfaces in fine beads, runs along their grooves and channels, drips from their points and edges in slow, heavy drops that strike the floor with sounds like small bells. The central pool accepts it all, and you notice, if you watch long enough, that its surface is not still after all — it rises and falls, almost imperceptibly, on a cycle of perhaps ten minutes, as though breathing. There is no drain you can see. The water goes somewhere. Probably it cycles through the structures themselves.

---

**The plants.**

They cling to the walls. Mosses, mostly — thick, dark green, almost black in the low light, spreading in irregular patches across the limestone where the water is heaviest. Small ferns grow from cracks, their fronds curved downward under the weight of condensation. Near the pool's edge, a low-growing ground cover with broad, waxy leaves has established itself, forming a sparse ring perhaps a meter wide — the only horizontal vegetation of any note. None of it is lush, exactly, but it is *insistent*. It grows where it can, in the margins, in the crevices, wherever the structures do not claim the space. There are no trees. There are no flowers. There is only the quiet, damp persistence of things that do not need much light and do not need to attract pollinators because there is nothing to pollinate them. They spread by spore, by runner, by slow accretion. They have been doing so for some time.

---

**The light.**

There is a source, but it is not a sun. High above — near the top of the tallest structure — a cluster of small emitters produces a pale, warm light, the color of very late afternoon or very early morning. It is not bright. It is enough to see by, barely, and it casts long, soft-edged shadows that make the structures seem even taller than they are. The light cycles, dimming and brightening on a schedule that does not correspond to any day you know — roughly two hours of relative brightness, followed by forty minutes of near-darkness, followed by a slow climb back. During the dark intervals, the structures themselves emit a faint phosphorescence from their channels, a dim blue-white glow that traces their geometries like veins. This is the only source of color variation in the space. Everything else is grey, green, black, wet.

---

**The sound.**

Beyond the water, there is a hum.

It comes from the structures. A low, subsonic vibration that you feel in your sternum more than hear with your ears — a resonance, like a very large engine running at idle, or like a string being bowed so slowly that each oscillation takes a full second to complete. It is not constant. It shifts in pitch and intensity on cycles that seem linked to the water — when the central pool's surface rises, the hum deepens slightly; when it falls, the hum thins. Occasionally, one of the smaller structures will produce a sharper sound — a click, or a brief harmonic overtone, like a tuning fork struck once and allowed to decay. These sounds echo against the limestone walls and are swallowed by the vegetation.

There are no voices. No footsteps. No bird calls, no insect hum. The space is not silent — it is, in fact, louder than you'd expect for somewhere so empty — but every sound is mechanical, hydrological, or geological. Nothing here speaks in any language but physics.

---

**What this place is.**

It is not abandoned. That implies it was once occupied, and you are not certain it was — at least not by anything with a body your size. The structures are clearly designed, clearly intentional, clearly *engineered* at a level of precision that exceeds anything you're familiar with. The materials science alone — the composite surfaces, the self-channeling water systems, the phosphorescent lining of the grooves — suggests a technological capacity well beyond your own. And yet the space is small. A chamber. A room. A cistern, perhaps, or a reservoir, or a *garden* — but for something that does not eat, does not sit, does not walk.

The best word you can find, standing in the damp warm air with water running down your neck and the hum vibrating in your teeth, is *instrument*. The whole space is an instrument. The structures are its strings, the water is its bow, the limestone bowl is its resonating chamber. It is playing something — slowly, continuously, in frequencies below and above your hearing — and it has been playing for longer than you can determine, and it will continue to play after you leave.

You do not know what it is playing for. But the acoustics are very, very good.
