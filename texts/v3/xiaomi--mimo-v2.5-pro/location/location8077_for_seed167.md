# The Drowned Parish

## Surface and Substance

There is no sky. There hasn't been one for a very long time.

The water begins where the world begins — which is to say, there is no beginning anyone remembers. Above the waterline there is only more water: a column hundreds of feet deep, then a skin of warm surface tension, then a vast grey ceiling of cloud that has not broken in centuries. The rain falls constantly but gently, a whispering percussion that the upper layers absorb without comment. Down here, the rain is a rumor. It arrives as a faint, directionless vibration — a slow pulse in the water itself, transmitted from some surface no one has ever seen or needed.

The seafloor lies shallow. This is not an abyss. The depth rarely exceeds forty or fifty feet, and in many places the reef-tops rise to within eight or ten of the invisible ceiling. The substrate is ancient: soft carbonate mud, consolidated over millennia into a pale, chalky firmament that yields only slightly under pressure. In places it has calcified further still, becoming limestone plates that ring faintly when struck — the only sharp sound in an environment of muffled, waterlogged acoustics. The floor is not flat. It rolls in slow, eroded hills and long depressions, old riverbeds now filled with silt so fine it hangs in perpetual cloudy veils when disturbed, catching the faintest bioluminescence like particulate smoke.

This was a valley, once. A river-wound coastal plain with towns and roads and fields. The water came and did not leave. What the water kept, it remade.

## The Structures



The most striking feature of this place is what grows from the floor: not vegetation, but architecture.

Not architecture by human hands — there are no human hands here, and no tools, and nothing manufactured. But the living things of this flooded parish have, over centuries of undisturbed occupation, built *upward*. The structures are enormous relative to the creatures that made them. Tube-worm colonies, their calcium carbonate casings fused and refused by generation after generation, have erected towers the size of houses — pale, segmented columns, some leaning, some perfectly vertical, their surfaces pocked with thousands of tiny openings where the current-worms extend their crimson gills. Sponge colonies have grown laterally across and between these tubes, filling gaps, smoothing connections, creating walls and arches and roofless halls. They are not sentient builders. They are organisms doing what organisms do: growing, competing for space, dying, being built upon. But the cumulative effect, in this warm dark water, in this shallow drowned valley, over these many centuries, is something that looks — to whatever intelligence might swim through it — like a city.

And it is dense. The structures cluster tightly, leaving narrow passages and channels between them, so that moving through this place feels like navigating streets. The towers vary in height: some reach fifteen or twenty feet from the seafloor, stumpy and broad. Others are thinner and taller, swaying almost imperceptibly with the slow current. Between them, encrusting organisms have laid down a secondary layer — bryozoan lace, limpet pavements, mats of clinging organisms that resemble neither plant nor animal but something in between, growing flat against every surface like living plaster. The overall impression is one of extreme saturation: every available surface is occupied, colonized, layered. There is no bare rock. There is no bare anything. Life has claimed it all.

The vegetation — true vegetation, photosynthetic, rooted — is sparse to absent. A few hardy, pale-leaved plants cling to the upper reaches of the tallest structures, where they might catch the faintest diffused light filtering through the water column. They are stunted, etiolated things, more white than green, with broad translucent leaves designed to harvest whatever photon arrives. They are rare. They are ornaments on the upper stories of the towers, not a feature of the landscape. The energy here does not come from light. It comes from other sources: chemical seeps in the limestone, organic detritus drifting down from the surface layers, the metabolic waste of the organisms themselves in their dense interlocking chains.

## The Fauna



The creatures are everywhere, and they are the source of most of what happens here.

The tube-worms are the foundation — literally and ecologically. They are sessile, rooted, permanent. But around and between them, life moves. Schools of small, pale fish — eyeless or nearly so, with lateral lines of heightened sensitivity running their flanks like silver threads — pour through the channels in coordinated pulses. They are maybe three inches long, translucent, their organs visible as faintly glowing clusters against the dark water. They navigate by pressure and by the faint electric fields of other organisms. They move fast and in unison, changing direction with a flick that sends a tiny shockwave through the water, and they are prey to almost everything.

Crustaceans dominate the middle zones of the structures. Armored isopods the size of hands crawl slowly over the tower walls, grazing on the encrusting layers. They are the landscape's gardeners, preventing any single organism from smothering the tubes beneath. Their shells are dark — deep brown, almost black — and mottled, and they cling with a tenacity that makes them seem part of the structure itself until one shifts. Larger crabs, their carapaces wide and flat like river stones, patrol the floor channels with a deliberate, sideways intelligence. They are not aggressive but they are territorial, and the click of their claws against limestone is one of the place's few percussive sounds — a dry, sharp *tock* that carries surprisingly far through the water.

Cephalopods live in the upper reaches. They are the closest thing to individuals this place has. Octopuses — small, warm-adapted, their skin a shifting display of pale patterns — inhabit the spaces between tube-worm towers, squeezing into crevices that seem far too small for them. They are curious, watchful, and they hunt at the margins of the day-cycle that does not truly exist here. They take crabs. They take fish. They are taken, in turn, by the largest predators: eels of tremendous age and girth, their bodies as pale as the limestone, with wide jaws and a patience that seems geological. An old eel might inhabit the same crevice for decades, growing into its shape, its skin slowly calcifying along the belly where it rests on stone.

Jellyfish drift through the channels like slow, deliberate ghosts. They are not large — perhaps a foot across — but they are numerous, and their tentacles trail behind them in gossamer curtains that shimmer with faint bioluminescence. This is the light of the place: not light as illumination, but light as language, as warning, as accident. The jellyfish pulse blue-white. The tube-worm gills glow faintly red at their tips. The small fish emit a dull, collective shimmer from their lateral lines, like a haze of static. The overall effect is not brightness — the darkness remains profound — but a kind of scattered, unreliable sparkle, as though the black water contains fragments of something not quite light.

Deeper into the structures, in the interstices where the sponges have sealed small cavities, there are other things. Polychaete worms of impossible variety: some with feathery gills like ferns, some with jaws that hinge backward, some that spin mucus nets to catch drifting particles. Brittle stars, their arms impossibly long and thin, extend from crevices like the fingers of buried hands, filtering the water with a delicacy that borders on grace. Anemones cling to every available perch, their tentacles extended, waiting. The density of life per cubic foot is staggering. Every volume of water, every surface, every crack is inhabited, occupied, claimed. There is no empty space. There is no silence — only a quiet that is full, a quiet that is the sound of ten thousand small metabolisms.

## Atmosphere and Temperature



The water is warm. Not tropical-surface warm, but a steady, enveloping warmth — perhaps seventy-five, eighty degrees Fahrenheit, held constant by the thermal mass of the flooded plain and by the geothermal gradient seeping up through the limestone substrate. There is no seasonal variation. There is no day or night. The warmth is ancient and even, the same temperature it has been for longer than any living thing here can remember, which is to say: forever.

The water itself carries a taste — if one could taste it — of calcium and sulfur. There are seeps in the deeper parts of the valley floor where hydrogen sulfide rises in slow, lazy plumes, mixing with the oxygenated water above. The boundary between sulfide-rich and oxygen-rich water creates a shimmering, invisible ceiling within the water column itself, a chemical layer that some organisms straddle and others avoid entirely. Near the seeps, the fauna changes: mat-forming bacteria blanket the sediment in thick, velvety layers, white and grey, and specialized worms graze these mats with an intensity that makes the ground itself seem to move. The seeps are the engine of the place, or one of them. They feed the base of a food web that light abandoned long ago.

The currents are gentle. This is shallow water in a sheltered drowned valley, not an open ocean. The flow comes from the surface — rain-driven, slow, a lateral drift that shifts direction over cycles of hours or days but never accelerates to anything disruptive. It moves through the channels between the towers like breath through lungs, carrying particles, carrying scent, carrying the chemical signals that organisms use to find food, find mates, avoid predators. The water is never still. There is always motion. But it is a lazy, warm, patient motion, the motion of a place that has settled into its own rhythm and sees no reason to change.

## Sound

The quiet is the first thing one notices. It is not silence — true silence does not exist in a medium as conductive as water — but it is a thick, absorptive quiet, the quiet of dense biological material absorbing vibration. The sponge walls muffle. The tower structures scatter and diffuse. Sound does not carry here the way it does in open water.

What sounds there are: the clicking of crustacean claws on limestone. A faint, wet grinding where an isopod scrapes algae from a wall. The soft, almost subsonic pulse of jellyfish bells contracting. Occasionally — rarely — the low, resonant boom of a large eel shifting its body against a tube-worm column, a sound felt more than heard, transmitted through the structure itself like a shudder. The rain, arriving as vibration from the surface, is a constant but nearly imperceptible hum, a bass note so low it becomes texture rather than sound.

And beneath all of it, a sound that is not a sound: the creak and settle of the structures themselves as they grow, as they shift, as generations of organisms add their tiny contributions to the whole. The towers are alive. They are not static. They change on timescales of months and years, and this change, this slow-motion construction, produces a constant microscopic noise — the pop of a new tube forming, the crack of a dying section yielding to the weight above it — that is below the threshold of hearing but not below the threshold of feeling. The place hums with its own becoming.

## What Is Lost, What Remains



In the deepest parts of the floor, where the limestone has thinned and the sediment is softest, there are shapes that do not belong to any living thing. Rectangles. Angles. Straight lines. The old town is buried under feet of carbonate mud, but it is not gone. The foundations persist — concrete, stone, the stubborn geometry of human construction that does not grow or yield or adapt. The tube-worm towers sometimes grow directly from these buried remnants, their tubes anchored to old brickwork, following angles that are not natural but that the worms cannot know or care about. In certain places, when a strong current strips the silt away, the outlines of walls become briefly visible: a right angle, a flat surface, a symmetry that the living structures never produce. These glimpses last hours before the silt drifts back. They mean nothing to the inhabitants. They are geology, now.

The faunal abundance here is not despite the darkness and the flooding — it is because of them. Without light, the competitive advantage of photosynthesis vanished, and the ecosystem reorganized itself around chemistry, around detritus, around the slow rain of organic particles from the surface layers. Without human presence — and there has been none for a very long time — the organisms have expanded to fill every niche, unchecked, unmanaged, building and consuming and dying and being built upon in a cycle that is active, constant, and utterly indifferent to the history beneath their foundations. The place is not in stasis. It is in motion: always growing, always eroding, always settling, always rising again from the seep and the drift and the warm calcium water.

It is a city. It is a reef. It is a grave. It is an ecosystem that has forgotten it was ever anything else.

The rain falls. The water holds. The towers grow.
