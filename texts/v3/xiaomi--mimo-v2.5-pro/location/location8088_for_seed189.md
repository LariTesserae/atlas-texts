# The Hollow Meristem

You are inside a plant that has forgotten it is a plant.

The chamber — if you can call it that — is perhaps sixty meters across and nearly spherical, though the geometry keeps *suggesting* that it's larger, or that the walls are farther away than they appear, or that there is a second ceiling layered above the first. The walls are alive. Not metaphorically: they are made of living tissue, a thick integument of vascular bundles and swollen parenchyma that pulses faintly with visible sap-flow, amber and green, through translucent channels the width of your forearm. Press your hand to the wall and it yields, warm, slightly damp, and you can feel a slow hydraulic rhythm — not a heartbeat, but something like one. The surface beneath your fingers is smooth in places, rough with callus-like nodules in others. Where the tissue is thinnest, light from *somewhere* filters through in diffuse golden sheets, casting no clear shadows.

This is below the surface. You know this because you descended to get here — through a shaft no wider than your shoulders, a gap in a root system somewhere in the world above, dropping into a throat of living wood and descending perhaps forty meters before the passage opened into this space. But the light has no source you can find. It comes from the walls themselves — bioluminescent, perhaps, but bright enough to read by, bright enough to see every detail of this impossible room. Normal light. Uncomfortably normal for a place that should be dark.



And it is **loud**.

That is the first thing that strikes you, and it does not stop striking you. The chamber rings with sound. A deep, structural groaning comes from the walls — the sound of growth, of cellulose fibers extending under tension, of lignin crystallizing in real-time. It sounds like a wooden ship in a storm, like a house settling into its foundation, except it never stops, only swells and recedes. Intermittent creaking reports punctuate the low drone — sharp, almost percussive, as sections of the inner wall split and re-heal. Somewhere above you, moisture condenses on the ceiling and falls in fat drops that strike the floor with an outsized, echoing *tock*, each impact ringing in the spherical cavity like a drum in a cathedral. The floor is a dense mat of aerial root-tips, fibrous and tangled, and the drops hit them with a sound like someone snapping small bones.

There are pockets of water — not pools exactly, but depressions in the root-mat where condensation gathers in shallow, warm lenses of amber fluid. It smells faintly sweet, faintly acidic, like fruit beginning to ferment. The air itself is humid but not sweltering; the plant exchanges gases across its inner surfaces in slow, visible convection currents that you can feel on your face, warm and damp, like breathing near someone else's mouth. This is a closed system. The air you are breathing has been made by this room, and will be unmade by it.



The vegetation density is total — there is nothing here that is not plant — and yet the chamber feels *empty*. The space is hollow. You stand in a void shaped by living walls, and between you and every surface there is nothing but air and light and sound. No other life moves here. Once, you thought you saw something — a small form, dark, scuttling along a vascular ridge thirty meters up — but when you looked directly, it was only a knot of tissue reshaping itself as it grew. The fauna parameter is almost zero. Whatever animal life might have existed in this system has been... *solved*. Absorbed. Made unnecessary. The plant handles everything. It is its own ecosystem, its own pollinator, its own decomposer. You are the most complex animal this place has seen in a long time, and the walls seem aware of this. Not hostile — just *aware*. The tissue nearest you grows slightly faster, reaching in your direction with new root-tips that uncurl like slow fingers.

But it is the **wrongness** of the space that will stay with you.

The geometry does not hold still. You can see, plainly, that the far wall is perhaps thirty meters away — and yet when you walk toward it, you do not seem to arrive any sooner than you should, or you arrive *too* soon, and the wall is suddenly close enough to touch, though it had not appeared to move. The curvature of the chamber suggests a sphere, but you cannot walk its perimeter; the path loops back on itself in ways that feel deliberate, as though the space is folding. Up and down exchange places in your peripheral vision. The light, which seems so normal, so warm, so reasonable, comes from directions that contradict each other — two shadow-angles at once, then none at all. The roots on the floor grow in spirals that, if you follow them with your eyes, become spirals-within-spirals that continue inward forever, and you have to look away before you believe this.

This is a young formation. The walls are fresh tissue — scarless, unworn, the vascular bundles still straight and un-kinked. Whatever this structure is, it has only recently *become*. The erosion profile is nearly zero: no weathering, no history, no accumulated sediment. Just new growth, new flesh, new space. As though the plant decided to hollow out this room and did so in a single effort, a single season, and the room is still settling into its own shape — still deciding how large it is, still learning its own geometry. The sharpness of the features — the crisp ridges where tissue-layers meet, the acute angles where branching walls converge — suggests that nothing here has had time to soften.

The sounds that bounce off these sharp surfaces distort. Echoes arrive too late, or too early, or from the wrong direction. A groan from the left wall returns from behind you, higher-pitched, as a whisper. The dripping condensation accumulates into patterns that sound, if you listen long enough, almost like language — not words, but the *rhythm* of words, the prosody of something trying to communicate through a mouth made of wood and water.

There is no technology here. No artifact. No sign that anything has ever entered this space from outside except you and the roots. The shaft you descended through may already be closing — you can hear, if you hold your breath, the slow wet sound of tissue knitting shut somewhere below you. The plant is not imprisoning you. It is simply continuing to be what it is. You are inside a meristem — a zone of active growth — and growth, here, means filling in what is hollow, closing what is open, converting emptiness into mass. The room will not exist forever. In a week, or a month, or however long this plant's calendar works, the space will be solid tissue, packed tight, every cavity filled. You will not be here for that. But the room will.

For now: warm air, amber light, the groan of living wood, the bone-snap of water on roots, a geometry that breathes and shifts, a silence that is not silence but the sound of something growing so fast it can be heard. You stand in the only empty space for fifty meters in any direction, and even this space is closing, and the walls are listening, and the light does not come from anywhere, and none of the angles add up, and it is beautiful, and it is not safe, and it is loud, and it is very, very warm.
