# The Basin of Lament

---

## The Ground

There is no soil here, and no word for soil in any language that has named this place. What you walk on is mineral — a composite of calcium carbonate, silica crust, anditeite bleached to chalk-white and stained sulfur-yellow. It is raw in the way that only young geology can be raw: the edges of every rock are sharp where water hasn't yet bothered them, the stratification lines in exposed faces are crisp and legible as the pages of a book no one has read. The bedrock is shallow. You can feel it through your feet, even through the layers of mineral deposit that have accumulated atop it like silt, like sediment, like something between the two that hasn't yet decided what it is.

The basin is maybe twelve kilometers across at its widest, oriented roughly north-south, though the people here have no word for north and no need for one. It is shallow — its deepest point is perhaps thirty meters below its rim, and most of the floor sits only eight to twelve meters below the lip of the surrounding ridgeline. From the rim, you can see the whole thing: a broad, pale, steaming concavity, not unlike the interior of a bowl that someone left on a stove. The ridgeline itself is nothing dramatic — low rises of dark volcanic stone, almost entirely uneroded, their flanks still showing the pahoehoe textures and gas-pocket scars of their formation. They formed, and then they stopped forming, and since then they have waited.

The water table is high. Everywhere, the ground is damp. In many places, it is more than damp — shallow channels of warm water, ankle-deep, run in braided courses across the basin floor, splitting and re-merging over beds of pale mineral mud. There are pools, dozens of them, some natural and some scooped or shaped by inhabitants, all warm, all mineral-laden, all faintly opaque with dissolved calcium and silica. Some are clear enough to see the bottom at arm's depth; others are milky, almost white, with suspended particles. The water tastes of chalk and iron and something faintly sweet.

## The Steam

The atmosphere is the thing that defines the basin more than anything else. It is warm — not oppressive, not scalding, but *consistently* warm, the way a room is warm when the fire has been burning for hours and the heat has soaked into the walls. The temperature hovers around thirty degrees during what passes for day and drops only slightly during what passes for night, because the geothermal input is constant and the moisture in the air holds heat like a blanket.

But the air is not merely warm. It is *wet*. Humidity approaches saturation. Visibility drops to a few hundred meters on most days, less on still mornings when the steam hangs unmoving in the basin and you can see perhaps fifty meters before the world becomes a pale, luminous suggestion. The light is dim — not because the sun doesn't reach here, but because the perpetual haze diffuses it into a uniform, directionless glow. Shadows are rare and faint. The world has the quality of a photograph slightly overexposed, all contrast softened, all edges uncertain. The sky is not visible. There is only the brightening above that indicates the sun's position, a washout, a rumor of light.

The steam comes from everywhere. It rises from the surface of every pool and channel in slow, wavering columns. It pours from vents in the rock — fissures in the bedrock where superheated water meets air and becomes vapor. It seeps from the ground itself, the mineral crust warm to the touch and sometimes visibly giving off moisture, the whole basin floor breathing. The effect is of a landscape that is alive with exhalation. You are inside a lung. The air moves slowly, lazily, in thermal currents that shift the steam in curtains and drifts. There is rarely wind from outside the basin — the ridgeline blocks most weather — so the atmosphere circulates on its own terms, governed by the geothermal gradients beneath.

The smell is mineral and animal. Calcium and sulfur, yes, but also the musk of the creatures that live here — a dense, organic sweetness underlaid with something brackish, like tidal flats. And the smell of the mud: rich, warm, faintly bacterial, ancient and present at once.

## The Sound

This is the loudest quiet place you have ever been. Or rather: this is the loudest place, full stop. The noise is not sharp or sudden — it is *constant*, a wall of sound that the ear learns to hear through but never learns to ignore.

The geothermal features produce the bass. The vents — there are perhaps forty or fifty of any consequence scattered across the basin — hiss with the sound of pressurized steam escaping rock. The larger ones roar. The sound is continuous, omnidirectional, and it blends into a low, throbbing drone that you feel in your sternum as much as you hear it in your ears. Several of the larger pools bubble vigorously, the water churning with subterranean heat, and the bubbling is not the gentle patter of a simmering pot but the heavy, irregular *glugging* of a thick liquid being forced upward by tremendous pressure. It sounds like a stomach the size of a building. It sounds like something digesting.

The animal life adds the treble and the midrange. The basin is *alive* with creatures, and they are *loud*. The most numerous are a species of amphibious crustacean — something like a crab, something like a crayfish, roughly the size of a human hand, with pale translucent shells and oversized mandibles they use to scrape mineral deposits from rocks. They are colonial, gregarious, and they communicate by striking their claws against hard surfaces. The sound is a rapid, rhythmic clicking — *tak-tak-tak-tak* — and when tens of thousands of them are clicking at once, as they do during the long warm nights when the steam thickens and the dim light dims further, the sound is extraordinary. It fills the air like static. It layers on top of the geothermal roar and the hissing vents and the bubbling pools and produces a wall of noise that can be physically disorienting.

There are other sounds too. A species of wading bird-analogue — long-legged, long-necked, something between a heron and a crane, with a crest of feathery filaments that may be chemosensory — produces a low, resonant boom by inflating a throat sac. They do this to establish territory among the pools. The boom carries far through the dense, wet air. In the colony areas, where dozens of them cluster, the booming is near-continuous, overlapping, turning the air into a drum.

There are insects of a sort — not true insects, but analogous: small, winged, with membranous wings that buzz at frequencies that become a pervasive hum beneath the other sounds. They swarm above the pools where microbial mats thicken the water's surface.

There is no silence in the basin. There has not been silence here in a very long time.

## The Life

There are no plants. No grasses grow on the mineral crust. No moss clings to the rocks. No algae — or nothing recognizable as algae — films the water surface. The absence is notable and total. Whatever conditions sustain this place — the warmth, the water, the mineral richness — they do not support photosynthetic life. The light may be too dim, or the mineral chemistry too aggressive, or the water chemistry too extreme. The inhabitants, when they have been asked (and they have been asked, by the occasional traveler from outside), simply say: nothing green has ever grown here. The ground does not produce that way.

What the ground produces instead is *abundance of another kind*. The microbial mats that thicken the surface of the warmest pools are not plant matter but bacterial — chemosynthetic communities that derive their energy from the mineral-rich water, from the sulfides and iron compounds that the geothermal system cycles through the ground. These mats are the base of the food chain. They are pale — white, cream, occasionally pinkish or faintly orange where iron oxidizes — and they are thick, rubbery, sometimes several centimeters deep, forming a skin over the water's surface that the crustaceans and other grazers tear at continuously.

The animal life is dense, diverse, and entirely adapted to this warm, wet, mineral, plantless world. The crustaceans have already been mentioned — they number in the millions, occupying every pool, every channel, every damp crevice in the mineral crust. They are the foundation species, the most visible presence, the sound-makers, the grazers of bacterial mats, the prey base for everything larger.

The wading creatures — the booming heron-things — are the largest animals in the basin. Adults stand perhaps a meter tall, with long legs adapted to the warm shallow water and a beak adapted to spearing crustaceans through their translucent shells. They are slow-moving, patient, and seem to regard the settlement and its inhabitants with something between indifference and mild curiosity.

In the deeper pools, there are eel-like creatures — elongated, pale-bodied, eyeless or nearly so, that move through the warm water with undulating motions. They feed on the bacterial mats and on smaller crustaceans. They are not aggressive, but the inhabitants of the basin avoid wading into the deeper pools without caution.

There are smaller things everywhere. Slug-like organisms that move across the mineral crust, leaving glistening trails. Filter-feeding organisms anchored to rocks near the vents, fanning feathery appendages to catch particulate matter from the steam. Swarms of the buzzing winged things above every body of water. The basin hums and clicks and booms and hisses with life. It is a rich place, an abundant place, a place where the absence of green growing things is more than compensated for by the sheer density and variety of animal presence.

## The Settlement

The people of the basin number perhaps eight hundred to a thousand. They live in a settlement — really more of a town — that occupies a relatively stable area of slightly elevated ground on the eastern side of the basin, where the mineral crust is thickest and the water table, while still high, is a few centimeters lower than in the basin's center. The ground here is firm enough to build on without it immediately subsiding into mud.

The construction is primitive in its materials but surprisingly sophisticated in its organization. The buildings are low — none more than two or three meters tall — and they are made from the materials at hand: mineral mud, packed and dried into thick walls; flat slabs of calcium carbonate crust, stacked and fitted; frames of the darker basaltic stone from the ridgeline, carried down and mortared withiteiteite mud that hardens to something approaching concrete when it dries in the warm air. Roofs are layered — overlapping slabs of mineral crust, sealed with mud, sometimes covered with the thick rubbery bacterial mats pulled from pools, which are surprisingly waterproof and insulating.

The buildings are dense. They are packed close together, separated by narrow lanes barely wide enough for two people to pass, with drainage channels running between them to carry away the ever-present water. The settlement has the feel of a place that has been built and rebuilt and added to over a long period — layers of construction, older buildings incorporated into newer ones, walls shared between adjacent structures, the whole thing organic and interconnected rather than planned. There are covered walkways, open courtyards where the steam rises and the clicking of the crustaceans is loudest, and raised platforms where food is prepared and social life occurs above the damp ground.

The people fish the pools with simple traps — woven frameworks of a reed-like material they obtain through trade with communities outside the basin, since nothing like it grows here. They harvest the crustaceans by hand, wading into the shallows and picking them from the rocks. They cultivate the bacterial mats in managed pools, thinning them, harvesting them, drying them into a dense, protein-rich food that keeps well. They are skilled at reading the water — knowing which pools are warming, which are cooling, where the mineral deposits are richest, where the eel-things are likely to be.

Their tools are simple. Stone blades. Bone hooks. Fiber cord. Hammered metal — copper, mostly, obtained through trade — for the few purposes that require it: knives, awls, the heads of hammers. They have no written language, or none that outsiders have recognized; their record-keeping is oral and tactile, with a system of knotted cords and carved mineral tablets that encodes genealogies, seasonal patterns (though the seasons barely change here), and territorial agreements with the wading creatures, whose booming territories they have learned to respect and work around.

The settlement is organized by family groups, each occupying a cluster of connected buildings, each with access to a designated section of pool and mat-field. Disputes are settled by a council of elders who meet in the largest courtyard, a space perhaps ten meters across, open to the sky and the steam and the constant noise. The council's authority is gentle and persuasive rather than coercive. There is a rhythm to life here that has not changed in living memory and, based on the depth of construction layers, has not changed in far longer than that.

## The Equilibrium

This is the thing that defines the basin most profoundly, once you have adjusted to the heat and the wet and the noise and the dim light and the mineral strangeness: *nothing changes*.

The geothermal output is constant. The vents hiss and roar at the same rate they always have. The water table does not rise or fall. The mineral deposits accumulate so slowly that no one has ever measured the process. The pools do not migrate. The channels do not shift course. The crustacean populations do not boom or crash — they are stable, year to year, generation to generation. The wading creatures maintain their territories. The bacterial mats grow and are grazed and grow again at a rate that balances perfectly.

The people of the basin speak of their world not as a place that exists but as a state that continues. Their language — a slow, tonal thing, spoken loudly to be heard over the ambient roar — contains no verb tense for change. There is only the continuous present. Things *are*. They do not *become*.

Outsiders find this unsettling. The basin looks young — the geology is young, the mineral deposits are fresh, the rock is unweathered — but it behaves like something ancient, something settled into a groove so deep it will never climb out. The contrast is disorienting. It is as if the world was created moments ago and immediately calcified into permanence.

The people of the basin do not find it unsettling. They find it home. They step out into the warm, dim, deafening morning — the steam rising, the crustaceans clicking, the heron-things booming, the vents hissing, the mud warm underfoot, the air thick and mineral and alive — and they feel the same thing they felt yesterday and the day before and the day their grandparents felt it: *this is how it is. This is how it has always been. This is how it will be.*

And in the basin, they are almost certainly right.
