# The Vigil Garden

The ceiling is perhaps four meters above you — you cannot see it, but you know it by the way sound dies differently up there, absorbed into something soft and old. The floor is stone, smoothed by ten thousand years of something like patience, and it dips slightly at the center where a shallow depression holds an inch of water so still it might be glass. Condensation gathers on everything. You feel it on your face before you see it on the leaves.

The cold is not bitter. It is the temperature of deep rock, of places the sun has never touched — perhaps four degrees Celsius, holding steady, having held steady for longer than you can meaningfully reckon. This steadiness is the place's most defining quality. Nothing here changes quickly. Nothing needs to.

**The growth.**

You are standing in vegetation so dense it has become architecture. Pale-stemmed ferns curl upward from cracks in the stone, their fronds unfurled and motionless, each one so thoroughly colonized by a film of translucent moss that the original leaf is barely distinguishable — a skeleton inside a body of something else. The ferns grow in clusters of six or seven, pressed shoulder to shoulder, their crowns interlocking overhead into a low canopy that you must part with your hands to move through. The moss is luminous. Not brightly — not the lurid neon of deep-sea creatures — but with a dull, grainy, silver-white glow, like the memory of phosphorescence. Enough to see your hands. Enough to see the next fern, and the next, and the pale expanse of fungal fruiting bodies spreading across a fallen stone like spilled milk.

There are no flowers. There is no wind. The plants here have not needed pollinators in an age, and they have responded by becoming entirely structural, entirely patient. Fungal networks run beneath the stone in hairline channels — you can see them where the floor has cracked, bright mycelial threads like veins of white metal, crossing and recrossing in patterns that suggest neither randomness nor design but something in between: optimization without intent. The mushrooms — if that word still applies — rise in shelf-formations along the walls, stacked in overlapping tiers, each shelf no wider than a palm, their surfaces slick with condensation. Some have grown around the artifacts.

**The artifacts.**

They are scattered. Not placed. Whatever put them here did not arrange them, or if it did, time has undone the arrangement with thorough indifference. Small structures — you might call them nodes — sit among the fern clusters and along the walls. They are dark, matte, warm to the touch, which is how you find them in the darkness: by running your fingers along the wall and feeling the patches of almost-body-heat. They are roughly the size and shape of a human skull, though less regular, as if they were not shaped but *grown* against some geometrical intention they have since relaxed away from. Their surfaces are inscribed — not with writing you can read, but with grooves so fine they feel like texture rather than meaning, like the grain of old wood.

These nodes are the technology. You understand this without understanding how. They are the reason the ferns photosynthesize without light — or rather, they are the reason the ferns no longer photosynthesize at all, having been *assisted* into a different metabolism entirely, one that operates on principles the nodes provide and the ferns have accepted the way a river accepts a channel. The energy comes from below. From the rock. From something in the rock that the nodes know how to speak to. The ferns do not know this. They simply grow, in the dark, in the cold, lush and pale and unhurried, drinking a language they cannot hear.

The nodes are still working. You know this because the moss is still luminous. You know this because the water — the thin, mineral-tasting condensation that beads on every surface — still tastes faintly of complexity, of dissolved trace elements in ratios too precise to be natural. You know this because you are alive, and the air is breathable, and you should not be breathing air this clean this far below the surface of anything.

But the nodes are old. Some of them have been overtaken — the mushrooms have grown over them entirely, absorbing their warmth, conforming to their shape, so that what you find is a fungal mass in the shape of a node, still faintly warm, still faintly humming at frequencies you feel in your molars rather than hear. These consumed nodes may still be working. They may be doing something different now. You cannot tell, and there is no one to ask.

**The sounds.**

The place is quiet in the way that the inside of a body is quiet. There is a baseline: the almost-silence of a closed system, of air not moving, of water not flowing. Over this, slowly — so slowly you have to hold your breath to hear it — there is a creak. It comes from the walls. The stone is settling, has been settling, will continue settling, and every few minutes (or hours; your sense of time has begun to soften) a seam somewhere shifts by a millimeter and the rock speaks in a low, wooden groan that reverberates through the chamber and then dissolves. The ferns absorb it. The moss drinks sound the way it drinks moisture.

Occasionally — once, twice since you arrived — a drop of water falls from the ceiling into the shallow pool at the center. The sound is enormous. It rings in the space like a bell struck in a cathedral, spreading outward, repeating off the walls, dying over the course of several seconds into a faint tonal smear that the moss and ferns and mushrooms slowly, collectively, metabolize into silence again.

You realize you have been here for a while. The pool has not risen. The water that falls into it is leaving through channels too small to see — or being taken up by something that is not visible from where you are standing.

**The ecology.**

There is no animal life. No insects, no worms, no microfauna that you can detect. The decomposition is fungal — the ferns that die (rarely; they grow so slowly that death is a geological event here) are consumed in place by the mushrooms, which creep over them over months or years, leaving behind only the moss, which colonizes the skeleton, which remains standing in the dark like a ghost of itself. The fungal networks transport nutrients laterally across the chamber, connecting fern clusters into a single distributed organism, or perhaps a single community — the distinction may not exist here, where individuality is less a fact than a convenience.

The nodes maintain the chemistry. The chemistry maintains the ferns. The ferns maintain the air. The air maintains you, if you choose to stay. Nothing maintains the nodes — or nothing you can identify. They may be maintaining each other. They may be drawing on reserves so large that depletion is a concept for other places. You do not know. You can only feel the warmth of them under your fingers, and the fine grooves inscribed in their surfaces, and the almost-imperceptible vibration that might be function, or might be the memory of function, or might be the slow, slow, slow unwinding of a clock wound so tightly once that it is still counting down an age later.

**The darkness.**

You have not seen this place. You have felt it, heard it, tasted it, smelled it — the mineral damp, the faint sweetness of fungal decay, the clean metallic tang of the air drawn through systems you cannot name. But sight is a gift the chamber does not offer. The moss gives enough light to navigate by, if you let your eyes adjust for a long time and then do not ask too much of them. Shapes emerge. Fern-fronds. The pale discs of mushrooms. The dark warm mass of a node against darker stone. The glass-still surface of the pool, which reflects nothing because there is nothing to reflect, but which you know is there by the way it refuses to make sound — a silence within the silence, a flatness that your footsteps detect as absence.

You have been here before, or someone like you has. There is a mark on the wall near the entrance — a scratch, made with something harder than the stone, in a shape that might be a letter or might be a tally or might be the first thing a hand needed to do in the dark to prove to itself that it existed. The moss has grown over it. The mushrooms have reached toward it. In a few more years, it will be absorbed, and the wall will be smooth again, and the chamber will continue its long, patient, luminous indifference to the question of whether anyone is watching.
