# The Stone Herds of the Upper Plateau

## The Roof of It

You stand on a shelf of the world that should not be this high and this large at the same time, but here it is — a highland plateau stretching in every direction to horizons that blur not from distance alone but from the curvature of whatever body this is. The ground beneath you sits above most of the clouds. Not above all of them. Some mornings the plateau is ankle-deep in fog that has no business being this far up, and you feel it bead on your skin, on the stone, on the woven wool of your coat. Condensation. The air carries enough moisture to remind you it's there but never enough to become rain except in fits — a sudden fifteen-minute drizzle from a sky that seemed clear, then silence, then dry cold again for days.

The elevation shapes everything. The light is ordinary — daytime is daytime, the sun crosses overhead with no particular drama — but the air is thin enough that shadows feel sharper than they should. You see farther than your eyes are comfortable with. Details at distances that shouldn't resolve somehow do.

## The Bones of the Land

The geology is *young*. This plateau was not here — not at this height, not this shape — recently. The mountains ringing it to the north and east are knife-raw: exposed granite and basalt in faces that have not yet learned to crumble. Glacial scars are fresh, the striations still legible, the terminal moraines unsorted and rough. Rivers that drain the plateau have cut only shallow gorges so far, their walls showing perfect horizontal banding of sediment that hasn't had time to warp or fold. In places, thermal springs push warm water up through fractures, and these become the damp pockets — small marshy areas where the ground steams in the cold air, where the only real trees on the entire plateau manage to root. The springs are the water story: not rivers, not lakes, but seepage, fog, condensation, and the occasional mineral-rich pool rimed with salt crystals that form and dissolve over the course of a season.

The rock itself is dense and dark. Underfoot it is mostly basaltic — flat, tabular, breaking along natural joints into surfaces that are almost walkable without modification. Almost. The city solved the rest.

## The Settlements

There are cities here. Not one. A chain of them, connected by roads that are simply the plateau's own rock with the worst irregularities broken flat by hand and packed with gravel. The roads are old enough to have been walked smooth in the center — not from wheels, there are no wheels that serve well here, but from generations of footfalls and hoof-falls and the dragging of sledges.

The largest of the cities — it has no single name, or rather, its name changes depending on which wall you enter through — sits in a shallow basin where three thermal seeps converge. It is *dense*. The buildings are stacked stone, dry-fitted with astonishing precision, two and three stories high, pressed shoulder to shoulder along lanes barely wide enough for two people abreast. The outer walls of the city are the outer walls of the buildings — there is no gap between settlement and wilderness, no ring road, no buffer. The city simply ends and the plateau begins. From outside, it would look like a single massive stone formation if not for the smoke and the faint lines of light from unglazed windows.

The construction is primitive in material — stone, timber hauled up from lower elevations with tremendous effort, animal hides, woven grass and wool — but the craft is not primitive. These people have been building for generations in a place that punishes poor work immediately. Mortar is unnecessary because the walls are fitted. Insulation comes from thickness — some walls are three feet of stacked basalt — and from the spaces between buildings being packed with dried grass and dung. The interior of a building here smells of animal fat lamps, of wool, of the faint sulfur from the thermal water that is channeled through the lowest rooms in stone-lined gutters for warmth. Every building has a warm room at its core.

The infrastructure is layered vertically. Lower floors are for animals and storage. Middle floors are for living. Upper floors are for drying, for smoke-curing, for observation. Rooftops are connected by planks and ladders — the city moves as much by its upper story as by its lanes. In the cold months — which is most months — the lanes are barely used. Life migrates upward and inward.

## The Fauna

This is the surprise. The vegetation is sparse — tufts of hardy grass, lichens that grow so slowly a patch the size of your palm might be fifty years old, low creeping shrubs in sheltered depressions near the thermal seeps. Yet the animal life is *everywhere*.

Large herds of a stocky, thick-furred ungulate — something like a yak but broader in the skull, with downward-curving horns that scrape the ground when they graze — move across the plateau in groups of hundreds. They are the economy. They give milk, wool, meat, leather, bone, dung for fuel. The people of the cities do not herd them so much as *accompany* them — semi-wild, the herds tolerate human proximity because the cities offer salt and windbreak, and the people follow the herds because the herds know where the grass is. There is a negotiation happening, not quite domestication, not quite wildness.

Smaller animals are profuse. Marmot-like creatures with dark fur and communal burrow systems that riddle the rockier ground — their alarm calls are one of the few sharp sounds that punctuate the quiet. Raptors with enormous wingspans that ride the thermal updrafts from the warm seeps, circling in spirals so wide they seem barely to move. Insects in the warmer months — clouds of small flies near the marshy areas, beetles that burrow into the dung. A predator — something like a snow leopard but longer in the body, solitary, nocturnal — that moves along the edges of the herds and occasionally takes one from the city's outer buildings, causing a brief clamor of shouts and the clang of stone on stone before the quiet reasserts itself.

The animals are not tame but they are *present*. They share the space without particular wariness. The plateau is vast enough that no species has been pressured into scarcity. There is room.

## The Quiet

It is quiet here. The wind is constant but thin — it moves over the plateau without the mass to make real sound except during storms, which are rare and brief. The cities hum at a low register: the murmur of conversation carried through stone walls, the scrape of hand tools, the lowing of animals in lower floors, the hiss of thermal water in its channels. No metal-on-metal clanging, no machinery, no engines. Voices carry a long way in the thin air, and so people speak softly by habit. A city of several thousand that sounds like a room of twenty.

At night — and the nights are long, the cold deepening until the stone itself seems to contract — the sound drops further. You can hear your own blood. You can hear the marmots settling into their burrows, the small clicks and shifts of the herd bedding down on the plain. Occasionally, far off, the crack of rock fracturing in the cold — the plateau still growing, still splitting, still young.

## The Pace

Nothing here changes fast. The herds move in annual circuits that barely shift from generation to generation. The cities add a room, a floor, a wall, across decades. The thermal water warms the same rooms it has warmed for centuries. Weather comes in slow cycles — a month of dry cold, a week of fog, a day of drizzle, then dry again. The young geology is the one fast thing, but fast on a geological clock — still slow by human measure. The mountains are still rising, the rivers still cutting, but the people living among them would need to keep records across many lifetimes to notice. Some of them do. There are marks on certain stones at the edges of the plateau — notches, tally marks, the oldest ones barely visible, the newest ones sharp-edged. No one remembers what they count. The counting continues.

---

You notice, standing here, how *normal* it all is. No strangeness in the ecology, no alien logic in the construction. It is simply a hard place that people have made livable with patience and stone and an alliance with animals who were already here. The dampness on your skin, the cold in your lungs, the quiet in your ears — none of it is hostile. It is just the plateau, being itself, slowly.
