# The Steaming Ward

You enter through a narrow gate — a break in the wall of rust-streaked brick that defines this place absolutely — and the air changes on your skin like stepping into a mouth. Hot. Wet. The humidity is so total it has weight. It presses against you from all directions at once, and within seconds your clothes belong to this place more than they belong to you.

## What you see

The ward is perhaps three hundred meters across, bounded on all sides by the same high brickwork, and the ground beneath you is a lattice of iron grating over dark water that moves slowly, endlessly, in channels too regular to be natural. The water is the color of strong tea — tannin-dark, mineral-warm, drawn up from wells that reach some deep aquifer heated by geology. Steam rises from the channels in pale, wandering columns. Where the steam thickens near the center of the ward, visibility drops to a few meters, and structures appear and vanish like thoughts you almost caught.

The structures are the factories — though *factory* might be too grand a word for most of them. Low brick buildings with peaked roofs of copper sheet gone entirely green, their walls sweating condensation, their windows (if they have windows) smeared to opacity. Smokestacks rise from some, but what they exhale is mostly steam, barely distinguishable from the ambient fog. Others are open-sided — just roofs on posts — and inside you can see the machinery: presses, vats, frames, all of iron and hardwood, all moving.

Everything is moving. This is the first thing that overwhelms you and the last thing you adjust to.

## The sounds

This is why people don't come here unless they must.

The machines are not quiet. There is a rhythmic *chunk-chunk-chunk* from the hydraulic presses on the south side, a sound so regular it becomes almost geological, like a mechanical heartbeat. There is the endless hiss of steam venting through pipes — not one hiss, but dozens, each at a slightly different pitch, creating a chord that never resolves. There is the gurgle and suck of the water channels, whose current is accelerated in places by pumps that groan on a cycle of about nine seconds. And beneath all of this, the low industrial hum of the district's boiler — an enormous thing, walled into its own building near the ward's center, which provides heat and motive power to everything else through a network of pipes that runs beneath the grating like veins beneath skin.

The combined effect is a kind of roar. Not sharp. Not painful. But *total* — a wall of sound at all frequencies that fills the space between the brick walls and presses against them from inside. You raise your voice to speak and it vanishes into the general noise. People who work here communicate with gestures, with a shorthand of hand-signals developed over years, and their hearing, after a decade of the ward, is not what it was.

## What grows

The vegetation is astonishing, and wrong-feeling, and beautiful in the way that beautiful things sometimes are when they should not exist.

Every horizontal surface that is not actively maintained — and some that are — has been colonized by plant life. Mosses first, in thick cushions of green so vivid they look synthetic, growing on the warm brick, feeding on the condensation. Then ferns, enormous ones, their fronds unfurling from crevices in the walls and pipes, their roots cracking mortar. Then broader-leaved things — plants you might recognize as relatives of philodendrons, of caladiums, of peace lilies — but larger than any houseplant, their leaves the size of serving platters, their stems thick as wrists, climbing the factory walls and hanging in heavy curtains from the rooflines.

The heat and the water and the mineral richness of the condensation — which carries dissolved calcium, iron, silica from the deep aquifer — have created a kind of accidental jungle. Vegetation in the ward grows at a rate that outside botanists find troubling. A frond unfurled in the morning is at full extension by evening. Moss can be scraped from a wall and the scrape-mark is green again within two days.

There is a tending crew — two people, usually, moving slowly through the growth with machetes and hand-shears, keeping the channels clear and the machinery accessible. They do not eliminate the plants. No one eliminates the plants. The plants are part of the district's license, part of its charter, and there are those who believe the vegetation is load-bearing in some way the city's engineers have never fully explained — that the root networks stabilize the water table, that the transpiration regulates the humidity, that without the green the ward would flood or overheat or simply stop.

## What does not grow

There are no animals here. This is the second thing that becomes apparent and the thing that stays unsettling longest.

No birds in the rafters, though the rafters are warm and sheltered. No insects on the plants, though the plants are lush and dripping. No fish in the dark water channels, though the water is rich with dissolved minerals. You might see the suggestion of movement from the corner of your eye — a ripple in a puddle, a sway in a fern — but when you look, it is only the machinery, only the steam, only the slow mechanical pulse of the ward doing its work.

The workers have theories. The heat is too much, but the plants manage it. The water is mineral-poisoned, but the plants thrive in it. Something about the frequency of the machinery, they say — though they say it quietly, in the gesture-language, because it is the kind of superstition that might get a person reassigned to a less desirable district. One woman, who has worked the tending crew for eleven years, believes the plants have simply outcompeted everything else. There is nothing left for an animal to eat that the plants have not already claimed. Every calorie, every mineral, every drop of water cycles through the green and the green gives nothing back that an animal could use.

She might be right. It is difficult to disprove, and no one with the authority to commission a study has ever found this small ward worthy of one.

## What is made

The ward produces. This is its purpose and its charter and the reason it was walled and provisioned and allowed to be what it is.

The vats hold dyes — brilliant, volatile pigments extracted from mineral-rich waters through a process involving heat, pressure, and the careful introduction of certain plant compounds. The presses stamp sheet metal into components for the city's infrastructure. The frames hold textiles being treated with waterproofing compounds that stink of something sweet and chemical and faintly rotten. Three or four different industrial operations share the ward's resources — the water, the heat, the steam — and each feeds byproducts into the others' processes in a loop that the district's original designer either understood completely or not at all. No one living is certain which.

What leaves the ward, through the gate or over the wall via a small crane-rail, is valuable. Dyes in sealed ceramic jars. Metal components wrapped in oiled cloth. Treated fabric bolts. The ward exports precision and color and impermeability, and in return it receives food, fresh water (the channel-water is for industrial use only, and you would know this from the taste, which is sharp and metallic even as steam), replacement parts for the machinery, and occasional new workers to replace those who have decided the roar is more than they can bear.

## The dimness

Light here is what filters through the steam, and it is not much. The ward is low-lying — lower than the surrounding city by several meters, a deliberate design choice that keeps the water table accessible — and the walls are high, and the canopy of industrial growth has thickened over the years into a loose ceiling of green that intercepts what little angle the sun manages. At midday, in the rare gaps in the steam, you might see a column of light — white-green, full of motes — striking the iron grating and making the dark water flash. These moments are brief. The ward's true light comes from gas lamps mounted on the factory walls, their mantles burning a steady amber that turns the steam gold and the moss into something almost amber itself, and from the dull red glow visible through the grate-slats where the boiler's pipes run beneath your feet.

It is the kind of dimness that you adjust to and then forget about, and then someone visits from outside and you realize you have been squinting for years.

## The strangeness

The ward is strange, but it is a *settled* strangeness — old enough to have its own logic, its own customs, its own way of being that no longer requires explanation. The workers do not think of it as strange. They think of it as the ward. The hot wet air, the roar, the green walls, the animal-absence, the particular way that time moves when everything is loud and dim and steaming — this is simply the shape of their days.

The strangeness lives in small details. The way the moss grows in patterns that look, if you stare too long, like script. The way the steam columns seem to lean toward people, as if curious, though everyone knows this is a convection effect of body heat in a humid environment. The way the oldest machines — the ones that have been running since the ward's founding — have begun to accumulate mineral deposits from the steam, a slow accretion of calcium and iron that gives them the look of geological formations, as if the distinction between machine and rock is beginning to soften.

The ward has been here for forty-seven years. The brickwork is weathered but sound. The machinery is maintained but not replaced. The plants grow and are cut back and grow again. The water flows and is heated and rises as steam and condenses and flows again. Everything cycles. Nothing leaves that was not meant to leave. Nothing enters that was not meant to enter.

It is, in its own way, a complete world. A small, loud, green, dark, wet, very hot world, held inside walls, making beautiful things from the pressure of the deep earth.
