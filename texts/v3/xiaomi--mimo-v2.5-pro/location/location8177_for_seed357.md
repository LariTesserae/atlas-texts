# The Canopy of Themselves

You are standing on something that was once a mountaintop. It is still a mountaintop — the air is thin enough that you notice it in your first few breaths, a slight giddiness, a pressure behind the eyes — but the stone is buried. Deeply buried. What you stand on is a surface of living tissue: pale green, faintly translucent, warm to the touch, and weeping.

Everything here weeps.

Water runs in thin rivulets from every surface, every joint, every fold. Not rain — the sky above is the white-hot haze of high-altitude heat, a sun that presses down like a weight. The water comes from inside. Condensation beads on walls of translucent membrane and runs in steady threads down structures that might be stems, might be walls, might be something for which there is no word because nothing like this has ever been built by hands. The air smells of sap and steam and something almost sweet, almost rotting, the way a greenhouse smells when every surface is alive and nothing has been pruned in a very long time.

There are no animals here. None. Not insects, not birds, not the skitter of lizards across warm stone. The only sounds are the sounds of growth.

And they are *loud*.

The dominant sound is a deep, arrhythmic groaning — the sound of structural tissue under enormous strain, expanding. It comes from everywhere, from below, from the walls, from above where the canopy-like structures shift and swell. Every few minutes, somewhere in the middle distance, a sharp crack like a tree splitting in a storm, and then a slow, wet collapse — a section of living architecture giving way, folding in on itself, collapsing into a heap of pale fiber that is already being overtaken by new growth even as it falls. Because everything here is growing. Everything is always growing. The rate is visible. Stand in one place and watch a wall-thick trunk push upward by the width of your finger in the time it takes to breathe. Press your hand to a surface and feel it pulse — not with a heartbeat, but with the hydraulic push of water being driven through tissue at speed, a vibration like holding a garden hose under full pressure.

The structures are sparse. That is the strangeness — because the vegetation is *total*, every molecule of the built environment is plant, and yet the plant structures are so vast and so widely spaced that the feeling is one of emptiness. Imagine a forest where each tree is a quarter-mile wide and the gaps between them are open bowls of hot wet air. Each structure is a single organism. They are roughly columnar, tapering as they rise, their surfaces ruffled with folds and shelves and protrusions that serve no clear purpose — or rather, serve purposes that are entirely internal to the organism, chambers for water storage, light-catching surfaces angled to track the sun, reproductive structures that periodically burst open and release clouds of spores so fine they hang in the wet air like smoke.

The ground between them — if you can call it ground — is a living mat. Root tissue, matted and fibrous, ankle-deep, spongy with water. Step on it and water wells up around your foot. Step off and the impression fills within seconds. This mat connects everything. The great organisms are not separate; they are outgrowths of a single substrate, a living floor that extends across the entire mountain range, climbing ridges, filling saddles, draping over cliff faces in curtains of green tissue. From above — from one of the higher shelves, if you could climb one — the landscape would look like a membrane stretched over the skeleton of the mountains, pulled taut in some places, sagging in others, pooling with water in the depressions, steaming in the heat.

The heat is oppressive. Not dry — never dry — but heavy, wet, close. The air is saturated. Sweat does not evaporate; it simply joins the general dampness. Your skin is slick. The surfaces around you are slick. Everything is slick. The translucent walls of the organisms glow faintly where light passes through them, throwing a greenish, diffuse illumination that makes it hard to judge distance. Shadows are soft and incomplete. The normal light of the sun is filtered, refracted, softened into something interior and strange.

The turbulence is constant. This is a landscape in the process of building itself and tearing itself apart, simultaneously. The organisms grow fast — monstrously fast — but they are also structurally unsound at this speed. Sections fail. Towers of living tissue that have been rising for days suddenly buckle, split along their length, and cascade downward in a slough of wet fiber and sap that pools and steams on the mat below. The mat absorbs it. New shoots appear within hours, pushing up through the debris, already tall enough to see. Elsewhere, a swollen chamber on the side of a great column ruptures and releases a torrent of stored water that races across the mat in a shallow flood, steaming as it goes, carrying fragments of tissue that will root and begin their own growth wherever they settle. The sound of this — the rupture, the rush, the slow absorption — is a sound like a dam breaking in slow motion, followed by a quiet, pervasive hissing as the water is taken back up.

There is a rhythm to it, but not a predictable one. The groaning rises and falls. The cracking is sudden and startling every time. The spore releases come in gustes when the wind — and there is wind, a hot high-altitude wind that moves through the gaps between the organisms like breath through teeth — catches the clouds of reproductive dust and carries them sideways in long, pale streaks. The wind sounds like a low whistle as it passes over the folded surfaces. Sometimes the folds resonate and produce a deep, hollow tone, like a didgeridoo, that rises from nowhere and fades after a few seconds. Sometimes several of them sound at once and the air fills with a chord — dissonant, enormous, felt in the chest more than heard.

You are standing in a place that is building itself into existence. The mountains emerged recently — geologically recently — sharp and raw, unweathered, their faces still fractured with the violence of their rising. And the plant found them. Or the plant was already here, waiting, a substrate of spores and root tissue that had been dormant in the deep soil and the fractured stone, and when the mountains pushed up through it, the plant began to build. It has been building ever since. The stone is being consumed — slowly, from the outside in — dissolved by root acids and replaced with living tissue. In a few centuries there will be no stone visible at all. The mountains will be entirely plant. Living peaks. A range of green, breathing, weeping towers, sweating water into a sky too thin to hold it, which will fall as condensation on the surfaces below, and the cycle will continue.

But for now the stone is still there, underneath. You can hear it sometimes — a deep, tectonic groan that is not the plant but the mountain itself, still settling, still sharp-edged, still young. The plant groans back. The two sounds overlap and become one sound, indistinguishable, the sound of a place that does not yet know what it is becoming.

The mat underfoot pulses. A crack sounds in the middle distance, followed by a long, slow, wet collapse. New shoots are already rising where the debris falls, pale and translucent, catching the filtered light. Steam rises. Water runs. The hot air shimmers.

Nothing watches you. Nothing eats here, nothing hunts, nothing calls to a mate. The only life is the patient, violent, structural ambition of the plant — which does not think, does not plan, but builds and builds and builds, and what it builds is everything.
