# The Decanting Terraces

## Overview

The plateau stretches at roughly 4,200 meters — not mountainous, but high enough that the air carries a faint metallic taste and the light arrives with the flat, merciless clarity of a place closer to the sun. There are no clouds. There have not been clouds for what seems like a geological opinion. The sky is a pale white-blue, overexposed, the kind of brightness that makes shadows feel like a separate substance rather than an absence.

Below the sky: a vast, tilting plain of mineral crust, stretching to heat-shimmer horizons in every direction. The ground is pale — bone-white and dove-grey, occasionally interrupted by dark seams of basalt or the rust-red stain of iron oxide weeping down a slope. Nothing grows here. There is no green. There is no leaf-litter, no root structure, no humus. The soil itself is not soil. It is a compacted mineral substrate — calcium carbonate, silica deposits,iteite calcium — layered and crusted and, in places, still forming. The earth crusts over itself like a scab.

## Water

And yet: water is everywhere.

Not rivers, not lakes in any conventional sense. The entire plateau is *wet*. Hot water — 60, 70, 80 degrees Celsius in places — rises from deep thermal channels and spreads across the surface in shallow, slow-moving sheets that barely qualify as flow. It seeps laterally through the mineral crust, appearing at the surface as glistening dark patches that look like wet stone until you notice they're faintly steaming. Condensation drips from every overhang, every ledge, every mineral shelf — a constant, quiet weeping. The terraces themselves have been built by this water: layered mineral deposits laid down over centuries as the thermal water cools and precipitates its dissolved content. The terraces step down in broad, scalloped shelves, each one holding a shallow basin of intensely clear water — turquoise, amber, grey-green, depending on the mineral composition of that particular vent. The water is almost supernaturally transparent. You can see every ridge and furrow of the mineral floor beneath, magnified and wavering.

The humidity is extraordinary. The air itself feels wet — not foggy, not rainy, but saturated, carrying heat and dissolved mineral in a constant mist that never quite gathers into droplets. It coats skin. It slicks every surface. The mineral crust is wet to the touch, warm, slightly gritty.

## The Fauna

There are no plants, but the terraces are alive.

The microbial mats are the foundation — thermophilic bacteria and archaea forming thick, colorful films across every wet surface. They are not plants. They do not photosynthesize in any way the eye recognizes. They are pink, orange, vivid rust-red, sometimes a deep purple-black, growing in concentric rings around the hottest vents where the water is nearly boiling and thinning to pale, translucent films in cooler pools. They smell faintly of sulfur and something richer, almost sweet.

On and in these mats live the larger creatures. Insects, mostly — but not any insect you would recognize as comfortable. The thermophilic beetles are large, slow-moving, their carapaces reflecting the mineral colors around them: opalescent grey, deep amber, matte black. They move in deliberate, unhurried lines across the mat surfaces, grazing. In the shallow pools, aquatic larvae — translucent, segmented, disturbingly large — undulate through the mineral-rich water, feeding on the bacterial films. Around the edges of the hottest vents, where the water is too warm for anything else, colonies of heat-adapted crustaceans cluster in the mineral deposits, their shells encrusted with precipitated silica until they look like small, moving stones.

Larger fauna occupies the margins. Pale, long-legged birds — heron-like but with shorter necks and heavier bills — stand motionless in the cooler pools, watching the water. They are exquisitely still. When they strike, they are fast, but between strikes they could be sculptures. In the mineral flats between terraces, small burrowing mammals have adapted to the heat; they come out at what passes for dusk (the light merely becomes slightly less aggressive) and move in quick, low runs between vent-holes, feeding on insects and crustaceans. Their fur is almost white, their ears enormous — dissipating heat, listening for predators that rarely come.

The predators do come, occasionally. Something lean and low-slung, reptilian, with heat-sensing pits along its jawline, moves through the terraces at night when the temperature merely drops from punishing to merely hot. It hunts by heat-signature, and the thermal landscape is so complex — so many warm spots, so much background radiation — that it must press its jaw flat against the mineral crust and read the ground like braille. It is patient. It is always patient.

And yet, for all this abundance, the terraces are *quiet*.

The silence is the strangest thing. The water barely makes a sound — too shallow, too slow, seeping rather than flowing. The mineral crust absorbs vibration. The air, thick with humidity and heat, dampens transmission. The beetles are silent. The crustaceans are silent. The heron-birds never call. The burrowing mammals make no alarm cries — they communicate, if at all, through seismic vibration through the crust, thumping their hind legs against the mineral floor in patterns too faint for human ears. The reptilian predator is silent because it has no voice at all; it communicates in scent and in the way it positions its body relative to the heat of the ground.

The loudest sound on the terraces is your own breathing. The second loudest is the faint, almost subsonic hum of deep thermal activity — felt more than heard, a pressure in the inner ear, as if the plateau itself is vibrating at a frequency just below the threshold of sound.

## The Settlement

Somewhere in the middle distance, where two broad terrace systems converge, there is a town.

It doesn't announce itself. There is no skyline, no noise, no smoke — though there is industry. The buildings are low, heavy, constructed from the same mineral crust that forms the natural terraces: cut blocks of calcium carbonate, slabs of silica-fusedite, mortared with aite calcium compound that sets hard in the humid air. The architecture is practical and strange — structures with wide, shallow footprints to distribute weight across the fragile crust, roofs that angle to shed the constant condensation into collection channels, walls that incorporate vent-holes to manage the interior heat without letting the humidity become unbearable. Some buildings are built directly into the terraces themselves, carved into the mineral shelves like cells in a wasp's nest, their interiors warm and damp and faintly luminous where the mineral walls have been polished to translucency.

The industrial infrastructure is visible in the long, low structures at the settlement's edge — processing facilities where mineral-rich water is channeled through a series of settling tanks and centrifugal extractors, pulling dissolved metals and rare earth elements from the thermal water. The machinery is powered by the heat itself: closed-loop geothermal engines that convert the temperature differential between the deep vents and the surface into mechanical work. The engines are quiet. Everything here is designed to be quiet. The people who maintain the processing lines — and there are people, several hundred of them, moving through the settlement with the unhurried economy of creatures adapted to heat — wear thin clothing that wicks moisture, wide hats against the bright sky, goggles that reduce the glare of the mineral surfaces without eliminating it entirely. They speak rarely, and when they do, in low voices. This is not cultural affectation. The heat and humidity carry sound strangely; raised voices distort, echo off the mineral surfaces in unpredictable ways, and the fauna — which is tolerated, even carefully managed — reacts badly to sudden noise. The reptilian predator, in particular, is startled by sound into explosive, dangerous movement.

So the settlement operates in near-silence. Orders are communicated by hand-signal, by written note, by the position of small colored flags mounted on poles visible across the flat terrain. The processing machinery hums, clicks, occasionally hisses steam — but these are expected sounds, ambient, below the threshold of alarm. At night, the settlement glows faintly — not electric light, though there is some of that, but bioluminescence. Certain of the microbial mats, when carefully cultivated in controlled conditions, emit a steady, cold, pale-green light. The settlement has learned to grow these mats in shallow trays mounted along the eaves of buildings, in the channels that guide condensation-water, along the edges of pathways. The effect is quiet and strange: a town that glows from within, like something alive, visible from a distance as a faint green constellation set into the white-grey expanse of the terraces.

## The Atmosphere of It

The light never fully retreats. Even at what the settlers call night — the hours when the sun is below the horizon — the mineral surfaces hold their brightness for hours, reflecting and re-reflected the ambient sky-glow until the entire plateau seems to exist in a state of permanent, gentle luminosity. True darkness, when it comes, is brief and startling. The stars, in that darkness, are overwhelming — but the window for seeing them is small, and most of the settlement's inhabitants sleep through it, their bodies aligned with the heat-cycle rather than the light-cycle.

The place feels ancient and settled and strange but not alien. It has the quality of a landscape that has been itself for a very long time — the terraces growing incrementally, millimeter by millimeter, the water finding its paths and remaking them over decades, the fauna adapted and co-adapted in ways that suggest deep time. The human presence is recent, in geological terms, and the settlement has the feel of something that is still learning how to fit — taking from the landscape carefully, listening to its rhythms, respecting the silence that seems to be not a lack but a condition, a property of the place itself, like the heat or the brightness or the wet.

You could walk across the terraces for days and see no one. You would see the water, the mineral formations, the slow deliberate life that fills every niche. You would hear almost nothing. You would feel the heat and the wet and the light pressing against you from every direction. And if you stopped — stood still on a terrace shelf, one hand resting on the warm mineral surface — you would feel, through your palm, the faintest vibration: the deep thermal engine of the plateau, turning over, patient and slow, the way the heart of a sleeping animal turns over, not quite silent, not quite audible, just *there*, underneath everything, the sound that contains all the other silences.
