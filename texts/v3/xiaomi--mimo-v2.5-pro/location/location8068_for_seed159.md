# The Throats

You descend through a wound in the earth that did not exist a season ago.

The ceiling of the uppermost chamber is perhaps forty meters overhead — fractured basalt, still weeping, still groaning. Dust hangs in the warm air like it hasn't decided where to settle yet. Slabs of raw stone lean against one another at angles that suggest they fell and caught themselves only hours past, and in fact some of them did. This place is not finished being born. The walls have no patina, no mineral staining, no lichen, no history. Where water runs down them it runs clear, carrying nothing but dissolved heat. The rock smells like a broken tooth — sharp, mineral, immediate.

And yet there is light.

Not from above, not primarily. The collapse-shaft you entered through admits a column of grey daylight, and others dot the ceiling of this first chamber like skylights in a cathedral made by accident. But the real illumination comes from the fauna.

---

**The creatures are everywhere.**

They coat the walls in layered, moving blankets — thousands of species, it seems, though you suspect the actual count is lower and the variation within each species is enormous. The most visible are the lamphangers: translucent, salamander-like things the length of your forearm that cling to vertical surfaces and pulse with a cold yellow-green light from organs along their spines. They are not synchronized. Each follows its own rhythm, so the walls appear to shimmer and crawl with slow lightning. Beneath them, smaller things move — eyeless beetles with heavy carapaces that click against the stone in overlapping rhythms, creating a constant low percussion. Pseudopods of something gelatinous extrude from cracks and retract on no schedule you can parse. When you look closely at any surface, the surface looks back with dozens of tiny dark eyes.

The floor of the chamber is a shallow lake — ankle-deep, warm, mineral-steeped — and it is *thick* with life. Eel-forms spiral through the turbid water in columns, occasionally breaking the surface with mouths that gape and gasp. Larger shapes move beneath: slow, broad, flat, disturbing the silt in long wakes. You cannot tell if they are predator or substrate. They may be both. The water smells like eggs and copper.

There is no plant life. None. Not a thread of moss, not a smear of algae on the walls — and this seems wrong until you realize the creatures *are* the vegetation. The lamphangers photosynthesize nothing; they feed on mineral seep and on each other. The beetles graze on biofilms of bacteria that you cannot see. The eel-forms eat the beetles. The flat things eat everything. It is a food web with no base you can identify, or whose base is chemical, geothermal, tidal — the slow exhalation of the earth's interior breath, feeding a system that has no need for sun.

---

The sound is not quiet.

You expected silence underground. Instead: the click of the beetles is a constant wash, like rain on a tin roof. The lamphangers hiss softly when disturbed, a sound like steam escaping through fabric. Water drips and trickles from a hundred sources, each with its own pitch — the chamber is *tuned*, almost, a random pipe organ of stone. And beneath all of this, the rock itself speaks: deep, arrhythmic groans as the walls shift and settle, punctuated by sharp cracks as new fractures propagate through the basalt. Once, while you stand listening, a section of wall the size of a door simply *separates* and falls into the lake with a flat wet concussion that sends the eels spiraling and the flat things surging. Silt blooms. The lamphangers on the nearby walls flare brighter in alarm, then dim, then resume their slow independent pulsing. The disturbance takes minutes to settle. By the time it does, you can already see small things moving toward the fresh rubble, colonizing it.

This is what 2.9 dynamic means in practice: the place does not wait. It is reshaping itself at a pace you can perceive. Passages open and close. Water tables shift. Entire chambers collapse or birth over the span of days or hours. The fauna does not mourn or pause — it simply flows into the new geometry, finds the new edges, adapts or dies and is eaten.

---

Moving deeper — because the system extends far beyond this first chamber, kilometers perhaps, a regional network of interconnected voids — the character shifts but the principles hold. Narrower passages, their walls still raw and unweathered, squeeze and widen. In places the ceiling drops to barely a meter above the water and you must crouch, and the lamphangers are so dense on the stone above you that their combined light is nearly bright enough to read by, and their collective heat makes the air shimmer. You are walking through the gullet of something alive.

In wider chambers, the density of fauna becomes genuinely overwhelming. You enter a space where the ceiling is lost in darkness above the lamphanger-line and the floor is a heaving mass of creatures so entangled you cannot distinguish species from species — a living carpet two feet deep that surges and parts around your legs as you wade through it. Things nip at your ankles. Things climb your boots. The sound here is a roar: clicking, hissing, the wet slap of bodies, the groan of stone. You feel the vibrations through your feet more than you hear them through your ears.

And then — scattered through this chaos, evidence of someone else.

---

The structures are crude and few. Stone cairns, stacked by hand from fallen basalt, three or four courses high, positioned at chamber intersections like trail markers. A lean-to of slabs against a stable wall, its interior blackened by fire, old. A wall — just a wall, two meters long, mortared with nothing, freestanding in the middle of a chamber for no apparent reason, now half-buried in silt and crawling with lamphangers. Whoever built these was here recently in geological terms and prehistorically in human ones. They left no writing, no tools you can find, no explanation. The structures are too sparse to be a settlement; they read more like an attempt, an exploration that did not continue. Or could not.

The creatures seem uninterested in the structures. They colonize them as they colonize everything — indiscriminately, urgently, as if the stone is simply more territory.

---

The warmth never varies much. It rises from below — you can feel it in the water, which is warmer at foot-level than the air above — and it holds steady around what your body reads as fever-temperature, the kind of warmth that makes you sweat gently and constantly. The damp is not oppressive but it is thorough. Your clothes will never dry here. Condensation beads on every surface the lamphangers do not occupy, running in thin rivulets downward to join the lakes and streams that thread the lower chambers. The water is never still. It moves in slow convection currents, in tidal pulses from some deeper mechanism you do not understand, in the wake of the large flat things that cruise its depths.

Light at 2.5/3 means: you can see. You are not straining. The lamphangers provide enough illumination to navigate by in most spaces, supplemented by the grey wash from collapse-shafts in the upper chambers. In the deepest passages, where the ceiling is solid and the fauna less dense, you would need fire — but you have not reached those passages yet, and you are not sure you want to.

The place feels like it is being *crowded into existence* — a world that is arriving too fast, filling up before it has finished forming, the fauna pouring into each new chamber before the walls have finished settling. You are not a visitor to an ecosystem. You are standing inside a process.
