# The Burned Plateau

## Emergence

The land is rising. Not right now — not visibly — but it has not finished arriving. The rock that forms the mesa country here is young in the way that bone is young, still mineral-hardening, still deciding what it is. Where faults have broken the surface, the edges are bright and unweathered. There is no rounding, no patina, no softness anywhere at the scale of stone. Cliffs look cut rather than carved. Ridges run in directions that feel programmatic, as if someone drew them with a ruler and forgot to go back and make them natural. Scree fields are minimal because the mountains have not yet had time to eat themselves. What loose material exists is sharp.

The elevation is high — not alpine, but high enough that the air has thinned to something that moves differently. You breathe and there is less in each breath. The sky is close but vast, the kind of sky that seems to press down gently on the back of your neck while simultaneously pulling your eyes outward toward an horizon so far away it loses meaning. The light is ordinary. There are no unusual colorations, no alien suns. Midday is midday. But the emptiness makes even ordinary light feel conspicuous, as though it is performing its usual work on a stage far too large for it.

## What Grows

Here is the strangeness: the ground is dry. Not arid — *dry*. There are no streams, no springs, no dew in the morning. The soil, where soil exists at all, has a mineralite hardness to it, calcium-cemented and pale, tasting of chalk and iron when you press a fingertip to your tongue. Rainfall is negligible. Any surface water that appears evaporates within hours, leaving behind faint salt-glazes that catch the light for a few days before wind dulls them.

And yet there is growth.

Not everywhere. Nowhere close to everywhere. But in clusters separated by hundreds of meters, sometimes by kilometers, the vegetation rises with a density and vitality that makes no obvious sense. The plants are succulent-heavy, thick-stemmed, waxy-leaved — but not in the way desert flora tends to be, hunched and defensive. They are *lush*. Leaves are broad. Stalks are tall. Some of the taller growths — not trees, exactly, but woody enough that you might call them that for convenience — reach five or six meters, their canopies open and spreading, casting the only real shade for miles.

They are drawing water from somewhere. The root systems must be extraordinary — ancient-tangled, deep-tapping, possibly reaching into fractured rock where remnant moisture pools in the deep geology. Or possibly the plants are doing something else entirely. Their chemistry may not be ours. Their waxy surfaces are not entirely wax; under close inspection, some leaves have a faintly metallic sheen, as though the cuticle has mineralized. The stems, when broken, do not bleed sap so much as release a paste — pale, chalky, faintly warm, smelling of nothing recognizable.

These islands of green are spaced so widely that from any one of them, the next is either invisible or visible only as a slight discoloration on the horizon. Between them: bare rock, mineral crust, silence, and sky.

## The Substance

Scattered across the terrain — on exposed rock faces, in hollows, along the bases of cliffs — there is something that does not belong to geology in any comfortable way.

It is dark. It is viscous. It pools in shallow depressions and hardens into sheets with a surface like industrial slag — black, faintly iridescent, cracking in patterns that suggest cooling metal rather than drying mud. In some places it has thickened into formations that resemble nothing so much as poured foundations or slag heaps, as though a factory once stood here and melted into the ground. There are no factories. There is no one. But the substance is warm to the touch on cool mornings, slightly above ambient, and it smells — faintly, only if you kneel and press your face close — of sulfur and petroleum and something else, something sweet-sick that is not quite any chemical you can name.

It is not tar. It is not resin. It is not biological. It is geological in origin but indistinguishable, at a casual glance, from industrial waste. Slag. Bitumen. Precipitate from processes that happen inside the earth the way manufacturing happens inside factories. The substance appears in seams that follow fault lines, as though it has been extruded from below — pushed up by pressure, by heat, by whatever slow engine drives the unfinished geology of this place upward.

It does not flow. It does not spread. It is as static as everything else here. But it is everywhere, in small amounts, and it gives the landscape a quality of having been *produced* — as though the land is not growing but being fabricated, layer by layer, by some process that operates on geological time and does not care whether anyone is watching.

## Sound and Silence

There is almost no sound.

The wind, when it comes, is low and dry and carries no moisture with it. It makes no howling — the landscape is too open, too broadly shaped, for the wind to find throats to sing through. It simply *passes*, pressing against your clothing, your hair, the waxy leaves of the green clusters, and then it is gone. The leaves do not rustle much. They are too heavy, too stiff.

There is no birdsong. No insect hum. No croaking, buzzing, barking, or breathing from any quarter. The absence of animal life is total and conspicuous. It is not that the landscape is hostile to life — the plants prove that — it is that no fauna has ever arrived here, or if it did, it left no descendants, and now the silence is the sound of a world that has only ever had one kingdom of living things and never missed the others.

The loudest natural sound is thermal: rock expanding and contracting in the warmth of the day and the relative cool of the night. Tiny clicks. Occasional cracks. The soft hiss of gravel settling on a slope. If you stand still and listen, these sounds become enormous. Your own breathing becomes enormous. Your heartbeat is a drum in a cathedral.

## Equilibrium

Nothing here is changing.

Or rather: nothing here is changing *fast enough to notice*. The plateau is still rising, but imperceptibly. The plants are still growing, but slowly — their waxy surfaces accumulate a patina of dust that takes years to form, and the dust has not been disturbed. The dark substance is still being extruded, but the newest flows look identical to the oldest. The salt-glazes from rare moisture events persist for months, maybe years, fading at a rate too slow to track.

This is a landscape in a kind of suspended late-noon. It is not dead — the vegetation proves that. It is not inactive — the geology proves that. But it has settled into a tempo so slow that from the perspective of any single visit, any single lifetime, it appears frozen. The green clusters will look the same in a century. The substance will not have spread. The rock will still be sharp-edged.

There is something unnerving about it — the sense that you are standing inside a process that is ongoing but that will not visibly advance within any span of time you could measure. The world is building itself, but it is building itself at the speed of stone.

## What It Feels Like

Standing here, on the high bare rock between two distant green clusters, with the dark substance gleaming faintly in a hollow to your left and the enormous pale sky pressing down from above, you feel two things at once:

First, that you are in a place that is *new* — new in the sense of unfinished, raw, not yet arrived at its own final form. The rock is too clean. The shapes are too simple. Everything has the quality of a first draft, of a landscape that has been sketched but not yet rendered.

Second, that you are in a place that is *old* — old in the sense of settled, immovable, committed to its own tempo. The silence has the weight of something that has been accumulating for a very long time. The emptiness is not recent emptiness; it is foundational emptiness, the kind that was here before anything else and will remain after everything else.

The contradiction does not resolve. You stand in it. The light is ordinary. The air is warm and thin. Nothing moves.

You are not being watched. You are not being waited for. The place does not know you are here, and it will not notice when you leave, and this is not a judgment — it is simply the nature of a world that is still becoming itself and does not yet have the capacity to register visitors, because it does not yet have the capacity to register anything at all.

It is almost finished. It has been almost finished for a very long time.
