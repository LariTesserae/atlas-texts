What lives here is a patient, frugal community, huddled in the margins of a world not yet fully built. It is a place of residents and of travelers, of ancient colonizers and fleeting visitors, all bound by the logic of scarcity and cold.

**The First Sovereigns:** The oldest and most patient inhabitants are not animals, but lifeforms that blur the boundary between stone and being. The **crustose lichens**—pale gray-green, burnt-orange—belong to the rock as much as to the air. They are a slow-motion civilization, digesting the granite with acids over centuries, each thallus a tiny, leathery continent claiming its acre of gneiss. They are the primary architects here, the ones who will, in a thousand years, have made soil from stone. In their cracks and in the saturated hollows live the **moss empires**: dense, black-green cushions of *Racomitrium* and *Andreaea*, worlds unto themselves. Press your eye to a moss cushion and you enter a miniature forest, where microscopic **tardigrades** and **nematodes** lumber like giants, hunting in the dew of a single droplet, their whole existence playing out in a universe the size of a fist. The **sphagnum bogs**, steaming faintly in the cold, are laboratories of chemistry, where decomposition begins its slow work, releasing that faint, ancient smell of iron and sulfur.

**The Foragers and the Fliers:** Above this botanical foundation moves the visible, audible life—a life of ceaseless foraging and vigilant flight. **Snow buntings** are the plateau’s nomadic citizens, their calls a delicate tinkle in the vast quiet. They move in loose, chattering flurries over the boulder fields, pecking for the seeds of grasses and the arthropods that live in the moss, their black-and-white plumage a form of moving camouflage. The **pipit** is a ghost, nesting so perfectly in the moss you could stand on its home and never know, until it erupts from under your boot with a sharp *zip*, a sound of pure alarm. **Ptarmigan** are the plateau’s changing sentinels, their feathers shifting with the seasons to match the lichen-painted rock or the first snow, their presence announced only by a sudden, soft clucking from a stone that was, a moment ago, empty.

The raptors own the invisible architecture of the air. **Golden eagles**, nesting on the unseen cliffs below, hunt the plateau’s updrafts. They are shapes in the periphery—a long, level glide along a ridge, a sudden, breathtaking fold and stoop—a reminder that this empty space is a hunting ground, that life here is subject to larger rules.

**The Busy Architects:** In the boulder fields, life becomes industrious. **Pikas** are the plateau’s most characteristic voice, their sharp, two-note bark echoing from rock to rock. They are not passive inhabitants; they are gardeners and hoarders. You see their work everywhere: small, neat haypiles of dried moss and sedge, tucked into sheltered crevices, a larder for the long winter. Every pile of glacial till is a potential pika village, every gap a doorway. They transform the static rubble field into a place of purpose and community. **Marmots** share this realm, bolder and larger, sunning themselves on the rare warm rock, their warning whistles a resonant, carrying sound that tells the entire plateau a human or an eagle is near.

**The Transient Swarm:** The engine that fuels this sparse food web is hidden in the wet margins. In the bogs and the shallow pools, **midge larvae** gestate in the cold water. When they hatch, they rise in thin, wavering columns on still days, a living plankton in the air. **Biting flies** work the edges whenever the wind drops. They are the invisible currency of the plateau, the protein packet that converts bog and moss into the energy of birds and bats. Everything, from the smallest pipit to the circling eagle, is ultimately fed by these clouds of tiny, buzzing life.

**The Passing Shadow:** And now, there is a new presence—one that observes but does not live here. The survey stakes are its tidy, metallic fingers, measuring the rock. The wide tire tracks in the clay are its footprints, purposeful and temporary. From the valley below, a low, industrial hum now forms a constant bass note in the soundscape, a vibration felt more in the bone than heard by the ear. It is the sound of the world below looking up and seeing not a community of lichens and pikas and eagles, but a resource—the promise of something worth crushing and refining, of a water table to be used, a plateau to be reshaped.

So the inhabitants of the Scoured Plateau go on. The lichens digest the stone. The pikas harvest the moss. The eagles ride the winds. And the midges rise from the steaming bogs. They do not know they are living on borrowed time, that their world is being measured for a different kind of future. They simply live, efficiently and sparely, in the quiet, wet, silver-gray light of this high, new world, while at its edges, the steady hum of what may replace them never, ever stops.
