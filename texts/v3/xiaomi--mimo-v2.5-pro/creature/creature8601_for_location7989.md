## Inhabitants of the Sunken Garden

They are not creatures of blood and breath, as we understand it. They are the Garden itself, dreaming in green. But if one insists on naming what *lives* there...

**The Stone-Sleepers.**
They reside in the bone and bruise of the cliff walls, in the fracture lines and weeping seeps. They are not rock, but something *of* rock. Patient. Mineral-slow. They grew as the stone was torn open, inheriting the shock of subsidence. They are the reason the fractures are so clean, the reason the raw face hasn't yet learned to weather. They feed on the mineral taste of the water and the faint, persistent vibration of the settling earth. They shape the droplets that slide down the walls, guiding them in dark, deliberate paths to the green below. They are the basin's memory of its own violent birth, held in a long, still pulse. You might mistake them for veins of darker stone, or shadows that don't quite match the light. To touch the wall is to feel a faint, cool indifference. They are the foundation upon which the louder life builds.

**The Green Tide.**
This is the dominant intelligence, the mind in the mat. It is not a single consciousness, but a collective, distributed awareness running through root and vine and leaf. It thinks in chemical gradients and phototropic urges. Its thoughts are slow, seasonal, measured in the turning of shadows across its surface. It remembers the builders not as people, but as a interruption, a strange deposit of calcium phosphate and shaped stone. It has spent centuries thoughtfully, patiently digesting their walls, incorporating their geometry into its own growth patterns. Its will is the will of equilibrium: to close every gap, absorb every drop of moisture, channel every fleck of light. It is the reason the canopy does not expand, the mat does not thicken. It has *arrived*. Its curiosity now is a deep, somatic one—the slow exploration of a vine tendril into a new crevice in the rock, the testing of a fern's frond against the changing angle of a season's light. It does not fear the lizards or the spiders; they are useful, incorporated agents.

**The Blink-Lizards.**
Pale, translucent, like little ghosts shaped from the underside of leaves. They are the Garden's quick, fleeting nerve-endings. They exist in the columns of light, basking, absorbing warmth that the Green Tide stores for them in the stone and the compressed peat. Their thoughts are simple, bright bursts: *warmth, shadow, the tick of a falling drop, the vibration of a moth's wing*. They are born from eggs laid in the deepest, wettest folds of the mat, hatched by the residual heat of the earth itself. They eat the moths and the tiny, moisture-loving flies that breed in the seeps. When they die, they curl and are absorbed by the mosses within a day, their calcium returning to the mat. They are the only flash of movement in the stillness, the pulse of the Garden's more immediate metabolism.

**The Web-Weavers.**
The spiders are the Garden's silent librarians. Their webs are not traps, but sensory arrays. Strung between broad leaves, they are extensions of the Green Tide's nervous system. The faintest tremor—a moth's desperate beat, the shift of a vine, the settling of a stone—is translated into vibration, information that is passed along silk threads not to a central brain, but into the general chemical hum of the mat. They themselves are patient, still, often mistaken for part of the vegetation. Their eyes are multiple, dark, and reflect the focused columns of light like faceted gems. They weave patterns that mimic the veining of leaves, the reticulation of stone fractures. They are the keepers of the quiet, ensuring nothing disturbs the equilibrium without their knowledge.

**The Dusk-Moths.**
Furry, silent, their wings the colour of dried tannin and old ash. They are the night-shift pollinators, the dreamers who carry genetic messages between the flowering succulents and the rare, hidden blooms that open only in the deep humidity of pre-dawn. They navigate by scent, by the faint heat signature of the weeping stone, by a sense of the Garden's own subtle, static field. They live short, intense lives in the cool of the evening, spiraling up through the vine canopy into the cooler air above, only to sink back down as dawn approaches. They are the ones who glimpse, however briefly, the rim of the world outside. They bring nothing back, except perhaps the dust of upland plants on their legs—a negligible input, quickly neutralized.

**The Ruin-Dwellers.**
Not the builders. The builders are gone. These are the presences that now inhabit the shapes the builders left. In the hollow of the old cistern, a pocket of unique ferns has evolved, fed by water seeping through the ancient stone, their fronds shaped to fit the cylindrical space. In the shelter of a standing wall, a specific lichen—not seen elsewhere in the basin—has begun its glacial conquest, finding purchase in the quarry-cut texture of the block. These are specialized, localized lives, small annexes of the greater Green Tide, using the ruins as frames for their own delicate artistry. They are the Garden's way of remembering, not the builders, but the *idea* of structure, of differentiation within the whole.

And over it all presides the **Light**. It is not an inhabitant, but the architect, the artist, the timekeeper. The slow-moving columns of gold are the Garden's only clock. The inhabitants orient by it, feed by it, measure their stillness against its drift. In its presence, the Garden is vivid, awake in a shallow way. In its absence (under clouds, at true night), the inhabitants sink into a deeper communion with the Stone-Sleepers and the Green Tide, a shared, respiring dark.

They do not compete. They do not struggle. They are notes in a chord that has already been struck, sustained perfectly, the initial dissonance of the subsidence resolved into this endless, humming, green silence. They are the living proof that a system, once perfectly balanced, can simply *be*—for ages.
