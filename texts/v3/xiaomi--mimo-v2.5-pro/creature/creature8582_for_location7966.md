# Who Lives in Stillrise

The people of Stillrise are human. Let me be clear about that first, because everything else about them will tempt you to revise the claim.

They are human in the way that a river stone is still a stone — recognizably, structurally, in every way that matters to the category — and yet so shaped by the force that held it that you would be forgiven for hesitating. They have hands. They have faces. They eat and sleep and carry water and tend the small green plots between their stone dwellings. They were born, presumably. They will die, presumably. But the distance between those two events has become very large, or very thin, or very still — it is hard to say which, because time in Stillrise does not pass so much as *pool*, and the people are whatever is at the bottom of the pool, settled and undisturbed.

---

## What They Are

They are patient. But not in the way we mean when we use that word — not the cultivated restraint of someone enduring a delay, not the practiced calm of someone who has learned to wait. They are patient the way the basalt is patient: because what else would they be? There is nothing coming. There is nothing to wait for. The moss is not spreading, the stone is not eroding, the clouds are not gathering into rain. They have no reason to anticipate, and so they have stopped anticipating, and the muscle of expectation has atrophied to something that no longer functions but does not need to be removed.

They are present. Intensely, luminously present. When a woman on the fourth terrace places a stone against its neighbor, that placement is the only event that has ever occurred. Her attention is total and effortless — not the strained focus of someone trying to concentrate, but the unselfconscious absorption of a thing that is entirely what it is doing. Her hands know the motion because they have performed it — how many times? A thousand? A hundred thousand? It does not matter. The number is irrelevant. Each repetition is the first and the last, the same and entirely new, and she does not distinguish between them because the distinction itself has no meaning here.

They are quiet. This is the thing you would notice first if you walked among them — not that they are silent, exactly, but that their quiet has a quality, a texture, like the quiet between raindrops, charged and full. They speak when they need to, in low voices that carry easily across the stone terraces without echoing, as though the air here is denser than it should be and swallows sound almost as soon as it is made. Their words are few and sufficient. They do not waste them on commentary or narration. A direction. A greeting. A name — though names here are used sparingly, as though each one is a small concession to the fiction that the speaker and the spoken-to are meaningfully separate.

They are calm. But not flat — not the dull calm of exhaustion or the medicated calm of detachment. Their calm has depth. You can see it in their eyes, which are clear and dark and focused in a way that makes you feel, uncomfortably, that you are being *seen* — not judged, not measured, not even particularly noticed, but seen the way a landscape is seen by someone who has stood in the same place for a very long time. They see you and they see the stone behind you and the lichen above you and the goat on the ledge beyond, and all of it is the same, and all of it is enough, and none of it requires a response.

---

## How They Live

Their days have no structure that you could chart. There are no bells, no whistles, no shifts changing or markets opening. There is no dawn chorus, because the birds do not sing, and no evening meal shared at a common hour, because the hours are not common — they are all the same hour, repeating, each one thick and damp and full of the same grey-green light.

And yet there are rhythms. The woman repairs the wall. The man mends the net. Someone carries water from the cistern to a dwelling on the fifth terrace. Someone else kneels in a small plot of moss, doing something — weeding? Grooming? Performing an action whose purpose is indistinguishable from its execution. These rhythms are not schedules. They are more like tides — slow, inevitable, driven by something internal rather than imposed. The people do not decide to do these things. They simply do them, the way the condensation beads on the stone, the way the clouds pass through the upper terraces: because that is what happens here.

Their work is careful. Obsessively, precisely, *quietly* careful. The stone channels that collect dew are carved to tolerances that would challenge metal tools, and they were made with stone. The pottery — simple, unadorned, elegant in the way that a perfectly round river stone is elegant — is fired at temperatures that produce consistent glaze without variation, piece after piece, year after year. The timber shingles on the roofs are each cut to the same dimensions, weighted with river stones of the same approximate size, layered in the same pattern. Nothing is sloppy. Nothing is extravagant. Everything is exactly what it needs to be and no more, and this precision has the quality of devotion — not to a god or a principle but to the act itself, to the doing, to the unbroken continuation of the practice.

They build, but they do not *make*. The distinction matters. To make something is to bring into existence a thing that did not exist before — a creative act, a departure from what was. To build, in the way the people of Stillrise build, is to maintain what already is. The stones the woman removes from the wall are identical to the stones she replaces them with. The wall before and the wall after are the same wall. She is not repairing. She is *renewing*, which is a different thing — an act of preservation so thorough that it becomes indistinguishable from stillness.

The man mending the net — I keep returning to him, because he is the clearest window into what is happening here. His hands move in slow, practiced loops, threading cord through cord, knotting, pulling tight. The net shows no damage. There is nothing to mend. And yet he mends it, with the same care he would bring to a net torn to shreds, because the mending is not in service of the net. The net is in service of the mending. The task is the point. The continuation is the purpose. If you asked him what he was doing, he might look up at you with those calm dark eyes and say, simply, *this*, and return to his hands, and you would understand that he had given you the most precise answer available.

## How They Are With Each Other

They live in proximity. The terraces are not large, the dwellings are close together, and they share water sources and green plots and the switchback stairs between levels. They see each other constantly. And yet there is a privacy to each person that has nothing to do with walls or doors — a wholeness, a completeness, that means proximity does not require interaction. They pass each other on the stairs with a nod, a murmured word, sometimes nothing at all, and the nothing is not cold. It is warm the way the stone is warm — slowly, deeply, without effort.

They do not argue. This is not because they have resolved all conflicts. It is because conflict requires a gap between what is and what should be, and there is no such gap here. What is, is. What should be, is. The two have collapsed into one, and where there is no gap, there is no friction, and where there is no friction, there is no heat, and where there is no heat, there is no anger. They live together the way stones in a wall live together: each one supporting and supported, each one in its place, each one complete.

There are children. You would see them if you watched long enough — small figures moving among the adults with the same deliberate quiet, the same unhurried purpose. They do not play, not in the way we understand play — not the chaotic, exploratory, boundary-testing play of children elsewhere. They practice. They sit beside adults and mimic their motions: stacking small stones, braiding cord, kneading clay. They are not learning skills in the utilitarian sense. They are being absorbed into the rhythm, sinking into it the way rainwater sinks into sand, becoming part of it without resistance or effort. Their faces are solemn but not unhappy. They have not yet learned to be anything other than what they are, and what they are is here.

The elders — and there are elders, though you would not always recognize them — are indistinguishable from the rest in their activity and bearing. Age does not mark them. Wrinkles, if present, are shallow and still, as though even the skin has reached its final state and stopped changing. They do not instruct the young. They do not tell stories. They do not sit apart, contemplating their long lives, because contemplation implies a distance between the thinker and the thought, and that distance does not exist here. An elder is simply a person who has been performing the same acts for longer than others, and this longer-ness has made them no different, because there is no *different* to become.

And between people — between pairs, between those who share a dwelling — there is something that might be called love, if love did not require vulnerability, and vulnerability did not require the possibility of loss, and loss did not require the passage of time. What they have instead is closer to *recognition*: the knowledge that this other person is here, is real, is part of the same equilibrium, and that they will continue to be here, will continue to be real, will continue to be part of the equilibrium, until — until what? There is no until. There is only this, and this is enough, and enough is the same as forever.

## What They Know

They know the mountain. They know which stones hold water and which shed it. They know where the moss grows thickest and where the ferns unfurl and where the lizards sun themselves on flat stones. They know the goats by sight — each one, individually, though they do not name them — and they know the flight patterns of the hawks and the paths of the clouds and the timing of the condensation drip in every channel on every terrace. This knowledge is not catalogued. It is not written down or spoken aloud. It is held in their bodies, in their hands and feet and the slow circuit of their attention, the way a river holds its own course — not by remembering the last time it flowed this way, but by flowing this way again because this is where the water goes.

They may know that they are unusual. They may have a memory — faint, faded, the color of old lichen — of a time when things were different. When the stone was softer, perhaps, or the air drier, or the moss spreading, or a wall crumbling that someone had to rebuild with new stones rather than the same ones. If such a memory exists, it has no charge. It does not haunt them. It does not make them long for change or fear its return. It is simply a story from before, and *before* is a word that has lost its meaning here, the way *tomorrow* has, the way *soon* has, the way all words that point to the passage of time have softened and flattened and become synonyms for *now*.

They do not know — I think — that there is another way to be. Not because they are ignorant, but because the knowledge, if it came to them, would be like a description of a color they have never seen: technically comprehensible, experientially empty. You could tell them about the world below the mountain, where moss spreads and buildings crumble and people age and die and are born and grow and change and leave and come back and fall in love and fall out of love and lose things and find things and build things and watch them fall, and they would listen — I think they would listen, with those calm clear eyes, with that total present attention — and they would understand every word, and they would understand nothing, because the understanding would have nowhere to land. Their minds are like the terraces: flat, level, smooth, and every drop of new information would bead on the surface and evaporate without sinking in.

## What They Are Not

They are not monks. They did not choose austerity. They did not renounce anything. There was never a moment of decision, a turning-away from abundance or complexity or change. They simply arrived at this place, and this place is what it is, and they are what it made them.

They are not dead. I keep saying this because it keeps being necessary. Their hearts beat — slowly, rhythmically, at a pace you might not detect unless you pressed your ear to their chest. Their lungs fill and empty. Their blood moves. They are alive in every measurable sense, and in some immeasurable senses too — there is a presence to them, a weight, a *thereness*, that the dead do not have. They are alive the way the mountain is alive: tectonically, glacially, at a tempo so slow that it is indistinguishable from stillness but is not, in fact, still.

They are not sad. Sadness requires a memory of joy, or a hope for it, and both of these have evaporated like the dew that never quite pools in the cistern channels. They are not happy either, for the same reason in reverse. They are *well*. They are in a state of wellness so total and so stable that it has no opposite, no shadow, no edge. If you could measure their contentment on some hypothetical instrument, the needle would not move. It would simply point, steady and sure, toward a reading that exists beyond the scale — off the chart, in the territory where the numbers stop and something else begins.

They are not waiting. This is perhaps the most important thing they are not. They are not waiting for something to happen, for someone to come, for a change, for an ending, for a beginning. They are not holding their breath in anticipation. They are holding their breath the way the mountain is holding its breath — not because they are about to speak, but because the breath itself has become the point, the inhale and exhale collapsed into a single sustained moment, a hum, a held note, a chord that rings and rings and never resolves.

---

## What Remains

When you leave Stillrise — and you will leave, because you cannot stay, because your body still keeps time and your mind still makes distinctions and your heart still beats at a pace that demands forward motion — you will carry them with you. Not their faces, which are calm and unremarkable. Not their names, which they did not share. Not their words, which were few.

You will carry their stillness.

It will settle in you like silt in a glass of water — not immediately, not visibly, but slowly, over days and weeks, until you notice it in moments of quiet. The way your hands rest on a table. The way your breath slows when you are not thinking about it. The way a held moment feels, for a fraction of a second, like it could last forever — not because anything has changed, but because you have, for that fraction, stopped expecting it to.

And then the feeling will pass, because you are not them, because you are still becoming, because the world you returned to is a world of moss that spreads and stones that erode and people who age and buildings that fall and moments that end and begin and end again.

But sometimes — in the early morning, when the air is damp and the light is low and grey, and the world is quiet in a way that reminds you of something you cannot quite name — you will feel it again. The held breath. The equilibrium. The deep, impossible calm of a place that has arrived and refuses to leave.

And you will wonder, as the condensation beads on the windowpane and does not quite drip and does not quite dry, whether it was a place you visited or a place you became.

And the answer, like everything in Stillrise, will be both. And neither. And enough.
