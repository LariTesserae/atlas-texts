What lives here is the negotiation itself.

First: the stone breathes, and others breathe with it. The primary inhabitants are the waters that are not quite rivers, the mosses that are not quite soil, the lichens that digest basalt into future ground. You can feel them thinking—slow mineral thoughts in crystal lattices, green thoughts in cushioned thalli. They do not have consciousness as you understand it, but they have purpose: to make the new rock old, to soften its sharpness into habitability. They are the first conversation.

Then come the migrants, the opportunists. The pale canines who have learned to read the weeping rock, knowing that where the stone exhales warmth, the shaggy herd-beasts will come to drink the mineral water, and where the herd-beasts gather, the moss will be cropped and the insects will swarm. These canines are patient geology made flesh—they hunt along the fracture lines, knowing the terrain is too young for ambush but too complex for chase. Their cough-calls are not territorial declarations but weather reports: *fog coming*, *herd moving east*, *ice forming on the northern face*.

The herd-beasts themselves are strange in their contentment. Their wide hooves grip the slick basalt not as an obstacle but as familiar ground. They have stopped being refugees from the lower territories and become Plateau Beasts, their shaggy coats growing denser in the condensation valleys, their digestion adapting to the silica-rich sedges. They move in patterns that mirror the fog banks, resting where the stone is warm, avoiding the cold hollows where snow accumulates and melts too quickly for foraging. They are learning the language of the exhalation.

In the marshes, the waterfowl have become philosophers of damp. Their conversational muttering is not random—it is a continuous calibration of nesting success against mineral crusting. They have learned that the slowly encrusting edges of sedge islands are dangerous (eggs get coated, sealed, fossilized in a season) and that the central, actively growing parts of the tussocks are safe. Their calls change subtly near the old edges, a higher pitch of warning. They are mapping the rate of mineralization through sound.

And the midges—those tiny, hovering economies. They are the true scholars of the condensation valleys. Each cloud is an information network, their swarming patterns shifting with minute changes in temperature and humidity, their feeding leaving traces on the mosses that other insects can read. The small birds that hover among them have learned to see these patterns as maps of warmth and damp, hunting not randomly but along the invisible contours of the microclimate.

Then, at the southern margin: the builders.

You notice them first by how still they stand. Not motionless—watching long enough, you see them move, bend, touch a wall—but they have a quality of stillness that matches the plateau's quiet. They are tall, but not unusually so; their bodies are layered in those muted mineral-colors, and their movements have a deliberation that might be cultural or might be adaptation to thin air and cold. They do not rush. The infrastructure anticipates, so they need not react.

Their settlement grows like crystal formation. When they say "stone conversation," they mean it literally: their technology is a form of listening and responding to the basalt's own tendencies. A wall is not built; it is *encouraged* along a natural stress line, the rock's own crystalline structure guided to interlock, to become load-bearing, to acquire internal channels that carry warmth or light or information. The subterranean levels are not excavated but *coaxed*—the rock given reasons to fracture downward in useful patterns, then stabilized once the space is formed. The city is a dialogue between intention and geology.

The builders themselves are shaped by this dialogue. They read the rock's weeping as you might read weather. A particular mineral crust on a seep tells them if the aquifer is shifting. The temperature of the condensation in a valley tells them if a new fracture is opening miles away. They perceive the plateau not as a static landscape but as a ongoing event—something that is still becoming—and they position themselves within that becoming. Their architecture grows because they never stop asking the stone to grow.

What moves through the streets is not so much a population as a pattern of occupancy. You see individuals going between low, curved buildings, but you also see entire families sitting quietly at the edges of a seep pool, their eyes focused not on the water but on the subtle changes in the rock face behind it. You see children—closer to the ground, quicker to notice the insects—taught to move without sharp sounds, because sharp sounds distort the water-sounds and the stone-sounds that are the settlement's true communications.

At dusk, when the fog rolls in and the pale canines cough their reports across the plateau, the builders light their homes. But "light" is not quite right. The walls themselves begin to glow—soft, warm luminescence emanating from the restructured basalt, tuned to the changing light outside. The settlement does not shine against the landscape; it glows from within it, a constellation of gentle fires in the stone, answering the last red reflections on the distant rock faces.

What lives here, in the end, is a collaboration across timescales. The moss and lichen work in decades. The herd-beasts and canines work in seasons and generations. The waterfowl and midges work in hours and minutes. The builders work in the moment and in the millennia simultaneously—their stone conversation listening to pressures that began millions of years ago and will continue millions of years hence, their daily movements shaped by the exhalation that happens now, this breath, this bead of water forming on fresh basalt.

They are all, in their ways, learning to speak plateau.
