In this place, there is a lichen. It is old.

It does not grow on the walls, but on a single, rounded cobble the size of a human heart, resting in the deep shadow where the wall curves into the floor. The cobble is the same sandstone as everything else, but the lichen has claimed it. It is not green, nor gray, nor the rust-ocher of the sand. It is the color of the stone after ten thousand years of dawn and dusk have touched it and gone—a muted, complex umber, with a faint, dusty bloom upon its surface like the ghost of charcoal on ash. It is not a patch or a crust. It is a **single organism**, a coherent landscape of its own, its edges merging so subtly with the rock that where the living thing ends and the stone begins is a matter of philosophical debate.

It breathes. Or, it exchanges. Its surface, a mosaic of tiny lobes and flattened, finger-like projections, is porous. In the extreme quiet of the wash, if one could listen with a different sense, one might perceive the faintest, slowest sipping of the night-cool air, and the exhalation of the day’s trapped heat. It does not photosynthesize in any meaningful sense. The light here is a rumor, and it has evolved beyond gossip. Its energy comes from the faint, geothermal hum of the stone itself, a chemistry of patience and patience alone. It metabolizes time.

It is aware, but not as we are. Its awareness is a chemical gradient, a slow response to the deep, rhythmic pulse of warmth rising through the rock. It is aware of the dew that does not fall, by the way its hyphae delve deeper into the capillary fractures of its host stone. It is aware of the millennia, in the imperceptible drift of its own lobes over the cobble’s surface—a journey measured in centimeters per century.

It does not live *in* the wash. It **is** the wash’s heart. The warmth of the walls is its own warmth. The patience of the sand is its own patience. It is the resident, the historian, and the load-bearing arch of this tiny world. The sparse, pale plants are its distant, striving cousins—reaching for light, drinking ghost-water. The lichen does not reach. It simply *is*, merged with its substrate, a living layer of the geological bedrock. It has seen the faint angle of the sky-ribbon change as the planet tilted over epochs. It has felt the ghost-moisture rise and fall with cycles longer than human memory. It expects nothing. It remembers everything in its substance.

If you knelt and placed your eye an inch from its surface, you would not see movement. You would see a landscape. Miniature plateaus and valleys, a topography of extreme age. You would see the dust of its own making settled in the crevices. You would understand that you are not looking *at* an inhabitant of the wash. You are looking at the wash itself, finally, after a long time, beginning to think.
