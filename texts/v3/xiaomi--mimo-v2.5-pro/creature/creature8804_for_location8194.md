They are not ghosts, though they move like them—economical, silent, weighted by purpose. They are the keepers of the pressure, the tenders of the fire in the drowned dark. You would call them a crew, but that implies a ship, a destination. They are more like organs of the mill itself, its circulatory and nervous systems made flesh.

**The Operators:** These are the core, the ones who have been here longest. Their skin has a permanent pallor, not from lack of sun, but from the sodium-vapor light that bleaches everything to a sickly yellow-white. They are etched with fine, silvery scars—old cuts from jagged metal, burns that healed into shiny patches. Their hearing is gone, replaced by a internal, constant hum they only notice when they surface (rarely) and find the world unnervingly quiet. They communicate through a pidgin of hand signals, written notes on waterproof slates, and a deep, intuitive understanding of the mill's own language—the pitch of a generator's whine, the specific groan of a wall under tidal stress. They do not look at you. You are a variable, a potential disruption to the balance. Their eyes are on gauges, dials, the shimmer of oil on water, the sweat beading on a pipe.

**The Runners:** Younger, leaner, more visibly nervous. They are the interface with the outside, the ones who meet the supply vessels at precise, hidden coordinates in the debris field above. They handle the dangerous ascent and descent through flooded corridors, wrestling with the infamous "wet locks"—chambers that flood to equalize pressure before opening to the next dry sector. They are guarded, their words clipped. They know the surface world only as a place of light, noise, and threat, a source of diesel, filters, and parts. They bring back rumors, too—of wars, of storms, of agencies looking for sunken things—which they pass on to the Operators in terse, whispered conferences by the thrumming generators.

**The Watchers:** Not all are human, or perhaps they are no longer *only* human. They reside in the upper, sealed levels, the ones you are told not to enter. You hear them, sometimes—a scrape above the ceiling tiles, a shadow that moves against the flicker of a light where no person stands. Those who work below do not speak of them, but their presence is a constant, low-grade anxiety. The Watchers are the reason certain doors are welded shut. They are the reason power is always routed upward, a silent tribute. Some say they are descendants of the chief engineer's paranoid inner circle, who never left. Some say they are something the geothermal warmth and the pressure and the deep-sea dark have *made*, a symbiosis of flesh and industry that monitors the factory's vital signs from within its walls. The laundry sways in the warm draft. A Watcher’s eye, or something like it, might glint from a ventilation shaft, patient and calculating.

**The Memory:** This is the truest resident. The memory of six thousand shift workers is in the worn smoothness of certain handrails, the ghost-steps in the stairwells, the faint, fossilized smell of wool oil that even diesel cannot entirely erase. It lives in the architecture itself, in the logic of the pressure vessels—a testament to a brilliant, paranoid fear. The current residents are stewards of this memory. Their work is not just to keep the water out, but to keep the *place* itself alive, to honor the colossal effort that built this defiant pocket of air. They are stubbornness incarnate, a human refusal to be erased by the sea. They do not hope for rescue or revival. They hope only for *tomorrow*, for one more turn of the generator, one more shift of equilibrium. They are the living callus on the wound of Vennock Deep, and in the roaring, shaking dark, they are exactly, terribly, beautifully themselves.
