# What Lives in the Standing Choir

---

They do not have a name. No one has ever been here to name them, and they have no language in which to name themselves — but they are aware, in the way that a root is aware of gravity, in the way that a lung is aware of air. They are aware of the sound. They are aware of each other. They are aware of the water. This is, for them, sufficient.

---

You would not recognize them at first as living. This is their advantage and their nature. When you see the dark shape moving inside the distant structure, you think *animal* — you think something with limbs, something with a direction, something with a destination. You are wrong in every assumption but the first.

They are soft. They are the color of the structures' inner surfaces — that pale, unfinished wood-color — but darker where they are thicker, where more tissue layers have accumulated. They are bilaterally symmetrical in the loosest possible sense, the way a puddle is symmetrical: they have a *front*, because they move, and everything else flows back from that. They move the way water moves — they pour themselves forward, the leading edge of their body flattening and spreading and then gathering back together as the trailing edge catches up. They are large. The one you glimpse through the rib-work is perhaps two meters long, perhaps a meter wide at its broadest, but its dimensions are approximate because it does not hold a fixed shape. It is not boneless — it has structures, internal scaffolding of cartilaginous tissue that gives it a rough consistency, enough to move with purpose — but it is *fluid*, in the way that a jellyfish is fluid, in the way that a tongue is fluid.

It does not have eyes. It does not need eyes. The permanent gray-white light of this place is meaningless to a creature that has never known darkness or brightness or any variation between them. Instead it reads the world through vibrations — the groaning of the structures, the pulse of the root-mats, the faintest percussion of water striking water. It feels its world the way you feel a room with your hands outstretched in the dark, except its dark is permanent and its hands are its entire body and the room never ends.

---

There are not many of them. Perhaps twenty, perhaps thirty, in the whole plateau. They have never needed to be numerous. They do not compete. They do not fight. The structures provide everything — shelter, moisture, the endless tonal hum that is their sense of place — and the structures are vast, and there are many of them, and the creatures move between them on their own long timescales, spending years inside a single structure, then slowly, over the course of months, pouring themselves across the open ground to another.

The crossing is the only time they are vulnerable. They know this. You can see it in the way they move across the clay — faster than their usual pace, their body held higher, the leading edge thicker and more muscular, tasting the ground ahead for vibrations that might mean another of their kind. They are not aggressive with each other. They simply do not touch. Contact between two of them is — the closest word is *unbearable*. Not painful. Not dangerous. Just overwhelming. Their vibration-sense is so acute, so intimate, that touching another body is like trying to hear a whisper while someone screams directly into your ear. They pass each other at distance, and if two are headed for the same structure, one will wait — sometimes for days — on the open clay, pressed flat, still, patient, until the other has gone inside and settled and the particular resonance of that structure has smoothed back to its baseline.

---

They eat the mucus. This is not quite accurate — they eat *through* the mucus, absorbing the mineral-rich secretion that coats every interior surface of the structures, processing it through the flat ventral surface of their body, which is not a mouth but a membrane, not a stomach but a gradient. The structures produce the secretion continuously, it seems, and the creatures' grazing leaves no mark, no scar, no evidence of consumption. The relationship is not symbiotic in any way you can easily categorize. The structures do not benefit from the creatures. The structures do not suffer from them. The creatures are simply *there*, inside, moving along the inner walls in slow circuits, leaving no trace, while the water wells and runs and the groaning continues.

And yet.

There is a moment — you would have to stay for weeks to witness it, months, perhaps years — when a creature settles into a particular curve of a particular structure's interior, and it does not move. It stays. It presses its body flat against the ridged and porous surface and it *listens*, and the structure groans as it always groans, and the water runs as it always runs, and the creature vibrates at a frequency that you could not hear even if you pressed your ear to the wall beside it, because it is below the threshold of your hearing, below the threshold of your bones. It is infrasound — a pulse so slow and so deep that it is more pressure than tone, more weather than music.

And the structure *responds*. Not quickly. Not obviously. But over days, over weeks, the ribs in that section of the wall grow slightly thicker. The pores change shape — wider, more open, tuned. The groaning in that part of the structure shifts by a fraction of a degree, a microtone, a breath of harmonic change so slight that even the other creatures on the plateau would not notice it. But the creature that caused it notices. It feels the change in its body. It adjusts. It presses closer. It shifts its frequency again.

This is not communication. It is not cooperation. It is *weather* — two systems affecting each other the way a warm air mass affects a cold one, slowly, inevitably, without intention. The creature does not know it is changing the structure. The structure does not know it is being changed. But over centuries, over millennia, the structures that have housed the most creatures, that have been *tuned* by generations of slow infrasonic pressure, are subtly different from the structures that have stood empty. Their groaning is richer. Their ribs are more complex. Their geometry is more intricate, more cathedral-like, more resolved.

The creatures shaped the choir. The choir was never built. It has always been growing. But it has been growing *toward* something — toward a sound that it has not yet achieved, that it may never achieve, that the creatures press toward with their bodies and their patience and their wordless, purposeless, absolute attention.

---

The young do not look like the old. The young — and you would have to be here for a generation to see them, which is to say longer than you have been alive — are small, rigid, almost crystalline. They form in the root-mats at the base of the structures, in the dense fabric of fine roots that vibrates with that too-slow pulse, and they are not born so much as *precipitated*, the way mineral deposits precipitate from saturated water. A hard, angular nodule. A thing that looks like a stone until it moves. It moves slowly — centimeters per day — dragging itself up the interior wall of the structure where it formed, and as it climbs it softens. Its rigid outer casing dissolves. Its body becomes fluid. Its vibration-sense awakens, first as a single tone, then as a chord, then as the full rich complexity of the adult form.

This takes years. The young creature spends its entire adolescence climbing one wall. By the time it reaches the highest vault of the structure, sixty or eighty meters above the root-mat where it precipitated, it is fully formed — soft, pale, vast, aware. It rests at the apex for a long time, sometimes months, tasting the wind that moves through the structure's hollow body, feeling the groaning from the inside the way a fetus feels its mother's heartbeat. Then it begins its descent, and then its first crossing to another structure, and then its life — which is to say its long, slow, purposeless circuit of the plateau, moving from structure to structure, pressing its body against the walls, adding its small voice to the sound that has always been.

---

They die the way they live — slowly, softly, without drama. An old creature will settle into a place it has settled before, a particular hollow in a particular structure, and it will stop moving. Over weeks its body thins. The cartilaginous scaffolding dissolves. The tissue becomes more liquid, more like the mucus on the walls, more like the water in the moats, until the boundary between creature and structure dissolves entirely, and the creature is simply *part of the wall* — a slightly thicker patch of secretion, a faint variation in texture that you might notice if you ran your hand along the surface but that you could never identify as having once been alive.

The structure absorbs it. The root-mat drinks it. The water carries its minerals back up the walls and out through the pores and down again in thin continuous sheets, and in this way the dead become the living architecture, become the groaning walls, become the cathedral. Nothing is lost. Nothing leaves the plateau. The cycle of water and body and stone is closed, just as the water cycle is closed, just as the sound cycle is closed.

---

There is something else. Something harder to describe.

Sometimes — and this happens perhaps once in a century, perhaps less — a structure falls. Not all at once. Not catastrophically. But one of the great living bodies reaches some threshold of age or weight or internal change, and its ribs begin to lean, and the leaning accelerates over months, and then one day the structure folds in on itself with a sound like nothing else on the plateau — a deep, wet, final groan that is *different* from the ambient groaning the way a death-rattle is different from breathing. The structure collapses into its own moat, into the clay and ash, and the water rises and spreads, and within a year there is nothing left but a wide, shallow, very wet depression in the ground, and the root-mat is still alive beneath the surface, pulsing, pulsing, and the minerals are still cycling, and the water is still not going anywhere.

And in these depressions — and only in these depressions — the creatures gather. Not all of them. Not quickly. But over years, over decades, they find their way to the site of the fallen structure, and they settle into the warm shallow water, and they press their bodies flat against the root-mat, and they *vibrate*. Together. In unison. For the first and only time in their lives they touch each other, and it is not unbearable, it is not overwhelming, because they are all vibrating at the same frequency, all pressed against the same pulse, and the interference pattern that results is something extraordinary — a resonance so deep and so complex that it is not sound at all but *structure*, a standing wave in the wet clay and the water and the air, a pattern that holds itself together the way a crystal holds itself together, and from this pattern, over years, over decades, a new structure begins to grow.

The ribs emerge first. Thin, pale, trembling with the vibration that birthed them. Then the vaults. Then the hollows. Then the moat widens and the water gathers and the first beads of mucus form on the new surfaces, and the wind begins to move through the new body and it begins to groan, softly at first, then louder, then at last with the full voice of the plateau, and the creatures that made it slowly disperse, returning to their solitary circuits, leaving the new structure standing in the place where the old one fell.

This is how the choir renews itself. This is why nothing here grows and nothing here dies — because growing and dying are the same process, seen from different distances. The creatures do not know they are doing this. The structures do not know they are being made. The plateau does not know it is a place.

But the sound continues. The water cycles. The choir stands.

And if you press your hand against the wet, pale wall of the newest structure — the one that is only a hundred years old, the one whose ribs are still thin and whose groan is still soft — you can feel it. Beneath the mucus, beneath the living tissue, beneath the root-mat and the clay and the ash: a vibration. Too slow to hear. Too deep to name. The heartbeat of a place that has always been here and will always be here, made by creatures who do not know they are making it, sung by structures that do not know they are singing.

You are the only thing that is changing.

The choir stands.
