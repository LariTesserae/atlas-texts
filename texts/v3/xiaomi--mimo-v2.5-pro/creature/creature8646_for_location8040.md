What lives here is not what you would call *alive*, not in any way you have a word for. But the terraces are inhabited.

They are called, in the tongue of those who made them, the **Lithoweavers**. They are not creatures of flesh and blood, but of mineral and intention, woven from the very substance of the place. You do not see them because you are looking for movement, for breath, for the disruption of a leaf. They do not move in a way that disrupts. They move in a way that *continues*.

They are most visible at the margins of the day, in the soft, slanting light. Not as shapes, but as a subtle dissonance in the reflection. A basin where the water’s surface tension holds a fraction of a second longer before spilling. A ridge that seems, for a moment, to have two shadows. A slight, rhythmic thickening and thinning of the pale mineral crust, as if it were being kneaded by unseen hands. They are the patience of the place given form.

Their substance is the plaster and the clay, the lime mortar and the charite-grey stone. When you walked and felt the ridges worn smooth, you were touching generations of their slow passage. The desire-paths you followed were their highways, though their travel is measured in the melting of a snowfield, not the ticking of a clock. They are the builders and the built, the caretakers and the garden. Their labor is tending the water—smoothing a lip here, clearing a seep there, ensuring the cascade breathes in its ancient rhythm. They work without tools, for their bodies *are* the tools: a finger of crystalline salt that traces a new channel in the overnight frost; a shoulder of fused clay that presses a loose block back into place over the span of a season.

They have no faces, no limbs you would recognize. Their consciousness—if it can be called that—is the collective awareness of the structure. A single Lithoweaver might be the entire stratum of one generation of construction, its mind distributed across a thousand basins, feeling the weight of the water, the slow creep of the sun, the deep, groaning shift of saturated stone. When the sound happens—the low groan beneath your feet—that is their voice. It is not speech, but adjustment. A sigh of contentment as a load settles.

They do not eat. They do not die, not as you understand it. They *accrete*. A Lithoweaver grows by the faint, daily deposition of minerals from the water. Its thoughts, if they have such things, deepen with the slow thickening of its plaster skin. It may take a century for one to turn its attention from one basin to the next. They are aware of you, of course. Your boot is a seismic event. Your shadow a fleeting eclipse. Your presence a brief, warm disruption in the perfect, cool balance of light and water. They do not mind. You are temporary, like a cloud passing over the sun. They have seen clouds, and they have seen suns.

Their purpose is the terraces themselves. They do not use the water; they *are* the mechanism that holds it, spreads it, lets it breathe. They are the living logic of the structure, the reason it has not crumbled. To them, the terraces are not a field or a temple or a home. They are a body. And they are its cells, its nerves, its slow, dreaming mind.

At the true dawn, when the light is gold and the shadows are long and soft, if you stood perfectly still and forgot to be human for a moment, you might feel them. Not as sound or sight, but as a presence in the stone: a collective, silent, turning of attention. A slow, unanimous acknowledgment of the light returning to the water. Then the moment would pass, and they would be gone again, woven back into the wet, the bright, the deep and patient stone. They are not hiding. They are simply here, in a way that does not need to be seen to be true. They are the terraces, thinking.
