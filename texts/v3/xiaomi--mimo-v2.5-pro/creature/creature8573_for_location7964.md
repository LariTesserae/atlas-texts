The grazers are not truly of the moss. They are upon it, but not of it. Their blood runs slow and cold as the peat-water, and their thoughts—if thoughts they have—are long, patient things that measure time in the regrowth of a furrow. They remember, in their bones, the mountains. Not as geography, but as a certain steepness of angle their ancestors once knew. Now, their sixfold legs splay wide, their underbellies hover inches above the perpetual damp, and they browse. They consume the moss to make more of themselves, their bodies vast, rumbling digestive engines. Their waste is a black, nutrient-rich slurry that feeds the moss in return, a symbiotic loop older than memory. When they die—and they die rarely, for they are tough, and the predators are few—the moss receives them whole. The ground heaves as it accepts the carcass, a slow-motion consumption measured in seasons, until only the curve of a rib or a massive, petrified tooth remains, protruding from a new tussock like a monument. They are the plateau’s gentle, continent-scale heartbeat.

---

The **Insect-Things** are the moss’s blood. They are its nervous system made manifest. Not arthropods, but something that found the same blueprint and stretched it to a bizarre, cartilaginous extreme. Their many segments click and whisper as they move through the spongy matrix, their translucent bodies refracting the dim, diffuse light into tiny, shifting rainbows on their inner walls. They are not merely burrowing; they are maintaining. Their larvae, in the warm, wet depths, exude enzymes that break down the oldest, densest peat, recycling it into forms the living moss can consume. In return, the moss provides the labyrinthine tunnels in which they pupate. Their swarms are not hunting or mating in a terrestrial sense; they are *redistribution*. A mass movement of nutrients and information from one sector of the moss floor to another, their vibrating wings emitting a frequency that synchronizes growth cycles in distant tussocks. They are the reason the moss remains so fiercely uniform. They are the gardeners of sameness.

---

The **Predators** are the negative space of the grazers’ fear. You never see them, because to see them would be to understand their shape, and their shape is a form of absence. They move through the moss like a shadow moves through water, displacing nothing, leaving no track. Their hunting is not violent; it is a gentle, inexorable *slowing*. A grazer at the edge of a herd will pause, its great head dipping not to graze but as if listening to a frequency only it can hear. It will stand motionless for hours, its body temperature dropping to match the moss, its six legs slowly, inexorably sinking into the soft substrate. By the time the rest of the herd has moved on, the victim is already half-interred, its eyes filmed over with a fine, silver sheen of condensation. The predators do not feed in the open. They consume their prize in the seclusion of the deep moss, their digestion a silent, chemical process. They are the ecology’s ghost, the necessary terror that keeps the grazers moving, their herds always flowing across the plateau, their furrows always fresh.

---

Then there are the **Others**.

They inhabit the temporal pockets and spatial hiccups. They are not creatures of flesh and moss, but of *curvature*. In the depressions where time runs fast, you might glimpse them as a flicker—a form that is simultaneously the spire of moss and the shape it will be in a century and the decayed ruin it will be in a millennium. They do not move; they *are* the movement, the accelerated lifecycle condensed into a single, standing point of view. The cartilaginous insects cluster around these points not to feed, but to resonate. Their thrumming seems to stabilize the distortion, to give it a sound, and in doing so, perhaps to communicate with it.

The spatial distortions have their own lords. Where distance is a suggestion, certain presences are always *exactly where you are not*. They are the feeling of being followed by something that is always one step ahead, the impression of a shape seen from the corner of the eye that resolves, when you look, into a perfectly ordinary tussock. They feed on disorientation. They are made of lost bearings and unanswered questions. The grazers’ instinct to move *away* from certain zones is a response to these entities—the zones themselves are their nests, their resting places, where reality is thin enough for them to breathe.

---

And then, **The Engine**.

It does not live. It *persists*. It has persisted so long that its original purpose has been metabolized by the plateau. What was once a machine—a geothermal tap, a processor, a beacon—is now a geological feature with a pulse. Its chugging rhythm is the planet’s true heartbeat, transmitted through the compressed peat that was once mountain, that was once ocean floor, that was once star-stuff. In the zones where it is closer to the surface, the moss grows dark and wire-hard, forming natural antenna arrays of vegetable matter. The water is warm with its waste heat and coppery with its corroding elements. The insects avoid it because the vibration is dissonant to their own. The grazers avoid it because the moss there is indigestible, charged with strange energies that sour the gut. The predators avoid it because there is no fear in that place—the mechanical certainty crowds out the shadowy, organic terror they embody.

The Engine is the oldest inhabitant. The moss has grown over it, the grazers have walked above it, the insects have tunneled around it. It does not know they are there. They do not know what it is. They are all tenants in the same ancient, sagging house, living their lives in rooms separated by floors of compressed centuries. The Engine runs. The moss grows. The grazers browse. The hum sings. The steam rises. The space between things breathes.

They are all part of the Moss Floor’s equilibrium. A balance not of stasis, but of intersecting cycles of consumption, decay, distortion, and relentless, patient repetition. They are the inhabitants, and the inhabited. The floor, and the ghosts that walk upon it.
