The city’s inhabitants are, first, the water itself—a resident that never rests, seeping and pooling and running in slow, meandering paths through every crack and channel. It is the oldest tenant, the one that shaped the land before anyone built upon it, and it remains, asserting its presence in the damp that settles on every surface, in the mist that never quite lifts, in the warm springs that hiss up from below. It is not passive; it moves, it rises, it evaporates and condenses in an endless, quiet cycle. It is the medium in which all other life here exists.

Then come the animals—the true denizens, the ones the city was built to harvest and live among. They are not pets or pests; they are the infrastructure, the economy, the texture of daily life. The crabs are legion, a thousand species hardened to the mineral waters, their shells ranging from paper-thin to plate-like, their claws adapted for crushing, for burrowing, for climbing. They clatter over the iron grating at night, a sound like slow, heavy rain. They burrow into the mud of the empty intervals between buildings, sometimes emerging in swarms that cover a whole block, only to retreat as the heat builds. In the factories, they are collected, sorted, processed—their oil lubricates engines, their shells become polymers, their meat feeds the workers. But they are also constantly present, nesting in the walls of housing blocks, scuttling under beds, found in sinks and drainage pipes. People coexist with them, shooing them aside, stepping around them, occasionally cursing when one pinches a child’s toe. It is a relationship of mutual necessity and weary tolerance.

The eels are slick and elusive, occupying every body of water—canals, drainage ditches, the standing pools in empty lots. They are long, muscular, and dark, with eyes adapted to the murky, mineral-tinted water. They hunt the smaller crabs and insects, and they themselves are hunted, caught in traps set in the canals, their flesh a staple food. Sometimes they find their way into the foundations of buildings, clogging pumps or causing short circuits when they wriggle into conduits. Workers in rubber boots and gloves deal with them, extracting them with practiced efficiency.

In the air, the insects are a constant presence. Heavy-winged flies drone through the mist, their bodies iridescent in the streetlamp corona. Mosquitoes and gnats breed in any standing water, and there are beetles and moths that are drawn to the heat of the factories, their wings beating against window panes. There are crawling insects in the mud—ant-like creatures that form vast, winding columns across the bare ground, and mites that infest the snails. The insects are food for the amphibians and the birds, but birds are scarce here; only a few species of rail or mudhen manage, their calls hoarse and intermittent, their nests hidden in the reeds that don’t quite grow, in the metal debris along the riverbanks.

The amphibians are the quiet observers. Pale, slick, with enormous dark eyes that seem to take in the low light, they huddle in the cool shadows: under pipes, in the hollows of stone walls, in the damp crawl spaces beneath houses. They are large—some the size of a human hand, others bigger—and they are mostly silent, except for occasional deep croaks at night. They feed on insects and small crabs, and they are left alone, considered good luck by some residents, who believe they eat the more troublesome pests. Their skin secretes mild toxins, so they are not eaten, but their presence is a sign that the environment is stable, that the damp is just so.

Snails are the slow decorators. Their shells, as large as fists, are a dull gray-brown, and they move with a deliberate, oozing grace. They climb everything: the walls of buildings, the legs of storage tanks, the drainage pipes. Their trails are silver streaks on every surface, shimmering in the faint light. They feed on the bacterial films that grow on warm, wet stone, and they themselves are eaten—their meat is considered a delicacy, their shells ground for calcium supplements. But they are also a nuisance, clogging gutters, weighing down roof edges, and leaving slime that has to be scraped away. Residents develop a habitual glance upward when walking, checking for a snail that might lose its grip.

In the warm pools, especially near the hot springs, colony organisms thrive. They look like mats of interlocking bodies, segmented and worm-like, sometimes pulsing gently. They are not fully understood, but they are known to filter the mineral-heavy water, and they are harvested for certain pharmaceutical extracts. They are avoided by children, who are taught not to touch them, as their secretions can irritate the skin.

The human inhabitants are a study in adaptation. They are not numerous for the city’s size—a few hundred thousand spread across a county’s worth of land—but they are concentrated in the housing blocks and the factory districts. They are a mix of generations: families who have been here for centuries, their skin often having a slight grayish pallor from the minerals in the water, their movements slow and deliberate to conserve energy in the heat. Newcomers arrive from other regions, drawn by work, and they are easy to spot—they sweat more, move faster, complain about the damp. But they adjust, or they leave.

They live in low, sturdy buildings with foundations sunk deep into the mud, often built on piles of packed earth or old stone. The lower floors are usually abandoned to the water and the animals, used only for storage or as workshops. Living quarters are higher up, but even there, the damp seeps in. Dehumidifiers hum in every room, their water tanks emptied daily. Walls are often tiled or lined with metal to resist the condensation. Furniture is minimal, functional, easy to clean. Windows are small, often cracked open to let out the heat but covered with fine mesh to keep out the insects.

Their work is the processing of the fauna. In the factories, they wear rubber aprons and gloves, their faces often protected by masks against the smells—sometimes fishy, sometimes sulfurous, always wet. They move with the economy described, knowing that haste leads to accidents: slipping on a wet floor, getting a hand caught in a press, disturbing a nest of crabs that might swarm. They work in shifts, the factories operating around the clock because the animals are always active, always breeding, always needing to be harvested.

At home, they eat what they harvest: crab cakes, eel stew, snail sausages, insects ground into protein flour. Their diet is rich and heavy, suited to the energy demands of the heat. They drink water that has been heavily filtered and mineral-adjusted, or they brew weak beers and tisanes from imported herbs that manage to survive in controlled greenhouses—though these are luxuries.

Their culture is shaped by the environment. There are festivals tied to the cycles of the animals—the Great Crab Run in early spring when the crabs migrate across the mud flats, the Snail Moon when the snails are most active and their shells are strongest. Art is often functional: pottery glazed to resist the damp, metalwork etched with patterns of eels and crabs, songs that mimic the drip and hum of the city. There is a quiet pride in living here, in mastering this difficult place. They speak of the “Delta Ways”—patience, attention to detail, respect for the water and the heat, a certain camaraderie born of shared hardship.

Children grow up knowing the animals intimately. They learn to identify the different crab species by their shells, to avoid the amphibians’ secretions, to watch for snails on overhead pipes. They play in the intervals between buildings, in the shallow mud, their games often involving catching and releasing small creatures. They are taught early that the city depends on this life, and that they are part of it.

The relationships between humans and animals are complex. On one hand, the animals are a resource, the reason for the city’s existence. On the other, they are a constant presence that must be managed, a force that can invade and disrupt. There is a grudging respect: the animals are tough, adaptable, and here long before the humans arrived. Some residents even keep certain animals as informal pets—a particular crab that patrols a doorway, an amphibian that lives in a basement wall. But mostly, it is a coexistence of necessity, a balance struck over generations.

The city’s rhythm is dictated by the environment. Work begins in the cool of the early morning, when the mist is thickest and the animals are most active on the ground. As the heat builds, activity slows; the midday hours are for resting, for maintenance, for indoor tasks. In the afternoon, when the heat peaks, the factories run on a skeleton crew, the workers moving even more slowly, taking frequent breaks in shaded, damp rooms. As evening comes and the light dims further, there is a second burst of activity—harvesting the animals that have emerged in the twilight, cleaning the day’s catch, preparing for the night.

At night, the city changes. The streetlamps glow with their yellow halos, and the mist becomes a moving, living thing. The sounds change: the crabs clatter more boldly, the eels splash in the canals, the insects swarm around the lights. The factories hum on, a constant bass note. Residents stay indoors more, their homes lit with soft, warm bulbs that don’t attract too many bugs. They eat their evening meals, listen to the radio, read, repair clothing or tools. Sleep is often shallow, interrupted by the drip of water, the scuttle of a crab, the distant hiss of a spring.

They are not unhappy. This is their home, the only life many of them know. There is a stability here, a settledness that comes from living in a place that has already reached its final form. The land is not changing; it is settled. The city is settled. And they, the inhabitants—human, crab, eel, snail, insect, amphibian, water—are all part of that settlement, each in their niche, each adapting, each enduring. The damp is the damp. The heat is the heat. And life goes on, slow and methodical, in the gray, wet, animal-rich delta.
