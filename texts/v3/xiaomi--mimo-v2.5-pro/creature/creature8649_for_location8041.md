The Drowned Throne Tablelands do not teem. They persist, and in their persistence, a world unfolds—a world of creatures so interwoven with stone and silence that they seem less like inhabitants and more like the land itself, thinking slowly.

## The Lithovores: Keepers of the Surface

They are the foundation, and they are legion. To call them “stone-eaters” is a simplification; they are the tableland’s breath, its metabolism. Each is barely larger than a thumbnail, its carapace a perfect mimicry of the sandstone it rests upon—ochre, cream, rust, depending on the layer. They have no eyes because sight is unnecessary; they navigate by texture, by the chemical gradient of bacterial films and mineral crusts invisible to larger senses. They rasp the stone with mouthparts of a crystalline hardness, not to consume the rock itself but to harvest the microscopic life that clings to it. The fine powder they leave behind is not waste; it is a contribution. Over millennia, this dust has mixed with rare rains to form a thin, nourishing grit that fills crevices and allows the moss to take hold. They move in slow herds that are not herds but aggregations—a silent, synchronized grazing that follows the sun’s path across the stone, seeking the warmth that encourages microbial growth. When condensation comes, they flatten themselves against the rock, absorbing moisture directly through their ventral plates. They are the reason the stone is so smooth, so polished; their constant rasping is the final stage of erosion, a biological sanding that complements the wind. They do not die of old age. They die when their carapace finally wears too thin, when they become part of the dust they once created. Their bodies are reabsorbed into the landscape within hours.

## The Stalkers: Patient Hunters

They move like thoughts—slow, deliberate, inevitable. Elongated, many-legged, their bodies segmented in a way that suggests a creature assembled from several blueprints and left to settle. They are the color of shadow, of the dark iron-rich substrate that surfaces in the deepest basins. They hunt the lithovores, but not with speed or ferocity. They hunt with patience. A stalker will spend a day moving across a single stone, following the faint chemical trail of a lithovore herd, its underside rippling with hundreds of hair-thin cilia that sense vibration. When it finds prey, it does not strike. It simply continues its approach until it is close enough to unfurl a set of delicate, pincer-like mouthparts from beneath its head and gently, almost tenderly, extract the lithovore from its hold on the stone. The motion is so slow it resembles grooming. They are solitary except during mating, which is a silent, days-long affair. Two stalkers will meet and circle each other in a slow, spiraling dance, their legs touching in complex patterns, until one deposits a small, hardened egg-case on the other’s back. The recipient carries it for months until it hatches into a miniature stalker that immediately disperses to find its own territory. They are not numerous; their population is carefully balanced with the lithovores. They are the keepers of the balance, the regulators of the base.

## The Breathers: The Living Sponges

They are the tableland’s secret, its great, soft, pulsing secret. Found in the basins where moisture gathers, they are not animals in any familiar sense. They are colonies of cells organized into a form that functions like a living aquifer. Their bodies are translucent, gelatinous, and almost entirely composed of water-filled sacs surrounded by a delicate lattice of protein fibers. They have no organs, no circulatory system, no nervous system to speak of—only a diffuse net of sensitive cells that allows them to contract when touched. They feed on moisture and something else, something in the air. They are not plants, but they perform a kind of respiration that is both inhalation and absorption. In the morning, when condensation is heaviest, they swell visibly, their translucence deepening as they draw water in. By afternoon, they are flatter, more opaque, as if holding their breath. They cluster in groups of dozens or hundreds, their bodies overlapping, and in these clusters, a microclimate forms. The collective absorption creates a region of higher humidity around them, which encourages the growth of the thin moss that carpets the basin floors. They are the reason the basins remain damp longer than they should. They reproduce by budding—a slow, geologic process where a small swelling on one’s surface gradually separates over years to form a new individual. They are indifferent to the lithovores that crawl over them and to the stalkers that avoid them. They are indifferent to everything. They are the tableland’s reservoir, its memory of water.

## The Gliders: Shadows on the Wind

They are not birds, though they fly. They are not bats, though they hang in the air. They are something older, something that has never needed to sing or call. Their wings are broad, membranous, and nearly invisible against the sky—a pale, dusty membrane stretched over elongated, finger-like bones. They ride the thermal updrafts that rise from the warm stone, circling the tableland at heights where they are mere specks. They descend rarely, and when they do, it is to the largest basins, where they land among the breathers with a careful, picking gait. They do not eat the breathers. They are attracted to the moisture. They will press their narrow, beak-like mouths against a breather’s surface and drink the condensation that clings there. They also scavenge: a dead stalker, a cluster of expired lithovores—anything organic. They are silent in flight and silent on the ground. Their wings make only the faintest whisper, a sound that is less heard than felt, like a sigh from the stone itself. They nest in the crevices at the very edges of the tableland, where the sandstone meets the ancient substrate, laying single eggs in hollows they line with dust and shed breather-skin. They are the tableland’s connection to the wider sky, its only ambassadors.

## The Unseen Weave

Beneath and between these, there is more. Micro-arthropods, smaller than a pinhead, that live in the dust produced by the lithovores, feeding on fungal spores that bloom only in the microscopic shade of quartz nodules. Mites that graze the lichen on the north faces of stones, their movements so slow they are almost imperceptible. Worms—not true worms, but elongated, soft-bodied things that burrow into the softer layers of sandstone, creating tunnels that allow air and water to penetrate deeper. They are the tableland’s decomposers, its recyclers. Nothing is wasted. A dead breather, over the course of a year, will be consumed by a succession of these tiny creatures until only a faint mineral stain remains.

## The Builders of the Circles

They are gone. But their ruins remain, and in those ruins, a shadow of them persists. They were not large. The size of the circular foundations—eight to ten feet across—suggests a species that valued shelter but not space. They were quiet, like the land they chose. They left no middens, no tools, no art. They built with the local stone, stacking it without mortar, using a precision that allowed the walls to settle into the landscape as if they, too, were eroding. They might have been bipeds, but more likely they were low to the ground, perhaps multi-limbed, able to move through the crevices as easily as the stalkers. They did not fear the breathers; the central slab in the largest basin is surrounded by breather clusters, suggesting a relationship of proximity, perhaps even of use. Did they harvest moisture from the breathers? Did they use the lithovore dust as a pigment or a paste? We cannot know. They left no bones because in this dry, consuming landscape, bones become dust in centuries. They left only circles—shapes that mimic the basins, the breather clusters, the pattern of life itself. They were not conquerors. They were guests who stayed for a time and then left, leaving the tableland unchanged except for the faint suggestion that stone can be shaped by more than wind and water.

## The Whole

To stand on the Drowned Throne Tablelands is to stand in the middle of a conversation held in a language of stone and moisture and time. The lithovores speak in rasps and dust. The stalkers speak in vibration and patient approach. The breathers speak in slow pulses of water. The gliders speak in the whisper of wings. And beneath it all, the ruins speak in the silence of circles. It is a community of mutual dependence and mutual ignorance, each part too slow to recognize the others as anything but landscape. The quiet is not empty; it is full of activity so measured, so ancient, that it has become the rhythm of the place itself. Life here does not fight for survival. It agrees to persist, and in that agreement, it has become eternal.
