The residents of The Throats are not inhabitants so much as expressions of it. They are the living, breathing, pulsating texture of a place that refuses to be static. To describe them is to describe the cave's very metabolism.

The **lamphangers** are the visible rhythm. Their bioluminescence is not for show but is a form of echolocation, mapping the constant shifts in their world with pulses of cold light. They are grazers of geothermal energy, their translucent bodies acting as conduits, slowly processing the chemical warmth rising from below. Their independent pulsing creates a shifting, disorienting lightscape—a language of light where sudden flaring means falling rock, and a collective dimming means the passage of something large in the water below. They are the first colonizers, clinging to raw rock the moment it stops moving, and their dying, shed skins form a soft, nutrient-rich sediment that feeds the next layer of life.

The **eyeless beetles** are the percussionists and the true foundation. Their heavy, mineral-ite carapaces are not armor against predators (for they have none truly specialized) but against the abrasive environment. They click and rasp over stone, consuming microscopic bacterial films that grow on every warm, wet surface. Their rhythmic soundscape is the cave’s background radiation, a constant reassurance of normalcy. When a new collapse occurs, their scouts arrive within hours, a clicking vanguard probing the new territory.

The **eel-forms** are the middle managers of the aquatic realm. They are not true eels, but elongated, muscular clusters of sensory tissue and snapping, circular mouths. They spiral in the warm, mineral soup not just to hunt, but to stir it. Their movements oxygenate the water and bring nutrients from the silt into the water column, feeding the gelatinous filters and the great flat things. Their gasping at the surface is not always for air; it is often to snag a low-flying lamphanger that strayed too close, creating a sudden flash of stolen light.

The **flat things** are the substrate and the apex. They are less an animal and more a mobile, predatory geology. Slow, broad, and textured like layered mudstone, they glide through the lakes and deeper pools. Their undersides are a living lacework of cilia and digestive membranes. They consume everything—dead lamphangers, beetle husks, silt, eel-forms, the occasional unlucky explorer of the gelatinous pseudopods. They do not hunt so much as *absorb*. Their wakes are the main architects of the cave floors, scouring silt into dunes and pushing rubble into new configurations. They may also be very old. A large one, its surface pocked with the embedded shells of consumed crustaceans, might be a living record of the chamber’s history.

The **gelatinous pseudopods** are the cave’s nervous system and its first responders. Seeping from every crack, they are a distributed intelligence of simple, reactive cells. They sense chemical changes—fresh water, decay, stress hormones from distressed lamphangers, the mineral signature of a newly fractured wall. They retract to safety or extend toward opportunity. They heal the cave in their own way, secreting a silica-based mucus that can temporarily stabilize loose rubble, buying time for the beetles and lamphangers to establish a new foothold.

They all exist in a state of **competitive symbiosis**. There is no peace here, only a frantic, interconnected urgency. A lamphanger dies, and before its light fully fades, the beetles are upon it. A beetle falls from the wall, and an eel-form spirals to catch it mid-descent. An eel-form is too slow, and a flat thing absorbs it silently. The gelatinous pseudopods mop up the waste and the lingering chemicals, their secretions triggering new bacterial blooms for the beetles. It is a perfect, self-feeding loop powered by the earth's own heat.

The **human traces** are a profound mystery to this system. The crude cairns and walls are simply *shape* to them—new angles for lamphangers to cling to, new surfaces for beetles to graze, new foundations for silt to build upon. They are not relics but raw material. The evidence suggests a visitation, a brief attempt to impose order—perhaps to map, perhaps to shelter, perhaps to *understand*—that was ultimately overwhelmed not by hostility, but by the sheer, indifferent, thriving pace of The Throats themselves. The humans did not flee an attack; they were likely simply outgrown. Their structures are being digested by the environment, slowly reclaimed by the same processes that build and break the stone. They are not an anomaly. They are just another fallen slab, another temporary feature in a world that is perpetually, furiously, becoming.
