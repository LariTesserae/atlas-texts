The Fenmouth breathes, and its breath is slow, damp, and full of creatures.

They do not "live" in the Fenmouth as one lives in a house. They are the Fenmouth. They are its pulse, its rustle, its watchful silence. To describe them is to describe the walls moving, the water rippling, the darkness itself gaining texture and purpose.

**First, the Foundation: The Rustling Mass.**
The arthropods are the bedrock. They are the constant sound, the omnipresent motion. Imagine the moss on the north wall, a shaggy, green-black curtain. Now see it not as a curtain, but as a river—a slow, silent, inexorable current of bodies. Pale, segmented, each no larger than your thumbnail, they flow. They flow upward to harvest the dying tips of the moss. They flow downward to process it in the saturated mat. They flow sideways, crossing the paths of their countless kin without collision, a mindless, efficient network of consumption. Touch the wall and the river parts for your fingers, a ripple of withdrawal that reforms instantly. They are not afraid. They are merely… accommodating. They are the ecosystem's primary engine, turning decay into life, their collective movement a soft, dry whisper that is the true sound of the Fenmouth's silence.

**Second, the Sentinels: The Watching Ones.**
Above the river of arthropods, perched on ledges, clinging to pale stems, sit the amphibians. They are statues of wet stone. A salamander, its skin the grey-white of old basalt, lies draped over a knot of moss, its large, black eyes reflecting the faint green glow of a fungal patch. A frog, no bigger than a walnut, sits motionless on a translucent leaf, its throat pulsing so slowly you might mistake it for a trick of the dim light. They wait. They are the still points in the rustling stream, gathering the focus of the dark. And then—a blur, a flicker too fast for the eye to truly catch. A tongue, pale and whip-like, darts out. One of the arthropods from the river vanishes. The sentinel remains, still as stone once more. Their patience is the counterpoint to the arthropods' restlessness. They are the eyes of the Fenmouth, a thousandfold gaze that sees every shift in the living tide.

**Third, the Pulse of the Deep: The Blind Swimmers.**
In the lagoon, the water is black and faintly luminous with drifting algae. Here, the eels move. They are not fish. They are something older, something smoother. Pale, sinuous, blind or nearly so, they glide just beneath the surface, leaving temporary galaxies of bioluminescence in their wake. When one rises to breathe (for they do breathe; you can hear it, a soft, wet *gulp* that echoes in the quiet), the entire system reacts. The frogs on the walls freeze mid-tongue-flick. The river of arthropods on the nearby wall seems to congeal, slowing to a crawl. The wrens in the upper dark hold their song. The eel is the regulator, the apex, the moment of breath-held silence in the constant, quiet activity. Its presence is a rule. Its movement dictates the rhythm of every other life here.

**Fourth, the Whisperers: The Winged Shadows.**
They are the closest thing to birdsong the Fenmouth knows: a soft, churring call, like two dry sticks rubbed together. The wrens are small, dark, and fast. They nest in crevices near the rim, where the light is marginally less dim. They descend in cautious flurries to the middle levels, their wings rustling the leaf-membranes. They hunt, but not randomly. They come only when the eels are deep, when the amphibians are less vigilant. They pick arthropods from the stems with delicate precision. They are the most aware of the outside world; you sometimes see one perched on the highest ledge, its head cocked, listening to the vast, empty silence of the plain above, as if remembering a different kind of life.

**Fifth, the Gardeners: The Symbionts.**
This is where the distinction between creature and place dissolves completely. Watch the bulbous, pale growths at the lagoon's edge—the soft, pulsing fists of tissue. Certain arthropods, slightly larger, with blunt mandibles, live on them exclusively. They do not devour the growth; they tend it. They meticulously clean its surface of fungal spores and debris. In return, the growth weeps a clear, viscous fluid that the arthropods feed upon. They are caretakers, and the growth is their living architecture and their source of life. They are a single, slow-metabolizing entity. The plants are not separate. The translucent membranes are hosts to micro-arthropod communities that fight infection. The fungal threads in the moss mat are highways for nutrient exchange, aided by the burrowing of the larger arthropods. Life here is not a collection of species. It is a single, sprawling, interdependent network, a biofilm of wet darkness.

**Finally, the Inhabitants: The People of the Dry Place.**
And among all this, within it, part of it, are the humans. They are short, broad, wrapped in cloaks of woven leaf-membrane that shimmer faintly with embedded bioluminescent algae. They move with a quiet economy, their footsteps muffled on the living mats. They know the rhythms instinctively. They fish the lagoon when the eels are in the deep water. They harvest the larger arthropods from the walls with quick, gentle hands, leaving the tenders of the growths undisturbed. They add dried peat to their lamps, and the dim, smoky glow joins the green fire of the moss and the blue-white of the fungi. Their voices are low. They speak of the "growing," of the slow deepening of the pocket, the rising water.

They are not masters here. They are fellow tenants in a living house that is constantly rebuilding itself around them. Their tools grow moss. Their walls sprout new growth. They are watched by the amphibians, stepped over by the arthropods, occasionally startled by the silent surfacing of an eel. They have their own dry place, but they know, with a bone-deep certainty, that the true dry place is a story they tell themselves against the damp, the dark, and the breathing, teeming, indifferent abundance of the Fenmouth. They are part of its fauna. The most complex, the most aware, and perhaps the most transient of its inhabitants.
