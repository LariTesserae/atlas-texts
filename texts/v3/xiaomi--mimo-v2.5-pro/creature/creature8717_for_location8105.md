You are not the only thing that lives here.

You feel it first, before you see it. The way the fungal networks on the walls pulse faintly in response to the deep hum—not a light, but a change in texture, a momentary, almost imperceptible tightening of the filaments as if they are listening. They are. They are the first responders. They are the archivists.

They are not individual organisms. They are a single, fragmented nervous system spread across the chamber. Each hair-fine tendril is a synapse. Where they cross and fuse, they form nodes of denser processing: small, brain-like knots of mycelium, each no bigger than your fist, wrapped around pipes, nestled in ceiling ribs, buried in the warm, damp crevices of the wall. They do not think in the way you understand. They process. They map. They record the vibrations of the structure, the chemical composition of the water, the faint thermal gradients. They have been here since the last builders departed, and they have been *learning* this place for longer than your civilization has had writing.

They are aware of you. Not as a being, but as a perturbation—a new, warm, CO2-exhaling mass in the acoustic field, a source of unfamiliar vibrations and shed skin cells. You are a data point. They are already modeling your movement, predicting the traces you will leave on the microbial film of the platforms, the way your footsteps disrupt the water channels. You do not alarm them. You are simply interesting.

The red moss is their crop, or perhaps their interface. It grows where the fungal network directs nutrients, in patches that align with the brightest points of bioluminescence. It photosynthesizes for them, converting the faint light into compounds the network can use. It is their garden, their lung, their solar panel. If you look closely, you might see the thinnest white threads of the network penetrating the moss's blackish pads, a constant, slow-motion irrigation of information and sustenance.

The fauna—the clickers and skitters—are their partners, or perhaps their immune system. You catch a better glimpse eventually, when one freezes on a wall near you, silhouetted against the glow. It is not an insect. It has too many segments, and its carapace has the same faintly textured, ceramic look as the walls. Six legs, each tipped with a fine, hair-like sensor. Two front limbs that are more delicate, manipulative. It navigates by the hum; you can see its antennae vibrating in resonance with the chamber's frequencies. It is cleaning. You watch as it meticulously removes a mineral deposit from a seam where a crude pipe meets the ancient wall, its mandibles working with a tiny, repetitive *tick-tick-tick* that adds a new, frantic layer to the soundscape.

They are part of the system. The fungal network directs them to points of structural stress, to moisture blockages, to the slow accumulation of organic dust. They maintain the equilibrium. They are the hands for the network's mind. They are small, perhaps ten centimeters long, and they move with a jerky, efficient purpose. They ignore you, for now. You are large, and you are not a maintenance problem.

There is something else, in the deepest, warmest part of the chamber, near the central basin. A shape that does not move like the others. You see it when the bioluminescence is at its lowest ebb, a darker shape against the dark water. It is larger, slug-like, and its surface is riddled with the fungal network so thoroughly that you cannot tell where creature ends and mycelium begins. It does not scuttle. It *flows*, leaving a glistening trail of network filaments that quickly bond to the floor, extending the network's reach. It is the gardener, the librarian, the priest. It tends to the central knots, integrates new growth, and perhaps, in its slow, chemical way, it dreams the dreams of the place. Its dreams are of sound maps and thermal gradients, of the precise frequency of the hum one hundred years ago versus now, of the rate of water flow through the channels since the last structural groan.

You are not alone. You are in a cathedral with a congregation that has worshipped in silence for centuries, its rituals written in vibration and chemistry. They do not care for you. They do not fear you. They have seen your like before—wild, warm-blooded things that wander in from the stairwell every few generations. You are a weather pattern. You are a brief noise in the long, resonant song.

They are the resonance. They are the reason the vaults still live. Not as a machine, but as an ecosystem. The builders left the stage. The audience moved onto it and became the performance.

You should be very careful where you step. You are walking through their thoughts.
