They call themselves the **Stillwater,** though rarely aloud. The name is a quiet understanding, like the city itself. They are not natives in the traditional sense—this place has no natives, only first arrivals who arrived thirty years ago and chose to stay as the seafloor became stone beneath their feet. Now, their children and grandchildren walk the damp earth with the same peculiar gait, a low-center-of-gravity glide, as if perpetually bracing for a gentle, subsiding wave that never quite comes.

**Their senses are tuned differently.** To them, the dim pewter light is not dim; it is complete. Their eyes have adapted, pupils often wide, making their gazes seem soft, absorbing. They see the borrowed luminescence of the flowers and the building-skin as the primary light source—the sky’s pallor is merely a canvas. Silence is not absence; it is the baseline hum of the world working. They can hear the faintest *sigh* of a building adjusting its molecular tension, the whisper-thin trickle of a seep-channel finding a new path, the near-inaudible pulse of the ground’s slow breathing. These are their weather reports, their news, their music.

**They move with economy and intention.** There is no hurrying here. The city’s great intervals between structures demand a gait meant for endurance, not speed. You see them pausing frequently, not to rest, but to listen with their feet—to feel through their shoes (which are thin-soled, almost a second skin) the subtle heave or settle of the ground. A pause on a bridge over a seep-channel might last minutes as they gauge the water’s newly chosen direction. This stillness is active, a state of deep consultation with the world.

**Their technology is their partner, not their tool.** The coherence engineering that forms the buildings is not something they operate; it is something they *converse* with. The gestures they make toward the vegetation are not commands but suggestions, impulses sent through the same embedded systems that make the walls ripple. They might trace a finger in the air before a climbing tendril, and the plant will slowly retract, coiling upon itself in a sigh of opalescent gray. They do not build or repair; they *persuade*. A wall that has become too opaque might be gently *reminded* of its transparency by a resident standing close, palm flat against its warm, skin-like surface, until it yields. They talk to their city in a language of intent and material response.

**Their society is one of stewardship and gentle calibration.** There are no leaders in the traditional sense, but there are **Tendrils**—individuals with a particular sensitivity to the drift of the vegetation or the moods of the ground. They might be found standing for hours in a district where the plants have surged, humming a low, atonal note that seems to settle the growth back into a less aggressive pattern. Knowledge is passed through proximity and practice, not in classrooms. A child learns the rhythms of a seep-channel by watching an elder navigate it over seasons, their feet reading the silt.

**Their relationship with the strange fauna is one of quiet coexistence.** The pale, scent-navigating insects are considered more kin than pest; their paths across a luminous wall are tracked with mild curiosity, as they often indicate subtle shifts in the building’s energy output. The translucent, six-legged amphibians in the channels are revered as oracles of the water table—their appearance or disappearance signals changes in the land’s hydration that even the engineers might miss. To harm one is unthinkable. The distant, circling birds are seen as emissaries of the older, airier world beyond the pewter sky; their aloofness is understood and respected.

**Their culture is permeable, like their ground.** Art is not hung but *cultivated*—patterns of bioluminescent moss coaxed onto a building’s face, or a room where the walls are trained to slowly shift hue over a week in response to collective mood. Stories are not just told but *replayed*: a resident might lead you to a particularly responsive wall, lay a hand upon it, and the surface will ripple into abstract, glowing shapes that evoke the feeling of the city’s last great *hiccup*, the collective sense-memory of the ground rising and falling beneath them. Music, if it exists, is the city’s own quiet symphony: the drip, the sigh, the deep, subsonic pulse of the land compressing.

They are a people in conversation with a place that is still becoming itself. They do not feel they live *on* the Littoral Terminus. They feel they live *with* it, in a slow, deep, and perpetual negotiation. Their defining trait is a profound patience, born of understanding that they are not the masters of this new land, but its first and most attentive listeners. To be Stillwater is to accept the ground’s uncertainty as a fundamental law, and to find in that acceptance not anxiety, but a kind of damp, luminous peace.
