The inhabitants of Oorat are not a single species, but a conversation held in stone, wax, dust, and time. To speak of who lives there is to list the participants in a slow, symbiotic monologue.

**First, the Foundational Inhabitants: The Stone and the Air.**
The true oldest residents are the terraced walls and the basin of still, warm air. They are the landlords, the architects, the very medium of life. The stone breathes, exhaling its geothermal warmth and the mineral memory of ancient water. The air, trapped and heavy, is not empty but saturated with the dust of their own being—the powdered basalt of eons. Everything else is a tenant, a guest, a dependent adaptation to the terms set by this ancient duo.

**Then, the Silent Builders: The Flora.**
They are the engineers of water in a waterless world. The pale succulents, their rosettes like handprints pressed in prayer against the rock, are the hydrologists, plumbing the stone’s secret veins. The translucent, gel-filled epiphytes are the alchemists, turning the slightest temperature shift and the faintest mineral dew into life. The lichens are the patient soil-makers, weathering the rock grain by grain over centuries into the faintest possibility for other roots. The rubbery vines are the weavers, their architecture a lattice of survival that offers shade, holdfast, and structure. They do not merely grow; they perform a continuous, slow-motion miracle of conservation, a green chorus singing in a frequency of deliberate stillness.

**The Choral Inhabitants: The Fauna.**
Their lives are set to the rhythms of the garden and the acoustic bowl.
The **birds** are the musicians and the timekeepers. Their dawn chorus, erupting in darkness, is not just song but a form of calendar, a tuning of the caldera to the coming day. They are also the sanitation crew, policing the insect populations of the terraces. Their nests, tucked into every crack and ledge, are a testament to the trust between species; they are tolerated tenants in the people’s architecture.
The **insects** are the pulse, the hum in the walls. The beetles are the pruners and the pollinators, the ants are the lichen farmers. They are the essential, often overlooked, middle management of the ecosystem, turning the slow work of the plants into faster energy for others.
The **bats** are the night-shift custodians. Their audible ticking, a dry insectile sound, is the rhythm of the caldera’s nocturnal breath. They circle the same small sky all their lives, a perfect, contained orbit.
The **lizards** are the ubiquitous audience, the visible measure of time. Their scuttle is the background noise of the stone, their stillness the pause between movements. They are the sentinels of the terraces, the clearest proof that the caldera is a world teeming with intention, even in its smallest, coldest-blooded residents.

**The Cognizant Inhabitants: The People of Oorat.**
They are the students, the librarians, the ceremonialists of this world. They are not its masters but its most observant participants. Their technology is a direct translation of the caldera’s language: their tools are from its bone (basalt), their cordage from its tendons (vine fiber), their mortar from its dust and skin (lichen and rock powder). Their settlement is not an imposition on the landscape but a continuation of its logic, a nesting that mimics the lizards and the birds.
Their culture is one of **adapted perception**. They mark time not by arbitrary years, but by the settlement’s own geologic evolution—“the time of the tall eastern wall.” They have a specialized sense of hearing, attuned to the subtle signal of dew meeting stone, the only true alarm clock. They rise in the dark not to conquer the day, but to receive the night’s offering. They are masters of patience, understanding that a harvest may take a generation, that a wall is a pact between the builder and the unbuilt future.
Their presence is warm, slow, and synergistic. They hunt the birds with respect, manage the insects with foresight, ignore the bats with wisdom, and snare the lizards with practiced economy. Their smoke and musk have joined the ancient scent of the air, making it subtly, recognizably human-tinged—another layer in the atmosphere of a very old home.

**Finally, the Inhabitant That Is All of Them: The Hollow Itself.**
Oorat is, ultimately, a single, slow organism. The plants are its green lung, filtering and storing. The stone is its skeleton and its memory. The air is its blood, thick and rich. The animals are its nerves and its voice, carrying signals and making sound. The people are its consciousness, its sense of itself, the part that looks down into its own depth and knows, in a non-verbal way, that it is whole.
It does not “house” life. It *is* life, expressing itself through mineral and verdant and animal and human forms. To live in Oorat is not to live *in* a place, but to be a functioning cell within its ancient, breathing body. They are all, together, the hollow’s way of being alive.
