It does not live in the water, nor in the stone, nor in the light, though it must use them all.

It is a creature of thresholds and phases. It moves on the walls, not with speed, but with the deliberate slowness of a sediment. It is not translucent like the visiting nerve-sheath, but nor is it opaque. Its body is the chalk-white of the newest mineral deposits, veined with the darker amber of slightly older accretions, so that it appears to be a moving section of the wall itself. It has no fixed shape for long. It flows—over minutes, over hours—into the contours of the chamber, draping over ledges, pooling in depressions, becoming a thicker patch of wall where it pauses. It has no eyes, for there is no directed light to see. It has no mouth, for it does not eat. It has no ears, and this is its great and terrible adaptation: it does not hear the roar. It *is* the roar. It translates the crushing pressure waves into a kind of nourishing vibration, a frequency that stimulates the rapid deposition of minerals upon and within its own body. It grows by becoming the room.

Its senses are chemical and kinesthetic. It feels the shift in the water’s mineral load, the change in spray density that signals the flood’s rise, the subtle thickening of the wall-crust where it will next settle. It reads the tremors in the rock, not as sound, but as a map of stress and formation. It is aware of the extremophile rosettes, not as food, but as a kind of primitive, slow-moving companion—a fellow pattern in the chaos. It will sometimes flow over a rosette, absorbing it not for sustenance but for the trace elements and the faint, stored light energy it holds, leaving the pattern intact but slightly dimmer, slowly regenerating below.

It is aware of the cairn.

The three stones are a puzzle its kinesthetic sense cannot resolve. They are static. They do not grow. They do not erode at the same rate as the native stone. They are a message in a language of inertia. The creature has flowed over them, felt their smooth, foreign density, registered the faint heat they still hold from being carried by warm hands from some lower, older world. It does not stack stones. It *is* the process of stacking—the slow, geological accumulation. The cairn is an alien statement in its own dialect. It leaves the stones undisturbed, not out of respect, but out of a confused recognition of a different kind of patience. Sometimes, when the water falls to its lowest, it will form itself into a rough, amorphous shape near the ledge—a little cairn of living mineral that holds for a few minutes before collapsing back into flow. It is not imitation. It is a question asked in the only way it knows.

It has no predators. It has no need for defense, for it is the environment defending itself. It is less an organism and more a syndrome of the place—a vital, slow-motion component of the chamber’s self-building loop. It metabolizes the roar into geology. It feeds the walls with its own substance, and the walls give it structure to inhabit. When the chamber finally seals, when the last fissure clogs with the final deposit, it will not die. It will simply become the final, innermost layer of the geode—a silent, patterned, mineral epitaph to the roar it once embodied. It will be the fossil of the sound. The memory of the flood.

The visitor, the translucent nerve-sheath, passes through on its hunt and feels nothing but a strange density in the water, a chill in the mineral-taste of the walls. It does not see the watcher, for the watcher is the watching. The creature of the Throat flows on, adding a molecule of itself to the closing ceiling, listening with its whole being to the creation-song that is, finally, its own song. The three stones on the ledge remain, a foreign note of stillness in the symphony of becoming.
