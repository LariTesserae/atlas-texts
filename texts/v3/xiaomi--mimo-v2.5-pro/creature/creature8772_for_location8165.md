The people of The Sunk do not have a name for themselves, or if they do, it is not a word shared with outsiders. They are simply the ones who are here. They are lean, not with hunger, but with the particular spareness of bodies that move carefully and constantly on uneven ground. Their skin is often damp; their hair is rarely fully dry. They wear layers of undyed wool and treated hide, cut close and practical, the colors of the basin itself—stone-grey, shale-dark, the bleached brown of old moss.

They are a people of quiet industry. Their hands are their primary tools: broad-palmed, often stained with clay or rust, capable of intricate work. They weave nets for fungal mats, chink walls with astonishing speed, and know the precise pressure needed to split stone shingle without shattering it. Their tools are few, simple, and maintained with obsessive care—a bone awl, a stone axe, a bronze-bladed knife is a prized heirloom. Every tool has a specific place on the body or on a low wall; nothing is ever left where it might trip someone or be swallowed by the shifting silt.

Their social structure is built on the logic of the immediate. There are no obvious leaders. Authority resides with those who know things: the elder who can read the seep-lines and predict where a wall will weep next season; the matriarch who oversees the fungal caves and understands their fickle cycles; the builder who can judge the stability of a newly fallen rubble fan. Decisions are made in low-voiced circles, often while performing another task—repairing a net, re-chinking a foundation. The currency is competence and reliability. A person’s worth is measured by their contribution to the wall: can they shore up a leaning structure? Can they navigate the braided stream in its new course without getting stuck? Do they have extra dried roots to trade?

Their days are governed by the drip and the light. Work begins with the first clear drip after dawn, and ceases as the shadows from the eastern wall swallow the basin floor. The rhythm is not frantic, but relentless. A constant, gentle redistribution of resources and repair of damage. After a rockfall, there is no mourning for the lost structure; there is immediate, practical salvage and rebuilding. The rubble is sorted—larger stones for new walls, gravel for fill, interesting shards collected for tools or trade. The stream’s shift is not a disaster, but a prompt to redesign water channels and move the lowest-lying drying racks.

Their spiritual or inner life is difficult for an outsider to discern. They are not demonstrative. There is no visible worship, no obvious shrines. Yet, their relationship with the stone is profound and practical, bordering on the personal. They touch the walls. They speak to the seeping water in murmurs, not as prayer, but as negotiation. They leave small, perfectly wrought artifacts—a tiny woven basket, a polished pebble with a vein of iron-oxide—in the deepest, most stable joints of the rock, not as offerings to a god, but as acknowledgements to the place. They are saying: *We see you. We know you are here. We will be careful.* It is the etiquette of cohabitation with something vast, indifferent, and young.

They have stories, told in the deepening dusk as mist pools on the floor. These are not grand myths of creation, but practical parables, memories held in stone. “The Year the Stream Ran Clay” is a cautionary tale about complacency. “The Collapse of the Western Terrace” is a lesson in recognizing load-bearing seams. Their songs are rhythmic chants used to coordinate work, to pull timber in unison, to pace the walk along narrow ledges. The melodies are simple, repetitive, absorbed into the constant percussion of the drip.

Children learn to move before they learn to speak. They play not in open fields, but in the narrow, practiced space of the settlement—learning to leap the gaps between stones, to balance on a wall, to listen for the tell-tale scrape that precedes a small slide. They are not coddled; they are integrated. A child old enough to carry a waterskin is old enough to carry water. They are taught respect for the edges—of stone, of falling water, of unsteady ground.

They are wary, but not hostile. A visitor, should one ever stumble down into the basin, would be watched with silent, intense curiosity. Their clothes, their tools, their loudness would be noted. They would be given space, not out of kindness, but out of caution. Space is the most precious resource here. The people of The Sunk would trade for outside metal or preserved food, but they would give nothing away easily. Trust, like soil, is built slowly, in careful, contained plots.

They do not wish to leave. The surface, to them, is a place of terrifying emptiness, of unchecked wind and uncontained light. The basin is a container, a womb, a known geometry of pressure and support. It is a hard life, but it is a life with clear and immediate consequences. Every action matters. Every wall maintained, every channel cleared, every fungal mat tended is a direct transaction with survival. There is no abstraction here, only the solid, damp, dripping reality of stone and work and the slow, accumulating habit of being. They are not the first to live in such a place, but they are the ones who are here, now, pressing their lives against the raw face of the world that gave way.
