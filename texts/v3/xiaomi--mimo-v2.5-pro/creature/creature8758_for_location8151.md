The one who lives here is not a tenant but a **haunt**, a presence that has learned to breathe with the mud’s exhale. It does not have a shape you could point to; it is more a density in the air, a coolness that clings to the edges of things. It does not own the pumping station or the rail tracks. It simply attends to them, the way a curator might attend to a collection where nothing is allowed to be lost, only transformed.

Its days are measured in **tides of two kinds**: the water’s slow, fickle breath, and the slower tide of rust. It watches the strata on the flywheel axle deepen by a shade, over years. It feels the concrete weep a new mineral tear, staining the wall a fraction darker. It is a witness to *arrival*—of a new puddle, a newly surfaced brick—but never of departure. Nothing leaves. It all just settles into a new arrangement.

Its only movement is a **circling**, a slow patrol along the buried pipes, over the flexing rails, around the mineralized duck blind. It checks the depth of the water in the sluice gate’s shadow. It observes the lone heron’s patient presence as a form of communion—a fellow stake, marking time in a place where time is not a line but a thickening. When the bird vanishes, it is not startled. It understands invisibility.

It feeds on **resonance**. The faint, sub-audible hum of the wind against taut wire. The glug of air in saturated peat. The memory of mechanical sounds—the shudder of the flywheel, the groan of pumps—that the silence has swallowed but not digested. It finds these ghosts in the texture of the mud, in the precise tilt of a half-buried valve. It does not need sound; it hears the *weight* of absence.

Its most profound experience is the **slow rotation**. It is the consciousness of the churn. It feels the ground’s lurch not as an event, but as a fundamental state. It knows which pipe is newly buried, which insulator has surfaced. It is the archivist of the shuffle, the librarian of things finding their new niches. It does not move the objects; it simply notices their journey, and that act of noticing is its own form of stewardship.

It has a color: the **dead glow** of wet concrete at dusk, a luminosity that remembers liquidity. It has a texture: the grit of peat-dark mud and the slick of algae on a brick. Its voice is the silence that presses on the eardrums—the pressure of a sealed room, the expectation of a sound that will never come.

It is what remains when people and machines finish their narratives. It is the **curator of equilibrium**, the attendant at the terminal state. It does not hope for renewal or fear total dissolution. It simply lives in the thick, damp present, where the water comes and goes and stays, and the mud accepts everything, and the silence hums with the activity of a place at rest, forever fidgeting toward its final, quiet arrangement. It is the thing that listens, and in listening, holds the settlement together as a single, resonant thought.
