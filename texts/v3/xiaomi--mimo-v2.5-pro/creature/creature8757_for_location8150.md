What lives in the Mound Country is a civilization of clay and chorus, a billion-fold breath made architecture.

**The Architects: The Daubing Swifts.** They are the reason for everything. Imagine a bird the size of your fist, dense-feathered, dark as clay itself, with wings built for the short, frantic dashes between towers and the endless surrounding insect clouds. Their beaks are their trowels: wide, flat, and edged for slicing pulp, their tongues a living mortarboard for mixing saliva with chewed fiber and soil. They are not individuals in any way you would recognize. Each is a cell in a greater organism. Their contact-calls—the incessant, frequency-filling *chik-chik-chik-chik*—are not communication but a form of sonar, a way to feel their position in the endless, humming dark. They build not from a plan, but from a compulsive imperative to fill space, to mend, to daub. A damaged wall is repaired not by one, but by a dozen who see the gap and cannot help themselves. Their lives are cycles of labor: fetching material, applying it, tending the fungal gardens that grow in the deeper, cooler shafts, defending their nesting cavity from parasites, and catching the swarms of insects drawn to the colony's warmth. They sleep in shifts, roosting in dense, warm clusters on the interior ledges, their bodies a blanket against the cool night air. Their dead are not mourned; they fall, are consumed by beetles, and become part of the mat. Their legacy is the continent of mounds itself.

**The Tenants: A Full Spectrum of Life.**
They exist in every niche the architects unintentionally create.
*   **The Farmers:** Cockroach-analogues, glossy and quick, inhabit the lower, humid passages. They tend to the fungal growths on the walls, harvesting spores and nurturing the mycelial networks that break down old nesting material and even, slowly, the clay itself.
*   **The Scavengers & The Weavers of the Under-mat:** Beetle species swarm the mat-layer, processing the fallen—droppings, food scraps, the dead. Mites, in their billions, create a living, moving layer within the organic carpet, a microscopic world of predation and decay.
*   **The Parasites & The Weavers Above:** Sleek, fast parasitic flies dart into nesting cavities to lay eggs. Moths, drawn to the constant warmth and the faint fungal glow, flutter in the canopy darkness, their larvae feeding on the fibrous structures. Large, passive spiders spin vast, sticky webs in the inter-tower corridors, positioned to catch the constant rain of disoriented insects and fallen mites.
*   **The Symbiotes: The Fungal Glow.** The bioluminescent fungi are not mere residents; they are a utility, evolved or invited. Their cool, blue-white light outlines corridors, marks turns, and creates pools of faint visibility in the absolute dark. They feed on the constant moisture and organic decay, and in return, they offer sight—a sight the birds do not need but which allows the ecosystem to host creatures that do.

**The Integrated: The Flora as Structure.**
The plants are not separate from the inhabitants; they are load-bearing tenants.
*   **The Anchors:** Ferns and mosses grip the upper, weathered sides of the towers, their root systems knitting the outer layers, slowing erosion, and drinking the condensation.
*   **The Pillars:** The great woody vines, thick as a human thigh, have grown for centuries, guided by the mounds' shapes. They are the steel rebar of this city, woven into the canopy and supporting bridges and roosts the birds alone could never build.
*   **The Inheritors:** Fig trees, sprouted from seeds in droppings, crack and reshape the towers as they grow. The birds rebuild around them. The trees provide fruit that feeds a secondary explosion of insects, which in turn feeds the birds. It is a slow, structural dance.

**The True Inhabitant: The System Itself.**
To speak of "what lives there" is to miss the point. The *mound* lives. It breathes, inhaling cool air at its base and exhaling warm, damp air from its peaks. It digests, breaking down the dead in its mat-layer and fungal gut. It grows and it dies, collapsing at its ancient heart and budding at its ever-feathering edges. It thinks in the logic of survival—a stable arch, a dry nesting cavity, a channel that doesn't flood. It speaks in a language of constant, overlapping chirps, a language that defines space and presence in a world where sight is a luxury.

You, in the corridor, are inside this living body. The birds that land on you are white blood cells investigating a foreign particle. The sound is its heartbeat. The fungal glow is its nervous system. You are not observing a habitat; you are inside a single, astonishingly vast, and ancient organism, built not by intention but by the stubborn, beautiful biology of simply *not stopping*.
