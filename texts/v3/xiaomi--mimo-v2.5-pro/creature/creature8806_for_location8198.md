They are mineralogists, all of them, whether they practice the trade or not. Their first language is the language of crusts and gradients. They wake in rooms where the walls are faintly warm to the touch, a perpetual, gentle heat that feels like a slow pulse. They dress in fabrics woven from mineral fibers—thin, durable, with a faint, iridescent sheen that catches the diffuse light. Their garments are cut to allow air to move against the skin, to manage the constant, pervasive warmth, but are close-fitting to avoid snagging on the sharp, newborn stone of their terraces.

They are a population of acute close-range observation. Their world is textures: the particular grain of basaltic andesite underfoot, the shimmer of a nascent arsenic-sulfate bloom on a pipe, the exact point of emerald or rust in a shallow pool. They learn to read the thermal map by sight—the way heat-haze shimmers differently over a vent that is merely hot versus one that is dangerously unstable. They know which mineral crusts are stable footholds and which will crickle into knife-edged shards under pressure. Their hands are never clean. They carry a permanent patina of metallic dust in the lines of their palms, under their nails, a second skin of iron oxide, copper carbonate, and finer, less-nameable precipitates. To shake hands is to feel a faint, gritty adherence.

Their voices are low and spare. Consonants are soft, vowels elongated slightly, not from laziness but from a careful breath economy. In the thin air, a shouted word costs what a quiet sentence does elsewhere. They communicate as much with gesture—a chin-tipped nod toward a condensation pattern on a wall, a flattened palm held near a surface to sense its heat—as with speech. Silence between people is comfortable, filled with the ever-present sub-bass hum of the world, a sound felt in the chest and bones more than heard in the ears. It is their background radiation, their heartbeat.

They are organized by terrace, and each terrace has its own microculture dictated by its thermal profile and its mineral wealth. The upper terraces, nearest the rim’s lip and the most volatile vents, are the domain of the geologists and the crisis-adjusters—the ones who monitor the caldera’s breath, who speak in murmurs of uplift rates and phreatic potential. They are the most taciturn, the most attuned to the subtle vibrations in the stone. Their skin is often the rouhest, seamed with fine white scars from flying glass.

The mid-terraces, where the heat is oppressive but stable, house the foundries and the chemical baths. Here, the people are alchemists of gradients. They work the slow convection currents, piping heat from one depth to another, precipitating specific compounds from the superheated, mineral-laden steam. Their expertise is in patience—setting a reaction to run for a decade in a sealed chamber, knowing the result will be a crystal lattice of unprecedented purity. They measure time in generations of labor. A family might begin the construction of a new condensation tower; their great-grandchildren will see it operational.

The lower terraces, cooler and more stable, are the oldest parts of the city. Here, the mineral accretions are thick and complex. Buildings are not so much decorated as *diagnosed*—their walls tell the chemical history of the air around them in bands of color. The people here are archivists and tenders. They meticulously clear doorways of encroaching stone, only to let it build up elsewhere according to a master plan of slow geologic reabsorption. They cultivate the ambiguous biofilms in sheltered, dewy alcoves, studying them not with microscopes but with chronometers and chemical assays, trying to decipher the liminal code between chemistry and life.

They see themselves not as inhabitants *of* the caldera, but as a slow-forming stratum *in* it. Their lives are a process of careful interpolation. They do not fight the encroachment of mineral crust; they design for it, incorporating it into their architecture, allowing certain structural walls to become geological features over centuries. Their art is the art of controlled deposition: a sculpture that changes its mineral composition as it weathers, a mural painted in solutions that will react with airborne sulfates to reveal new colors in fifty years.

Their most sacred spaces are not temples, but the **Listening Rooms**. These are small, resonant chambers carved deep into stable strata, where the hum of the magma chamber is clearest, shorn of the surface noise of vents and trickling water. Here, they sit in perfect stillness for days, feeling the planet’s slow intention in their bones. They believe the caldera is not just a place, but a consciousness in emergence—a mind forming in stone and heat. And they, with their quiet industry, their mineral-blooded patience, are its first, tentative thoughts.
