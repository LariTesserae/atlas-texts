**The Lithophages**

They are not frequent. Perhaps a dozen individuals in the stable basins, maybe twice that number in the deeper defiles where the stone has held for a subjective age (a month, two months). They are not visible at first glance, or second. They are the color of the stone, the exact dark basalt, their forms so integrated with the rubble and fractured ground that the eye slides over them.

They are slow. Their slowness is profound, geological in pace. A movement—a shifting of a limb, a turning of a head (if what they have can be called a head)—takes hours. They do not hurry. The world around them fractures, collapses, and rebuilds itself, but their timescale is different, synced to the slower, deeper pulses of the hum. They are attuned to it. When the subsonic swells rise, their bodies, if you watch long enough, seem to resonate. A fine dusting of the cream-colored mineral powder, shed from their surfaces, vibrates in visible patterns before falling still.

**Anatomy and Appearance**

They are low, broad, quadrupedal in a general sense, but their limbs are more like buttresses or roots—blunt, solid, tapering to wide pads that meld with the rock face they rest upon. Their surfaces are not smooth skin or scales, but a mosaic of their environment: plates of dark basalt, shot through with the familiar veins of the lighter, cream-colored mineral. These veins are not just decoration; they are functional. They seem to serve as conduits, glowing faintly with a dull thermal radiance when the hum is active, perhaps channeling the ambient heat into some internal process.

Their heads, if they have them, are featureless domes. No eyes. No mouths that are apparent. Sensing seems to be done through the stone itself. They feel the tremors, the shifts in pressure, the warm currents in the air through vibrations conducted through their fused basal pads. Their entire body is a sensory organ.

**Feeding and Metabolism**

They eat the **varnish**.

The dark, iridescent film that settles in sheltered pockets is their sustenance. They move to it (over a day or two) and settle over it, their broad undersides making full contact. There is no chewing, no ingestion as we understand it. The film seems to be absorbed through this contact, disappearing from the rock surface over the course of several hours. The lithophage’s body, during this process, shifts color subtly—the cream veins flare brighter, and the dark basalt plates seem to deepen in hue. They are mining the settled, complex minerals from that thin film, using the planet’s innate heat as the energy to metabolize it.

They leave nothing behind. No waste. The consumed film is converted entirely into mass and energy for the creature’s own glacial processes.

**Purpose and Behavior**

They do not build. They do not seem to communicate in any observable way. Their purpose is to *be*, very slowly, and to consume the only stable, complex material in this violent place. They are the anchor points, the recorders. While the landscape has no memory, *they do*, in their bodies. The layers of mineral in their plates are a stratigraphy of their existence, a slow-accumulated record of the conditions they have absorbed.

The **cairns** are not their work. The lithophages ignore the cairns and the wall completely. They are aware of them as stable rock formations, nothing more. The creator of the cairns remains unseen, a different kind of presence—perhaps one that moves at the speed of thought, not geology.

Occasionally, two lithophages will rest near each other in a basin for what seems like a year. Their movement toward each other is imperceptible moment to moment, but over weeks, they drift closer. When they finally touch, the contact is made along a full side, their mineral veins aligning for a time. They remain joined like this, unmoving, for another long period. It is not reproduction in a biological sense. It is more like **mineral accretion**—a sharing and recombination of their crystalline structures, a slow exchange of the internal record their bodies hold. After the joining, they separate again, each a slightly different mosaic than before, and drift apart on their separate, slow paths toward the next patch of varnish.

**Their Relationship to the World**

They are the antithesis of the place’s turbulence, yet utterly dependent on it. The constant fracturing creates the rubble fields that shelter the varnish deposits. The heat from below suspends the minerals in the air and allows the varnish to precipitate. The lithophages are the patient outcome of this specific environment—a form of life that has abandoned all speed, all complexity beyond the need to absorb and integrate, all ambition except to become a more perfect, more deeply recorded part of the stone from which they are made.

They are not *in* the Becoming Spine. They *are* the Becoming Spine, given a timescale of consciousness. They are the world’s memory, written in basalt and cream, walking at the speed of cooling rock. When you leave, when the haze swallows the view behind you, they will be the only things that remember you were ever there at all—a faint pressure pattern in the stone, a vibration absorbed and integrated into their ancient, mineral slowness.
