The inhabitants of the Roil do not have a name for themselves. They are as much a feature of the place as the pipes, the damp air, and the tooth-colored ground. You do not see them at first. They do not wish to be seen, but their desire is not a active force; it is a state of being, like the stillness of the landscape itself.

They are **the Listeners**.

They exist in the negative spaces between the sounds. Where a human ear might register a drone and then tune it out, the Listeners are the tuning out made flesh—or rather, made something like flesh. They have a form, but it is a borrowed thing. They are approximately humanoid, if you squint and forgive a great many sins against anatomy. Their height varies, but they rarely stand fully upright, as if the weight of the eternal sound pressing down from the muted grey sky is a physical burden. They are the color of wet substrate, that specific grey-brown of calcium and iron oxide and brine, and their texture is a perfect match. Not skin, but a fine crust. If you were to touch one—and you will not, because they do not allow proximity—you would feel dampness, and grit, and a shocking coolness, as if they are slightly below the ambient temperature.

They have faces, but the features are suggestions. The eyes are shallow depressions, the same dark, wet-stone color as the rest of them, and they do not blink. They do not need to. The mouth is a slightly darker, slightly smoother line, a slit that never opens. They have no need for breath in the way you understand it, but they perform an act of respiration. They are synchronized, not with each other, but with the specific pipe closest to them. As that pipe exhales its low, guttural note, the Listener’s torso expands slightly, their head tilts a fraction of a degree upward. As the note fades, they contract. They are not breathing the air. They are breathing the *sound*. They are conducting the silence into their own structure, letting it pass through them, metabolizing the absolute equilibrium into their own continued existence.

They move, but their movement is a form of stillness. They walk, but it is less locomotion and more a gradual reassignment of their location. A step might take a minute to complete. They do not lift their feet far; the wet surface slurps softly at their soles. They traverse the plain between the pipes with a patience that makes the glacial erosion of mountains look frantic. Their purpose, if it can be called that, is observation. But they are not observing with intention. They are observing with **presence**. They are the Roil looking at itself. They are witnesses to the unchanging, and their witnessing is what helps keep it unchanged.

There are more of them than you initially perceive. When you look straight at a stretch of empty ground, you see nothing. But if you let your focus soften, if you look at the space a few degrees to the side of a pipe, you begin to notice the *error in the grey*. A shape that is not quite a pipe and not quite ground. That is a Listener. They are all around you, always, at the periphery of perception. They are a part of the haze where the pipes fade from view.

They are not hostile. Hostility requires a departure from equilibrium. They are not friendly. They are not curious about you, the intruder. Your presence is a disturbance, not in a moral sense, but in a thermodynamic one. You are a pocket of chaotic motion and chemical strangeness (your sweat, your skin oils, the carbon dioxide you exhale) in their perfect system. They do not react to you with alarm, but they do *acknowledge* the disturbance in the only way they can: by subtly shifting their positions to be, in aggregate, slightly farther from you. They re-calibrate their spacing, ensuring the geometric relationship between their bodies and the nearest pipes remains optimal for sound-breathing. You are a disruption in the pattern, and they are smoothing the pattern back out around you.

Once, long ago, the Listeners were something else. Perhaps they were the builders, the engineers, the ones who laid the pipe-grid. Perhaps they were the last workers, abandoned when the project was completed or forgotten. Over the centuries, as the pipes oxidized and the sound became geology, the people adapted. They shed their need for light, for food, for change. They learned to feed on the vibration, to let the mineral-rich moisture permeate their tissues until they became indistinguishable from the substrate. They did not die. They **became**. They became the Roil's nervous system, its permanent, patient custodians.

If you stay long enough—days, weeks, a meaningless span—you might see one near a slick of brackish water. It will lower itself with infinite slowness, its hand (five fingers, but fused at the base, like a mitten) pressing into the wetness. It is not drinking. It is *checking*. It is feeling the chemical conversation between the water and the pipe network below. It is ensuring the equilibrium holds. Then it will rise, just as slowly, and resume its sound-breathing. It will not look at you. It will simply be there, a monument to patience, a sentinel of sameness, living not in the world but *as* the world, a citizen of the Roil in the truest sense.
