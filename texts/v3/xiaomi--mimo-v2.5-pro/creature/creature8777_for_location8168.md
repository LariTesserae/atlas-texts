The inhabitants of the Steaming Ward are not many, but they are distinct. They belong to the place in a way that feels less like occupation and more like adaptation.

**First, the Workers.** There are perhaps thirty in total, though shifts overlap and faces blur in the steam. They are not tall. Prolonged time in the heat and humidity seems to compress the spine, to soften the edges of the body. Their skin is perpetually damp, and they carry a particular sheen—a mixture of sweat and mineral condensation that dries in fine, pale streaks on their clothes and hair. They move with a slow, deliberate economy of motion, conserving energy against the heat, and their gestures are sharp and clear, their sign-language a silent, efficient dialect born of necessity. Their hands are their primary tools and voices: calloused, stained with dyes and grease, often marked with tiny, deliberate scars where the vegetation or machinery has claimed a toll. They do not speak of the outside much. When they do, it is with a quiet bewilderment, as if describing a dream of a place too dry, too quiet, too vast. Their eyes are often slightly narrowed, a permanent squint against the dimness and the ever-present haze.

**Next, the Tending Crew.** Two souls, usually. They move through the green not as conquerors but as curators. One is an older woman, her hair tied back under a wide-brimmed hat of oiled canvas. Her machete is an extension of her arm, its blade dark and notched. She knows the growth patterns intimately—where the moss will thicken overnight, which fern frond will uncoil fastest, where a new tendril of the great philodendron-relatives is probing for a pipe joint. She does not hate the plants; she regards them with a kind of weary respect, the way one might regard a powerful, restless tide. She believes in their necessity, in the unseen load they bear. Her younger partner is quieter, learning the rhythm, his shears flashing in the amber lamplight. They are the ward's gardeners, its surgeons, maintaining the boundary between ordered industry and encroaching green.

**Then, there is the Green.** It is the ward's most dominant life, and it lives with a voracious, silent hunger. The mosses breathe and drink and spread in carpets so thick they muffle the iron grating beneath. The ferns are ancient and primordial, their spores drifting in the steam like smoke. The larger leafy things—a riot of philodendrons, caladiums, and nameless hybrids—climb and sprawl, their enormous leaves sweating back the condensation they drink, their roots digging into mortar, wrapping around pipes, sometimes cracking brick. They are not decorative. They are an occupying force, held in check only by the two keepers and the ward's own physical limits. They give nothing to animals, only to the air and the water and themselves. To stand still near a wall is to feel the almost imperceptible brush of a new tendril seeking purchase.

**The Machinery itself** feels like a living thing. The great central boiler has a pulse—the deep, nine-second groan of its cycle felt more in the bones than heard. The hydraulic presses are its steady heartbeat. The steam venting through a thousand pipes is its breath. And after forty-seven years, they are becoming part of the geology. Calcium and iron deposits lace their surfaces like crystalline growths, blurring the line between iron and rock. The oldest presses are lumpy, organic-looking forms, no longer pristine machines but mineral-encrusted monoliths. They seem to *endure* with a patience that feels biological.

**And the Steam and Water.** They are the ward's atmosphere and its blood. The water moves in its channels with a slow, purposeful current, warm and dark, carrying the minerals of the deep earth. The steam is its exhalation—rising, clinging, condensing, falling back as rich dew upon the leaves and iron. In the thick central fog, the steam seems to possess a faint agency. It curls around legs and arms, pools in low spaces, and moves in slow, swirling patterns that sometimes, just for a second, resemble the shapes of faces or reaching hands before dissolving. It is the medium in which everything here exists.

**What is absent** is also a presence. The silence of the birds, the lack of a fly's buzz, the absence of a fish's flash—this void is felt. It makes the growth of the green feel more solitary, more triumphant, and strangely lonely. The workers' theories about this absence are part of their folklore, a quiet superstition they share in hand-signs when the roar of the boiler dips for a moment.

They live in a symphony of adaptation. The workers have adapted their bodies, their communication, their very perception of time. The plants have adapted to thrive on steam and mineral sweat. The machinery has adapted by becoming landscape. Even the light—gas lamp gold and boiler red—has adapted, creating a perpetual twilight where growth and industry exist in a hot, wet, roaring embrace. They are all inhabitants of a complete, if improbable, world.
