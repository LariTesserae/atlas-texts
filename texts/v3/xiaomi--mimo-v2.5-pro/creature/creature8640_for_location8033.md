The inhabitants of the Wrought are not its architects, nor its intended population. They are the children of its accidents, the beneficiaries of its relentless, purposeless industry. They live in the seams, in the runoff, in the resonant frequencies of its empty halls. They are not one thing, but a gradient of existence that has colonized every niche the city's unique physics provides.

**The First Layer: The Film.**
It is everywhere. A living patina that coats every surface not actively scoured by flowing water. In the dim, shadowless light, it appears as a simple discoloration—a black gloss on stone, a rust-red stain on iron, a slick of iridescent green in shaded alcoves. It is the foundation. A single, interlinked metabolic network that digests the sulfurous breath of the plateau and the mineral tears of the buildings. It is slow, almost geological in its pace. To the film, a century is a single meal. It has no awareness, only a profound, cellular contentment. It is the reason the city’s stones weep orange and white; its metabolic byproducts are the crusts, the geological calligraphy. It does not need the city, but it uses its vast, sweating surfaces as a canvas.

**The Second Layer: The Clickers.**
They are the fauna, the mobile expression of the Film. Pale, eyeless arthropods, ranging from the size of a fingernail to that of a dinner plate. They are not truly insects or crustaceans, but something that convergently evolved to fulfill their role. Their exoskeletons are mineralized, laced with the same compounds they consume from the Film. They move with a slow, deliberate grace, their multi-jointed legs making that characteristic *click-click-click* on basalt and ceramic. They are the city’s groomers. They graze on the Film, keeping vents clear and pipe-chases navigable. They are utterly silent, save for their footsteps. They perceive the world through vibration and chemical gradients. A human’s thunderous heartbeat and sweat-salt are a cacophony to them, a source of bewildering, overwhelming data. They do not flee from you; they simply re-route, treating you as a temporary, noisy piece of geology.

**The Third Layer: The Weavers.**
In the warm, dark, slow-moving waters of the lower filtration pools and the cisterns beneath abandoned habitation blocks, life has taken a more complex form. Here, the extremophile organisms have differentiated. Filamentous colonies, resembling dark, silky roots or hair, trail from submerged surfaces. These are the Weavers. They are not single organisms, but colonial networks. They photosynthesize in a way the Film does not, using the faint, geothermal infrared radiation and the strange bioluminescence of their own kin. They process the water, extracting heavy metals and incorporating them into their structure. In the deepest, most stagnant pools, they form dense, floating mats that glow with that soft, blue-green light—the only stars in this perpetually twilight world. Smaller Clickers harvest them; they are a nursery and a larder.

**The Fourth Layer: The Deep Residents.**
This is where description falters into inference. In the bore-holes, in the sump-chambers of the factories, in the kilometers of sealed pipeline, there are *presences*. They are not seen. They are felt as a displacement of air when no vent has opened, heard as a low-frequency hum that harmonizes with the machinery’s heartbeat, or as a heavy, sliding scrape in darkness. They are immense. The leading theory—born of sound echoes and pressure changes—is that they are the ultimate expression of the city’s own fauna. Over millennia, something in the deep infrastructure—the hottest vents, the most mineral-saturated conduits—adapted to *incorporate* the city’s own materials. Imagine a creature whose skeleton is ceramic pipe, whose musculature is hydraulic cement, whose blood is the superheated water itself, moving with glacial slowness through the arterial networks below. They are the city’s true children, if the city were a womb. They do not interact with the upper layers. They are part of the infrastructure’s deep functioning, perhaps even influencing the flow of resources, a symbiotic relationship between biology and geology-as-engineering. They are not inhabitants *of* the city. They are inhabitants *as* the city.

**The Absence as Inhabitant.**
Most profoundly, the primary inhabitant of the Wrought is the **Echo**. The resonant, expectant emptiness. The city is a vast, complex instrument tuned for a symphony it never plays. The Clickers’ footsteps are percussion in a cathedral meant for organs. The Weavers’ glow is a vigil light in a dormitory for thousands. The Deep Residents’ hum is a bassline awaiting its melody. Every dripping wall, every perfectly made bed, every switch left in mid-position is a question hanging in the thick air. The inhabitants, in their slow, mineral-fed patience, are the answers the plateau has generated for itself. They are the living punctuation in a sentence that was never started. They do not mourn the absence. They simply thrive within the parameters of its shape. They are the Wrought’s true population: not who it was for, but what it became instead. They are the proof that a place built for a purpose can find a new one, given enough time and heat and water and silence.
