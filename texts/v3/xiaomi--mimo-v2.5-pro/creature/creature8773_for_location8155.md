They are wet.

This is the first and most honest thing to say about them. They are wet in a way that goes beyond weather, beyond discomfort, beyond any single word their sparse vocabulary might offer — though they have many words for degrees and textures and durations of damp, words an outsider would hear as nearly identical. They are wet the way the thatch is wet, the way the stone floors are wet, the way the air itself is wet: persistently, without protest, as though the question were never relevant. Their skin has the particular quality of people who have never been fully dry as adults — smooth but not soft, a little translucent at the knuckles and the bridge of the nose, the pores fine, the hands calloused into a kind of permanent leather that sheds moisture the way good thatch sheds rain. Their hair is cut short or braided flat and tight against the skull. They do not think of themselves as wet. They think of outsiders as dry, and they mean it the way you might describe a room that has been emptied of furniture — technically functional, but missing something essential.

---

There are perhaps a hundred and twenty of them. Maybe fewer. They do not count in the way outsiders understand counting — not because they lack the capacity, but because the number shifts constantly. People come in along the tidal channels, stay for a season or a year, leave again. The channels carry them. Some return. Some don't. The core, the ones who have been here since the first posts were driven — or whose parents drove them — number perhaps forty. They are the ones who know where every foundation timber sits, which walls share load, which rooms will be shin-deep in water by the next high tide and which will merely weep. This knowledge is their inheritance, more valuable than anything physical they own. They carry it in their bodies, in the way they step through a dark room without hesitation, in the way they can tap a wall with a closed fist and know from the sound whether the timber within has begun to rot.

The ones who have arrived more recently are recognizable by the things they do with their hands. They grip too hard. They set stones too carefully. They haven't yet learned the economy of movement that the place demands — that the long-settled inhabitants practice without thinking, the way a river knows its own banks. A newcomer will carry a bundle of reeds across the settlement braced against their chest, careful and slow. An old-timer carries the same bundle on one shoulder, angled to clear the narrow passages, moving in a half-turn that is already choreography, already the shape of the place expressing itself through a human body.

---

The core families — if that is the right word; it may not be, since the arrangements are more fluid than the term suggests, shifting with the seasons and the tides and who has lost a room to the bog this year — are distinguished from one another mostly by *where* they are within the settlement. Geography is identity here. The family that maintains the rooms nearest the central tree is different from the family that manages the eastern channel-wall, and both are different from those who occupy the high cantilevered rooms on the southern edge where the thatch is thickest and the air drips most heavily in the afternoon. These positions are not chosen so much as grown into, the way a post grows into its clay socket. You stay where your knowledge is deepest. You know your own walls.

Within each cluster, there is a person — usually older, usually someone whose hands have gone pale at the creases from decades of constant damp — who holds the spatial memory. They are not chiefs. They are not leaders in any way an outsider would recognize with that word. They are more like the load-bearing timbers of the group: the others arrange around them not out of deference but out of structural necessity. Ask them where a wall was three years ago and they will tell you. Ask them where the northern channel will eat next and they will point to a specific place, and they will be right, not because they have any mystical insight but because they have watched the water's grammar for forty years and can read its syntax as fluently as their own intentions.

There is no formal council. Decisions about shared walls, about salvaging a collapsing structure, about cutting a new drainage channel through a floor — these are made in the passages, in the sideways-pause between rooms where two people stand face to face, close enough to feel each other's breath, and speak quietly because there is no need to speak loudly when you can hear your neighbor's stomach growl through a reed-mat wall. The conversation is brief. It is always brief. Words are not wasted here — not because they are stingy with language, but because the situation is usually clear to anyone who has been paying attention. The ground has shifted; the wall must move. The bog has crept; the room is abandoned. There is nothing to debate. The only question is how, and that is answered by whoever has the most relevant knowledge, and everyone knows who that is.

---

The children move through the settlement like water through a channel — quickly, efficiently, finding the gaps. They are small and lean and startlingly quiet. A child of five can carry a bundle of reeds across the full breadth of the Quarter without once losing balance on the slick stone floors, without once pausing at a passage too narrow for their load, because they have watched the adults do this every day of their life and their body has simply absorbed the geometry. By eight they can set a cobble in a wall. By twelve they can thatch a roof in the slow rain, working the material while it's damp enough to flex but not so wet that it will rot from within — and they know the difference the way they know the difference between their mother's voice and their aunt's, by texture, by feeling, without needing to name it.

They do not play in the way outsiders understand play. There are no games with rules, no structured contests. What they do instead is *work alongside*, and within that work there is something that looks like play from the outside — a kind of absorbed, intense, joyful concentration. Two children cutting channels into a floor will develop a rhythm together, their bone awls tapping in alternation, and the rhythm will accelerate and decelerate and they will laugh when the water comes through faster than expected, laughing at the surprise of it, the way the ground continues to surprise. This is their version of delight: the unexpected behavior of water, the sudden seep, the crack that opens overnight. The ground is their toy and their antagonist and their teacher, and they treat it with the particular affection you reserve for something you cannot fully trust.

The second generation — the ones born here, who have never stood on ground that didn't move — has a particular quality that the first generation does not share. They are utterly unburdened by comparison. They do not think of this place as marginal or difficult or temporary, because they have nothing to measure it against. The wet is just the world. The seeping floors are just floors. The narrow passages are just passages. They move through the Quarter with a fluidity that the first generation, even after decades, cannot quite match, because the first generation still carries in their bones the memory of wider rooms, of firmer ground, of a world that was not saturated at every point. The second generation is born to the saturation. They are its children. And they are, in their way, the most complete expression of what this place makes: people for whom the boundary between living structure and living person has become almost moot, who understand the settlement not as shelter but as an extension of the body, a second skin made of timber and reed and stone, a skin that leaks and shifts and must be constantly tended the way you tend your own hands.

---

They eat what the margins and the tidal flats provide. Shellfish, mostly — the flats yield them at low tide in sufficient quantity, and the inhabitants know which beds regenerate fastest, which channels produce the fattest specimens, how to harvest without depleting. The copper knives — worn thin, reshaped, their origins unspoken and perhaps forgotten — are used for this more than anything: prying, cutting, cleaning. There are small fish in the tidal channels, caught with woven traps that the inhabitants set and check on the tide's schedule. The salt-tolerant grasses yield small, hard seeds that are gathered and ground on flat stones and mixed with water into a paste that is cooked on heated shale, though "cooked" is generous — it is warmed, dried as much as the air will allow, and eaten dense and slightly gritty. The succulent plants that grow in the wall crevices provide something — moisture, a sharp green taste, a supplement that the inhabitants seem to value for reasons beyond nutrition. There may be medicinal knowledge there, passed along the wall-line from one family cluster to the next.

There is no feast. There is no famine. There is simply the ongoing act of provision, as constant and ambient as the dripping of water, and they do not speak of it as though it were remarkable. Food is gathered the way walls are maintained — because the day requires it, because the body requires it, because stopping would mean sinking.

---

The tree at the center is where they come closest to something an outsider might call ceremony, though even that word overstates it. There are no rituals of the dramatic kind — no chanting, no fires, no sacrifices. What happens at the tree is quieter and, to them, probably unremarkable. People stand near it. They lean against the cobbled wall. They rest. In a place where every structure is temporary, where every wall may be softened to paste within a decade, the tree is the oldest thing and the most permanent, and they orient toward it the way a compass needle orients toward north — not consciously, not with effort, but with a kind of bodily inevitability. A person carrying a heavy load of stone will set it down near the tree before continuing. Two people who need to have a difficult conversation — about a shared wall, about a room that must be given up, about a child who has gone to the eastern channel and not come back at the expected tide — will have that conversation near the tree, where the ground is slightly higher and the air moves slightly more freely and the pale leaves cast a small trembling shade.

They do not speak about the tree's meaning. If asked — and they would find the question strange — they might say that the tree was here before the posts were driven, that it was here when this was still a mudflat island, that it has roots in ground that has been ground longer than anyone can remember. They might say that the cobbled wall keeps the bog from reaching it. They might say nothing at all, because the tree is the kind of truth that doesn't survive translation into words, the way the sound of water doesn't survive translation into description.

---

They carry things. This is perhaps the most defining gesture of their daily lives — the constant movement of material from one place to another. Stone up from the flats when the tide is low enough. Timber in from the estuary's edge, the waterlogged hardwood dragged or floated or carried on shoulders made strong and strangely shaped by the specific demands of this load. Fresh reeds from the narrow margins where they grow, harvested in bundles and stacked against walls to dry as much as anything dries here, which is to say: partially, slowly, never quite. A person walking through the Quarter without carrying something is either resting or unwell. The carrying is so constant that it has become a kind of language — you can read what a person is doing, what they need, what part of the settlement is failing, from what they hold in their arms and the direction they are moving. A woman heading north with a bundle of reeds means the northern channel wall needs patching. A man heading south with cobbles in a hide sling means a floor has collapsed and must be rebuilt. The carrying is communication. The Quarter speaks through its inhabitants' arms.

---

The fog-bog to the west is spoken of differently than the other boundaries. The tidal channels are active, dynamic, exciting in their way — they reshape the edge, they bring new material, they are dangerous but generous. The bog is neither. The bog is patient. It moves inward a handspan each year, softening what it touches, converting structure to paste, wall to mud, room to seep. The two structures already abandoned to it are visible from certain upper-story windows — their upper timbers still standing, their lower halves already indistinguishable from the ground, sinking in slow motion, the thatch darkening and slumping. The inhabitants watch this without visible emotion but with a quality of attention that is its own kind of feeling. They know the bog will come for more. They know, in some inarticulate way, that the bog is the ground's truest expression — that the bog is what this place was and may be again, that the settlement is a temporary interruption in the ground's long project of becoming water.

And yet they stay. They patch. They carry. They thatch. They set new timbers into the wet clay and wait for the mud to grip. They move sideways through the passages of their lives with the practiced economy of people who know that comfort is not the point, that the point is the tree and the walls and the channels and each other and the ongoing, stubborn, saturated act of *being here* on ground that has not yet decided to hold them.

---

There is a quality to their faces that is hard to describe and easy to misread. Outsiders sometimes call it resignation or grimness, but that is wrong. It is closer to the quality of a surface that has been shaped by persistent contact with something larger than itself — the way a cobble is shaped by river, the way a timber is shaped by clay. The features are worn smooth, not by age but by attention, by the constant low-level awareness that the ground beneath them is alive in a way that ground is not supposed to be, that the walls around them are in conversation with the water, that the air between them and the sky is thick with moisture that will settle on every surface by afternoon. Their eyes are clear and watchful and slow to widen, because there is very little here that is surprising — only the ongoing surprise of still being here, still standing, still dry enough, still enough. They blink against the damp the way you might blink against sunlight: reflexively, without urgency, as a basic negotiation with the medium in which they live.

They do not think of themselves as remarkable. They think of themselves as people who live in a place, the way all people live in places. The fact that their place is still becoming a place — that the ground is still deciding, that the water is still negotiating the terms — is, to them, simply the condition of things. All ground was once water. All walls were once reeds. All settlements were once a single post driven into uncertain earth. They simply remember this more clearly than most.
