The silence here is not empty. It is occupied.

They move through it like stones rolling through deep water—slowly, with a kind of submerged purpose. You see them in the late mornings when the mist thins to a gauze, tending the terraces. Their posture is bent, not from weakness, but from a lifelong conversation with the low ground and the stubborn, shallow soil. They wear woven fibers, a coarse fabric from the sedges, turned grayish-brown with time and damp, layered against a chill that is more about moisture than temperature. The cloth is rarely dry. It smells of the plateau: moss, smoke, wool.

Their faces are the landscape. Skin weathered not by sun, but by damp wind and the mineral patience of stone—a pale, almost lichenous quality, etched with fine lines that map not years of harshness, but years of steady attention. Eyes are pale, too, perhaps to match the perpetual overcast sky, and they hold a stillness that is unsettling to outsiders, a focus that looks inward as much as outward. They watch water flow. They watch moss encroach. They see shifts too subtle for an uninitiated eye: a new seep where there was none, a patch of sedge yellowing a half-season too early, the slow dance of lichen staining a wall.

Their voices, when they use them, are low and unhurried, meant to carry only a few steps across a courtyard. The language is full of soft consonants and elongated vowels, sounds that mimic the drip of water and the whisper of wind over stone. Long silences between phrases are not awkward; they are necessary, spaces where the plateau itself can breathe. Much communication happens with hands—a gesture toward a cloud bank, a touch on a wall indicating a new crack, a simple tilt of the head signifying *yes, we harvest soon.*

They are not artists. There is no decoration. But they are caretakers of pattern. A family will repair a wall with stones that fit the existing jigsaw with an almost intuitive precision, passed down not through blueprints but through the muscle memory of generations. The irrigation channels are maintained with a reverence usually reserved for sacred texts—their course is truth, their function is prayer. To alter one is unthinkable; to neglect one is a sin against the community’s tenuous hold.

Their diet is simple, monastic. The pale, fibrous tubers from the terraces, boiled into a thick, mineral-rich stew. Dried sedge tips, chewed for sustenance. Occasionally, a ferment of the wildflowers, not for intoxication but for a faint, clean taste that is the closest they come to sweetness. Water is the constant companion, drunk straight from the seeps, carrying the faint tang of limestone.

They know the plateau as a body. They know its pores where water weeps, its bones of sharp limestone, its thin skin of moss and soil. They know its moods: the way the mist lies heavy and silent before a rare, drizzly day, the way the light turns glassy and clear when the wind will shift. This knowledge is not intellectual; it is somatic. They feel a change in the air pressure in their joints. They taste the mineral shift in the water before a seep dries up.

Children are rare, and quiet. They do not run or shout. They are taught to listen first—to hear the different sounds of water (the *drip*, the *seep*, the *gurgle*), to feel the firmness of ground underfoot. Play is an imitation of work: moving small stones, digging tiny channels in the mud, watching how water behaves. Their laughter, when it comes, is a startling, liquid sound, quickly shushed by the surrounding silence, not in anger, but in gentle correction. *The plateau does not know this sound,* their parents’ eyes seem to say. *Learn its language first.*

There is no concept of progress here. The past and present are a single, damp layer of stone. A man mending a roof slab uses the same knot his great-grandmother tied, not out of rigid tradition, but because the knot *works*. It is the solution the plateau allows. Innovation is not forbidden; it is simply unnecessary. The problem of survival was solved long ago, and the solution was equilibrium. Their art, if they had a word for it, would be the art of not disrupting that balance. Their heroism is in the daily, unending labour of maintenance—shoring up what water and time seek to dissolve, gently guiding what seeps and grows.

In the deepest part of the night, when the mist is a solid, cool blanket and the silence is so profound it has a weight, they sit in their dim, smoky interiors. They do not tell stories of gods or distant lands. They might share a observation: *"The pool near the western ridge is lower this year,"* or *"The green moss has crossed the path to the third terrace."* These are not trivial comments. They are the news of their world, the chronicles of their slow, epic saga. They listen, and they nod. The information is absorbed, filed against the long memory of the plateau.

They are not sad. They are not joyful. They are *present.* They are as much a part of the wet, quiet, persistent seep of life here as the moss itself—rooted, patient, growing imperceptibly, their lives measured not in dramatic seasons but in the slow deepening of lichen stain on a limestone wall, and the endless, gentle work of holding a world of soft edges together on a bed of sharp, new stone. They are the people of the damp breath, the quiet drip, the warm silence that presses in from all sides. They are the plateau, looking back at itself through pale, still eyes.
