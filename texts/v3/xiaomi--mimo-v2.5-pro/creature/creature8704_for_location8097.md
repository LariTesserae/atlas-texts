The engineers of Ešduun do not merely *inhabit* their city; they are symbionts within it. To live here is to become a specific, quiet creature, shaped by verticality, water, and stone.

They are lean and deliberate in their movements. Not gaunt—the synthesis chambers provide precise nutrition—but their bodies have learned the economy of space. On the railless walkways, their steps are measured, their center of gravity kept low and fluid. They navigate the void not by looking down, but by reading the subtle text of the hold-surface underfoot: the faint vibration that means "sound," the slight tackiness of the composite in high-humidity zones, the minute thermal gradient that warns of a fog-bank advancing from a lower depth. Their hands are often out, not gripping, but floating just above the handrails or the walls, feeling for the city's pulse.

Their senses are tuned differently. They hear in layers: the ever-present dripping is not background noise but a diagnostic. A change in pitch or rhythm along a wall segment can signal a shift in aquifer pressure hours before the sensors flag it. In the dense fog of *the breath*, their hearing sharpens further, mapping the space in echoes and whispers carried between the narrow walls. They have names for the different silences: the *still-silence* of undisturbed air, the *wet-silence* of a newly seeped wall, the *deep-silence* that pools in the lowest, uninhabited chasms.

They dress in layers of pale, functional fibers that wick moisture and blend with the bone-colored holds. Colors are muted, personal flourishes found in subtle embroidery or a patterned inner lining, rarely in outer garments. In the fog, they become ghostly forms, identified more by gait and the particular timbre of their voice than by face. They speak softly, as the fissure commands, but their conversations are dense with technical nuance and unspoken understanding—a language of flows, tensile strengths, and chemical balances.

Their work is not a separate activity from their living. There is no commute. An engineer might wake in a residential shell on the west wall, cross a bridge to the engineering halls on the east, and along the way, their eyes would automatically note the weep patterns on the rock, the condensation on a structural strut, the hum of a nearby bio-reactor. They carry small, elegant tools: sonic calipers that listen for micro-fractures in the composite, spectrometers that can identify mineral signatures in a single drip, data-slates that display the city's vital signs in real-time flows of light.

They are gardeners, but of stone and water. Watching a new hold being grown is a communal ritual. They gather on distant walkways, observing the slow, silent bloom of the ceramic-metallic material as it extrudes from the rock, guided by shaped energy fields. They point, discuss, and marvel at its almost organic willfulness. This is their art: coaxing structure from chaos, building a future on a wounded cliff face.

There is a deep, unspoken pride, but it is austere. They do not boast of conquering the environment, but of *negotiating* with it. Their greatest cultural achievements are not monuments, but balances: the precise calibration of the water-return systems, the artful distribution of load across a centuries-old anchor point, the silent efficiency of a fog-clearing draft channeled through a newly grown vent. Failure is not a catastrophe in the abstract; it is a shift in the rock's breath, a change in the drip's song, a millimeter of creep in a wall they must sleep next to.

In the communal holds during the still hours, they unwind. Music is played—soft, resonant instruments that harmonize with the drip and hum. They tell stories, not of surface lands most have never truly known, but of depth-levels, of legendary repairs, of the "deep-folk" myths about the barely inhabited lowest reaches where the water is warm and the rock itself is said to whisper older truths.

They are children of a narrow sky and a deep, wet earth. Their faces are often turned not outward to the distant line of surface light, but inward and downward, to the shimmering, dripping, humming cathedral they have grown within the wound of the mountain. They are its keepers, its neurons, its living, breathing, quietly active heart. To be an engineer in Ešduun is not a profession; it is a mode of being, as fundamental and perpetual as the seeping of water through stone.
