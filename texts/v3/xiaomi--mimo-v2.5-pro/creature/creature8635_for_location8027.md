Who lives here is not a tenant but a symbiont. They are not the grove’s owner, but its slowest-moving organ.

You see them only in the evidence they leave, and in the gaps their presence creates. They are the reason the engines have not been swallowed entirely by root and fern, the reason the water channels still run true, the reason the resin is collected before it hardens in the basin. They understand the system not as a human understands a machine, but as a gardener understands a complex perennial—knowing which cuts will heal, which pressures will redirect growth, where to introduce a drop of copper sulfate to discourage a particular mold.

They move through the passages with a gait that has learned the floor’s give. Their footsteps are quiet, not because they are stealthy, but because they step on the specific root-knuckles that are firmest, the wooden slats that have been worn smooth by generations of such steps. They leave no permanent mark; their tools are placed against the trunks in places where the bark has been conditioned by tool-oil and grip to be slightly smoother, slightly drier.

They are likely not tall. The ceilings, where the lattices dip low, are not high. They are patient in a way that is biological, not just psychological. They can spend a morning observing the flow of sap through a single cam-driven press, adjusting the tension of a belt by a single notch, watching the rhythm of compression change by half a second. Their own heartbeat, over the years, has likely slowed to match the grove’s.

Their diet is, in part, the grove itself. The amber resin, when cured, is edible—a dense, caloric, faintly spicy substance that sustains them between the rare forays into the fog-bound exterior for other needs. They may cultivate the small, colorless amphibians, or at least respect their presence, for the creatures’ translucent bodies are indicators of water purity, living litmus tests.

They are not alone in their consciousness, but their companions are not human. They converse with the grove in a language of cuts, ties, and pressures. They understand the deep groan of a shifting trunk as a sentence, the sudden thicken of condensation as a warning. The slow tearing sound of shedding bark is to them as familiar as the creak of a door.

They are the memory of the original builders, but mutated. The builders sought to impose a structure upon the living wood. The current inhabitant has surrendered to it. They do not shape the grove so much as they *negotiate* with it, a diplomat in a slow-motion treaty between industry and growth. They ensure the engines run not to conquer the plant, but to participate in its cycle: extracting its sap to power the pumps that give its roots water, in an endless, gentle loop of self-service.

At night, when the fog outside thickens and the air within cools further, they likely rest in one of the higher rooms, where a major trunk forks into a comfortable hollow. They listen to the drip, the hum, the settling of wood on wood. They are not a keeper. They are a part of the Verdine Works’ metabolism, as essential as the moss on the belts, as deliberate as the grafting scars, as quiet as the water running in its ancient grooves. They are the part of the system that remembers it is alive.
