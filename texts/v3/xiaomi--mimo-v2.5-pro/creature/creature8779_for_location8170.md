The inhabitants of the Thundering Highs do not merely live upon the plateau; they are its voice, its movement, its texture. They are not separate from the ancient stone or the living carpet, but expressions of it, shaped by an age and a volume that human senses can only approximate. To describe them is to trace the contours of a world that speaks in bellows and drones and cracks, that moves in slow herds and swift, silent pairs, that grows in patient layers that outmeasure human memory.

First, there is the **dominant groundcover** itself—not merely the substrate for life, but the primary architect and sustainer of all others. This is not a plant in any terrestrial sense. It is a **colony-sentience**, a vast, woody network whose individual fronds are but the outermost sense-organs of a being that spans kilometers. Its leaves, dark and jade-hard, are not just solar panels; they are listening devices, trembling at the vibrations of bellowing calls, orienting toward the faint warmth of a geothermal seep. The mounds are not just growth; they are **memory-archives**. The oldest cores, those dense, peat-like hearts, hold chemical records of a million years of rainfall, pollen drift, and the heavy footfalls of creatures long evolved into new forms. To the inhabitants, these mounds are landmarks, shelter, and history books written in compressed cellulose and resin. They are the true elders, and the plateau’s life orbits around them.

Above and within this green empire, the **insects** are the ceaseless system-operators. They are not a swarm, but a **living weather system**. Their wings, polished and translucent, are not for long-distance flight but for this hovering—a permanent, suspended rain of bodies that filters the air, feeds on microscopic blooms in the dew, and serves as the constant auditory baseline for the world. Their forms are shard-like, seeded from an older, more geometric blueprint of life. They are pollinators, scavengers, and the primary food for the air’s other children. Their drone is not random noise; it is the plateau’s **baseline frequency**, the carrier wave upon which all other sounds are modulated.

The **great herbivores**—the broad, low-slung grazers—are the landscape’s mobile geology. Their shells, dark and ridged like the exposed bedrock, are not bone but a dense, keratinous material that absorbs and radiates the geothermal warmth. They move in **deliberate aggregations**, not herds, each individual maintaining a precise, infrasonic dialogue with its nearest neighbors kilometers away. Their bellow is not just a call; it is a **sonic palpation**. They use it to map the density of the vegetation, to sense the firmness of the ground, even to communicate with the mounds themselves, their deep frequencies resonating in the ancient cores. They are patient, slow, and seemingly eternal. Where they graze, they do not destroy; they cultivate, their wide mouths trimming the groundcover in patterns that encourage new growth in certain ways, their droppings fertilizing specific mineral beds. They are **landscape-tenders** by instinct, though they would never comprehend such a term.

The **predators** are the sharp punctuation in the plateau’s endless sentence. Sleek and long-necked, they are built not for sustained chase but for **startle-and-ambush**. Their eyes, forward-facing and amber, perceive the world in a spectrum slightly shifted toward the thermal. They see the warm shapes of the grazers through the misty air, even when obscured by a mound. Their signature crack is produced by a **hydraulic syrinx**, a bony chamber in the throat that builds and releases pressure with terrifying speed. This sound does not merely stun prey; it disrupts the local sound-field, creating a momentary pocket of silence that disorients. They hunt in coordinated silence otherwise, their movements a flowing, almost liquid advance through the vegetation, their footfalls absorbed by the spongy groundcover. They are **artists of interruption**, introducing sudden violence into a world of constant, thrumming life.

Then there are the **soaring ones**—the bird-like creatures. They are not birds but **thermal-riders**, their broad, flat wings evolutionary masterpieces for exploiting the precise updrafts from every geothermal vent. Their bodies are lightweight, almost hollow-boned, and covered in feathers that are less for insulation and more for channeling air and displaying subtle shifts in color. Their calls are high, penetrating, and **information-dense**. A single shriek can encode the location of a predator, the discovery of a blooming flowering spike, or a shift in weather. When they spiral in columns, they are not merely flying; they are **performing a collective computation**, weighing all this information in a dance of shadow and sound. They are the plateau’s sentinels and news-carriers, their eyes missing nothing on the vast plain below.

And finally, the **People of the Stone and Stem**. They are few, and they are old. Their skin is the color of the exposed bedrock, weathered to a deep ochre, and their hair is often stiffened with the resin of the mounds, giving it a dark, woody texture. They move with a **quiet that is almost gravitational**, their bare feet feeling the vibrations of the great grazers long before they are seen. Their language is a soft, layered thing, built on clicks and low hums meant to travel beneath the constant insect-drone without adding to it. Their shelters are not homes but **listening posts**—places to rest while attuning to the plateau’s voice. They do not own the land; they **interpret** it. Their tools are **transitional objects**, used and then often returned to the groundcover to be slowly reclaimed. The hand-axes are for shaping their shelters, but also for tapping on the flowering stalks in specific rhythms to encourage pollen release. The sinew-bound stems are **listening-sticks**, pressed to the earth to hear the movements of distant grazers. The bowls are for collecting dew, but also for amplifying the faint hums of the mound cores.

They live in a cycle dictated not by the sun, but by the **sound and movement of the fauna**. They follow the grazers in a loose, respectful orbit, taking only the weak or the already dead, their hunting a ritual of acknowledgment more than conquest. Their greatest art is **silence**—the ability to still their own noise, to let the plateau’s voice pass through them without interference. They are the quietest inhabitants not out of weakness, but out of a profound, inherited understanding that in a place this loud, the greatest power lies in listening.

As dusk falls and dew beads on every surface, the People move through the mounds, their forms nearly invisible. The insects settle lower, their drone deepening. The grazers bellow in the purple distances. A predator cracks somewhere, and a column of soarers wheels home on the last thermals. The groundcover creeps, imperceptibly. The ancient stone holds its warmth. All these inhabitants, from the root-network to the listening human, are engaged in the same eternal activity: **being loud, being quiet, and being part of a world that never, ever stops speaking.**
