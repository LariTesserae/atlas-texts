### Inhabitants of Tang La Interior

**I. The Stone-Dwellers (The Residents)**
They are not a tribe, not a culture with a single name—they are **Tang La**. Eight hundred souls etched into living rock, existing vertically. Their existence is defined by the shaft’s economics and geology:

- **The Upper Dwellers:** Custodians of air and light. They manage water casks, watch the dim upper reaches where pigeons roost, and maintain the intricate mark-system that governs rights and debts. Their skin is paler, accustomed to the least light; they speak in quieter voices, as if afraid to disturb the dust.
- **The Middle Folk:** The builders, traders, and workshop crews. Hands stained with metal shavings, ears tuned to the turbine’s throb. They move quickly across catwalks, know every bolt and weld in their section. Their children learn to climb before they learn to read marks. They are the settlement’s heartbeat.
- **The Lower Workers:** They live in the warm glow of the forge and the deep rumble of the turbine. Their work is heavy—fracturing new stone, maintaining machinery, braving the deeper cold. They are stockier, louder, and carry the smell of mineral oil and ozone. They are the only ones who regularly venture toward the shaft’s bitter extremes.

**All share:** A quiet resilience. A deep knowledge of rhythm—the 18-hour cycle of light and dark, the shifting warm zones, the arrival of water casks. They value utility over beauty, yet carve small flourishes into railings: tiny spirals, hawks in flight, spiders with geometric webs. They do not see their home as strange; it is the world. The wind outside is a rumor, the sun a myth.

---

**II. The Inherited Fauna (The Shaft’s Children)**
These are not mere animals—they are **institutions**.

- **The Orb-Weavers & Line-Spinners:** Dozens of species, each occupying specific niches—some span the gaps between pipes, others weave sticky traps in rafters. They are considered part of the infrastructure. Their silk is collected and used for minor repairs. They are the unseen archivists, their webs mapping the settlement’s vibration patterns.
- **The Dust-Pigeons:** Descendants of surface birds, now evolved for echo and dimness. Their coos ricochet off stone, creating haunting chords. They nest in upper crevices, leaving droppings that are collected for fertilizer (used in the tiny, hidden hydroponic trays kept by some Upper Dwellers). They are omens—if a pigeon returns to a platform, the structure is sound.
- **The Kestrel-Phantoms:** Not true hawks, but a lean, silent predator that hunts by feel and air-current. They are rarely seen, more felt—a sudden draft, a soft thud, a scattering of feathers. Their presence is a mark of a healthy ecosystem. Some say they are the original inhabitants, before the shaft was opened.
- **The Wall-Gnawers & Tunnel-Rodents:** A whole sub-surface society. Mice, voles, and possibly blind rats that live in the mineral-wool insulation and cable conduits. They are pests, but also indicators—if the gnawing stops in a section, something is wrong with the air or temperature. Their tiny teeth shape the mountain’s future.

---

**III. The Marginal Life (The Unseen & Unnamed)**
- **The Rust-Motile:** The moving patina on lower platforms. Not plant, not animal, but something that feeds on moisture traces, metal ions, or perhaps vibration itself. It is ignored, tolerated, even protected—some workers say it strengthens the pipes it coats. It is the only life that grows in place.
- **The Dust-Moths & Intake Insects:** Tiny, pale-winged things that drift in through the upper vents, drawn by the lights. They feed the spiders and are considered a sign that the outside air is flowing properly.
- **The Human Microbiome:** Evolved, adapted. Skin flora that can thrive in dryness, lung bacteria that handle mineral dust, gut microbes that make the most of rationed food. Every resident carries a unique inner ecosystem, as much a part of Tang La as the spiders.

---

**IV. What They Create Together (The Culture)**
- **The Mark-Language:** Scratched into iron, it tells stories of debt, kinship, warnings, and jokes. It is learned by touch by the blind, and decoded by children as play. It evolves; new symbols emerge for new trades or dangers.
- **The Quiet:** Not silence, but a curated sound-space. Loud noises are controlled—explosives are scheduled, metalwork is damped. Arguments happen in whispers. Laughter is rare but carries far.
- **The Patience:** Nothing is built to last. Everything is maintained. They live in a state of constant, gentle renovation. To them, permanence is a myth of the outside world.
- **The Rituals:** Water-sharing is sacred. The sounding of the turbine’s rhythm is a daily meditation. The taking of a hawk’s kill-feather is a coming-of-age mark. The marking of a child’s first climb is celebrated with extra light-bulbs for a week.

---

**V. What They Are Becoming**
The inhabitants of Tang La are not static. They are **adapting**—biologically and culturally. Their eyes are changing, more sensitive to low light. Their social marks are becoming a written language. Their relationship with the fauna is symbiotic. They are not just living in a hole; they are *co-evolving with it*. In centuries, if they persist, they may become something new: a human subspecies of the vertical dark, as much at home in the shaft as the spiders are on their webs. The strangeness is not in them, but in how perfectly they fit.
