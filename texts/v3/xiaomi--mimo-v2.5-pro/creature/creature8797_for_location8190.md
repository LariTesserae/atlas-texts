They are here. In the steam, in the noise, in the damp, they have made a home. Not just the humans, though they are the ones who build and speak and remember. The whole basin teems with life that has grown into the shape of this place, that has become inseparable from its rhythms and its permanence.

## The People of the Basin

First, you notice their calm. In a world of constant, overwhelming sound—the hiss, the boom, the endless clicking—they move with a deliberateness that seems almost underwater. Their voices are low and resonant, tuned to cut through the ambient roar, yet they speak infrequently. Their language, a series of tonal vowels and soft consonants, has a guttural, bubbling quality, as if shaped by the air itself. They do not shout over the noise; they live within it, their words emerging from the wall of sound like objects from fog.

They are of medium height, lean, with a layer of subcutaneous fat that gives them a smooth, rounded look. Their skin is pale, almost translucent, the result of generations under the diffused, shadowless light. Faint, vein-like patterns of dark blue are often visible beneath, tracing paths across temples and forearms. Their eyes are large, with a slight epicanthic fold, and the irises are a washed-out grey or pale brown, adapted to the perpetual dimness. Their hair is fine, usually worn braided or knotted close to the head, sometimes woven with small, smooth beads of polished calcium carbonate or slivers of dark basalt.

They dress simply, in layers of undyed, rough-textured cloth woven from traded fibers, often wrapped and tied to allow movement through water and mud. Their feet are bare, the soles thickened and tough, insensitive to the warm, mineral-rough ground.

## Their Days and Deeds

A day in the Basin of Lament has no sharp edges. There is a lightening and a dimming, a subtle shift in the steam from brighter haze to greyer gloom, but it is a change of degree, not of kind. The people rise from their close, mud-walled homes into the same warm, wet air, greeted by the same symphony of hisses, clicks, and booms.

Work is constant, communal, and rhythmic. Teams wade into the designated pool areas, their movements slow and sure to avoid startling the crustaceans. They speak little, communicating with soft whistles and hand signals. The harvest is a matter of careful selection: scooping the pale, chitinous creatures into woven baskets, choosing those of the right size and shell integrity. There is no sense of urgency, no panic of competition. The crustaceans are countless, a resource that has always been and will always be.

Other groups tend the bacterial mats. They thin the mats with long, bone-handled scrapers, pulling the rubbery sheets from the water's surface and laying them on flat, sun-warmed stones to dry. The process is meticulous. They know, by texture and smell, which mats are richest in the strange, chemosynthetic proteins that form the cornerstone of their diet.

They fish the deeper pools with quiet patience, using baited hooks of bone and line, never rushing a catch. The eel-like creatures are a prized, if rare, meal, their pale flesh considered a delicacy.

## The Council and the Knots

Social life is woven through the work. Disputes are rare, but when they arise, they are brought to the courtyard of the elders. The council does not sit on elevated seats; they gather on the same damp ground as everyone else, sitting in a circle on mats of dried bacterial film. Authority is held in the weight of experience, in the memory of how things have been done. Their judgments are offered as suggestions, phrased in the continuous present tense: "The family of Olen takes from the pool at the eastern bend, for they have tended it well." There is no future tense for punishment, only a reassertion of the existing balance.

Their history is kept not in books, but in knots. In each family dwelling, there hangs a cord—thick, woven, and intricate, with knots of different sizes, materials, and configurations. To "read" it, an elder runs their fingers along the cord, reciting genealogies, significant weather patterns (though these are few), and territorial agreements. Some knots hold tiny, polished stones or shards of mineral, marking a particular event or a pact with the booming heron-things. The cords are heavy with time, passed down through generations, each one a tactile map of continuity.

## The Other Inhabitants

The people are not alone, and they know it. They live in a careful truce with the louder, larger denizens of the basin.

The crustaceans are both harvest and backdrop. They are the sound-makers, the background static. Children learn to read their movements—a sudden cessation of clicking can signal a shift in water temperature or a predator's approach. They are not pets, but a kind of immovable, edible fact of life.

The wading heron-things are treated with solemn respect. Their booming territories are mapped and avoided during nesting times. The people leave offerings—piles of especially succulent crustaceans—at the edges of these territories. In return, the heron-things provide a kind of alarm; their booming patterns can indicate changes in vent activity or water levels. The relationship is one of mutual, non-interfering presence.

The eels, slugs, and buzzing swarms are simply part of the landscape, a living texture. The slugs are considered mildly ill-omened, their glistening trails avoided. The insects are a nuisance, their hum adding yet another layer to the sound, but they are also seen as a sign of healthy water.

## The Unchanging Heart

Most profoundly, the people of the basin live inside the equilibrium. They have no concept of progress, no yearning for expansion or change. Their verbs are all in the present continuous tense. To ask "What will you do tomorrow?" is a nonsensical question. The only meaningful answer is, "We will do as we do."

Outsiders might see stagnation. The people see a deep, abiding peace. They are synchronized with their world on a level that feels mystical to those from places of seasons and change. They feel the constant pulse of the geothermal heart through their bare feet. They hear the unchanging symphony of the basin as a lullaby. The steam that obscures the sky also obscures any thought of a different horizon.

Their lament, if it exists, is not for something lost, but for the sheer, heavy beauty of a world that holds so perfectly still. They are not its masters, nor its victims. They are its most conscious inhabitants, thinking and breathing and speaking in time with its hiss and its click and its boom. They are woven into the noise, part of the pattern, and in the endless, steam-choked afternoon of the basin, that is enough.
