The inhabitants are a quiet, saltwater-boned people. They are not born here, not exactly—they are rotated in, born in annexes or on orbital stations or in shallower, greener seas—but from the first submersion, the structure begins to grow into their bones. Their skin carries a permanent chill, not from cold but from constant pressure adaptation. They speak in a dialect softened by earpiece static and acoustic distortion, full of clipped terms for pressure differentials, salvage rights, and biofilm clearance.

They live in pods of six or twelve, housed in modular pressurized cylinders welded into the walls of service corridors. Their quarters are cramped, warm, and dry—a shocking contrast to the vast wet world outside. They sleep to the heartbeat of the hydro-pumps. They eat algal protein and cultured fish, flavored with mineral salt. They do not dream of open sky; their dreams are of unlit chambers and failing seals, of the hum stopping.

They are custodians, not conquerors. Their jobs are maintenance, monitoring, and slow expansion. A crew chief might spend her life studying the acoustic map of a single mega-chamber, knowing its currents like a shepherd knows hills. A welder might memorize the particular song of hull-plate in sector 7, how it groans at different pressures. They mark their bodies with tattoos of schematic diagrams, pressure tolerances, and the names of lost colleagues—lost to accidents, to the eels, to the occasional sudden failure of a bulkhead.

They have a reverence for the structure’s breathing. When the great hydraulic cycles begin—the slow continent-wide tides—they will pause in their work, steadying themselves against a handrail, feeling the water move around them like a shared inhalation. It is a liturgy.

The fish are not pets, but they are neighbours. Workers learn to distinguish the warning click of a territorial moray in the pipes, the silvery shimmer of a mackerel school signaling stable water quality, the deliberate, heavy drift of a chamber-shark near the nutrient outflows. Some old-timers claim the creatures know the schedules, that they avoid the construction zones and gather to watch the cargo subs like a crowd watches a parade.

And then there are the other inhabitants. The ones mentioned in cautionary logs and pointed silences. The Things in the Decommissioned Cores. Engineers who map the deep chambers with long-range sonar pick up shapes that are too large, too organized, too *patient* to be mere geological remnants. They are not monsters; they are consequences. Life, finding an endless, dark, resource-rich niche, will eventually explore all possible forms. Some of those forms have grown wise in the dark. They do not attack. They *inhabit*. Their presence is felt as a damping of noise, a subtle shift in current, a sense of being watched by something vast and slow and utterly indifferent.

The humans do not hunt them. They do not seek them out. They log the data, adjust the routes, and give them space—like acknowledging a mountain or a weather system. It is part of the cost of living in a world you did not fully build, in an ocean that remembers it is an ocean.

In the end, the greatest inhabitants are the machines and the water itself. The drones are as familiar as dogs, the generators as constant as the sun, the silt-laden currents as reliable as seasons. The water is not a barrier; it is a medium of existence, as fundamental as air. It carries sound, warmth, waste, and warning. It presses on every surface, a constant, unifying presence.

They are all—the people, the feral fauna, the deep things, the humming machines, the architecture of absence—bound together in a cold, loud, slow, and magnificent symbiosis. The structure breathes, and they live in its lungs. It is not a home they chose. It is a home that chose them, because they could endure it. And in enduring, they have become something else: the first generation of true continental inhabitants, a species defined not by land, but by the intricate, endless possibility of the built deep.
