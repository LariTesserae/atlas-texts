Here is who lives in that place.

They are not creatures of the air, though they have it above them. They are creatures of the **threshold**—of the surface where the living green meets the dead black, where water is held in a tension between sky and peat, where the constant hum from below becomes a felt thing in their bodies. They are the **Fauna of the Mat**, and they do not live *on* it so much as they live *as part of its texture*.

**The First Folk are the Jumpers,** the springtails. They are the static, the grain, the primary fact of motion here. They are not truly animals in the way you think; they are more like a **weather phenomenon**. A single one is a punctuation mark, a mote of life with a singular imperative: to leap. But in aggregate, they are a **seismic field**. Their hopping is not random; it follows the capillary paths of moisture in the moss. Where the mat holds more water, they swarm, their collective impact creating a faint, percussive susurrus that is the first layer of the mat’s voice. They are the cleanup crew of the microscopic, consuming fungi, algae, and the decayed bits of their own kind that settle into the weave. Their lives are measured in millimeters and moisture gradients. They are the living, hopping skin of the peat.

**The Second Folk are the Watchers and the Walkers.** The mites are **jewels set in the axils**, orange and red, processing the slow decay. They are philosophers of rot, moving with a deliberate, eight-legged gait that ignores the frantic jumping of the springtails. The spiders are **architects of the margins**. They do not spin webs here—there is nothing to anchor them to. Instead, they are vagabond hunters, their impossibly long legs treading lightly on the moss-surface like snowshoes, sensing the vibrations of the jumpers through the mat itself. They hunt by patience and by touch, in the quiet spaces between the louder, leaping life. The beetles are **pilgrims on a journey without destination**. Their iridescent shells catch the flat, grey light like oily water. They move with purpose from capsule to capsule, inspecting the pale stalks, tasting the condensation, their mandibles working with a faint, inaudible click. They are the mat’s quality control, testing the health of the fruiting bodies.

**The Third Folk are the Hidden and the Fast.** Beneath the surface, in the upper layers of peat where the green begins to bleed into olive, live the centipedes and their kind. They are **rumors in the substrate**, pale and blindingly quick when disturbed, like nerves firing. They are the first true predators you sense—a hierarchy now confirmed. They move through the peat like thoughts through a mind, hunting the larvae and the eggs hidden in the weave. Their presence explains the equilibrium. The clicking, stridulating sound that fills the air is not just communication; it is a **language of location**. *I am here. I am this size. I am moving left.* It is a constant broadcast that allows the jumpers to avoid the slow-walking watchers, and the watchers to triangulate the hidden and the fast. The mat itself is the **transmitter**. Its spongy body is a superb conductor, turning the footsteps and drummings of billions into a grainy, ambient roar—the sound of a billion tiny agreements not to step on each other’s feet.

**And then there is the Drone.**

They do not hear it as you do. To them, it is not a sound but a **state of being**, like gravity. It is the vibration in their bones, the hum that synchronizes their pulses. It is the **heartbeat of the deep substrate**, the ancient machine that, perhaps, warms the deepest peat layers just enough to keep the metabolic rate of this entire system above the threshold of true dormancy. The fauna have not merely adapted to it; they are **tuned** to it. The rhythm of a springtail’s leap, the oscillation of a moth’s wing at rest, the predatory pause of a spider—all are subtly phased to the cycles of that deep, mechanical note. They are living in the **key of the Machine**.

**Who lives here, then?**

They are a **single, distributed organism**. The mat is the body. The fauna are its active metabolism, its nervous system, its cleanup crew, its immune response. The vibration is its heartbeat. The constant, clicking roar is its thoughts. You see individuals—a mite, a moth—but to understand them, you must see the **whole system**. They are a **continuity**. Their births and deaths are not events but **phases of a nutrient loop**. A springtail dies and is consumed by a mite; the mite’s waste feeds the fungus that feeds the next generation of springtails. Nothing is lost. Everything is cycled. Their predation is not a drama but a **bookkeeping exercise**, a quiet balancing of accounts that has been running in the black for millennia.

They are **patient**. Their time is the time of the peat—slow, layered, cumulative. They have settled into the final configuration. Your footstep is a cataclysm. But already, they are working to erase it. The springtails investigate the edges of the print. The moss begins its imperceptible creep into the disturbed soil. The vibration hums on. They will be here long after your bootprint has been composted into the next millimeter of peat. They are the **living will of the place**, the part of the mat that moves, and eats, and sings in a frequency just below your hearing, a community so stable it has forgotten the concept of change.

They are the answer to the question: **What lives in a place that has already become eternal?** The answer is: **Everything you see. And it is all the same thing.**
