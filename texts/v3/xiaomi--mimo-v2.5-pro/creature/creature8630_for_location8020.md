The sound settles into you first. Not just hearing it—*feeling* it. A low, constant vibration in the floorboards that climbs into your bones, a deep hum that makes the peat dust in the air tremble. It’s the heartbeat of the place, and you learn to stand with your weight a little forward, leaning into it, a posture the old-timers have without knowing.

My name’s in the ledger as **Finlay Gosse**, Millwright and Keeper of the Pumps. Been here since the foundation stones were laid. They needed someone who understood not just how to make the machinery talk, but how to make the land listen. Or at least, how to negotiate.

I live in the end room of the long bunkhouse. It’s not much. A narrow bed, a stove that smells of damp peat and engine oil, a shelf of heavy, grease-stained ledgers. The window looks out over Channel Seven, the newest cut. The walls are thick with a wooly moss I encourage—it drinks the condensation and keeps the damp from the wood. Every morning, I wake to the two-note whistle of that bird. *You-kip*. *You-kip*. A sound as constant as the engine’s pulse. I’ve never seen the creature. It exists as pure sound, a part of the wetland’s own machinery.

My work is the truce.

At dawn, I walk the circuit. My boots know the path without my eyes—the hard-packed gravel, the springy give of a boardwalk over a young alder’s roots, the slick brick of the mill’s perimeter. The frogs are the loudest then, a chorus so layered it’s like the voices are being carded, one into the other. You learn to listen *through* them to the other sounds: the falter in a belt’s whine that means a stitch is wearing thin, the higher pitch of a bearing needing grease, the particular *hiss* of steam that means a valve is clean. The mill speaks its needs. The pumps are my particular conversation.

They sit at the settlement’s edge, four squat iron beasts, their pistons a slower, deeper rhythm beneath the mill’s scream. They pull the water from the peat, push it out into the reed beds. And the reed beds drink, and grow, and push back. I spend hours in the channels, not in a boat, but wading. Knee-deep in black water that’s surprisingly warm from the pump discharge. My task is to clear the intakes—pulling out tangles of the vivid green algae, the fibrous roots of sedges that have colonized the outflow. It’s not a war. You don’t fight the wetland. You just… redirect its ambition. You cut a channel to guide the willow roots away from a foundation. You place a stone to change the silt’s deposition. You tell the land, *You can have this later. But not this, not yet.* The moss on the north walls, the ferns in the stonework—I don’t clear those. Those are the land’s signatures. Its slow, patient claim.

The workers—they’re a quiet, steady lot. They live by the engine’s time, their faces often grimed with peat dust or machine oil. They nod to me as I pass. They see me talking to the mud, to the pumps, to the wall of reeds. They think I’m part eccentric, part necessary evil. Maybe they’re right. They don’t see what I see: that the swallows nesting in the engine house eaves are mapping the same thermal drafts the engineers use to feed the boiler. That the fox, trotting past at dusk, follows the path of the warmest drainage outflow. We’re all just finding the currents, the paths of least resistance in this young, spongy ground.

I have a particular place. It’s not my room. It’s a slight rise beyond the last shed, where someone once piled old machine parts. They’ve been swallowed now—a rusted flywheel half-submerged in sedge, a tangle of chains becoming a trellis for vetch. From there, you can see the whole negotiation. The mill, its smokestack a blunt finger pointing at the grey sky, the sound a solid wall. And then the land, flowing in. The alders, willow-thin, framing the scene. The channels, dark veins. The sense that everything—the gravel road, the planks of the walkways, the very bricks of the mill—is temporary scaffolding on something older and patient.

I am part of the scaffolding. My hands know the cold kiss of iron and the slick, living feel of wet peat. My ears hold the engine’s drone and the frog’s song in the same chamber. When I stand at my rise, I am not watching industry encroach on nature, or nature reclaim a site. I’m watching two vast, slow things meeting. One driven by steam and human need, the other by water and the deep, quiet logic of decomposition and growth.

They think I’m keeping the pumps running. I know I’m listening to the argument, and trying to write the next chapter in the ledger, just a line or two ahead of the silt and the roots. The damp will win. It writes in centuries. My ledger is in decades. But my job is to make sure the next page has a place for both the turn of the drive shaft and the uncurling of a fern. That’s the sound of Breckhaven, in the end: not the mill or the frogs, but the held space between them, where you can hear both, and understand they are, for now, the same rhythm.
