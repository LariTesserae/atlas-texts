The Weeping Terrace is not merely occupied; it is *inhabited*. Not by a teeming crowd, but by a quiet, ancient congress of things that have woven themselves into the very fabric of the place, becoming its living expression. What lives here does not dominate; it *endures*, *transforms*, and *participates* in the slow, humid conversation between stone, water, and heat.

**The Dwellers in the Water and Stone:**

First, there is the **Thermophryn**. The elongated, multi-limbed amphibian glimpsed in the basins is not an amphibian at all, not in any taxonomic sense you would recognize. It is a *Thermophryn*, a "heat-lover." Its six limbs, tipped with wide, paddle-like digits, are as much for navigating the viscous, mineral-rich water as for moving on the warm, slick rock. Its coppery eyes are not just reflective; they perceive heat gradients and mineral concentrations, seeing the water as a landscape of shifting energy and solute. Its skin is a mosaic of chromatophores and osmotic patches, allowing it to absorb vital metals directly from the water and shift its hue from the dark basalt grey to the rufous red of the fern undersides. It is patient, carnivorous, feeding on the **Silica-Striders**—those large, gelatinous-winged insects that land on the pale-barked trees. The Thermophryn’s hunt is a slow-motion ambush, a single, deliberate surge from the water over a period of minutes. They are solitary, communicating through subtle vibrations in the mineral crust, and their eggs are laid in the hollow stems of the humming trees, where the constant, sub-audible vibration aids their development.

**The Canopy of the Air and Bark:**

The **Silica-Strider** (or *Geloptera*) is not an insect in the chitinous sense. Its "wings" are structured membranes of silica and organic polymers, harvested from the very mineral-rich waters. They fold with a soft, crystalline sound. It feeds not on leaves, but on the biofilms of extremophile microbes that coat the wet stone and the mineral deposits rimming the pools. Its life cycle is tied to the weeping: larvae are aquatic, developing in the shallowest, warmest basins before pupating on the leathery fern fronds. The adults are drawn to the humming trees, where the vibration seems to aid in their mating ritual—a delicate tapping of their hardened ovipositors against the pale bark in rhythms that sync with the tree’s hum.

The **Trees** themselves—the **Basalt-Bones** or **Veinwood**—are the most profound inhabitants. They are not trees in the conventional sense. They are symbiotic complexes. The pale, peeling bark is a living mineral crust, secreted by the tree to protect its vascular system from the corrosive water and direct heat. The geometric veining visible in their semi-translucent leaves is a network of both fluid transport and a kind of biological circuitry. They do not merely photosynthesize; they *piezoelectrically harvest* the constant low-level vibrations from the geothermal thrum of the mountain, converting it into chemical energy that supplements their photosynthesis. The hum is the sound of this process, a resonance between the stone's pulse and their own biology. Their thick, coral-like roots are not just anchors; they are mining filaments, slowly dissolving and absorbing specific minerals from the rock, which are then processed and exuded in their leaves and bark, contributing to the terrace's unique mineralogy.

**The Tenants of the Crust:**

The **Black Ferns** (*Noctifilix*) and the **Chartreuse Mosses** (*Luxmus*) are the primary architects of the visible biosphere. The ferns' waxy, leathery fronds are coated in a heat-reflective pigment. They do not just root; they *fuse* with the mineral crust. Their rhizomes are a hybrid structure—part root, part mineral-depositing organ. They actively participate in building the scalloped rims of the basins, secreting ions that accelerate mineral precipitation in patterns that guide water flow, creating a feedback loop between biology and geology. The mosses are even more integrated; they form a living skin over large areas of wet rock, their brilliant green a sign of an almost wasteful efficiency in capturing the spectrally unique light that filters through the humid air. They are the first step in the food chain, grazed upon by microbial mats and, occasionally, by the larvae of the Silica-Striders.

**The Echoes in the Stone:**

The **Artifacts** are not truly separate from the living system. They are the **Paleolithe**—the Old Stone. They are remnants of a previous cycle, a prior expression of this place. The dark embedded surface with its faint pulse is not a machine, but a fossilized organ of a much larger, now-vanished organism or ecosystem that once thrived here when the mountain was young or perhaps even before its uplift. The terrace's current inhabitants interact with it subtly. The Thermophryn sometimes rests upon it, its coppery eyes seeing the faint pulse as a landmark. The Veinwood's roots sometimes grow around it, sensing its residual vibrations. The mineral deposits are slowly, reverently, incorporating it back into the stone. It is a memory, still faintly resonating, being slowly read and rewritten by the new life.

**The Cycle Itself:**

In essence, what lives here is a **geothermal biome in mid-expression**. It is a single, slow organism with multiple, cooperating parts. The *Thermophryn* balances the *Geloptera* population. The *Veinwood* stabilizes the rock and creates microhabitats. The *Noctifilix* and *Luxmus* build the substrate. The weeping water is its circulatory system, the rising mineral crust its growing bone, the hum its heartbeat, the pulse in the Paleolithe a remembered dream. Nothing is invasive; everything is endemic. They live not *on* the terrace, but *as* the terrace—the living, weeping skin of a geothermal mountain, breathing at a pace measured in decades and centuries, forever in the process of becoming itself.
