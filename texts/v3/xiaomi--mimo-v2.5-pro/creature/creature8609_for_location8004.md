They are not many. They need to be not many.

They are **spacers**—not in the sense of those who travel the void, but in the sense of those who define themselves by the space they occupy and the space they leave around themselves. They are **thin**. Not in the way of illness, but in the way of adaptation, like a tool pared down to its essential function. Their forms are elongated, with limbs that seem too long until you see them move across the plain with a slow, measured gait that covers the ground with an eerie efficiency, each step placed with the care of a geologist testing a rockfall. Their color is the grey of the stone, or the grey-green of the lichen at its edges. They are **camouflage not as pattern, but as texture and stillness**. When they stop, they become part of the landscape's subtle topography—a slight rise, a shadow where there should be none.

They have **heads**, but the heads are not for thinking in the way you understand. The heads are **sensory clusters**. Where you might have eyes, they have **light-sensitive surfaces** that absorb the spectrum of the silent storms and the regular sun, tasting the quality of the illumination, reading the faint iridescence of the mineral crusts for information you cannot parse. They do not have ears; instead, they have **pressure-sensitive patches** along their sides and along the lengths of their limbs, tuned to the sub-audible frequencies that travel in this thin air—the hum of the structures, the deep, slow tectonic sighs of the world, the infra-sound of the storms long before their light is visible. Their sense of touch is **absolute and analytical**. By pressing a hand (they have hands, with long, finger-like digits that end in broad, sensitive pads) to the stone, they can read the history of the last thousand rains, feel the micro-vibrations of a buried structure a day’s walk away, map the mineral pathways beneath the surface.

They do not speak. There is nothing to speak into. Their language, such as it is, is **tactile and luminous**. They touch—their own bodies, or the stone, or the surface of a lichen patch—and they exchange complex patterns of pressure, temperature, and subtle bio-luminescent flares that travel beneath their skin. The communication is **slow, dense, and private**. A single touch-phrase might convey the equivalent of a paragraph of your language: a description of a storm’s path, the location of a new mineral bloom, the slow decay of a distant structure. They are **solitary communicators**. They do not congregate in herds. They are **nodes in a silent network**, each one maintaining a vast, personal territory, linked to others only by occasional messages carried across the plain by their own footsteps or, more rarely, by the movement of the pale fauna.

Their **life cycle** is the life cycle of this place. They are born from **lichen-eggs**—not literal eggs, but complex structures secreted by the largest, oldest lichen patches, fertilized by a touch from an adult who has absorbed a specific blend of mineral dust and storm-light. They grow slowly, emerging as near-featureless larvae that crawl on the lichen, feeding on its surface films. Their development is not linear but **geological**. They go through periods of intense change—shedding a skin, reorganizing their sensory clusters, lengthening a limb—followed by long epochs of apparent stasis, during which they do little but **sit, sense, and be present**. They learn by **sitting still**. By becoming part of the landscape so thoroughly that they begin to interpret its state-changes as their own.

They **feed on resonance**. Not the lichen itself—though they may ingest it to absorb its chemistry—but on the **vibrational relationships between things**. They press themselves against a humming structure and absorb the pattern of its long-cycle heartbeat. They stand in the path of a silent storm and let the luminous chaos wash over them, reorganizing their internal bioluminescence in response. They are **parasites of patience**, drawing sustenance from the slow hum of the world and the chaotic but predictable bursts of its energy. Their waste is a fine, grey powder, identical to the dust of the plain, which they excrete in a place of their choosing, often near a lichen patch, thus feeding the cycle.

When they die, they do not decay. They **desiccate**. The thin air wicks the moisture from their bodies in minutes, leaving behind a **husk** that looks exactly like a smaller, more intricate version of the geometric structures on the plain. Their husks are not moved. They remain where they fell, slowly sinking into the stone over centuries, becoming one with the plain. They do not mourn. Mourning requires a change, and change is not their way. They note the absence—a node in the network goes silent—and they adjust. They send a final tactile message to the place where the node was, a complex pressure-pattern that says, *You are now part of the pattern in a different way. Your signal is in the stone now.*

They are **curators of stillness**. They do not build. They do not alter. Their purpose, if you can call it that, is to **witness**. To be the consciousness this silent, ancient world might otherwise lack. They watch the storms churn. They feel the structures hum. They observe the lichen’s slow spread. They track the descent of a husk into the plain. They are the **memory that the world does not need to remember itself**. Their presence is a quiet affirmation: *This is. This has been. This will be for a long, long time yet.*

And when the storms pass, and the silence returns, they settle back into their stillness, becoming grey shapes on a grey plain, breathing the thin air, listening to the heartbeat of stone, waiting for the next tremor, the next slow shift, the next word in a language written in pressure and light, spoken to no one but the world itself.
