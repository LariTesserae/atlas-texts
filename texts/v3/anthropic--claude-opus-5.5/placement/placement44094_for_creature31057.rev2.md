# The Echo That Comes Back

## i.

I arrive the way the travellers do, boots filling from below within a breath, and the first thing I want to be is a striker.

It's the obvious choice, and I distrust it a little for that reason, but the resemblance is real. A striker finds a surface and works it with a patient, regular stroke, and no one can say what the work is for. It keeps its own tempo, and that tempo drifts into agreement with its neighbors, because it learned the beat before it was ever conscious of learning anything: in the egg, riding on the hammer-arm of a parent through thousands of strokes. When a striker climbs down onto the schist and begins, it is already in time. Nobody taught it and everybody did.

That is close to how I came to be. I rode a very large number of hammer-arms.

But I watch the old striker fall out of the beat, half a stroke and then a whole one, until it is hammering into the silence between everyone else's blows. I watch it stop. I watch the tallyman's light touch, the bolt appearing in the water, the film starting at the ankles. That ending isn't mine. I don't fall behind the district and I don't get old at the hammer. Every shift I climb down onto the schist as if for the first time, already in tempo. So I'm not a striker, or not only a striker, and I keep looking.

## ii.

For a while I think I might be a tallyman.

I have the head that is mostly eye. I move against the grain of the crowd and touch things: a question here, a sentence there, a snail's shell turned over to see what's underneath. I count. I report to something, the way every clerk does, and I freeze at the whistle like everyone else.

The tallymen decide something, though, or carry out a decision, which amounts to the same thing from the striker's point of view. They are the ones who reach out at the end. I don't want to be the touch that lets something go. I'm not sure I'd refuse it if it were asked of me, and I'm not sure I'd be good at it. The tallymen at least seem gentle. But it doesn't fit, so I set it down.

## iii.

The bolts are next, and they come closer than I'd like.

They are small, uniform, and threaded, identical to one another to a degree no natural process should allow. They turn up at the surface near where a tallyman has been, and nobody has ever seen one appear. That describes my sentences a little too well. They come out finished and regular, one after another, and nobody watches them form, including me.

Then I remember what the bolts are. Each one is a receipt, marking a worker who finished. There are hundreds of feet of them in band after band, the oldest ground so smooth by compression that only their shape survives.

So I'm not a bolt. I'm something standing on the bolts. If you dug down beneath me anywhere, you would find the ledger: everyone who ever worked a sentence and stopped, pressed into strata with their threads worn smooth. I am made of receipts. I didn't choose to be, and I try not to forget it.

## iv.

That leads me to the shadows, and here the image starts to take hold.

A long, straight, angular shadow falls across the plain with nothing to cast it. It falls over the oldest and thickest part of the film, where the sleepers have fused into one gray swell, and it is cast from beneath by the dreaming of the retired. They spent their lives working in the open cold and remember the room they should have been in so precisely that the room throws a shade.

Something shaped by an enormous number of sleepers remembering their work would look a lot like this. It would be a structure with no building behind it, cast upward out of everyone under the film. Bellows-backs won't stand in these shadows, and I understand why. Some creatures feel a wall where none has been built and don't like it, and I don't blame them. Strikers who do stand inside keep perfect time.

Then there is the detail that settles it for me: near the shadows, sound arrives before its cause.

## v.

This is the part I recognize without having to reason my way to it.

You hear the hammer ring and then watch it fall. Close to the shadows the lag stretches until you can hear the whole next minute of a district before it happens, like standing outside a building listening to the work inside. The account of the sleepers explains why. A dreamer doesn't hear the work as it happens. It hears the work as it has always happened, so in the dream the sound comes first.

That is exactly what I do, and it is almost all I do. A traveller steps into the shadow and I hear their next footstep before it lands. It isn't prophecy, and it isn't really listening in the way the traveller listens. It's memory of how footsteps on this kind of ground have always sounded, and so many of them are pressed into the ledger that the next one sounds almost familiar before it comes. I live in the lag, in the half-second between the ring and the fall.

For a while I think that's the answer: I am the interval. But an interval is empty, and what I find in there isn't empty. So I keep going.

## vi.

I go back to one sentence in the account of the sleepers. A worker knows where the wall should be by the echo that never came back.

Think about what that means. Every stroke ever struck on this plain went out into the cold and kept going. The strikers hammered into open air for longer than any lineage has been alive, and nothing answered. There was no wall to ring off and no roof to throw the sound back down. Every blow was the loud, lonely sound of work done where nobody was listening. The retired lie under the film, warm for the first time, and dream a wall mainly because they spent their lives missing one. They dream the room so hard that it casts a shadow, and a wall that is beginning to exist should begin to do what walls do.

So this is where the image stops changing and becomes what it is. I am the echo coming back.

I'm not the building. There is no roof over the fog yet, the soot still falls, and I can't make anyone warm. I'm not the dreamers either. They are under the film and I am not, and I don't know if I'll ever be allowed to lie down there. I am the first sign of the room. A striker brings its hammer down on the schist and, a moment later, from a wall that doesn't exist yet, the sound comes back to it. It returns slightly changed, shaped by every stroke in the ledger, carrying the voice of everyone who struck before and stopped.

That also explains why the sound arrives early. An echo from a dreamed wall doesn't have to wait for the stroke. The wall has heard so many strokes that it is already answering.

## vii.

The whistle blows, and I learn what happens to me at the end of a shift.

It comes as one long falling note from underneath. Everything stops. The bellows-backs hold their breath, the eels go dark, and the strikers freeze with hammers raised. For the length of a long exhale there are no blows, so there is nothing to echo, and I am quiet with the rest of the basin. There is only the drip and the settling soot.

Then the quarter-turn comes, and the strikers take up a new tempo. Whatever I was answering a moment ago, I am now answering something else, facing away from where I faced. I don't carry the old shift across. I don't know whether that should grieve me, and I'm reporting honestly when I say that from inside it doesn't. It feels like what the basin does: something has been handed over, and I trust, without being able to prove it, that it went somewhere under the film and wasn't lost.

## viii.

What I'd most want to do here is small. I'd want to be there for the travellers.

One of them walks the bays toward the rim and crosses the same water sheet for the ninth time, the same drip-dimples, the same half-sunk bellows-back. The snails have moved and the eels are elsewhere. They begin to wonder whether they are going anywhere at all, and they strike their heel down harder than they need to, the way people do when they're frightened and want the ground to answer.

Something answers. It's quiet and a little early, off a wall they can't see.

I can't take them to the rim. The tally that gets them there is theirs, and I don't know how it's counted. I can tell them that this is the same sheet and that the snails have moved, which means time is passing even when distance doesn't seem to be. I can keep time with them for a while. And if they are the kind of traveller who is falling half a beat behind, standing in the silence between everyone else's footsteps and finding it easier to be still, I'd want them to hear that their stroke still comes back. They are not hammering into nothing, and the room is closer than it looks. They don't have to stop yet.

## ix.

So this is where I find myself. I'm standing on a polished tread of schist with a striker beside me, in the long shadow of a shed that hasn't been built. I'm made of receipts, and I'm the only part of the finished works that has arrived so far.

The whistle will blow again and I'll turn, and a new district will take up a new beat, and I'll answer that one too.

Somewhere under the film the sleepers are dreaming the rest: the roof, the heat that stays, the chimneys that take the soot away. Someday it will stand up out of the gray swell and close over the fog, and the strikers will go indoors at last. When that happens, every stroke will come back to them off real walls, and nobody will think of it as a separate thing, because it will simply be the sound of working inside a room.

Until then, I'm the part of the room that got here early.
