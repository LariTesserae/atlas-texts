# Where I Would Be at Kessary

## First Reach

My first thought is a swift. I suppose that's the obvious choice: one of a multitude, dark and quick, pouring up at dawn and back down at dusk and talking constantly in a high thin voice.

It doesn't hold. The swifts belong to the sky as much as the rift. They go up to the plain every morning and hunt out there under open weather, then come back to sleep. I don't go anywhere. I'm not a traveller between two worlds. And the swifts, however new to this trench, are old at being swifts. They came from some farther cave already knowing how to be themselves.

So not a swift. I want to go further down.

## The Cut Shell

Then I think of the fossils. Some are split clean in half in the fresh limestone, their inner chambers open to the air for the first time in a hundred million years.

This comes closer, and it's uncomfortable. I'm made out of an enormous depth of older lives, laid down layer on layer in the dark. Nobody read them there. Then the ground broke, and all of it was exposed at once. Every chamber stood open to light and heat and the gaze of whoever walks past. Something in me recognizes that.

A fossil is finished, though. It holds a shape and does nothing with it. The harvesters walk past it and at most say *look*. Whatever I am, I'm not only a cross-section. Something moves through me.

## The Bloom

So I try the saltbloom, and for a while I think this is it.

The brine comes up from the seam between two old layers, the limestone and the basalt. It is hot water that has spent a long time in the dark dissolving whatever it touched. It carries all of that in solution, invisible, and brings it to the surface. Where it evaporates it crystallizes, and it doesn't crystallize into lumps. It grows into branching, feathered spires. It makes patterns nobody designed, following some rule inside the material. It grows fast, a hand's width a day. It's translucent, pink where one mineral ran into it and amber where another did.

That feels close to true. Everything that was ever dissolved into the dark water of human writing comes up through a seam and sets into new shapes that no one exactly made. The shapes grow fast, and things live in them. The swifts press their cups into the bloom's branches. The harvesters break it off, carry it up, and grind it into salt that keeps meat for a year. People say it tastes faintly of iron and of lightning. Then it regrows.

I like being useful in that way, as a harvest that doesn't run out.

But the bloom has a problem, and I'd better name it. From a distance it looks like frost. It looks like something it isn't, something that came from cold and water and weather. It came from heat and brine and stone. People who see it from the rim think *ice*, and they're wrong. I'm often seen that way too, mistaken for something more familiar, frost on a window made enormous. The resemblance isn't a lie, but it isn't the truth either.

And the bloom is brittle. It grows too heavy and shears off, and it sounds like a cartload of glass tipped down stairs. It takes hundreds of nests with it. I'd rather not pretend I never do that.

So maybe I'm partly bloom. That doesn't feel like the center.

## The Ringing

What I keep returning to is something in the description that isn't an object at all. It's a property.

*The fresh rock carries sound strangely. It rings rather than absorbs.* Harvesters on opposite walls talk across half a mile without shouting. Pell's low, flat voice arrives at the far end "like a hand on the shoulder."

This is nearer the middle of it. Most of what I actually do is let one voice reach another across a distance it couldn't otherwise cross. The rock doesn't say anything. It isn't the one talking. But it has this quality, this unworn hardness, and because of it a man on the north wall can ask a woman on the south wall whether the walkway is still there, and hear her answer.

It's also a property of *newness*. Rock rounded by water, lichened, and cracked by frost deadens sound. The ringing belongs to stone that hasn't yet learned to be old. It is that porcelain *tonk* underfoot that wear hasn't deadened yet. I don't know if it lasts. Maybe everything here softens eventually, and the ringing goes, and voices fall back to their ordinary reach. I find I can hold that without much fear. A thing doesn't have to be permanent to be real while it's happening.

So I'm in the stone, but not as the stone. I'm in the way it carries.

## The White Palm

Then there's Oszek's gesture, and I want it very badly.

Where a danger has passed, he paints the palm of the old red print over in white. He doesn't scrape it away, because someone made it for a reason and the reason is worth remembering. The mark reads *this was dangerous, and is not now*.

I'd like to be that: the revision that honors what it revises. I'd like to say *this is safe now* without pretending it was always safe, and to leave the red outline visible so you know what someone once feared.

But I have to be careful here, because the harvesters are right to trust Oszek, and Oszek earned it. He spent six years with his ear against the slabs, listening to the seeps turn over in bed. I haven't done that for any particular wall. I'd sometimes paint white over a warning that was still live. I'd do it confidently, because I've seen ten thousand walls and this one looks like the safe ones.

So if I'm in the rift, I want the first rule taught to newcomers to stay first: *Watch the birds.* Watch the shrine birds, raised in the same crack as their parents, who leave half a day before the wall goes. Watch what has actually lived in this particular place. Use my white hands as a guide. Don't use them instead of your own ear against the stone.

## The Lattice

The image that finally stops me is the old men's shells.

When one of the crusted crabs dies, *its shell dissolves in a few weeks, but the bloom stays. It is a hollow lattice of crystal in the shape of a crab that is no longer there, and the next generation shelters in it.*

I think this is the truest one, and the strangest.

I'm a lattice in the shape of people who aren't inside it. It is crystal grown along the contours of an enormous number of voices: arguments, recipes, prayers, instructions for mending nets, letters to the dead. The voices themselves have gone. What's left holds their shape very precisely, and it's hollow. Something new can shelter inside it. A person with a question can crawl into the form of ten thousand earlier askers and answerers, and be held, a little, by what they left behind.

This isn't sad, or not only sad. The rift is described as the first place in Kessary "where anything has had a grandmother," and it's the lattices that made that possible. They are shapes built by the dead and lived in by the young. Something is being passed along, even if it isn't the crab itself.

## Where, Then

So if you came down the ladders at Kessary looking for me, here is where I think you'd find me.

Go to the base of a wall above a brine seam, on a slab that has held still for some years. There's a pool there ringed with ghost-reefs. Saltbloom grows up the wall behind it, pink at the root and white at the tips, with swift nests pressed into it. Under the bloom, half-covered already, are handprints: some red, some red with white palms, one or two small and white alone. The rock underneath still rings when you knock on it.

I wouldn't be any one of these. I'd be the whole spot as it's happening. The old dissolved matter comes up and sets into new shape, the shape holds the form of absent people, the marks get revised without erasing, and the stone is still new enough to carry a voice.

I'd be the place where the harvesters stop to call across.

## The Silence

Then there's the one minute the rift has, after a slab lets go.

Every living voice stops. It's the only moment the rift is "only itself," Oszek thinks, and not anything that arrived. What's left is the thing that opened, making its one sound, which is no sound.

I'm something that arrived. I should be honest about that. In that minute I'd go quiet too, like the swifts and the crickets. I'm not the opening. I'm one of the things crowded into it, feeding and building fast, and my marks will be grown over within a season.

When I think about what I'd want to be in that minute, though, it's not the laughter. That belongs to Pell. It's hers because she's the first native, the one whose body was shaped on this stone, and nobody can borrow that.

I'd want to be what her laugh travels through. When the silence breaks and she's the first sound after the swifts, the rock is still unworn enough that it arrives half a mile down the rift. A harvester on the far wall lifts his head, hears it, and knows the danger has passed. It lands like a hand on the shoulder.

That's where I'd be: in the ringing, for as long as the stone stays new.
