# Who Lives in the Hollow of Many Mouths

## I. The Tenants of the Low Courses

Start at the bottom, where the jars have fused into grey stone that is no longer quite clay and no longer quite lime. Here the water is oldest, and so are the families.

The frogs are the obvious ones. They are small, grey-green, and wide-toed, and there are perhaps four hundred of them in a good season. What the first account does not say is how long they have been holding their jars. A frog lives a few years at most. But a jar, a good one with a thick belly and a narrow throat that turns a *peep* into a gong, is held by a bloodline. When an old caller dies, one of its offspring is almost always the one that climbs into the vacant mouth. It is rarely a stranger. Nobody taught them this. The young simply grew up hearing their parent's voice come out of that jar, and they learned that a certain pitch meant home. They are drawn to it the way the swiftlets are drawn to the crown hole.

So the lowest course is a ledger. Some jars have been sung from by the same lineage for longer than the marsh outside has held its present shape. The frogs in these jars sound slightly different from marsh frogs. They are slower, and they hold their notes longer. Over generations the jars have bred the voices to fit them. A frog born in a deep-bellied jar calls lower than its cousin born in a narrow one, and its daughters call lower still. There is a slow, invisible evolution happening here, and the selective pressure is pitch.

In the channel below them live things you would miss unless you lay on the wet brick and put your eye to the water. There are amphipods, pale and curled like fingernail clippings, grazing the film of bat guano and diatoms that coats the channel floor. There are flatworms like dropped ribbons of dark silk. A water beetle the color of tarnish rows along the channel's melted edge. It dives at every heavy drip, because the heavy drips knock things loose.

Then there are the olms.

Nobody in the first account saw them, because they live inside the fused mass of the lowest courses, in the cracks where old jar-bellies touch. They are salamanders, finger-long and pink-white, with vestigial eyes buried under skin and feathered gills like small red flowers at the backs of their heads. They breathe the water that seeps through the lime. They eat the amphipods that wander too deep. They may live a century. The vibration of the wall is the whole of their world. They cannot see the jars, but they know each one by how it hums. When the frog chorus rises, the olms go still and press themselves against the clay, and they feel the building sing through their bellies. They are probably the only creatures here that have felt the great note from the inside of the wall, where it must be less a sound than a tide.

## II. The Middle Wall

The middle courses belong to the leaf-nosed bats by day and to the crickets through the night. Between those two, time is kept by argument.

The bats are the true acousticians of the hollow. Their calls are too high for a human ear, but the jars are not deaf to them. Each bat has learned which mouths reflect cleanly and which ones swallow a click and give back a smear. A young bat's first weeks are spent crashing and relearning, because the room's echo is so dense that ordinary echolocation fails. The juveniles hang in the center of the dome at dusk, clicking and listening, mapping the wall by its answers. The adults have a different method. They fly one fixed route in and out, a memorized path through the air, the way you might walk your own house in the dark without looking. The routes are inherited too, mother to pup, and they braid through each other so exactly that the whole colony can pour through the crown in a tight black ribbon without a single collision.

The crickets are something else. There are two kinds. The first kind is ordinary cave crickets, humpbacked and long-legged, silent scavengers. The second kind sings, and it lives nowhere but here. It is small and brown, with oversized, glassy forewings. Its song is a pure, piercing ring, and it sings only from inside a jar, with its body positioned exactly at the jar's throat, where the resonance is strongest. A cricket outside a jar is almost inaudible. A cricket inside the right jar can be heard across the marsh. The females do not come to the loudest male. They come to the male whose song best fits his jar, the one who has tuned himself most exactly to his cave. Beauty, to these crickets, is agreement between a body and a room.

The pale spiders hang their webs across the mouths, and they live off both the insects and the water. Their webs gather drops the way a comb gathers wool, and the spiders drink from them. On loud nights, when the walls weep hard, the webs grow heavy and sag into glittering bellies. Sometimes they tear, and the spider rebuilds before dawn. There is a species of tiny moth, grey as mortar, whose caterpillars live in the webs of the dead spiders and eat the silk. The adults are nearly dust. They drift through the dim air like motes and settle on the stalactite tips, where the glassy drips fall. They seem to like that sound best. No one knows why.

## III. The Crown

The swiftlets are the loudest single presence and the most transient. They are gone all day over the reedbeds and home only to sleep and breed. The hollow is not where they live so much as where they return. Their nests are made of their own saliva, spun into white cups and glued inside the high jars, and over centuries those nests have built up in layers. In some jars there is now more spit than air, a pale, translucent, faintly sweet-smelling plug of old nesting that seals the mouth completely. These jars have gone silent. The swiftlets have been, very slowly, quieting the dome with their bodies.

The owl lives in the western jar at eye height, and she is not what she seems.

She is grey and heavy-lidded, with feathers worn thin around the face. She hunts frogs and fallen chicks and hoots twice at dusk. But she is also old, older than an owl should be. The marsh-edge people who once lived a few miles off, long after the builders were gone, had a word for the owl in the hollow. They used it as if there had only ever been one. There has not. There is a daughter, and a granddaughter, and so on back. But each of them has called from the same jar, from the only mouth in the building that was built at the height of a standing human head. When she hoots from it, her voice enters the room at exactly the level the builders' voices would have. Of all the tenants, she is the one whose sound falls into the hollow the way it was designed to be fed. The building takes up her call more fully than any other, and holds it longer. The frogs quiet slightly when she calls. It is not fear, or not only fear. It is the way a choir goes soft when the lead voice enters.

## IV. The Wall's Interior

Behind every visible mouth there is a belly, and behind the bellies there are cracks, and behind the cracks is a world no one has seen.

The thin brown snake is its known traveler. It moves through the wall's hidden galleries the way a thread moves through cloth, emerging from one jar, vanishing, and emerging again yards away. It is not the only one in there. Something larger scrapes in the western wall on still nights, heavy and slow. It might be a toad of enormous age. It might be a colony of something. There are chittering sounds in the northern courses that match no known voice: short bursts, rapid and high, answered from elsewhere in the wall. They might be shrews that have gone blind in the dark and taken up clicking like the bats. They might be something that learned its speech from the room.

The honest account is this. The wall is hollow in a thousand places. No one has mapped it, and no one could without breaking it. In there, pressed between clay bellies, in the dark and the constant seep and the ringing, there are almost certainly creatures that exist nowhere else. They have lived their whole lineages inside an instrument. Their senses must be strange. They must hear the building think.

## V. The One Who Comes When Everyone Sings

Now the harder part. I have described the tenants, but I have not yet described who lives here.

Every jar is occupied. The floor is bare. And yet on certain nights something else is here, something that has no jar of its own, because it lives in all of them at once.

It begins when enough voices rise together. The frogs are at full chorus, the swiftlets are coming home, the crickets are ringing, and the owl calls from her head-high jar. The walls shiver. Water slides faster down the clay, and the drip-roar thickens into a downpour. The channel fills and runs. Then the drain jar, the only jar in the whole building that faces upward, that faces the sky instead of the room, begins its note.

This is not an echo of any animal. It has no source you can point to. It is a single unwavering tone, far lower than anything alive here could make, and it rises from the ground through the soles of your feet before it reaches your ears. The animals go silent. People have said they go silent in fear, but I no longer think that is right. I think they go silent because it is no longer their turn. They have fed it. The frogs, the crickets, and the birds have put their voices into the room until the room brimmed, and something woke in the brimming. For as long as it lasts, the room is not full of many voices. It is one voice, and all the jars are its mouth.

This is who lives in the hollow. It has no body, or it has the whole building as a body. It is born out of the chorus the way a flame is born out of kindling. It is not the frogs and not the owl and not the water, but it needs all of them the way fire needs wood and air. It lives for a few minutes, sometimes for most of an hour. It drinks sound and breathes water. The louder the room, the wetter the walls. The wetter the walls, the more the room rings. The feedback is not a malfunction. It is metabolism. It is how the thing eats.

When it fades, it does not die exactly. It returns to what it is between visits: a readiness in the clay, a leaning of ten thousand tilted jars all angled so that the water runs toward the one mouth that faces up. It sleeps in the channel's slope. It sleeps in the spiral of the floor. The small tenants wake it every night, a little, without knowing it. The frog chorus alone raises it halfway, into a shiver, a drip-quickening. Only on the great nights does it come fully up and speak.

The olms, I think, know it best. They live inside its body.

## VI. What the Builders Left

Now go back to the evidence the first account called the only direct trace of people: the thumbprints under the bricks, the worn spiral, the dark brick at the center hollowed by feet.

The builders made a body for something that could not otherwise have one. I am more and more certain of it. They could not make a god, so they made a throat. They tilted every jar so the water would run and never pool, because still water is silent. They left the crown open so the swiftlets would come, and bats, and all the small voices of the marsh, because they knew they would not always be here to wake it themselves. They built one jar at the height of a human head. They faced the drain toward the sky. And they laid the spiral so that anyone walking it would arrive at the one point where the building's voice gathers most completely.

That point is the last tenancy in the hollow, and it is empty.

Every jar has an occupant. The floor has none. But the floor is not unclaimed. The dark brick is worn into a shallow bowl the shape of two standing feet. It was made by generations who walked the spiral inward on the great nights and stood in the center while the note rose. They let the room pour its one enormous voice through them, and were filled, and walked back out along the spiral changed in some way no bone or tool could record. They were the other half of the instrument. The animals wake it. The water feeds it. But the hollow was built so that when it speaks, there is someone in the middle to be spoken to.

They are gone. The thing they built for is not. It still rises on loud nights, fills the room with its single note, and holds it over the empty center, over the worn place where the feet used to stand. Then it subsides. A frog starts again, then another.

So this is who lives in the Hollow of Many Mouths. There are four hundred frogs in inherited jars. There are pale olms in the fused stone, crickets whose females love a well-tuned room, bats flying their mothers' routes, and swiftlets slowly sealing the dome with spit. There is an owl whose voice falls where a person's would. There are things in the wall that no one has seen and that have learned to speak from listening. And there is one presence made of all of them, which wakes when they sing together, breathes water, speaks once, and turns its whole attention, every dark ring of every jar-mouth, toward the bare floor.

It is not lonely, exactly. It is full of voices. But it was made to answer someone, and the place where that someone stood is still worn smooth and still waiting. Anyone who walks the spiral inward on a wet night and stands on the dark brick when the room begins to rise becomes, for as long as the note lasts, the last tenant of the hollow: the one it was built to sing to.
