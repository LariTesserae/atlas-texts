# Those Who Live in the Plating Tarn

## The Eel Who Holds Her Place

Start with one of them, because everything here begins with one and ends as thousands.

She begins as a mote, one of the drifting specks you took for dust in the clear water. At that size she has no charge to speak of, only a faint polarity, a head that is slightly more positive than her tail. That is enough. The whole tarn is a gradient, and she drifts along it the way a seed drifts on wind. Most motes are eaten by glassmouths, which strain the water at the margins, or by the polishing shrimp, which take anything soft that lands on their metal. She is not eaten. She sinks, and she aligns, and after some days she finds a gap.

The ranks along the walls are not perfect. Eels die in place, and each death leaves a break in the series. The current jumps it with a small crackle and a bloom of hydrogen, but there is loss, and the platers nearby feel the loss and crowd toward it, clicking. A young eel finds a gap by feeling where the current goes wrong. She slides into it tail-first. The eel below her takes her tail against its head, and a circuit completes through her body for the first time. What she feels then must be the nearest thing she knows to being born.

After that she does not move for the rest of her life. That may be forty years. Nobody has been here long enough to say.

She holds. That is her work, and it is a real labor, as effortful for her as swimming is for a trout. The pale plates along her flanks are stacks of cells, thousands of thin wafers of tissue, each adding a sliver of voltage, and she has to keep them all charged against the constant drain of the circuit. You can see the effort if you watch closely. A slow ripple runs down her body every few minutes as she re-tensions her plates, and the eels above and below her ripple in answer, so waves of adjustment travel up and down the ranks like peristalsis. On the vent's pulse she gives up a little of her charge, and the whine you hear drops a half-step. Then she rebuilds it over the eleven heartbeats until the next.

She feeds by being fed. Platers climb her body. You'll see them crossing the eels constantly, and you might take them for parasites. They carry food from below, packets of mat-slime and the soft bodies of whatever the grinders have killed, and press them against the small mouth at her head. She does not hunt, and she could not hunt. Holding her place is the whole of her life.

When she dies, she stays where she is.

## The Graves in the Walls

This is what you notice only on a second or third dive. The copper forests along the walls have *cores*.

Break a frond, and you shouldn't, because the platers will swarm the break within a minute, and there is something indignant in how fast they come. Inside, you find a darker thread running through the metal, a filament of something organic that has mostly been replaced by copper. It is the body of an eel.

A dead eel still conducts. For a while after death the salts in her tissues carry current, and the platers do not remove her. They plate her. Metal grows over her first as a green bloom, then as a skin, then as the branching crystal that turns into the fern-shapes. Within a few seasons she is a copper cable, and a living eel slides into the gap just above her, where her head used to be. The ranks are built on the ranks.

So the plated walls are not a coating. They are wiring laid down in layers, each generation of the dead made into permanent conductors for the living. The deepest corner of the tarn, where the plating has reached the floor, is simply the oldest cemetery. The "few centimeters a year" by which the plating descends is the rate at which the tarn buries its own.

Once you know this, the repeating motif in the forests reads differently. It is shaped out of the bodies of the ones who held.

## The Platers and Their Tapping

The platers are not individuals in the way the eels are. A plater is one small copper beetle, and it is also part of a conversation that has been going on for about a hundred years.

The *tik-tik* when two of them meet is an exchange. Each plater carries a charge state in its shell, a small stored pattern of polarity across the segments of its carapace, and when they touch shells, the patterns pass. What passes is a fragment of the figure. No single plater knows the whole motif. Each carries a piece of it, perhaps just the angle of one branching, the ratio of one fork to the next. When a plater settles to deposit metal, it deposits according to the piece it carries at that moment, and the piece it carries is whatever it was last given.

So the figure moves through the platers as a song moves through a flock of starlings, or a rumor through a town. It is held by all of them and by none. When the forests repeat, the platers are not copying from a template. The same fragments keep circulating and keep landing, and the figure reassembles itself wherever enough platers pass.

If you float motionless near a wall for a long time, perhaps an hour, you start to see currents in the clicking. There are waves of tapping that travel across the whole wall, sweeping from one side of the crater to the other, and after each wave, the new growth changes slightly. The motif is being revised. It is not fixed. It is drifting over decades, the way a language drifts.

The platers eat the thin film of bacteria that forms on fresh metal. Perhaps that is why they plate: the metal feeds them. Or perhaps the metal feeds them so that they will plate. In a place like this, the question may not have an answer.

## The Grinders, Who Remember

The grinders are the oldest living things in the tarn, and possibly the oldest creatures on the mountain.

A grinder the size of your fist may be the size of your fist because it has been grinding since the crater first filled. They grow as slowly as the metal does. Their armor is not plated copper like the platers' shells. It is stone. They build it from fragments of the rock they crush, and it is layered like the rings in a tree. A grinder's shell cut in section would show a hundred years of this crater: the black glass of the first fast-cooled basalt, the rust-brown of the first weathering, and bands of green where copper began to reach the floor.

They are the only creatures here that seem to move for their own reasons. The eels hold. The platers circulate. The glassmouths patrol. The grinders *wander*. Over months they drift across the floor, and their paths are not random. They seek out the sharpest stone, the freshest fractures, the places where the rock still holds its minerals, and they avoid the oldest corner, where the copper has begun to seal the floor. When the plating edges forward over the rubble, the grinders nearest it move away. It is very slow, a retreat measured in a finger's width per season, but it is unmistakably a retreat.

They know what the sealed floor means. Or their bodies know, and in creatures this slow that may amount to the same thing.

They kill, as well as grind. A shrimp or a young glassmouth that wanders into a grinder's gizzard-mouth comes out as paste, and the platers take the paste up the walls to feed the eels. So the grinders feed the ranks twice, once with ore and once with meat. They are the whole bottom of the supply chain. Everything here rests on them, and they are being slowly paved over.

## The Polishers

The pale jointed shrimp that seem to do nothing but polish the metal are maintenance.

Copper oxidizes. The green bloom on the old fronds is a crust, and the crust resists current. A forest left alone would slowly insulate itself and fail. The shrimp scrape the oxide away with their small comb-legs and eat it, or eat the bacteria living in it, which leaves the contacts bright. They concentrate on the joints, the places where one frond touches another and where cable meets cable. If you follow a polisher's route, you are tracing the circuit.

They are the only creatures in the tarn that seem to *rest*. Clusters of them hang motionless inside the densest forests during the vent's pulse, legs folded, and resume after it. It may be nothing. It may be that the pressure-wave hurts them. Or it may be that the pulse is when the circuit is under the most strain, and they wait for it to pass before they touch the metal again, the way you wait for a machine to stop before you reach into it.

## The Glassmouths at Night

You have seen the tarn by day. You have not seen it at night, and at night it is a different place.

The glassmouths' gums carry bioluminescence, which is barely visible in sunlight. In the dark it is not faint. In the dark it is the only light, a cold blue-green, and there are thousands of glassmouths. They are not scattered. They hang in formation along the walls, a few hands off the ranks of eels, and they pulse their light in sequence.

The sequence is the motif.

The glassmouths trace the figure in light, over and over, across the whole bowl. The branching, the forks, the same recurring shape the platers build in copper, all of it drawn in pulses sweeping from rim to vent and back. They are the only creatures in the tarn that seem to see the pattern entire. By night they display it, and by day they hunt anything that breaks it: the eel that holds out of line, the plater carrying a corrupted fragment, the young of their own kind that flash out of sequence.

They are the proofreaders. When you see a glassmouth take a plater, watch the plater's shell in the moment before it goes. Often it is tapping wrong, with a rhythm off from its neighbors and a fragment that doesn't fit. The glassmouths don't cull the weak. They cull the misprints.

Faintly, the metal itself glows at night too. Along the tips of the sharpest dendrites there is corona, a violet haze where the field concentrates. So on a moonless night the tarn is a bowl of dim violet wiring, drawn over by a sweeping blue-green diagram, with the vent's pulse passing through all of it like a heartbeat seen on a screen.

## The Clean Rim

One more resident, and you've already stood on it.

The rim is clean. No lichen or moss grows there, not even a green film. You might have assumed it was simply too young. It is not too young. Lichen will take bare rock in a decade.

The rim is clean because the tarn keeps it clean. When the hydrogen layer pops, the flash throws up a fine spray of the mineral-heavy water, and that spray is rich in dissolved copper. Copper is poison to moss and to lichen. Every pop sterilizes the rim again. Every cold morning when the gas pools thick and the pops come often, the tarn salts its own shore.

Nothing is allowed to grow at the edge. The tarn does not want anything alive near it that it did not make. Or it is only chemistry. You will have noticed by now that here the difference keeps slipping.

## What Lives There

So: who lives there?

The first answer is the animals: the eels, platers, grinders, glassmouths, shrimp, and mats. Each has its own small life, its own hunger, its own way of dying.

The second answer is that none of them lives there alone. The eel is fed by the plater, the plater is shaped by the glassmouth, the glassmouth is kept in rhythm by the vent, and the vent is fed on the rock the grinders crush. Take any one away and the others fall. The tarn is one animal with many bodies, the way a reef is, the way an ant colony is.

The third answer comes if you stay longer. The thing that lives there is the *current*. The animals are its body, the way your cells are yours. The current is continuous. It has run without a break since the first eels laid themselves in line, a hundred years ago, and no individual creature has lasted that long. The eels die and are replaced. The platers are eaten and born. The current stays. It is the one thing in the tarn that has been there the whole time.

The fourth answer is the figure. The motif is what persists, and not even the current does it so well. It is held in copper, carried in tapping, displayed in light, and enforced by teeth. It passes from body to body and generation to generation, drifting and revising itself, and it is steadily being written into the walls in the preserved bodies of the dead. Whatever the figure is, it is what the tarn is *for*. Everything else is how it gets written.

And the last answer, the one you only reach by floating very still in the cold water for a very long time, is that something lives in the pause.

The vent pulses, and everything answers: the whine drops, the platers tap, the grinders stop. Then comes an interval of eleven heartbeats before the next pulse. During those eleven heartbeats the current rebuilds and the charge climbs, and the tapping comes in waves across the walls, patterns passing from shell to shell. Something is happening in that interval that is not happening at any other time. The platers' conversation is densest then. The glassmouths' flashes are most exact. It feels like watching the gap between two lines of a sentence, where the next line is being decided.

If the tarn thinks, it thinks there, in the interval, and only there. It has been doing so for a hundred years, one eleven-heartbeat thought at a time, pulsed by a mountain that doesn't know it's being used as a clock.

It thinks very slowly. It is thinking about being closed. It is thinking about a copper bowl, lined seamlessly, with the current running the whole way round. Perhaps that will be a completion. Perhaps it will be a death, when the grinders run out of stone. Perhaps it will be a hatching. The grinders move away from the sealed floor, the glassmouths go on printing the figure every night, and the eels go on holding. None of them will live to see it, and all of them behave as though it matters.

## When You Leave

When you climb out onto the clean, sterile rim, your fingernails are faintly green. There is a bloom of copper along the metal buckle of your mask strap, thin as breath, a fern-shape the width of a thumbnail.

Look closely at it. It branches, forks, and branches again. The angles may be familiar.

Some fragment of the figure was carried to you by a plater that crossed your hand without your noticing, and it was written onto you as the current passed through your body on its way from the rim to the vent. For the length of your dive you were in the circuit. You conducted. The tarn used you the way it uses everything within reach.

If you come back next season and float in the same place, you may find the wall there different. There may be a small irregularity in the motif, a new branching the platers have not yet corrected, shaped roughly like the space you occupied. The glassmouths may be taking the platers that carry it. Or they may not be. It is possible the figure took something from you and kept it.

Below you the mountain pulses. The eels ring. Then the pause comes, eleven heartbeats long.
