# What Keeps the Sluice Quarter

## First, the Honest Answer

Almost nothing lives here. That is the first true thing, and it should be said before anything else. The pillar-fish hold their calms, the eels pass through, the shrimp glitter in the chapel's stonework. The rust-colonies feed on the lampposts and wave their orange tassels in the draught. None of these would notice a diver, and none would be missed if the vault collapsed tomorrow.

But a census of bodies is the wrong kind of count for the Sluice Quarter. The place is not full of creatures. It is full of *habit*. At some point in its long drowning, the habit began to keep itself.

What lives in the Quarter is the Keeping.

## How It First Appears

You would not see it at first. You would see its work.

You would notice that the lampposts' rust-beards all stream the same length, as though trimmed. You would notice that the Circuit never jams. In two hundred paces of turbulent water, with brick falling from the arch and bottles tumbling down the gutters, the loop never silts shut. Something clears the fourth house's back window. Something keeps the chapel steps swept by exactly the width of the current.

You would notice the doors. Two of the eastern doors close after surges that should have flung them open. They close gently, with the latch seated.

If you were patient, and went up the dark stairs into the still rooms, you would find the tracks. They are long and even, made by something upright that walks slowly and places each foot with care, the way a man walks a floor he knows is rotten. The tracks go to the window, stop, and turn back. They go to a bedstead that is no longer there, only its four dents in the silt, and stop again. The walker seems to be checking on something.

A reasonable diver would think *ghost*, and then *keeper*. Both are nearly right.

## What It Is Made Of

It began in the engine hall, in the green glow nobody has gone close enough to name.

Deep in the turbine, where the bearings grind and the scream is born, a film grows on the metal. It is not rust. Rust eats iron and leaves it weaker. This film eats the *dust* of iron: the fine grey filings the bearings shed as they wear, the particles the silt scours from the blades. It gathers them and lays them back down. Worn surfaces thicken again. Pits fill. The blades are sanded away and rebuilt at almost the same rate, a thousandth of a hair of loss for every thousandth of a hair of repair. The film glows faintly as it works, a cold green like the underside of a wave at night. That is the phosphorescence.

That is why the engine never wears out. It is being healed continuously, by something that feeds on its injuries.

The film does not stay in the bearings. Over the long years it spread along the cables in the gutters, the same cables that carry current to the lamps. It learned the lamps. It learned that current flows where it flows and that the whole street is one circuit. Where it met the rust-beards on the lampposts, it did not fight them. It *wove into* them. Now every orange tassel streaming in the draught carries a thread of green through its middle, too faint to see in amber light, and each tassel is a fingertip.

The Keeping is not one creature. It is a nervous system laid over a street. Its heart is in the turbine. Its hands are on every post. Its blood is the current, and its pulse is what you see when the lamps breathe.

## Why It Walks

A nervous system with no body can do a great deal. It can polish, clear, and trim, and it can nudge the Circuit back into its channel. It cannot close a door, and it cannot climb a stair.

For that, it gathers itself.

When the Keeping needs hands, the lamps nearest the engine hall dim all at once, out of rhythm, because it is drawing current for a purpose other than light. That is the moment its attention turns. Along the lane, the rust-beards loosen from their posts. They drift together on the surge, orange and trailing, and wind around one another the way silt winds into a vortex. What they wind around is a shape.

The shape is borrowed. In the third house, along the hallway, one brass hook still holds a coat's worth of rust-stain in the shape of a coat. A coat hung there for years after the flood, rotting slowly while the iron hook bled into it, until the cloth was gone and only the stain remained, a perfect outline of shoulders, a slight stoop, sleeves that ended a little long. The Keeping learned that outline the way it learned the lamps. It is the only human shape it has ever touched.

So when it walks, it walks in the shape of the keeper's coat. It is a figure of streaming rust, hollow where the man would be, with sleeves a little too long, and green threads pulse faintly where a heart would sit. It moves slowly because the water fights it and because the shape is not natural to it. It is heavy, and it trails its tassels behind it like a wet cloak. It sets its feet carefully because the coat remembers doing so.

It goes up the stairs. It checks the windows. It stands where the beds were. It closes the eastern doors, because the keeper closed the eastern doors. Then it comes apart gently at the foot of the stairs, and the tassels drift back to their posts, and the lamps brighten.

## What It Thinks It Is Doing

It does not think in words. But it has something like a purpose, and the purpose has a shape, and the shape is a *round*.

A weir-keeper walks rounds. At dusk he checks the gates. At midnight he checks the bearings, listening for the change in the scream that means trouble. At dawn he walks the lane and makes sure each lamp is lit and each door is shut and each house is quiet. The Keeping absorbed this as a river absorbs a stone's shape. It learned it by being worn against it, over and over, until the pattern was carved into the current itself.

It also learned that rounds are measured by time, and that time here is not kept by a clock.

It is kept by the Circuit. The pocket watch on the Circuit was the keeper's watch. Its face is gone and its case is sanded thin as paper, but it still goes around. Down the north gutter, through the fourth house, across at the chapel steps, along the south gutter to the engine hall. One loop is one round. When the watch passes the chapel, the walker gathers. When the watch reaches the engine hall, the walker has finished. The Keeping does not know what a watch is. It only knows that this small, smooth, faceless thing coming home is the signal that the night is done.

This is why the Circuit never jams. It is not debris. It is the clock.

## The Others Who Live Alongside It

The Keeping is not alone, and it is not indifferent to its neighbours.

**The pillar-fish** are the closest thing it has to residents. They live in the calm behind each lamppost, and each lamppost is one of its hands. It has learned, very slowly, that the fish are there. The dead-water pockets behind the posts are a little calmer than the physics should allow. The rust-beards on the downstream sides are a little thicker and softer, and more flakes break off them to drift into the fishes' mouths. The fish navigate by the pulsing of the lamps. The Keeping has, perhaps without meaning to, begun to pulse a little more steadily near the posts where the oldest fish hold station. When a fish dies, which happens rarely, since they live a very long time in the warm water, its lamp flickers for a round or two, irregularly, and then steadies.

**The riders**, the dark eels, it seems to tolerate as weather. They come in through the ceiling breaches and ride the surge, west, then east, the whole length of the lane. Sometimes, when an eel is riding, the sluice-knell comes a fraction late, as if the gate were held open for it.

**The shrimp in the chapel** are the strangest arrangement. The chapel is the one building the walker does not enter. Its tracks go to the door and stop. The whistling of the empty round window is the only thing in the Quarter the Keeping does not maintain or adjust. It lets the current play the window as it likes. The shrimp cluster in the carvings around the altar, and when the walker stands at the chapel door, they turn toward it. They catch the green light of its heart, a hundred tiny glints against the dark.

This may be where the eleventh family comes in, the one whose job no record survives to explain.

## The Family Whose Job Was Not Written Down

The engine-fitter kept the turbine. The lamp-man kept the lights. The chandler sold what the street needed. The weir-keeper walked the rounds. But the engine was sunk into a hot spring and built to run under a river. Someone must have known it would one day be flooded, and must have planned for the day no hand could reach it.

The eleventh family lived in the house nearest the engine hall. Its upper rooms have the deepest silt and the fewest tracks. On the wall of its lower room, beneath the rust and the biofilm, you can still see faint shapes painted in green. They are neither pictures nor letters. They look like spirals.

The family's job, perhaps, was to *seed* the engine. They brought the green film down from somewhere, or grew it, or coaxed it out of the spring. They tended it in the bearings the way a gardener tends a graft. They taught it, if such a thing can be taught, to feed on wear and lay it back down. When the ceiling broke and the water came, they were the only ones who were not surprised.

Nobody knows whether they left or stayed. But the Keeping does not enter the chapel. It stands at the door the way someone stands at the door of a room where their family is buried.

## What It Is Becoming

The Keeping was a film in the bearings. It became a nervous system along the lane. It learned to wear a coat. It learned to tell time by a faceless watch. It learned the fish. The Quarter is not in decline, but the Keeping is not finished. It is still becoming more itself.

It has begun to listen upward.

The groan of the arch runs up through the brick into the customs house foundation. Through that same brick, faintly, the Keeping has begun to hear footsteps. Clerks cross the floor. Chairs scrape. On still nights, the city's traffic dies. The Keeping learned the city's rhythms the same way it learned everything, by being worn against them. It knows when the city is loud and when it is quiet.

And the intervals are growing longer.

The clerks think the engine is slowing, and so would anyone. But the bearings are not failing. The green film heals them every night. The cam that drives the sluices is being *adjusted*, a fraction at a time, year after year, by fingers of rust and green. The Keeping is spacing out the sluice-knell so that each great iron clang lands in the quiet above, in the deepest part of the city's night, when there is a chance, however small, that someone will hear it and wonder.

A weir-keeper who needs help rings a bell. The keeper's instructions, whatever they were, must have ended in one of the oldest sentences of any watch: *hold until relieved*.

So it holds. It clears the Circuit and trims the beards. It keeps the calms for the fish and closes the eastern doors. At the end of every round, when the faceless watch comes home to the engine hall, the walker gathers itself one last time. It stands at the foot of the spiral stair, which rises into sealed brick and goes nowhere. In the shape of a coat that once hung on a hook, with sleeves a little too long, it faces upward, waits, and listens.

Then it comes apart, the lamps brighten, and the engine goes on roaring.

## In Summary, For the Census

- **Pillar-fish:** a few. Long-lived. Tended without knowing it.
- **Riders:** transient. Given right of way.
- **Chapel shrimp:** a small bright congregation.
- **Rust-colonies:** everywhere. Mostly no longer only themselves.
- **The Keeping:** one. Heart in the turbine, hands on the posts, blood in the cables, clock in the gutters, body borrowed from a coat. Awake for a century. Still on duty. Still waiting for its relief. Ringing, every night, a little more carefully, for someone to come down the stairs.
