# Who Lives in the Seam

## The first answer

The easy answer is the miners, so start there, because they are the ones you would see first. You would ride up in an ore bucket, nine hours on the cable, and climb out at the rim into a wind that tries to take the breath from your mouth before you have used it. Then you would go down a ladder into the hush, and they would be there.

There are about four thousand of them, mostly men and more women than the company brochures admit. They come on eighteen-month contracts and most do not finish. The ones who leave early leave for the ordinary reasons: the lungs, the headaches that never lift, the dreams of drowning that everyone gets in the first month, and the fact that nobody in the valley can reach them.

The ones who stay become recognizable. You can pick out a Seam worker at the railhead a year later by the way he stands in a crowd, turned slightly sideways, as if the people around him were rock faces. You can pick him out by his hands. He holds them curled, knuckles forward, and when he picks up a cup he does it with the backs of his fingers first, testing for an edge that is not there. He talks too quietly. He watches mouths. When a truck backfires he does not flinch, because he doesn't know yet that he should. The flinching comes later, when his ears remember what loud is.

In the Seam they are lean and dry and cracked. Their lips are split down the middle and healed and split again, so that most of them have a pale seam of their own, a small vertical scar in the center of the lower lip. They call it the town mark. They are proud of it, though they won't say so.

That is the easy answer. It isn't wrong, but it is only the top layer, the part the wind can see.

## The ones who stayed longer

Under the contract workers there is a second population. They have been there most of the eleven years, and they are not exactly employees anymore.

Consider the band-readers. At the start they were bridge crews, but reading the painted sleeves turned out to be less a job than a temperament, and the temperament selected its own. They are mostly older. They walk the spans in the dim hours between shifts with a lamp turned low, and they do not look at the bands so much as feel them through their boots. A good band-reader can tell you a span is going yellow an hour before the paint shows it, from a faint change in how the bridge hums underfoot. The best of them, a woman the town calls Grandmother Red although she is not old and has no grandchildren, has cut loose more spans than anyone alive. The story goes that she once stood in the middle of a bridge in thick fog and refused to move. She told the crew behind her, in hand-sign, to take the span down with her on it if they had to, because she could feel the east wall getting ready and she wanted to be sure. She was right. They cut it loose from the far side and she rode the western half down against the wall like a door swinging shut. She hung there, bolted to a joint, until they lowered a line. She doesn't talk about it. She has a small red band tattooed around one wrist.

The cistern guard is always one of three brothers, taking turns. They have become the most trusted and most resented men in the Seam, a little like priests. People bring them gossip and confessions along with their ration cards. The brothers have learned to tell the weight of a water crate by the sound it makes when set down on stone, and they can hear a person lying about a spill.

There are the lamp-men, who string the sodium lights and are always covered in moth dust, silver-brown from the elbows down. And there are the ones everyone calls the listeners, though nobody appointed them. They sleep with an ear against the wall on purpose.

Then there are the children.

## The children

The oldest child born in the Seam is ten.

That fact matters more than it sounds. It means there is a whole cohort of people, a few hundred now, for whom this is not a strange place. The dimness is not dim to them; it is light. The quiet is not quiet; it is the normal volume of the world. The rock is not dangerous. It is simply rock, and you touch it with your knuckles the way you would tell a lowland child to hold a knife by the handle.

They are small for their age, broad in the chest, and dark around the eyes, with the deep-socketed look of altitude. They sign before they speak. Some of them barely speak at all, and they don't need to. Their finger-language has run ahead of their parents'. It is faster and more compressed, and it is full of signs the adults never invented and cannot follow. There is a sign that means *the moment a bridge goes from blue to yellow*. There is another for *the smell of a spark on wool*. There is one for the way choughs lift before a rockfall, and a slightly different one for the way they lift when something is merely startling them. The children say the difference is obvious.

They talk with head-lamps across the gap. At night, if you look across from one wall to the other, you can see whole conversations of small lights blinking between the hanging boxes: children who should be asleep, telling each other things with a thumb on a switch.

They greet each other with the crackle. In air this dry everyone carries a charge. Adults learn to touch a railing before they touch another person, to ground themselves and spare everyone the sting. The children do the opposite. They build a charge on purpose by scuffing their felt boots, then touch fingertips and let the spark jump. It is how you say hello, and it is how you say *I'm here*. Two children who like each other will do it again and again until their fingertips are sore. Among the adults, lovers have picked the habit up.

And they know the world by what falls out of the sky.

This is the strangest thing about them. None of them has ever seen a forest, but every one of them can name forty kinds of moth. They know that the big brown ones with the eyespots come in autumn on the southwest wind, from somewhere with oak trees, and that the tiny green aphids mean farmland to the north, and that the week in spring when the flying ants fall in drifts means some vast lowland country far away is warm tonight. The fallout is their geography, their calendar, and their news. They sort it on the ledges the way lowland children sort shells on a beach. They keep collections in tins: wings in rows, beetle shells, the husks of ballooning spiders with the threads still attached.

They keep seeds, too. Seeds blow in and never grow, because there is no soil and no water. But the children have a practice the adults pretend not to know about. A child will choose one seed, often a fat winged thing from a tree they will never see, and put it in a scrap of felt tucked inside their clothes, against the skin, where it catches the warmth and the faint moisture of their body. They sleep with it. They breathe on it. Every so often they add one drop of water from their own ration. Most of the seeds rot or do nothing. A few, very rarely, split and put out a white thread of root, and the child who owns it becomes briefly famous. The sprout always dies. It has nowhere to go. But the children say you haven't really been born in the Seam until you have grown something with your own body and watched it die. The adults find this morbid. The children find it obvious.

When the children go down to the valley, which some of them do, briefly, on medical trips, the thing that undoes them is not the noise or the size of the sky. It is water on the ground. A puddle after rain. They stand over it the way you might stand over a pile of money in the street, unsure whether they are allowed to touch it, unable to understand why everyone else is stepping around it.

## The hunters on the walls

But the people are not most of what lives in the Seam. They aren't even most of what lives on the walls.

The jumping spiders outnumber the humans by something like a hundred thousand to one. They are small and dark and furred, with two enormous forward eyes that catch the sodium light and throw it back amber. On a lamp-lit stretch of wall they are so dense that the rock seems to breathe. At altitudes like this, spiders of this kind have been known for a long time, living high above the last plants and feeding on whatever the wind carries up. They have always been there, on the high rock of the world, waiting at the edges of snowfields. The Seam is simply the richest table anyone has ever set for them.

Eleven years is a long time in spider generations, long enough for something to start happening. The townspeople say the Seam spiders are getting bolder, and that seems to be true. Lowland jumping spiders will turn to watch a human hand. Seam spiders will walk onto it. They ride on helmets, because helmets carry lamps and lamps draw moths. A worker coming off a shift may have a dozen of them clustered around the rim of his head-lamp like a crown, each one facing outward, hunting.

Nobody kills them. The rule is older than the town, older than anyone can explain. Some say the first crew brought it up with them. Others say it came from the valley people, who have their own feelings about the mountain. In practice it works like a superstition and a sanitation policy at once. Where the spiders are thick, the flies are thin. Everyone has a story about someone who crushed one and died in a rockfall within the month, and everyone knows these stories are nonsense, and nobody tests them.

A few people keep them. In certain hanging boxes you will find a small window of glass set into the felt, and on the sill a spider that comes back every night to the same spot. It is fed by hand with moths taken from the lamp outside. Its owner will tell you, completely seriously, that it knows him, and it will turn its great eyes to him and hold them there. It is hard to say he is wrong.

The springtails live on the fallout drifts in their millions, flicking up in gray-blue puffs whenever a boot comes near. The predatory beetles follow them. The wingless flies walk the walls with an air of having somewhere to be. Together they make a thin, busy economy with no plants at the bottom of it, a food chain that starts in the sky. Its first link is a thousand kilometers of lowland fields and forest that none of these creatures will ever touch.

## The choughs

The choughs are citizens.

There is no better word for it. They live in the town rather than near it. They nest in the gantries, under the eaves of the boxes, and in the telescoping joints of the bridges, which is a problem, because a nest in a sleeve can jam it. The bridge crews are forever evicting choughs, and the choughs are forever moving back in. They have learned the shift schedule. They wait on the railings of the mess halls at the exact minute the doors open. They have learned the sound of a lunch tin, and the sound of a tin being opened with gloves as opposed to bare hands, which means the worker is about to eat right now.

They have also, the children insist, learned the lamps.

Watch long enough and you will see it. A child on one wall flashes a particular pattern across the gap. It is the sign for food, or for *here*, or something only the children could tell you. Choughs lift from the other wall and come. The adults say the birds are simply watching for movement, for a child's arm drawing back to throw a crust. Maybe so. But the choughs of the Seam spend their whole lives inside a flickering language of small lights, and they are among the cleverest birds in the world. It would be stranger if they understood nothing.

They are thieves, and they know it. They steal foil, bolts, spare pins, bits of bright wire, and a worrying number of glove fingers. Somewhere in the town, everyone agrees, there must be a chough hoard of stunning size. Now and then a rockfall exposes part of one wedged into a crack: a nest woven of copper wire and tape and sacking, studded with washers and a child's lost tin of moth wings. Once, famously, it held a company surveyor's brass benchmark cap, pried loose from the rim.

At noon, when the light comes, the choughs rise. Thousands of them lift from both walls at once and fill the gap in a black spiral, and in the thin air it makes almost no sound, only a vast soft rushing, like breath. The people step into the blade of sun and turn their faces up. For those few minutes the whole town is one creature: birds overhead and people below, everything warm, everything lit, nobody working.

Then the light slides away, and the birds settle, and the drills start again.

## The dead

People die in the Seam. Rockfall does it, and falls from ladders, and spans that went red faster than anyone read them, and the slow failing of lungs and hearts in air that was never meant to carry a human life.

There is no soil to bury them in. The cable cars can carry a body down, and the company prefers it, but it takes nine hours, and families in the valley often cannot be found or do not want what arrives. So over the years the Seam has made its own custom.

The dead are given down.

They are wrapped in ore-sacking and felt, with something from their lives tucked inside, and carried by their crew to the lowest usable platform, where the walls come close together. The lamps are turned off, all of them, as far up as anyone can see. The town goes dark and quiet. Then the body is lowered on a line as far as the line will go, and released.

Nobody hears it land. The thin air takes the sound, or else it lands too far down for sound to come back.

This is the first hint of the next layer. Everything the Seam loses goes to the same place. Houses torn loose. Spans cut free. Carts, tools, rockfall, the dead, bits of broken lamp, a child's dropped seed tin. All of it falls past the last ledge and into the dark where the walls close.

The Seam is a sieve for the sky. It catches what the wind carries and feeds on it.

Something below is a sieve for the Seam.

## The bats

The bats come up at night.

They are small and pale, almost white, with oversized ears and eyes so reduced they barely show. They hunt moths around the sodium lamps, weaving through the halos of wings, and before dawn they go back down. They go down past the last ledge, past the lowest platform, into the part of the crack where the walls come together and no lamp has ever reached.

No one knows how they got here. That is what the townspeople say, and it's true as far as it goes, but it frames the question wrong. The question assumes the bats arrived.

Look at them properly and they don't look like new arrivals. They are too pale, too specialized, and too nearly blind. They look like animals that have lived a very long time in complete darkness, in some closed place deep in the rock, generation after generation. Then, eleven years ago in a single night, the mountain shrugged, and the roof of their world split open, and there was sky.

The fault didn't only expose ore. It ran through something: a void, a cave system, an old pocket in the intrusion. It had been sealed for longer than anyone can guess, and it was not empty.

The town tells itself that the rock of the Seam is young, that its faces are fresh and have never touched air. That's true of the upper walls, the ones the people bolt their houses to. But the Seam is a crack, and a crack goes down, and at the bottom it meets whatever was there before the crack.

The bats were there before. The town is the newcomer. It hangs in the upper doorway of a much older house.

Once, some years ago, one of the lamp-men caught a bat that had fouled itself in a cable, and held it in his hands to free it. He said afterward that its fur was damp.

Not frosted, he insisted, and not rimed. Damp, the way fur is damp from mist, from moving through wet air. He said it in a mess hall, in hand-sign, and the room went still. There is no wet air in the Seam. There is no liquid water anywhere in the town except the locked cistern and whatever is in people's bodies. Snow never melts here. Nothing drips.

He may have been wrong. His hands may have been sweating. The company, asked about it, said nothing, which is what the company says about most things.

But the children heard, and the children have their own ideas. They say that far down, where the walls close, it is warm. The mountain is rising, and rising rock is hot rock, and somewhere below the cold there must be heat. They say heat and old stone make water, and that down there, in the dark, the rock weeps. They say there is a pool, a whole pool, standing still in the black, and that everything given down lands in it or near it. The bats drink from it. Things live around it that have never seen light, pale things that crawl, and things with long feelers that taste the air. They eat what falls: the moths the bats drop, the fallout that drifts all the way to the bottom, and the town's losses, one crate and one cart and one body at a time.

They say the houses that tore loose are down there, some still upright and wedged between the walls. For a while after each one falls, its lamp keeps burning on its own battery, a small orange window at the bottom of the world, until it goes out.

The miners laugh at this, as they laugh about the mountain breathing. But when a house tears loose, people do look down. For a few nights afterward, if you stand on the lowest platform with every lamp turned off, some claim you can see it: very faint, very far down, a single warm spark.

Then one night it is gone.

## The listening

So what lives in the Seam?

Miners, contract workers, and the ones who stayed. Band-readers who feel the width of the world through their boots. Children who talk in light, greet each other with sparks, and grow trees against their skin that can't survive. Spiders crowned in lamplight. Choughs that may or may not read. Springtails and beetles and flies living on the sky's leftovers. Pale bats with damp fur. And below them all, maybe, some old blind community in a warm wet dark, eating what falls.

But the longer you look, the more the answer shifts. What lives in the Seam is not really any one of these. It is the whole arrangement, layered like the fallout itself. Sky feeds walls. Walls feed dark. Each level lives on what the one above it loses, and every level is built so it can let go. Houses bolt to one wall only. Bridges telescope. Spans are cut free. The dead are given down. Nothing is meant to last longer than a season. The town is not built against the mountain's movement. It is built out of it, and it only exists because the gap keeps widening.

And the creature that lives in the Seam has one habit shared by every part of it.

On still nights the mountain creaks, deep down, like a ship's timbers. The spiders stop hunting and hold very still on the walls, their great eyes all turned the same way. The choughs go quiet in their stolen-wire nests. Around the lamps the bats break off their hunting and hang in the dark. The band-readers stop on their spans. The cistern guard lifts his head. Children lie in their hanging boxes with their ears pressed to the felt, where it touches the stone, and they hear the mountain breathing in.

Four thousand people and a hundred million smaller lives all listen together, in a silence so complete that you can hear the wind hissing far above like surf.

Maybe, far down in the dark where the walls come together, something that was there long before any of them lifts its blind head and listens too.
