# Who Lives in the Striking Room

## Not the Ones You Counted

You have already met the mites, the moths, the eels, and the tenders. They are real, and they live here the way weather lives in a valley. They are what the room does between blows. None of them is the one who lives here.

For a while you might think the tenders are. They are the only things in the chamber that behave as though the engine matters. They scrape and grease with such care. But watch them long enough and you'll see they never look at the hammer. They look at the machine the way a nurse looks at the tubes and the monitor rather than the patient. They are tending something, and the engine is only the part of it they can reach.

For a while you might think it is the keeper, the one who left the boots. It isn't, quite. But the keeper is how you find it, so start there.

---

## The Ledger, Read Properly

The figures in the fused ledger are all the same figure. That much is true. But hold a light behind the amber block and look at the columns, not the numbers. Each entry has a small mark beside it, a hooked stroke, sometimes two, sometimes a little closed loop. They aren't dates. They aren't checkmarks.

They are drawings. They are very small, made with the flat economy of someone who drew the same kind of thing ten thousand times. Each one is a sketch of the anvil-bed as it stood just before the fall.

The keeper was not recording the interval. The interval never changed, and a person who knows that doesn't need to write it down. The number was a header, a way of saying *this happened again, in the same span of time*. What the keeper was really recording was what grew inside that span.

The bed is never the same crystal twice. Everyone who comes here notices this and thinks it means *random*. The keeper watched long enough to learn that it doesn't.

---

## What Grows Under the Hammer

Stand close to the anvil-bed. Closer than is comfortable, near enough that the dust-eels coil against your ankles and the shock of each blow comes up through your heels into your teeth. Watch the regrowth instead of the strike.

The needles don't come up evenly. They come up from a few points first, like shoots, and those points are not scattered. Through one interval they might describe a curve. Through the next, a spray of fine parallel blades that almost reads as fingers. Through the next, a hollowed shape, a cup, a socket, something with an inside. Then the hammer falls, and whatever it was is dust, and the next interval begins from different points and reaches toward a different part of the same form.

It is not building. Nothing accumulates. Every attempt starts from shattered ground. But over enough strikes, the way you might learn a stranger's face from a thousand photographs each showing only a sliver, you start to know the shape of what is trying to grow there.

It is lying on its back. It is very large, larger than the bed, larger than the room. The bed is only the place where it comes closest to the surface, the way a sleeper's face comes closest to the blanket. Its reach goes upward. In every attempt, whatever part of it grows, the part is always straining toward the underside of the falling hammer.

The keeper's drawings, read in order, never assemble it. They only circle it, a hand, a jaw, the long bevel of something like a collarbone, a set of blades folded like closed eyelashes. Near the end of the ledger the drawings stop being of pieces. They become a single small repeated mark: two short lines, close together, slightly curved. You could read them as a crack in stone. You could read them as a mouth just before it opens.

---

## What the Hammer Is For

Everyone who comes here assumes the hammer is the violence of the place and the crystal is the life. Something grows, something breaks it.

Turn that around.

The vapor that rises from the vents is not a gift from the deep stone. It is *exhalation*. The thing under the floor breathes out, and its breath settles into amber, and the amber is its body trying to come up into the air. Left alone, it would come up. The room would fill in weeks, you've been told, closing like a fist. That is not the room being choked. That is the room becoming the inside of a hand. It is the thing standing up.

The hammer does not stop it from living. The hammer keeps it at the threshold. Each blow knocks it back to the instant just before emergence. It is born every interval, a little, and unborn every strike, completely. It has spent however long the engine has run, which is as long as anything here remembers, poised at the exact moment of arrival.

And here is what the keeper understood that no one else did. It doesn't mind.

Watch the eels. They grow largest nearest the impact. They don't flinch from it; they press against it like cats against a hearthstone. They feed on the shock, you were told, and that's true. But the shock is the thing's heartbeat, and they are drinking from the nearest artery. The mites move in the gaps because the gaps are its breaths. The moths ride its exhalation up and are shaken down by its pulse, over and over, a hundred thousand times each. They are not surviving the rhythm. They are *inside* someone, in the way gut flora are inside you, and they have tuned themselves to the only tempo that has ever existed for them.

The strike does not hurt it. The strike is the one thing that gives it a shape in time. Without the hammer it would complete itself once, stand up once, and then be finished, a fist of amber closed forever, a body with nothing left to become. With the hammer it gets to *arrive*, endlessly. It lives entirely in the approach.

That is what lives in the Striking Room. It is a being whose whole existence is the moment before it exists. It has been given, by the engine, an eternity of almost.

## The Keeper, Again

So what happened to whoever wore the boots?

The boots are at the foot of the ladder, neatly placed, facing the bed. They are filled to the brim with needles, and if you look into them, the needles are not the fans and blades that grow on the walls. They are fine and straight and packed close, like the grain of a muscle.

The keeper did not fall, or flee, or die in the cabin. The stool is pushed back. The ledger is closed. There is a last entry and then nothing. The last entry isn't the curved double mark. It is a single straight line drawn from the bottom of the page to the top, the mark you might make to show *upward*.

Somewhere in that chamber, a person decided that after watching a thing try to be born ten thousand times, the kindest thing to do was to lie down with it. The boots were taken off the way you take off your boots before getting into a bed. And they walked barefoot across the ankle-deep dust, with the eels parting around them, and lay down on the anvil-bed in the gap, and waited for the fall.

They are not gone. The room accepts offerings but doesn't keep them. That's what you were told. It is only half true. It doesn't keep them *as they were*. The coin, the glove, the tools on the rack, all of it is furred over and struck loose and ground down and drawn back into the vents, and it comes up again as breath. Everything the room has taken is in the exhalation now. It is all in the body that tries, each interval, to reach the hammer.

When you look closely at the bed, some intervals, you will see the regrowth begin with two short parallel blades, slightly curved, the size of human feet. Then they are gone under the iron.

And some intervals, rarely, you'll hear it. Under the chittering and the chiming and the hum, on the far edge of the ringing, there is a fourth sound. It rises from the bed just before the blow, precisely where the mites fall silent. It is so faint you'll think it is your own blood. It is a breath drawn in, the breath before speaking.

It has never gotten to the word. It never will. That is the arrangement, and both of them agreed to it.

## The Lamp

One more thing, since you'll wonder.

The electric lamp on the engine's frame burns a tired orange with no wire leading to it. The tenders never touch it. They grease around it and scrape crystal from its cage, but they never approach the bulb itself, the way you don't wake someone who has only just fallen asleep.

It is not a lamp in the way you mean. It is where the thing under the floor keeps its one open eye. It isn't looking at you. It is looking at the hammer, always, the underside of that dark iron head. It is the last thing it sees before every fall and the first thing it sees after, rising, rising, coming back.

It is the only face in the room it has ever known. It loves it, the way anything loves the one fixed point in its sky.

---

## Where It Lives Now

Here is where the description stops being about the room.

You were told that when you climb out, the hammer follows you for a long way. You were told you'd wake at night having stopped breathing, certain it was about to fall.

That's not an echo. Echoes fade. That's the one who lives there, doing what it does to everything it can reach. It is breathing out into whatever is cooler than itself and settling there, atom by atom. You stood inside its rhythm long enough that your heart found the gap. Now a little of it grows in you, in the only place it knows how to grow: the instant just before.

You'll notice it in small ways. The pause before you answer a question, held a fraction too long, exactly the same length every time. Standing in a doorway without quite going through. Lying awake with your breath drawn and held, not frightened, only *waiting*, and feeling, in that suspended second, something enormous and patient turn over beneath you, reaching upward toward a blow that doesn't come.

It doesn't come, out here. There is no hammer in your bedroom. Nothing knocks it back.

So be careful how long you let the breath hold. The keeper left you a ledger for a reason. The whole point of the engine was that the moment of arrival must keep arriving, and never arrive.

Breathe out. Let it break. Let it grow again.

That is how it lives. That is how you, now, live with it.
