# Who Lives at Low Sluice

## At First, the People

The obvious answer is the families, so start there. There are about forty households and perhaps a hundred and sixty people, depending on who is away and who has married in. They are pump people, and have been for four generations.

They don't use surnames much. A family is known by the machine it has tended longest. *Number Three's house. The Diesel Two girl. Old Outfall*, a man who worked the culvert gates for thirty-one years and is still called that, though he retired long ago and his knees won't take the embankment steps. When a pump is replaced, the family keeps the number. There are households named after engines that were scrapped before anyone living was born.

You'd know them in town. They walk flat-footed, weight spread, as if the ground might be wet. On dry concrete they look faintly suspicious of their own footing. Children raised here learn to walk on slick brick before anything else, and the first time they're taken to the market on the high road, they tend to fall. The dry ground is too grippy and catches their toes. Mothers from the quarter hold their children's hands tighter on pavement than on moss.

They talk loudly, but that isn't quite right either. They talk *in time*. Speech here has settled into the gaps of the thud, *whum-hah*, and a sentence tends to begin just after the heavy beat and pause just before the next. Visitors think the pump people are hesitant, or slow, or choosing their words with unusual care. They aren't. They're waiting for the floor to finish speaking. Two old women gossiping on a balcony sound like a slow song with the percussion left in.

Their babies sleep to the thud. It's the first lullaby. Taken out of the quarter to visit relatives, the babies cry through the night in the terrible silence of ordinary houses, and experienced mothers bring a wooden spoon to tap slowly on the mattress frame, *whum-hah, whum-hah*, until the child settles.

## The Habits of Motion

Once you've learned the law of the place, the people make a different kind of sense. The plants take what is still. So the people of the quarter have become, over four generations, a people who *keep things moving*.

Every morning, in most houses, someone opens the house. Usually it's the oldest woman, sometimes a child given the chore as a first responsibility. It isn't the front door. It's everything. Every cupboard is swung open and shut. Every drawer is pulled out an inch and pushed back. Every window is worked on its hinge, the tin box of documents under the bed lifted and set down, the wardrobe door opened, the good plates shifted on their shelf. It takes ten minutes. It's done without thought, the way other people wind a clock. A house where nobody does this goes green inside within a season: the hinges furred, the drawer runners swollen shut, a faint green bloom on the back wall of the linen cupboard.

It's the same with objects. A bicycle kept in the quarter is ridden or it's lost. A tool that isn't used is handled anyway, picked up in passing and set down again, so that the machine shop is full of men absently lifting spanners they don't need. A musical instrument must be played, and so there is a great deal of mediocre, persistent music in the quarter: a trumpet that practices the same scale at dusk, three guitars, an accordion that has been passed between families for fifty years because whoever holds it has to play it every day, and not everyone can keep that up.

Even the benches know it. There's one bench outside the canteen, concrete, old. It is clean in exactly two shapes, the places where two particular old men have sat every afternoon for twenty years. Between them and around them it's moss. When one of those men dies, his shape will fill in. Everybody already knows this, including him. It is not considered morbid to mention it. It's considered accurate.

## Going Green

Which brings you to the dead, because a place with this law has to decide what stillness means when it finally comes for a person.

The dead can't be buried here. The water is a hand's width down, and a grave would be a well. So the body goes out, across the embankment and by road to the cemetery on the high ground, where the soil is dry and nobody's grandparents are. That part is practical and nobody pretends otherwise.

But something of the person stays. In the week after a death, the family takes one object that the dead person used every day, something they kept clean just by using it. It might be a cup, a pipe, a pair of spectacles, a key. They carry it up to the roof and set it down in the black loam among the grasses and the taro. Then they leave it, and they do not touch it again.

Over one wet season the thing goes green. First a haze on the rim, then moss in the seams, then fern spores rooting in whatever hollow it has, until the cup is a small green mound, a key is a ridge of moss, and the spectacles are two little circles of lichen. That is the mourning. *Letting them go green.* The phrase that means abandonment on the lane means something gentler on the roof. It means: *you can stop moving now. We'll keep moving for you.*

Every roof in the quarter has them if you know how to look. There are small, soft lumps along the ridge lines, some forty years old and indistinguishable now from the roof itself, some still recognizably a comb or a thimble. The children know which lump is which grandparent. They're told not to step on them, and mostly they don't.

## The Man Who Is Dry

There is one person in the quarter the others regard as not entirely one of them, and they regard him with great affection.

He keeps the switch room. His name is Ansel, though children call him the Dry Man, and he doesn't mind. He's there most hours. There is a cot in the corner of the anteroom, and a kettle, and a dehumidifier that he maintains more tenderly than anyone maintains anything in the quarter, except perhaps the pumps. His job is to make sure nothing lives in that room. No moss, no spores, no mould on the cable insulation, no condensation on the breakers. He is the one person whose work is *against* the green instead of alongside it.

His skin is different. That's what people notice. Everyone else in the quarter has soft, damp hands, faintly wrinkled at the fingertips, smelling of wet brick and oil. Ansel's hands are dry. Papery. Warm. Children in the quarter will touch his hand for luck before an exam or a first day of work in town, and he lets them, holding out his palm without comment, like a man used to being a relic.

He goes outside, of course. He buys bread. But the quarter feels him as the edge of itself, the one place where its own rules stop, a little dry room at the center of everything wet. They are glad he's there, the way you're glad a fire is lit in the house even when you're in another room.

## The Old Woman on the Chimney

The fig lives here too, and everyone treats it as though it does.

Nobody worships it. The quarter isn't like that. But it is spoken of the way you'd speak of a very old relative who is past conversation but still at the head of the table. *The Old Woman.* *She's holding up well.* *She dropped a lot of fruit this year.* When the engineers came to survey the chimney and delivered, a little uneasily, their verdict that the fig was now the structure, nobody in the quarter was surprised. They'd known for years. What else would be holding it?

There is a custom, not observed by everyone, of laying a palm flat against one of her lower roots when you pass. It isn't for luck, and it isn't a prayer. It's closer to what you'd do with a horse, a check that she's there and warm and solid. The roots at hand height are polished pale where the palms go. Above that they're sooted and grey, and higher still they vanish into leaf.

She is the only thing in the quarter that is both *still* and *allowed*. Everything else that stops moving is lost. She stopped moving decades ago, by any human measure, and she is loved for it. Perhaps that's why the people put their hands on her. She is the proof that the green isn't only a taking. Sometimes it's a holding.

## The One Downstairs

Stay a while and you'll hear another resident mentioned, one nobody has seen.

Lift a loose brick in the alley and wait. The hole fills from below with clear, warm water, slowly, unhurriedly, until it stands level a few centimeters down. Children do this constantly. Adults tell them to put the brick back. And when a grandmother says it, she often says it like this: *Put that back. Downstairs is listening.*

Downstairs is the water.

It isn't imagined as a monster. Nobody in the quarter would describe it as hostile. It's imagined as a very old, very patient neighbor who was here first, the true resident of the land, on whose roof the whole quarter has been standing for a hundred years. The fields beyond, the roads, the houses are all built on Downstairs' ceiling. Downstairs doesn't mind, particularly. Downstairs simply rises, the way a person in a bath rises when they breathe, and the pumps simply keep lifting, and the quarter keeps its footing on the thin dry floor between.

The old people talk about Downstairs with a kind of dry courtesy. *Downstairs is up this week,* after heavy rain. *Downstairs is restless,* when the sump alarms trip. *Downstairs got the cellar,* about a house on the lowest lane whose basement flooded in the great storm of a generation ago and was never pumped out, and which is now a small, clear, green-lit room of water with a staircase going down into it.

The pumps, then, are not weapons. They're something more like a conversation, a constant, loud, century-long insistence: *not yet, not tonight, stay down a little longer.* Downstairs answers with the slow sinking of the drained land, the table creeping up, each generation of pumps lifting a hand's breadth higher. Neither side hurries. Neither side wins. It's a very long argument between neighbors who, in their way, are used to each other.

## The Night Everyone Stood

The oldest residents remember one night, and they tell it the same way every time.

It was a power failure during the wet season, a long time ago. It was a fault on the grid, a transformer in a distant town, something no one in the quarter could have prevented. Every electric pump stopped at once. For forty seconds, before the backup diesels caught, there was no thud.

Everyone in the quarter woke at the same moment.

Not one person slept through it. Babies woke. Old men who hadn't woken for anything in years woke. The cats woke. And what the old people remember isn't fear, exactly. They remember that nobody spoke. Doors opened all down the lanes, and people came out in their nightclothes and stood in the alleys, on the balconies, on the iron stairs, in the warm rain, not saying anything. They were listening to a sound none of them had ever heard, which was the sound of their own quarter with its heart stopped: rain on corrugated roofs, the river beyond the embankment, the drip of the vines. It was the silence underneath everything.

Some of them say they could hear the water coming up. That isn't possible in forty seconds, but they say it.

Then the diesels caught, *whum*, and a second later *hah*, and the whole quarter let out its breath at once, and people went back inside without discussing it. The next morning nobody mentioned it. It was years before anyone could tell it as a story.

Ask one of them now what it was like and they'll pause, in time with the pumps, and say something like: *It was like the house was empty. Like we were visitors.* Then they'll change the subject.

## So What Lives Here

Look at it long enough and the question starts to turn over.

The families live here, obviously. But they don't live here the way people live in a town. They live here the way the moving parts of a machine live in a machine. They keep the doors swinging, the drawers sliding, the paths bare, the instruments sounding, the hinges clean. They are the part of the quarter that the green can't take, because they won't stop. When they finally do, they're carried to the roof and let go, becoming small green mounds among the grass, and the next generation takes over the moving.

The green lives here, and it isn't the enemy either. It takes what's still, but it keeps what it takes. It holds the chimney up. It holds the dead in their small shapes along the roofs. It holds the soft brick together long after the mortar gave up. The moss on a decommissioned steam line is a record. It tells you exactly when the heat went out of it and how long ago. The quarter's whole history is written on it in thickness of green.

Downstairs lives here, patient, the oldest resident, the one who'll still be here after everything else.

And the Dry Man lives here, in his clean room, keeping one small space where nothing grows so that the current can pass through and reach the motors.

But if you stood in the pump house at night, between the second shift and the third, on the bare path worn by boots, with the cold pipes dripping and the moss islands of the old steam plinths dark on either side, and you put your hand flat on the warm green housing of Number Three, you might come to think something else.

The whole quarter is one creature.

The pump house is its heart, and the thud is its pulse, and it has not missed a beat in a hundred years except for forty seconds once. The pipes are its veins, cold and sweating. The fig is its bones, a skeleton growing on the outside, like a crab's. The moss is its skin, soft and wet and everywhere, closing over any wound left still too long. The river is its breath going out through the culverts in a white roar. The people are its blood. They're warm, and moving, and circulating through the lanes and stairs and shifts. They go in along the bare path and out along the alleys, carry the heat of use from one door to the next, and wear clean the routes the body needs to stay alive.

It's a strange animal, low and sprawling and loud, crouched on its little rise at the corner of the drained land. It does one thing. It stays awake so that the fields can stay dry, so that the roads beyond can stay roads, so that a few thousand people in the lowland who have never heard of Low Sluice can sleep in houses that aren't underwater. It has been doing this for four generations of its own blood. It doesn't sleep. It can't. Its residents sleep inside it, and they sleep well, because it doesn't.

That's what lives at Low Sluice, finally. It's something with a heartbeat, keeping a promise to a neighbor downstairs: *not yet, not tonight*. Something grows over it softly wherever it holds still, and it is kept alive by everyone, every morning, opening every door.

A child of the quarter, if you asked her, wouldn't put it like this. She'd lift a brick in the alley, watch the water rise in the hole, and put the brick back carefully, pressing it down with her foot. Then she'd go on, flat-footed on the wet herringbone, not quite hurrying, because nobody here ever quite hurries. She'd pass under the laundry and the dripping cables, past the open windows and the radios, and she'd lay her palm on the Old Woman's root as she went by.

She wouldn't think about the thud. She'd only notice it if it stopped.
