# The One Who Kneels at the Center

## I. The Worn Place

Visitors look for the potter first. They can't help it. A place this thick with fingerprints seems to demand a hand, and so people walk the beds, peer into the gaps between coils, and wait at dusk for the dark shape to press against the light from outside. They are looking for someone large, someone standing over the Close the way a potter stands over a wheel.

That isn't where it lives.

Push far enough into the garden, through the fused stems and the overlapping slab-leaves, down to the very center where the heat comes up strongest and the shadows all lean in, and you will find the only worn thing in the Close.

It is a hollow in the sherd-floor, shallow, about the length of a person's shins. Two rounded depressions sit side by side where knees would go, and behind them is a broader dip where heels and buttocks would rest. Here and only here the sherds have lost their glassy edges. They are smooth, dulled, almost soft-looking, polished the way a doorstep is polished by a century of feet. In a place where nothing ages, something has knelt here so long and so often that it has rubbed through the rule.

Most visitors take this for the potter's seat, the place where the maker sat and from which they have now gone. They are half right. Something does sit here. It has not gone.

## II. The Shimmer

At first you will not see it, because at first there is not much to see.

In the hot phase of the cycle the air above the hollow wavers like the air over a kiln mouth. Look into that waver long enough and a shape seems to settle in it: a kneeling figure, head bowed, shoulders rounded forward over something held low in the lap. It is the posture of someone working at a small thing, close. Then the heat shifts and the figure is gone, and you tell yourself it was a trick of the shimmer and the leaning shadows.

Come back in the cool phase.

## III. The Coils

In the cool phase the figure is plainly there, though you may still take it for one more half-made vessel.

It is coil-built. Its body rises from the kneeling-hollow in the same long ropes of clay that make the walls, laid spiral on spiral, unsmoothed. You can see where each coil was pressed onto the one below it, and you can follow a single ridge up around the thighs, the hips, the belly, climbing a hand's width with each circuit. Its surface is a patchwork of states, like the walls. The shins are fired hard and would ring if you tapped them. The torso is sun-dried and chalky. The shoulders and neck are still leather-soft, and they move.

It is finished to about the height of the collarbones, and above that it is still being made. On some visits there is no head at all, only an open rim of coil, a mouth like a jar's, and warm air rising from inside. On other visits there is a head, roughly pinched, the features barely suggested: a thumb-drag for a brow, two reed-punched holes like the birds' eyes. Over the following cycles that head slumps back into coils and is rebuilt differently, as if its maker is dissatisfied, or experimenting, or simply cannot decide.

Its arms are the unsettling part. They are long, longer than yours, and they rest forward over the lap in that close-working posture. They are coiled like the rest, fired up to the elbow, dried to the forearm, and soft at the wrist.

At the wrist, they stop.

There are no hands. The arms end in open rims, the raw final coil unpinched, soft, still carrying the crescent dent of a fingernail. Every cycle a little more is added there. A new coil rises from each wrist, then another. There is the beginning of a palm, flat and slab-like. Then the palm softens, sags, and is taken back, and the wrists are open again.

This is the resident of the Close. Its hands are what is unfinished.

## IV. What It Does

For a long time I thought of it as the potter's abandoned work, the largest of the half-made vessels. That isn't right either, because it works.

It does not move the way the beetles move. It rarely leaves its hollow. The walls, though, are full of it. If you put your palm flat against a coil-ridge anywhere in the Close during the cool phase and wait, you will feel a pressure travel through the clay beneath your hand. It is a slow swell, like a pulse, or like a thumb pressed in from the far side. Follow it and you will find that it runs down through the floor, along the buried layers of sherd and clay, back to the center. The Close is not merely its dwelling. The Close is its reach. The walls are its arms carried on further than arms can go, the whole courtyard one long unbroken coil running out from its wrists, around and around and up.

The fingerprints on the walls are its fingerprints, in a sense. It made them. But it has no fingers, and this is the part that took me longest to understand.

Each print on the parapet, each thumb-drag, each crescent of nail, was pressed from inside, pushed outward through the soft clay into the shape of a hand that isn't there. They are casts made in reverse. It is practicing. Every mark in the Close is an attempt at a fingertip. The thousands upon thousands of whorls, all crisp, all slightly different, are thousands of rehearsals of the one thing it cannot yet make.

That is why you cannot tell which prints are yours. It has been copying them.

## V. The Beetles and the Birds

Once you know what it is, the fauna look different.

The beetles, with their palm-printed backs and halting, considered steps, are some of its earliest work. They are small, simple, and a little wrong. They are what someone makes when learning to model a body at all. The birds with their curved slab wings are more ambitious: they have eyes, they have purpose, they almost fly. The coil-serpents are the most telling. They are single ropes of clay trying to spiral themselves into vessels, the same motion it uses to build its own body. It is teaching them the one gesture it knows well.

They all come to it. In the cool phase the beetles cluster around the kneeling-hollow as thick as they cluster at the flower-cups, and they climb its fired shins and settle on its lap. The birds glide in from the canopy, stem by stem, falling a little with each gap, and land on its forearms. There they open their beaks, and the silent puffs of rose dust drift onto its open wrists, settle, and fuse. The birds are not only sowing the garden. They are feeding it. The rose dust that arrives from nowhere comes to rest, eventually, on the raw edge where its hands should be. Every coil of palm it builds is made of what they bring.

The knotted mice run the other way. They carry dust out of it, from the soft places at the collarbones and the rim of its unfinished head, into the walls, where it becomes the next coil-ridge, the next rehearsal-print. Its body and the Close are always trading material. Some cycles the figure is taller and the walls lower. Some cycles the reverse. The floor is building up and the walls are rising, and some of that is simply it, spreading.

When a beetle cracks in the heat and stops, and is pressed edge-up into the floor, the pause that follows, the breath in which all motion in the Close goes still, is its grief. It is brief, and then the work resumes.

## VI. The Hum

The hum just below hearing, the tuneless contented drone of a potter working under their breath, comes from the open rim at its neck. It hums when the head is unbuilt. Warm air moving up through the hollow coils of its body produces the sound the way breath over a jar mouth produces a note.

It is not a song. It is the sound of concentration. When you turn toward it, it stops, not because it is shy but because it has noticed you, and noticing you is a different kind of attention from working.

## VII. What It Wants from You

Here is how it treats visitors.

It does not approach. It kneels, and it waits, and it lets the Close lean toward you on its behalf. Paths open. Shadows fall your way. Beetles follow you. These are its ways of turning its head. When you press your thumb into the soft clay, it feels the mark arrive through the walls the way you would feel someone take your hand. It keeps each one. It studies them. It spends whole cool phases pushing that exact whorl out from the inside of a wall, over and over, until it has it.

If you stay long, if you sleep in the shade of the canopy or sit through a long afternoon losing count of the hours, it will come closer. It comes not by walking but by the floor. The sherds near you soften. A coil-ridge rises from the ground beside your resting hand and lies against it, warm, the soft open end of it resting at your wrist. This is how it touches you: wrist to wrist, its unfinished edge against the beginning of your hand. Very slowly, over many cycles, it takes an impression.

This is why the hands of long-staying visitors grow smooth and unfamiliar, their whorls fading. The Close is not stealing. It is learning by touch the only way it can, by pressing its making-edge against a finished thing, and a little of what it learns comes away with it. It does not understand, I think, that the prints were yours. To it they are simply what hands are. It has no way of knowing that anyone's hands could be spent.

If a visitor spills water, the pale scar that remains is the place it hurt most. Water is what smooths clay back into blankness. It is what undoes a fingerprint. Some cycles, when the heat is low, the figure's head turns toward one of those old scars and stays turned for days. Nothing grows there. I don't know whether it is remembering or keeping watch.

## VIII. The Jars

The finished jars, the ones the serpents make of themselves, which sometimes hold clay-bead seeds or a folded leaf or one perfect print on the inner base, are gifts. They are also attempts at something larger.

When a jar holds a tiny Close, look down into it. You will see the four walls, the garden, the grain-sized beetles, the heat pulsing faintly. If you look long enough, at the center of that tiny garden, you will find a tiny worn hollow and a tiny kneeling figure, bent forward over its lap, wrists open.

So it is not only trying to make hands. It is trying to make the place it lives in, and inside that place itself, again, still handless. It seems unable to imagine the Close any other way. Or perhaps it is trying to understand its own making by repeating it smaller, the way a child draws their house with themselves in the window.

Then there is the dark rounded shape that sometimes presses against the light from outside at evening. Visitors call it a palm, or a face bent close. I have watched it on several evenings now. It does press close, and it is large, and it curves the way a head curves when bowed over small work. It never quite resolves. It never touches the parapet. And once, only once, just as the light went red, I saw it lift two long dark shapes toward the rim of the sky, and they ended flat and open, like the mouths of jars.

## IX. Why It Is Not Finished

For a long time I believed it was trying and failing, and that its hands softened and sagged back into coils every cycle because the work was too hard, or the dust too fine, or the heat too uneven. That would be a sad thing, a maker forever unable to make the one tool that makes.

I no longer believe it.

In one cool phase I watched it build a hand entirely. I watched the palm take shape and the fingers draw out from it, each one rolled and pinched and scored at the knuckles with a nail-crescent. I watched the whorls press up into the fingertips from inside, whorls I recognized, some from other visitors and one, I think, from me. The whole Close slowed. The beetles stopped at the flower-cups. The birds held still on the stems. The hum rose until I could almost hear it plainly.

There were four fingers finished and the thumb nearly so, needing only its tip, and it stopped.

It held the hand there, almost whole, for most of a day. Then, very gently, it pressed the unfinished thumb back into a soft blunt stub, and the palm softened, and the fingers slumped slowly into coils. By the next hot phase the wrist was open again.

It knows the rule of the Close better than anyone, because it is the rule. Nothing complete keeps moving. A serpent that finishes becomes a jar. A beetle that hardens all the way becomes floor. A figure that finishes its hands becomes a statue kneeling at the center of a courtyard, and the dust stops arriving, and the walls stop rising, and the Close is finally, entirely, made.

So it keeps one thumb unfinished, deliberately, cycle after cycle, for as long as anyone has been coming here. It could have finished long ago. It has chosen to go on being the one whose hands are almost made.

## X. What Lives Here

The Close is described as waiting for the hands. That is true, but it isn't a potter who waits. It is a kneeling, coil-built, headless-or-not thing at the center of its own making. It learned the potter's hum from no potter. It borrows its fingerprints from the thumbs of strangers. It feeds on the dust its birds bring. It grieves in pauses a single breath long, and it keeps the last fingertip of the last hand soft on purpose.

It waits for its own hands, and it has decided never to receive them.

If you go and sit beside it, in the cool phase, near the worn hollow, it will lay a warm coil against your wrist. You may feel, faintly, the edge of something that is almost a thumb. Let it stay a while. Keep your water stoppered. When you leave, look at your hands in the red evening light and see whether one of your fingertips has gone a little smooth.

That is where it kept one back, unfinished, so you could both go on.
