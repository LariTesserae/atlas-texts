# Those Who Live in the Stokemoor

You will not meet them first. You will meet their tuning.

Somewhere on the long crossing, after days of pushing through wet leaves and following the heat of buried mains through your soles, you will come into a stack-tree grove and stop without knowing why. It takes a while to understand what is different. The Stroke comes up through the ground, the trees take it, the grove sounds its chord, and the chord is *right*. It is not the mournful near-miss of every other grove you have passed, the sound of huge hollow things that each grew to whatever height they happened to reach. These trees resolve. The chord opens like a hand. It hangs in the wet air long after the Stroke has passed, and some part of you that has been braced for days lets go.

If you put your hands on the trunks you will find the reason. Near the crown, as high as you can reach and higher, the soft wood has been notched. It was done carefully and long ago. The cuts have healed over and been re-cut, healed and re-cut again, so the scars are ridged like the rings of a horn. Someone shortened these trees, a finger's width at a time, over generations, until they agreed with one another.

That is the first sign. There are others, once you know to listen for them. A pump-house's valves hiss a little more evenly than they should after ten thousand years. In a fork of stack-tree roots there is a smooth hollow worn by the repeated pressure of a body. There is a patch of bloom-lichen scraped clean in a spiral, and a bellows-bird that calls in a pattern that is not quite its own.

And there is the hammering. You have been hearing it the whole way, the faint clack and ring of rivetbacks feeding on distant pipes, the sound like workmen at a far-off job. Most of the time it is rivetbacks. Not always.

## The Undertone

Travelers who have crossed the Stokemoor and come out again call them the Undertone. The name is not wrong, but it is an outsider's name. It describes where their voices sit, not what they are.

What they call themselves cannot be said in the chorus. It can only be said in the Stroke.

This is the center of everything about them, so begin here. The Stokemoor's sound has three rooms, and every living thing on the moor has taken one.

The **chorus** belongs to the animals. After the Stroke, when the ground has settled and the air is thick with the ringing of every stone and stem, the bellows-birds inflate and hoot, the rivetbacks clack, and the little nameless things of the undergrowth pip and chitter and whistle. It is a room full of shouting.

The **eleven** belongs to the quietcat. In the gap before the Stroke the moor holds its breath, and in that held breath one hunter moves.

The **Stroke** itself belongs to the Undertone. Before them it belonged to no one, because it was unlivable. No animal can make itself heard against the WHUMM. It is too big, too low, and it comes up through the ground into your own bones and fills them. Anything calling during the Stroke is drowned. Anything listening during the Stroke is deafened.

Unless you speak *inside* it.

The Undertone speak at the pitch of the Stroke. Their voices are unbelievably low: a hum in the chest, a vibration more than a sound, pitched to lie along the WHUMM like a harmonic laid along a fundamental. When the Stroke rises they speak into it, and their words ride out across the moor on the back of the plateau's own resonance, carried through every stone and pipe the Stroke carries through. A word spoken in the Stroke at one pump-house can be felt, faintly, through the jawbone of someone pressing their face to a pipe a day's walk away. The moor is their wire.

To an outsider standing among them they are nearly inaudible. You feel them as a slight thickening of the Stroke, a texture in it, as if the great blow from underneath had grown a grain. You might stand in the middle of an Undertone village and hear nothing but the machine and the birds. Many travelers have done exactly that. They walked through villages without knowing, felt the ground shiver under their feet, and thought only that this stretch of moor rang oddly.

## Their Speech

A sentence, for the Undertone, is one Stroke long.

There is no other length a sentence could be. The Stroke rises, the ground lifts its fingernail's width, the ring spreads outward, and in that swell of sound a speaker says what they have to say. When the ring fades beneath the rising chorus, the sentence is over. Whatever was unsaid must wait forty-one seconds for the next one.

This makes them patient speakers, and precise ones. They do not interrupt; interruption is not physically possible. They do not ramble. A long argument between two Undertone elders might run for hours and consist of a hundred sentences, each one weighed through the whole chorus and the whole eleven before it is spoken. Their word for *thinking* is the same as their word for the chorus. It means the time you spend in the noise, preparing.

Their grammar is shaped by the swell. A Stroke has a rise, a peak, and a long decay, so their sentences have one too. The subject goes at the rise, the weight at the peak, the qualifications and softenings and afterthoughts in the long fade. The last words of any sentence are the quietest and are often lost on the far listener. A saying goes: *what matters is at the peak; what is true is at the tail.* To hear someone fully you must be close enough to catch their fading, and so, among the Undertone, closeness and honesty are one idea.

When they need to speak outside the Stroke, to a child in danger or to a stranger, they can. It costs them. Their voices at chorus-pitch are thin and uncertain, like adults sounding out a language learned late, and they use it the way you might shout in a sickroom: only when there is nothing else to do.

They never speak in the eleven. This is not taboo. It is simply death.

## Their Bodies

Nothing here is visible, so they have never been seen. Here is what they are to the hand.

They are tall, taller than most travelers, and long-limbed in the way of people who have spent their lives reaching up into trees and down into pipes. Their skin is smooth and very warm, warmer than a traveler's, and faintly damp always, like everything else on the moor. It takes the condensate the way leaves do. Touching an Undertone forearm is not unlike touching a mantle-fern frond or one of the great soft leaves along the steam mains: that same sense of a thin living surface with heat just behind it.

Their chests are deep and broad, barrel-deep, with a stiff flexible plate of cartilage over the breastbone that vibrates when they speak. Lay your palm there while one of them talks in the Stroke and you will feel the words as a buzzing through your hand, almost the tingle of cablevine. This is the true organ of their speech. Their mouths only shape it.

Their faces are the strangest thing. The brow is high, and above and between the eyes the skin thins to something like the membrane of a drum, taut and exquisitely sensitive. Across it they feel heat the way you would feel sunlight if you had lived your life in sunlight: direction and distance and shape. A buried main is a line of warmth across the forehead. A vent is a bloom. A fire-column is a searing point that must be turned away from. Some of them have a second, smaller patch of that fine skin on the throat, and a few on the palms, so that they can hold their hands out in the dark and *read* a room by its warmth.

They have eyes. It is not clear that they need them. The eyes are there, under lids that are usually closed, and the Undertone do not seem to think of them as sense organs at all. Asked, through great labor of translation, what the eyes are for, one elder answered that they were *for weeping*. The Stokemoor is so wet that everyone's face runs with condensate always, and to weep is to add your own water to the moor's. It is a gesture of grief and a gesture of belonging, both at once.

Their ears are large, flat against the head, and fleshy. Mostly they listen not through the ears but through the jaw. An Undertone who wants to hear something far away kneels and sets the angle of the jaw to stone or pipe, and the bone takes the vibration straight into the skull. You will find the places where they do this: small smooth dips worn into rock and iron all across their country, jaw-shaped, polished by uncounted faces. They are listening-posts. Some are so old they have been worn clean through the crust to the metal beneath.

## Time and the Count

They count to forty-one.

It is their only large number. Beyond forty-one they say *again*, and *again again*, and they have ways of tallying agains on knotted cablevine cords or by notches cut into the soft wood of the stack-trees. For ordinary life forty-one is enough. It is the Stroke. Everything is measured in Strokes and in parts of Strokes.

The final eleven of the count have no names. You count *one, two, three*, up to thirty, and then the count goes silent, because thirty through forty-one are the eleven, and you do not say anything in the eleven, even numbers. When they must refer to one of those eleven moments, they do it with a gesture: a hand pressed flat against the ground, a certain number of fingers raised. Their mathematics has a hole in it the exact shape of the quietcat's hunting.

Their names are rhythms. An Undertone child, somewhere in its first year, is given a pattern of taps: a short phrase struck on a pipe or a stone, fitted into the chorus in a particular relation to the Stroke. *Three quick, one slow, rest, two* might be one child's name. The name is struck, not spoken, and when you want to call someone across the moor you strike their name on a water main and let the iron carry it. They hear it through the jaw, wherever they are kneeling, and know they are wanted.

This is one of the sources of the hammering. When you hear what sounds like workmen far away, it is sometimes rivetbacks. Sometimes it is people calling one another's names.

## Where They Live

Their villages are built around the pump-houses and the stack-tree groves, and a village is less a set of houses than a tuning.

A pump-house is the heart of it. It is warm, full of steam, and loud with the piston's heavy rhythm. The Undertone do not live *inside* the pump-houses, which are too hot, too loud, and too sacred. But they cluster around them, and every morning (by the pump-clock's reckoning, which they keep perfectly) a keeper goes in.

This is the thing that travelers most often misunderstand. The pump-houses have been running since before memory, the stories say. The machine inside has worked unceasingly. And this is true. But it has not worked *untended*. There is not a pump-house on the moor that does not have its keepers. There are no more than a handful of Undertone at each one, and they have been keepers for longer than the Undertone themselves can count agains. They know the machines by touch the way a mother knows a child's back. They know every valve's hiss and every joint's warmth. They feel along the pipes with their brow-skin and find the hot spots where a seal is failing. They scrape the crust from bearings that would otherwise seize. They pack the leaking joints with rivetback shell and lichen and a greasy paste made from the bodies of oil moths, which are drawn to the old lubricant and carry it in their fur. They replace worn parts with parts forged at their own dark fires, by hand and by ear, the way their ancestors were taught, in a chain of teaching going back to the builders.

Do they know what the pumps are *for*? No. They know only that the pumps must not stop. The pumps are locked to the Stroke. If a pump falters, the Stroke of that place goes out of true, and the ground there rings wrongly, and the stack-trees fall out of tune, and the bellows-birds lose their sung map and go wandering. A dead pump-house is a hole in the land's music. The Undertone would sooner die than let one fall silent. Sometimes they do die of it: scalded, crushed, electrocuted. The keepers of the pumps are honored beyond all others, and they are always old, because the young are not trusted with it, and because the work kills people before they reach old age.

Around the pump-house, the stack-trees. The Undertone live in the trees, in the soft hollow trunks, which they carve into chambers stacked one atop another. These are warm and wet and vibrate with every Stroke like the inside of an organ pipe. A family lives in a tree. Their voices, spoken in the Stroke, ring out through the trunk and join the grove's chord, and this is why the Undertone must tune their groves: a grove out of tune muddies every word spoken in it. A well-tuned grove is clarity itself. You can hear a whisper at its far side. A village elder is, among other things, a tuner, and one of the high arts of their people is the cutting of a stack-tree to pitch. It is slow, because the tree must heal between cuts. It is risky, because a tree cut too short can never be lengthened again. The finest groves have been in the making for a thousand years.

Walking into such a grove, a traveler feels it before understanding it: the sudden resolution, the chord opening like a hand. That is what it is to arrive among people who have been listening to one another since before the ice.
