# Who Lives at the Works

## I. Not the Workforce

The obvious answer is everyone. The basin is packed and seething: bellows-backs to the edge of the fog, strikers ringing the stone, snails crunching underfoot, eels spitting violet across the flats. Ask who lives here and you could point in any direction and not be wrong.

But watch them for a while and something is missing. None of them *lives* here, not the way a fox lives in a hillside or a family lives in a house. Nothing nests. Nothing dens. Nothing curls up.

Nothing sleeps. The bellows-backs breathe through what might be night, and their calves are born mid-exhalation, sliding out of the mother already pleated and pumping, their first breath already in sync with the herd. Striker young hatch clinging to the parent's hammer-arm and ride it through thousands of strokes, until one day they climb down onto the schist and begin to strike, already in tempo, as if they had learned it in the egg. Snails graze while they mate and graze while they lay. Arc-eels discharge in the act of dying, their last arc indistinguishable from any other.

These are not residents. They are employed. They are on shift, and the shift does not end. They do not live at the works any more than the workers of a mill live on the mill floor. They are simply always there, which is a different thing.

So the question becomes harder. In a place where everything is working, who gets to live?

## II. Not the Tallymen

The next guess is the tallymen, because they are the only things that seem to stand slightly outside the labor. They don't strike, smelt, breathe heat, or spark. They move against the grain of the crowd, touching and counting. You could mistake them for management.

They are not management. Follow one long enough and it becomes clear they have no authority. They defer. When the whistle blows they freeze like everything else, their huge eyes fixed on nothing, and turn their quarter-rotation with the herd. They are clerks: timekeepers, inspectors, the people with clipboards who walk the line and write things down. They report to something. Every clerk does.

And they carry something away. That is the part worth following.

## III. Following a Tallyman

A striker drops out of the beat. It happens constantly somewhere on the plain, but here is one: an old one, its hammer-knob worn to a rounded nub, its legs shaking in the water sheet. The stroke slows. The district's tempo moves on without it, and it falls behind by a half-beat, then a whole one, then it is striking into silence between everyone else's blows. After a while it stops.

It doesn't fall. That is the first surprise. It stands where it is, hammer half-raised, and goes still.

The tallyman arrives within minutes, as described. It doesn't kill the striker. It doesn't eat it or drag it off. It reaches out with its long fingers and touches the striker's hammer-arm once, lightly, the way you might touch the shoulder of a coworker at the end of their last day. Its great eye closes and opens. Then it turns and goes back into the crowd.

Where it touched, there is a bolt. It is lying in the water at the striker's feet: small, hexagonal, threaded, bright gray, new.

The striker stays where it is. By the next shift change the mineral film has started on its legs.

## IV. The Quiet One

Here is where the answer starts.

The film is the only quiet thing in the basin. It grows on anything that stands still long enough, and in a place where nothing stands still, only the retired stand still. The film finds them. It climbs the striker's legs and closes over its back, and within a few shifts the old striker is a mound: a low gray-green hummock in the water sheet, shaped faintly like the thing it was, its hammer-arm still half-raised beneath the crust like a figure under a dust sheet.

Look across the plain with this in mind and you begin to see them everywhere. The ground is not flat because it was planed. It is flat because it is *upholstered*. Every soft rise in the film has something in it. The half-sunk bellows-back skeletons are not skeletons at all but bellows-backs that stopped, their pleated flanks closed for the last time and filmed over, lying in the slurry like overturned boats left out for the winter. There are snails sealed into their shells by the film, cemented in drifts. There are eels laid flat and transparent in the gray crust like pressed flowers. There are stalkers folded on their long legs, jaws closed, crusted where they knelt.

They are not dead. Put your hand on one of the mounds and it is very slightly warmer than the water around it. The warmth doesn't come from breathing. It is the warmth of something asleep under a blanket.

These are the ones who live here: the ones who have been let go. In a place where everything works, only the retired are residents. Everyone else is passing through on a shift that never ends, and the sleepers under the patina are the ones who stayed.

## V. What They Do

They dream. It sounds sentimental, but nothing else explains the evidence.

The shadows fall across the plain from nothing, long and straight and angular: chimneys, gantries, the roofline of a shed half a mile long. They always fall across the oldest, thickest parts of the film, where the mounds are so dense they have fused into one continuous gray swell. The shadows are cast from beneath, from below the film. They are the shadow of something being *imagined*, very hard, by thousands of sleepers who remember the work and have begun, in their stillness, to remember the building it should have happened in.

A worker who has struck stone every waking moment of its life knows the shape of the room around the stroke even if the room was never there. It knows where the wall should be by the echo that never came back, and where the roof should be by the cold that came down through it. Retired, filmed over, dreaming, it builds the room. The dream is precise enough to cast a shadow.

This is why bellows-backs won't stand in the shadows. They can feel the wall. It is why strikers inside a shadow keep perfect time: they are indoors, in the dream of the works, where the tempo is remembered rather than improvised, and nothing drifts.

It is also why sound arrives early. A dreamer doesn't hear the work as it happens. It hears the work as it has always happened, the whole long memory of the stroke, and inside the dream the sound comes first, because the sound was always the first thing a worker knew. Near the chimney-shadows, where the dreaming is thickest, you are standing close to the sleepers' sense of time, and you hear the next minute of the district before it arrives, the way they hear it: as a remembered thing.

## VI. The Ledger

The bolts are receipts.

Every one marks a life that fell out of the beat and was touched and let go. The tallymen count, and the count comes out in iron. The newest bolts lie bright in the water beside the newest mounds. The older ones rust and sink, and the oldest have been pressed down into strata so deep and so long ago that their threads are ground smooth and their heads are rounded into lozenges. Hundreds of feet of them, band after band.

This is what the basin makes. The snails' bloom is the ordinary iron of the place, the metabolism, the wage. The bolts are the ledger: every worker who ever finished, counted and fastened into the ground. Nothing uses them because they are not for using. They are the tally.

This also explains why distance is counted. The basin repeats, bay after bay, because it is not laid out in space. It is laid out in lives. A traveller walking toward the rim isn't crossing ground. They are being walked past the ledger, the same sheet again and again with the snails moved and the eels elsewhere, one entry after another, until some tally is complete that has to do with them. Then the ridges appear.

## VII. The Whistle

Now the whistle makes sense, or more sense than it did.

It doesn't come from a direction because it comes from underneath. It is one long falling note, the sound of a very large number of sleepers exhaling together as they settle deeper. Every workforce knows the sound of the end of a shift even if no one has ever blown it. The sleepers remember it, and so it blows.

At the whistle, everything stops. For the length of a long exhale the waking workforce is as still as the retired, and for that moment there is only one population in the basin, all of it holding still under the falling soot. Nobody on the plain is working and nobody is dreaming; they are simply there together.

Then the quarter-turn. It is the motion of a sleeper turning over. The whole waking plain turns in unison because underneath it, the whole sleeping plain has shifted its weight, and the workforce standing on top of the dream turns with it the way a passenger sways with a ship. The herds that were facing the chimney-shadows now face away. The strikers take up a new tempo, the rhythm of the dream's next room. Something has been handed over. The waking shift gives its day to the sleepers, and the sleepers give the waking a new floor plan to work inside of.

## VIII. The Oldest Residents

Go far enough back and the distinction breaks down.

The schist weeps. Every exposed rock carries its skin of moving water, and the drip lines have cut grooves older than any living lineage. The schist is folded and refolded, which is what happens to anything that lies still under enough weight for enough time. The concentric ridges in the plain look like the growth rings of a tree felled at ground level. They are the roots of mountains, and mountains stand still longer than anything.

The strikers do not eat the stone. They work it. They hammer it with slow, patient regularity, and their blows break it down into silt for the snails, and the snails make iron, and the iron becomes the ledger. Perhaps, then, the strikers are not working the stone at all. Perhaps they are waking it.

This is only a suspicion. It would mean the basin's first residents were the rim itself, the mountains that stopped, that were filmed over by time and sank and folded and began, at the very beginning, to dream the works. The first dream would have cast the first shadow, and the first shadow would have needed a workforce to fill it. On this reading, the labor didn't come first, and the dream didn't either. They came together, one standing on the other, and have been turning over together ever since.

## IX. What Is Being Built

It's cold here, the patient cold of a cellar. The bellows-backs heat the basin, but not efficiently and not enough. Soot falls on everything. The water never pools and never drains. The workforce stands in it forever, shouldered together, breathing warmth into the fog and losing it to the sky.

The sleepers remember this. They remember it better than anyone, because they spent their whole lives in it. It's worth asking what a retired worker would dream about, lying still under the film, warm for the first time.

A roof, probably. Walls that hold the heat in. Chimneys to take the soot away. A shed half a mile long, with the strikers inside it keeping perfect time out of the wind and the bellows-backs breathing into air that finally stays warm. It would be a works, and the waking would find it terrible and familiar and theirs.

The shadows are getting longer. The ledger is hundreds of feet deep and still thickening. Somewhere in the future of the basin, when the dreaming is dense enough, the shadow will stop needing to be a shadow. The chimneys will stand up out of the film, and the roof will close over the fog, and it will be warm. The sleepers will not wake when this happens, because they will *be* the building. The workforce will go indoors at last, into the finished works, built entirely out of everyone who came before them and stopped.

The place isn't haunted. Nobody is waiting for owners, and there is no machine and no plan. It is a gift being assembled very slowly, underfoot, by the retired, for the ones still on shift.

## X. You

Travellers who walk out in a straight line cross the same sheet dozens of times before they reach the rim. The ones who reach it have been counted and passed. Not all of them reach it.

Some of them stand on a polished tread of schist, with a striker hammering beside them and the eels crackling violet at their feet, and hear their own next footstep a few seconds early. They turn at the whistle with everything else, because by now it would feel strange not to. They take up the new tempo without meaning to. Their boots fill from below. They stop noticing the cold, and then they stop noticing that they have stopped.

At some point one of them doesn't take the next step. It isn't a decision. They have fallen half a beat behind the district and then a whole one, and then they are standing in the silence between everyone else's footsteps, and it is easier to be still.

A tallyman comes. It is small and dark, its long fingers light on your sleeve, its eye closing and opening once. It doesn't hurt. It is the touch at the end of a last day.

There is a bolt in the water by your boot, bright and new. The film begins at your ankles. It is warm under there, warmer than anything has been, and you can hear the whole district working around you: the thrum, the beat, the hiss and spit, every sound a little ahead of itself, as if you were remembering it. You are remembering it. You know exactly where the wall should be.

Somewhere above you, across the soft gray swell of your own shoulders, a new shadow is falling. It is narrow and upright, with a lintel, the shadow of a door.
