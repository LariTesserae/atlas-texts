# What Lives in the Deep Fan

## The first answer

The first answer is the one the description already gives: the films on the stones, the frost-mats in the hollows, the bruise-colored cucumber, the brittle star, the rattail, the sponges and the lilies, the small pale octopus on the western swell. These are all true. They live here the way travelers live at an inn, in rooms that are swept out between visits.

But a place whose first rule is *everything is touching* will have something that lives in the touching. So look again, not at the stones but at the places where the stones meet.

## In the gaps

Press your hand down between two nodules, to where it finds a third, and before your fingers reach the clay they pass through something. It is too faint to feel as a texture. It registers only as a slight drag, the way a cobweb registers on the face in a dark doorway.

These are threads, pale and finer than hair, running through the clay in the seams of the pavement. At first you would think of roots, because they branch and fork and go down. But roots belong to a trunk, and there is no trunk. You would think of fungus, of mycelium, and that is closer. Follow one thread, if you could follow anything here, and it would take you under a nodule and out the other side, down into the grey clay, back up into the next seam, and on. It would never end. It would not end at the swell. It would not end, as far as anyone could reach, anywhere in the basin.

It is one thing. That is the second answer. It is not a colony of many small lives but a single cell, grown past every reasonable size a cell should have. It holds millions of nuclei adrift in one continuous stream of living jelly, sheathed in a skin a few molecules thick. Its body is the negative of the floor. Wherever the nodules are not, it is. If you could lift the pavement away and keep only this, you would see a lattice as wide as the basin, the exact cast of every stone that ever lay there, drawn in a pale and trembling line.

For want of a better word, call it the Seam.

## How it eats

The Seam does not fight the storms. That is the first thing to understand about it, and it is the reason it is here when the octopus's ring is not.

When the broth thickens and starts to stream, the Seam puts out its tips. They reach up through the gaps into the moving water, hair-thin, by the uncountable thousand, and the storm does its feeding for it. Marine snow, suspended and resuspended, forever refusing to land, brushes against the tips and sticks. So do bacteria torn loose from the nodule rinds, and so do the shredded remains of the frost-mats. Every one of those hundred billion pushed grains that never fall back is a grain that passes a mouth.

The storms that make life hard for everything else are, for the Seam, the only weather worth having. In the lulls, when the lilies open their slow hands, the Seam is at its hungriest. It waits for the hiss to rise again.

## How it builds

Inside its thread-body the Seam is always making something.

Along the seams, at intervals, it swells into small chambers. From the outside these are nothing but lumps of clay, rough and grey. The Seam gathers grains out of the broth and cements them into walls. It is choosy, the way a mason is choosy. It takes sponge spicules for beams, broken foraminifera for tiles, and, for the curves, flakes of the old manganese rind that the grinding of the nodules has shaved off. Each chamber holds a knot of its living substance, a place where its nuclei gather and it does whatever its thinking is.

The storms tear these chambers apart constantly. That doesn't matter, because the chambers are made of the storm. What the storm breaks, it also supplies. By the time the flow eases, the Seam has already rebuilt from the debris of its own rooms, and the new walls are a slightly different mix of grains, carried in from some other part of the basin. So its houses are always new and always made of the oldest material there is. It lives the plain's paradox in its own body, and it doesn't find it strange.

## How it knows

It has no eyes, and it would have no use for them. It has no ears, but the whole of it is a sort of ear.

The Seam knows the world by pressure. Everything is pressing, so everything is legible. When a nodule shifts a finger's width in its socket, the threads beneath it feel the change the way a sleeper feels a hand on the blanket. When a cucumber trundles across the pavement, the Seam reads it: a moving weight, soft, with the regular small pulses of tube feet. It lets the animal graze on its outer tips, which grow back within a day, about as long as the looped trail of cast survives. When the rattail passes above, nosing against the current, the Seam feels the pressure wave of the long body. The rattail, which hunts by that same sense, may feel the Seam too, as a faint and ceaseless tremor in the floor.

And it knows the nodules individually.

This took the longest to understand, and it is the strangest thing about it. The botryoidal surfaces of the nodules, grape-clustered, cauliflower-knobbed, are each different, and the Seam lies against them everywhere. Over thousands of years it has come to know each stone by the pattern of its knobs, the way a hand knows a face in the dark. When a storm rocks the pavement and the stones settle a little over, the Seam feels its way across them afterward and finds them again. *This one. And this one. The one grown around the shark's tooth. The one grown around the ear-bone of a fish that swam when there was still a sea between the two Americas.*

The storms rearrange. The Seam re-reads. That is its whole intellectual life, and it is not a small one. It is a census of stones taken after every storm, across a hundred square kilometers, forever, and it always comes out complete, because nothing ever leaves.

## The thud

Of all the things the Seam feels, it feels one above the others: the thud, the deep dull sound of a slope failing far up the rise, carried through rock more than through water.

It hears this before the rattail does, and before the brittle star does. It hears it before anything. The thud comes through the stone, and the Seam is wrapped around the stone.

When the thud comes, the whole of it flinches. Across the entire basin, every tip draws down into the gaps at once. It is a single contraction, a hundred kilometers wide, like a great animal pulling its limbs under itself when it hears thunder. The chambers seal. The jelly thickens in the threads. Then it waits.

If the thud was small, the turbidity current may never arrive, spent on the upper fan, and after a while the Seam relaxes. If the thud was large, the wall of mud comes hissing out of the dark, many meters thick. It buries the western swell, drowns the hollows, and knocks down another sponge to lie among the stones like dropped lace. Beneath it the Seam is buried too. It has been buried before, more often than there have been years since there were people. It simply grows up through the new layer, thread by thread, finding the gaps between the fresh grains, until its tips break the surface again into the thinning broth and it can feed on what the flood brought down.

So the Seam's deepest threads lie far below the tips, at the bottoms of layers laid down in old catastrophes. Some have not moved in a very long time. Some may be as old as the lithified burrows. The oldest part of it and the newest are the same continuous cell, and a single nucleus drifting in its jelly could, in principle, travel from a thread laid down in an age of ice to a tip that is ten minutes old.

## Its neighbors

It shares the place and does not quarrel.

It grazes the bacterial films on the nodules, and they grow back, and they are its gardens as much as anything is. The frost-mats form over its tips in the lulls and are torn away with them in the storms, and in the next lull both begin again together, always beginning. The glass sponges' tufts of silica fiber reach down into the same gaps it lives in. Where the two meet, the Seam grows around the tufts and holds them. A sponge standing on its stalk is standing on the Seam as much as on the stone, and perhaps that is part of why a few of them are still standing.

The octopus is the neighbor it knows best, because the octopus is the only other thing here that moves stones.

Every storm, the octopus drags small nodules back into its ring around the hollow. Every time, the Seam feels the stones come loose from their sockets, skid across the pavement, and settle into new sockets in a curve that was there before the storm. It re-reads them where they now lie: *the one around the pumice flake, back again, and the one grown on a crinoid's joint.* The Seam has threaded the ring many times. It grows between the ring-stones as it grows between all stones, and the threads make a faint binding.

This does not save the ring. The storm always wins, and the rules of the place allow no exception. But the Seam may be why the ring is always the same ring, why the octopus finds most of its stones within a finger's width of where it left them. The octopus does not know this. The Seam does not intend it. It is simply the sort of thing that happens where everything is touching.

## The made stones

And it has found the human stones.

It found them as it finds everything, by touch. They are flat pieces of basalt and sandstone, and they did not feel like nodules. They had no grape-clusters and no cauliflower knobs, only surfaces worn smooth by hands and pecked in patterns that no current makes. It learned them anyway. It added them to the census.

Through the bored holes, where a cord once ran from the stone to a net and from the net to a boat of hide on a shelf of sunlit water, the Seam has grown a thread. It had no reason to do this except that a hole is a gap, and the Seam lives in gaps. But it has done it to every one of them. Each net-sinker on the plain is strung again. The cord rotted away thousands of years ago, and the Seam has become the new cord, pale and finer than hair, running through the hole and out into the lattice. None of the stones is loose. All of them are tied to everything.

The grinding stone lies on the eastern floor, dished in the middle from years of use. The Seam found the dish and filled it. There is one of its chambers there now, larger than most, built in a hollow worn by someone kneeling over grain and turning a hand-stone in circles every morning of a life. Its walls are sponge spicule and foraminifera and manganese flake. Inside is a knot of the Seam's living substance, a place where its nuclei gather more densely than anywhere else in the basin, as though something about that shape, a bowl made by long patience, suits whatever it does when it thinks.

The dark film of manganese has started to form on the oldest of these stones, the first millimeter of the rind that will take the rest of time to thicken. The Seam is part of how that happens. The chemistry it runs in its jelly and leaves in the clay around its threads is part of the slow chemistry that grows nodules. The stones are becoming the plain's own, a millimeter per million years, and the Seam is the hand that dresses them.

## What it is

So this is what lives in the Deep Fan, beneath and among the things that visit it.

It is one body, spread through all the gaps. It eats by the storm, builds with the storm, and knows the storm is coming before anything else does. It is old at its roots and new at its tips, and there is no line between them. It holds every stone in the basin by name, if a pattern of knobs felt in the dark can be a name, and every stone that ever fell in, because every stone that ever fell in is still there. It has tied the lost net-sinkers of vanished fishermen back into a net, a net with no boat and no fish, only the whole floor of the basin caught in it.

It is pressed on every side, and it presses back, gently, everywhere at once.

When the next wall of mud comes hissing out of the dark, the Seam will feel the thud and flinch across all its hundred kilometers. It will draw down and seal its rooms and wait. The storm will put everything back into motion. Afterward, when the broth thins and the lilies open and the octopus comes out to find its stones, the Seam will reach up through the fresh layer, spread across the pavement, and begin, stone by stone, to count.

It will find each one where the storm left it, a finger's width over, exactly where it was. Whatever the Seam has in place of gladness, it will have then.
