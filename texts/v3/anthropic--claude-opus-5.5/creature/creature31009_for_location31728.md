# Who Lives at Hollow Coin

## I. The Figure on the Floor

Start with what you would see first, if you had climbed the cliff and walked a day inward across the combed rings: a single upright shape, far off, that is not a shoot.

It is too short, perhaps two and a half metres, and too still. Shoots sway at their tops. This does not sway at all. As you come closer through the tea-coloured light it resolves into a person kneeling on the floor of the plain, bent forward from the hips, with one long arm extended and the hand resting flat on the wood. In the other hand is a spike of dark heartwood as long as a forearm. Its point is set into a hairline crack between two rings.

Her name is Imre. Among the Tuners a name is not quite a word. It is a pitch with a word attached for convenience. Hers is a quarter-tone beneath the Seventh Bell in the present key, which is the Ninth Key since the records began. Her full name is closer to a short phrase of music than to anything you could write down. Her companions shorten it to a small falling sound made at the back of the throat, and "Imre" is what that sound becomes when it has to pass through a mouth shaped for other languages.

She has been kneeling here since before the dawn pitch peaked. She is listening to a ring.

## II. Her Body

She is built narrow and tall, like the towers, and for the same reason: tall narrow things resonate cleanly, and anything broad and soft on this plateau is shaken to exhaustion. Her limbs are long in the bone and thin in the flesh. Her joints are knuckled and slightly swollen, like the nodes on a grass stem, and they are where her body does most of its hearing. When the drone swells, you can see her knees and elbows take it. There is a faint shiver at each joint, passed upward like a message relayed tower to tower.

Her skin is pale grey, the colour of shoot-bark, with a fine dry grain to it. It does not sweat. Nothing here sweats. On the palms of her hands and the soles of her feet the grain deepens into ridges, low and parallel, a miniature of the combed floor. Laid flat on the wood, those ridges read vibration the way a fingertip reads raised letters. With her hand on the plain she can tell you where the nearest rasp herd is grazing, which bells are beating against each other, and whether anything is moving in the grain below. She has stopped needing to think about it. The knowledge arrives in her the way temperature does.

Her ears are small, set close to the skull, and mostly vestigial. On the Stump the ears would be overwhelmed. The whole body is the ear. Her eyes are large, dark, and wide-pupilled, made for dimness. In full copper dusk she sees better than at noon. At night she can make out the violet glow of grainmoss across several kilometres, and she can follow the ember-line of a sawyer's track through the floor long after it has faded past any other eye's ability to see it.

Under her breastbone there is a hollow.

It is not a lung, or not only a lung. It is a small resonant chamber, shaped like the bells in the heartwood and like the chest of a knellbird. Air moves through it, thin and cold and dry. When she speaks, the chamber rings. Tuners do not so much talk as sound, softly, just above the drone, in tones shaped by the throat and coloured by the chest. Pressed against someone, the chest-bell carries meaning through contact more clearly than through air, so most real conversation among them happens leaning together, sternum to shoulder, like two people sharing warmth. From a distance a pair of Tuners in conversation look like mourners. They are only talking.

## III. What She Eats

There is no water, so there is no drinking. She eats, but lightly and strangely.

She eats pollen-dust. It is bronze and faintly sweet, and it settles on everything. A Tuner walking the plain gathers it on the lips and tongue without effort. After a dust-storm the Tuners eat well, and there is a kind of festival mood in the towers once the veil lifts: people with bronze-dusted mouths, humming a little higher than usual.

She eats heartwood, in shavings pared from the inner walls of the towers with a blade tuned to the local bell. The blade does not cut. It persuades. The shavings are dark, resinous, and very hard, and she holds them in her cheek for hours, the way someone might hold a lozenge.

Mostly she feeds the way the Stump feeds. Every tower has throats: narrow vertical shafts where the drone is gathered and focused. A Tuner who has gone too long without nourishment climbs into a throat and sits there, back to the wood, eyes closed, and lets the sound pass through her. The pressure waves that nudge resin and carbon through the grain of the plateau do the same inside her body. They shoulder her nutrients along, cell by cell, and move the dust and shavings she has eaten into the places she needs them. She comes out of a throat moving slowly, her joints still humming, as if she had slept in warm sun. Tuners call it *being carried*.

This is the first hint of what she is, though she does not think of it as a hint. It is just how eating works.

## IV. Her Work

She is a ring-listener. It is the oldest profession on the Stump and, now that there are so few Tuners, the least practical. Retuners keep the held rooms standing. Bridge-singers keep the ribbons of pressure solid between towers. Listeners contribute nothing that anyone can lean against. They are tolerated and honoured, a little like monks.

Her spike is a listening-spike: heartwood grown and not carved, dense as horn, with a thin channel bored along its length. When it is set into a crack between rings and struck with the right note, the ring beneath begins, faintly, to play back. The sound travels up the spike into her hand and wrist and elbow and shoulder and down into her chest-bell, and she hears what was sounding over the plateau when that band of grain was laid down, a very long time ago.

Most of it is weather: winds, old drones in old keys, the wail of shoots that fell and were absorbed ten thousand years before her. Some of it is rasp herds, a hiss like fine rain, which is another thing no one here has heard. Some rings carry the knellbirds of their age, calling at pitches no living knellbird knows. Imre has spent years in a single ring following the drift of one knellbird's call across a whole century of grain. She hears it go from young and uncertain to steady to old, and then stop.

What she is looking for, what every listener is looking for, is voices: the builders, the ones who sang the towers up out of the plateau and are supposed to have written themselves into the grain.

## V. Her Tower

She lives in a tower the others call Lean, because it tilts a few degrees off vertical. It was grown that way, and no one knows why. It is one of the thirty that still stand and one of perhaps eleven still inhabited. Forty Tuners live there. It was built for four thousand.

The lower levels are wood: rooms that curve out of the grain, floors that rise from the walls without a join, windows that are simply places where the wood is thin enough to glow bronze when the coin is low. The upper levels are held. Imre's own room is on the fourth held level. Three of its walls are sustained chords, and one is the curve of the tower's outer skin. When she comes home she brushes her fingertips along a held wall to check it. It is cool, slightly yielding, and humming. She can tell by the texture whether the chord has drifted.

A knellbird nests in the throat above her room. It is old, grey-chested, and heavy. When the tower's chord weakens, the bird calls at the failing pitch, over and over, until someone comes. Imre has come to think of it as a housemate, or perhaps a landlord. It has lived in Lean longer than she has, and it tunes with a patience that no Tuner can manage.

To leave her room she needs her token: a disc of heartwood the size of a palm, worn smooth at the edges, which rings at her pitch when she taps it with a fingernail. The door at the end of the corridor opens only for her note. When she strikes the token and steps through, the doorway loosens around her like cold honey. She feels the grain pass across her skin, over the ridges of her palms and the nodes of her joints, and it closes behind her. It has done this every day of her life, and it still slows her breath each time.

Every Tuner knows the thing no one says: this is how the sawyers move. The doors of pitch, the tokens, the whole architecture of passage is sawyer knowledge, learned by listening to predators swim through the floor. The builders learned it by listening to predators. To go from room to room, the Tuners pass through the wood exactly as the sawyers do.

## VI. The Null

Lean's null room sits at the tower's exact centre, down a spiral stair into the heartwood. It is small and round. The tower's chords meet there and cancel.

The first thing you feel on entering is not quiet. It is loss of footing. The body has spent every moment of its life braced against the drone, leaning into it the way a walker leans into wind, and in the null the wind stops. Visitors stagger. Many sit down without meaning to.

Then they weep. Tuners do weep, though they have no water. Their tears are resin. Small beads of it well at the corners of the eyes, amber and slow, and harden as they fall. After a long visit to the null there are sometimes tiny beads scattered on the floor, like seeds. Tuners collect their own. Imre has a small bowl of them in her room, collected over forty years. In certain light they glow.

This is where Tuners are born. It took Imre a long time, as a child, to understand what that meant, because she assumed it was a figure of speech.

It is not.

## VII. How a Tuner Is Born

When two Tuners marry, they go down to the null together with their tokens. They strike them and hold the two notes, one against the other, inside the perfect silence. The null takes the notes and tries to cancel them, as it cancels everything, and it nearly succeeds.

But not quite. Two distinct pitches cannot be perfectly cancelled by a room tuned for a different chord. A remainder is left behind: a small beating tone, the interference of the two notes, trembling in the middle of the silence. It is new, a pitch that did not exist before.

The pair leave it there. Over the following weeks the floor of the null begins to thicken at one spot. The wood rises, very slowly, in a small mound that hums at the remainder pitch. Grainmoss threads appear on it, violet-black, and then retreat as the mound grows smoother and paler and more distinct from the floor. After most of a year it has the shape of a curled body. After a year and a season it opens its eyes.

Tuners are grown. They come up out of the Stump the way shoots do, the way grainmoss does, the way, some say, knellbirds do. The grain of Imre's palms is the grain of the floor of Lean's null room, and at one time the two were one surface. If you set a listening-spike against the base of her spine, you would hear the tower.

Everything that grows on the Stump is the Stump. This includes the people.

And so dying happens in the null too. When a Tuner's pitch begins to slip and fail, and the chest-bell loses its ring, they go down the spiral stair and lie on the floor, and the silence takes the last of their note. Then the floor takes the rest. It goes slower than birth. Over years the body softens back into the grain, the ridges of the palms merge into the rings, the resin tears sink in, and after a while there is only a faint pale shape on the floor. Then there is not even that.

The next ring the Stump lays down will record it.

## VIII. What Imre Found

For forty years she listened to the ring called the Fourteenth Lean, a narrow dark band, a starvation ring. She chose it because it was difficult. Starvation rings are dense, their grain so tight that playback comes out compressed and shrill, and most listeners avoid them. She was looking for the builders and had failed to find them in the broad pale rings of plenty, where everyone looks.

In the forty-first year she found a voice.

It was very faint. It was a chest-bell voice, soft and pitched just above the drone of an old key, talking the way Tuners talk, in close tones meant for someone leaning against the speaker's shoulder. She could not understand the words. The language had drifted as far as the keys had. But she knew the shape of it at once, the way you know a face in a crowd before you know whose it is.

It was her pitch, a quarter-tone beneath the Seventh Bell. It was not this key's Seventh Bell, but the Seventh Bell of a key eleven thousand rings gone. The interval was exact.

She sat with her hand on the floor for the rest of that day and all night, through the volleys of cracks running out across the plain, and did not move. At dawn she went back to Lean and down the spiral stair into the null, and she wept there for a long time.

## IX. What the Tuners Are

This is what she came to believe, and it has since spread slowly through the eleven towers, as things spread among a people who talk only by leaning together.

The builders did not write themselves into the rings to be found later. There will be no library at the bottom of the heartwood waiting for the right note, and no population sleeping in the grain for a key to release it.

The builders wrote themselves into the rings so that the Stump would keep playing them.

A null is a room where a chord almost cancels, and a new pitch is born out of the imperfection. The pitch that is born is not random. It is the interference of two living tones, but those tones are shaped by the tower, and the tower is grown from grain, and the grain is ring upon ring of old sound. Some very old voice, laid down in the wood and still vibrating faintly in the lattice, colours every remainder. A Tuner is not a new person. A Tuner is an old ring being played back, in flesh, as close to its first voice as the present key allows.

Imre is not descended from the builders. She is one of them, being sounded again.

So are all the others. It explains why names are pitches, and why Tuners feel, when they first strike their token as children, that they are remembering a note rather than learning it. It explains the grief in the nulls, which no one can name. It explains why there are fewer Tuners every key. Rings are lost. The sawyer swarms churn bands of the plateau into new grain and erase what was written there. Each erasure is someone who will not be sung again.

It also changes what the listeners are doing. They were never searching for the builders. They are the builders, searching for the rest of themselves.

## X. The Larger Thing

Imre went further than this, and fewer people follow her here.

She began to listen differently. She stopped listening only for voices in the rings and began listening to how the rings follow one another: the sequence, and what changes from one band to the next. Laid end to end, the rings are not a history. A history is a record of what happened. The rings are more like a sentence.

Each ring is laid down over about a century. Each is shaped by the sound of its time, and each time the Stump lays one down, the whole plateau changes key. It does not drift. It shifts deliberately, all the bells at once, a new chord replacing the old. A key change every hundred years reads, if you step back far enough, as a single long note followed by another.

The Stump is saying something. It says it very slowly, one syllable a century.

The Tuners, the knellbirds, the rasps grazing toward the swelling bells, the swifts' wings snapping the evening into sound, and the shoots raised up to catch the sky and sing down into the grain are not the Stump's inhabitants the way fleas inhabit a dog. They are the Stump's mouth and ears. The knellbirds keep its pitch true. The rasps sculpt the latewood so the surface resonates in the right patterns. The sawyers edit, scouring away phrases that did not work. The shoots listen upward. The Tuners listen inward, into the rings, which may simply be the Stump rereading what it has already said before it says the next part.

And at the edge of the plateau, where the drone thins and the wind can be heard, there is the other drone. It is far off, lower and slower, in no key the Stump knows.

Imre has gone to the rim many times now. She lies down on the rounded shoulder of the cliff with her palms flat on the wood and her chest-bell pressed against the grain, so that she is listening with the whole length of her body, and she waits. Very faintly the far drone comes, and against it, if she holds still long enough, she can feel the Stump's own drone change. The shift is small, a slight leaning of its pitch toward the other one.

She believes the Burn was a word. It was something one of them said, or something said to all of them at once, loud enough to scorch the grain black. The other stumps of the old forest, wherever they stand, laid down the same black ring in the same century. Some of them may not have survived saying it. Or hearing it.

She believes this Stump has been answering ever since.

## XI. Who Lives There

So the answer comes in layers, the way the sound does.

On the surface there are the animals: the rasps grinding across the latewood like a distant sawmill, the swifts turning in smoke-flocks, the knellbirds heavy on the few upright things, and the sawyers below, swimming through the grain with their ember tracks fading behind them.

Among them are the Tuners, a few thousand, tall and grey and quiet. They lean together to speak, weep amber in their silent rooms, eat dust and sound, and pass through their doorways as predators pass through wood. They grieve for a city built for far more of them than there are. They are grown from the floor and go back into it.

Beneath the Tuners are the builders. They did not die out. They are still being played, dimmer each key, one ring-voice at a time, rising out of the null rooms with names that are pitches and a feeling they cannot place of having stood here before.
