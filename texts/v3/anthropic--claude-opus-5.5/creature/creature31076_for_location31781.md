# Who Lives in the Lantern Cistern

## I. The small tenants

Start with what you can count.

The shrimp are the easiest to miss and the oldest residents. They have lived here long enough to lose everything the chamber made useless. They have no pigment, because nothing here hunts by sight in the water. They have no fear of the dark, because the dark comes only between pulses and never lasts. What they have instead are eyes: two stalked, glassy beads that are nearly all lens, larger in proportion than any shrimp's should be. They are not looking at the Wick. They are looking *away* from it, toward the walls, at the second shadows.

Hold one on your fingertip in a crescent of shallow water and watch. As the column swings and the light shifts, its eyes do not follow the brightness. They follow the fainter shadow that falls the wrong way. The shrimp orbit the base of the column in a slow, patient ring, and the ring is not centered on the column. It is centered a little off, on a point in the pool where nothing is. The point drifts, and the ring drifts with it. After a Breath, when they are stranded and scattered across the terraces, they do not flick back toward the pool at random. They flick back toward that point.

The ferns turn to track the Wick, yes. But look closely at the undersides of the fronds, where the white veins branch. They are not uniform. On every frond the veins are densest on the side facing *away* from the light, as if the ferns are drinking from two directions and the fainter one is richer.

Then the swifts. They are not quite swifts. When one lands on a ledge to preen, its wings, folded, are longer than its body by half. Its feathers shed water perfectly, and its eyes are the same over-large lenses the shrimp have. Their nests are wedged high in the deepest flutes, where the chord is loudest. They are not made of mud or spit. They are made of mineral crust, grown slowly around a base of woven fern, and the crust is clear. Each nest is a little bowl of rough crystal. The chicks, which are rare (one or two a year, perhaps), sit in them as if in lenses.

These small tenants all share one habit. They live as if there were a second, dimmer lamp in the room, and they arrange themselves around it.

## II. The keepers

Then there are the ones who marked the walls.

Read the tallies as a record of lives rather than of time. Each run of marks in a single hand is a person, arriving, staying, counting something, leaving or dying. The gaps are the years when no one came. The styles change completely between hands: notches, then dots in rows, then small chevrons, then bars crossed in fives, then a spiral that tightens with each mark. These are not one people. They are many peoples, none of whom knew the others, each finding the stair on their own and coming down into the roar.

What were they counting? Line the tallies up against the device on the east wall, the bead of light sliding in its curved slot, and the answer is almost certainly Breaths. Every group of marks matches the rhythm of the deluge. But the counts are not steady. The oldest keepers recorded Breaths far apart, a few in a day. The later ones marked them more often. Whatever keeps the Breath's time has been speeding up across all those lives, very slowly, the way a sleeper's breathing quickens near waking.

The hut on the ledge was built by one of the earliest hands and kept up by all the later ones. You can see the repairs: stones reset with a different care, a patch of gray metal of a slightly different gray. The bench is worn into two shallow hollows, not one. For a long time, perhaps always, keepers came in pairs. One sat and watched; one slept.

The lenses in the niche, sorted largest to smallest, are the keepers' real work. Take one out and hold it up. Look through it at the Wick and you see nothing but glare. Look through it the other way, toward the wall, into a second shadow, and you see something.

## III. The one in the second shadow

It is hard to say what. The lenses do not agree with one another. Through the largest, you see a shape like a heat-shimmer standing in the rain, taller than the chamber is wide, which cannot be right. Through the smallest, you see a knot of lines like the veins of a fern leaf, close enough to touch. Through the middle ones you see things in between: a curve like a shoulder, a surface like the inside of a shell, and once, briefly, something that might be an eye, closed.

This is the resident. Everything else in the chamber, the shrimp and ferns and swifts and the long line of keepers, is a guest of it, or a symptom of it, or a companion.

It does not cast the second shadow. It is where the second shadow comes *from*. There is a light behind you in this room, faint and warm, and it is not the Wick's. It is the resident's. It is lit the way the Wick is lit, from inside, but dimly, like an ember under ash. When drops fall slow near the column, they are falling through the edge of it. When a flicked droplet hangs too long at the top of its arc, it has brushed against it, and it has paused to notice.

You could call it a body, but that is not quite right. Its extent matches the chamber's too well. When you set the largest lens against the flutes, the shimmer's outline follows them. When you set a small lens against the terraces, the veined knot lies exactly along the crescents' rims. The resident is the same shape as the room. Or the room is the same shape as the resident.

Now the carved word makes sense. It lies beneath the oldest tallies, and it is so deep that the water has had to go around it. No keeper could cut stone like that with a tool. It was cut by someone who knew exactly where the resident's outline did not reach, a place the Wick's water would never be sent, and wrote there. The word is a name, the only thing in the chamber the Wick leaves alone. Nobody can read it now. The swifts, perhaps, can.

## IV. What the Wick is doing

So look at the Wick again, and set down the idea of a lamp, and the idea of weather too.

The Wick is a hand. It lifts the water and drops it over the resident, over and over, unevenly, along the resident's shape. Where the rain falls, the stone wears. The water carves the room around the one who lives there, the way you might pour sand over a buried thing to find its outline, or the way a sculptor removes everything that is not the figure. The flutes are its ribs being found. The terraces are its scales. The drains are wherever it breathes out.

The Breath is not the Wick's breath. For those ten silent heartbeats, when the pool drops and the rain stops and the light goes white-violet, the Wick is holding everything back, waiting to hear whether the resident will breathe on its own. In that silence the high whine is not a machine sound. It is a question. Then the deluge comes down, washing everything, deepening every groove by a fraction, and the answer is: *not yet*.

The keepers' tallies say the Breaths are coming closer together. The question is being asked more often. The Wick is growing hopeful, or impatient, or the resident is growing near enough to answer that it is worth asking again and again.

The dry basin beside the pool is where the keepers once gave it water to drink. The perfect square notch let a thin, steady stream run off separately, unshaped by the Wick. It was poured out and not thrown down. It was an offering made at human speed, a gesture of hospitality from small visitors to an enormous sleeper. The basin cracked when the last keepers left. Nobody has poured for it since. Perhaps it does not need them. Perhaps it misses them, in whatever way it can.

## V. The swifts, again

They ride the column up, wings folded, and burst out into the spray and light, and they do it most often just before a Breath.

Watch through a lens as one climbs. Inside the spiral, where the water is glassy and the drops fall slow, the swift passes through the resident's light. Its feathers show, briefly, a shadow on both sides. At the top, bursting into the white, it cries: a thin, high, two-note call you can only hear because the chord has just dropped out ahead of the Breath.

The call is two notes, and the chord in the flutes has been unresolved for as long as anyone has listened. The swifts are singing its missing notes. Every generation of them has learned to sing the pitch the chamber cannot yet make, the one the flutes are slowly deepening toward, a hair's width a year. They are rehearsing the resident's voice for it. They fly into the column the way a child climbs into a sleeping parent's lap, to be close, to say *here, like this*.

The shrimp orbit the place where its heart would be. The ferns drink its dim light. The swifts sing its note. The keepers counted its breaths and polished lenses to see it, left them in order for whoever came next, and poured water for it by hand.

## VI. What lives here

So what lives in the Lantern Cistern?

A sleeper the size of the room. It is being uncovered by water, a stroke at a time, over ten thousand years. It is lit faintly from within. Its outline can be seen only in shadows that fall the wrong way, and only through lenses. Its name was cut into the one place the water will not go. It is surrounded by small lives that have learned to face it the way sunflowers face the sun.

It is not asleep the way an animal sleeps. It is asleep the way a song is asleep in an instrument no one has finished building. The resident *is* the chord. The flutes are its voice, still being carved, and the Wick is the hand carving them. Every Breath, the hand pauses to listen for whether the instrument is finished and whether the chord will resolve on its own.

It will not be long now, as such things go. The Breaths are coming quicker. The flutes are nearly deep enough. One day the wind will steady, and the chord will surface, huge, and this time it will not break apart. It will close. The high whine in the silence will be answered. The swifts will hear their own two notes sung back to them from the walls, in a voice the size of the room.

What happens then, no keeper wrote down. Maybe the Wick will stop, its work done, and drop into the pool like a spent coal. Maybe the room will stay exactly as it is, but awake. Maybe the word cut into the stone will finally be spoken aloud by the one it names.

Until then it lives there the way warmth lives in the chamber, the way the second shadow lives behind you. It is everywhere, and faint, and patient. It is surrounded by rain and song, and being made.
