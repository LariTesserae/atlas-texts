# The Seated

## I. What Stays

Almost nothing on the Foundry Steppe lives there in the sense a lowlander means.

The stokers are passing through. Their circuit takes thousands of years, but it is still a circuit, and by that measure they are travellers. The bearing-beetles roll away downhill. The wire-moths rise and fall and rise. The ticks go where the flanks go. Even the tin wanders, sweating up at noon and running to the low places. The Steppe is a great slow traffic, and traffic does not live anywhere.

To find the residents you have to look for whatever stays put. You have to look in the empty kilometres.

## II. The First Answer

At first you take them for stones.

A stoker is one kilometre off in one direction and another is a kilometre off in the other. Between them, in the dead centre of a pan, there is a low ochre shape that the powder has half drifted over. It has a rounded back, and where the wind has scoured it, it shows the same dull sheen as polished bedrock. You would walk past it. Most people do, and then find they cannot. The Steppe's uneven distances fold them back, so that an hour's march east leaves them standing beside it again.

Sit down beside it for long enough and it will turn its head.

The Seated are roughly the shape of people. Some of them may once have been people. The oldest cannot remember, and on the Steppe that is not the same as not knowing. They sit cross-legged or kneeling, or in whatever posture they were in when they stopped, and they do not get up. Their skin has taken on the oxide colours of the pans, ochre over the broad surfaces and deepening to blood-brown in the creases. It is not paint and it is not dust. It is what skin becomes when it has not sweated water for a very long time.

They do sweat. In the hottest hour, when tin beads up in the low places of the pans, it beads up on them too, at the temples, along the forearms, in the hollow of the throat. It runs slowly down and hardens when the shadows reach it. An old Seated is laced all over with grey-silver threads, a filigree that follows the pull of their own bodies downward: from the crown of the head over the shoulders, down the arms, pooling in the lap. Seen from a ridge, a Seated has the same faint venation as the plateau. Up close, the resemblance is not faint at all.

They breathe slowly and dryly. The air that comes out of them is warmer than the air that goes in. If you hold your palm in front of an old one's mouth you can feel it, the small oven-breath of something with a fire banked very low inside.

## III. Why They Sit

Every Seated will tell you the same thing, eventually, in a sentence spread over several days: *Walking toward a thing pushes it away. Stopping brings it.*

They learned it the way you are learning it now. A stoker you march toward for an hour comes no nearer. The moment you sit, it is beside you, the ground shaking and the firebox glow hot on your face. The Seated took this seriously, all the way down. They stopped reaching for anything, and they found that everything they needed came to them. It simply came slowly.

**Food.** They do not eat rock. At noon they lick the tin from their own wrists, and in the long hours they take charge. When the moth hour comes and the air thickens with static, they lift their hands above their heads, palms up, and wait. The hair on their arms, what is left of it, stands and glitters. Their fingertips spark. Sometimes a moth cloud directly overhead discharges. The arc that leaps from moth to moth to ground does not reach the ground. It goes into a Seated, through the raised hands, and for a moment the whole figure is outlined in blue-white fire, every tin-thread incandescent at once, a diagram of veins drawn in lightning. Afterward they glow faintly for an hour, the way the stunned moths do. You come to understand that this is a meal, perhaps the only one they take in a season.

**Company.** The bearing-beetles roll to them. A drift of beetles crossing a pan will part around a Seated like water around a stone, and some always stay behind, nestled in the lap or against the ankles. At night they roll off toward the low places, and new ones come in the morning. The Seated seem fond of them. The beetles seem to use them as landmarks.

**Speech.** A Seated speaks in the intervals of the stokers' whistles. Every hour or so a stoker's valve lets out its long descending note and the others answer across the horizon. In that hour, a Seated will say one word. So a sentence takes a morning, and a real conversation takes most of a year. If you mean to speak with them you must become, a little, like them. People who try often find their legs have gone stiff and heavy when they finally think to stand, and that the stiffness has a sheen.

## IV. What They Keep

For a long time you think the Seated are simply hermits of a very extreme kind. Then you notice that they are not scattered at random.

Each one sits in a gap: the silent place exactly between two stokers, the deepest point of the Steppe's vast emptiness. When the stokers move a few dozen metres a day, the Seated do not move with them. But the stokers never pass over them either, and over centuries the herd's slow drift keeps each Seated in its gap, the way a river keeps a stone at the same bend.

The Seated say they are not in the gaps by chance. They say the gaps are where they are needed.

This is what they do there. They count.

Every Seated keeps a count of the stokers visible from where it sits. You know the count does not hold. You tried it yourself from the ridge and got a different number every time. The Seated know this better than anyone. They do not try to make their counts agree. Each keeps its own count and is sure of it, and each count differs from every other. When two Seated compare numbers over a conversation of many years, they do not argue. They treat the difference as information, the way two surveyors on different hills treat the angle between their sightlines.

What they are triangulating, they say, is *when*.

The oldest Seated put it roughly like this, over most of a decade. There is one stoker. There has only ever been one. It is walking every moment of its life at once across a plateau that has folded its own history flat. In some places its moments lie too close together and would touch, and a thing must not touch itself. Those are the gaps, and a gap is not empty. A gap is the place where the stoker is not. Something has to hold the place where a thing is not, or the moments would slide together, youth into age, prime into senescence, and the beast would collapse into a single instant and be gone.

So the Seated hold the gaps. They are the spacers between one moment and the next, the distance in the crowd made of distance.

This is why, when you sat between two stokers, you heard footsteps pass straight through you from an animal still on the horizon. You were sitting where a Seated sits. The footsteps were the stoker's future walking across its own absence, and the absence was you. The Seated feel this all the time. Every minute a footfall that has not happened yet goes through them, and they let it. They say it feels like a hand on the sternum, the same place the midday roar presses. It is the nearest thing they have to a heartbeat. After enough years, it is their heartbeat.

## V. The Lowest Places

At night the beetles leave the Seated and roll downhill, following the silver threads toward the low places where the tin gathers. If you ask a Seated where they go, it will take a full day to answer, and the answer is one word.

*Home.*

Follow them, if you can. It will take a long time going east and almost none going west. The pans descend so shallowly you would not know it, rim below rim below rim, until you come to the lowest pan on the whole Steppe. It is not ochre. It is silver: a mere of tin that stays liquid even at night, flat as a mirror, reflecting the thin enamel sky and the far orange windows of the stokers' flanks. Around its edge the beetles have arrived in their tens of thousands, and they are doing something the Seated have watched for longer than they can say.

They are unrolling.

A bearing-beetle at the edge of the mere opens, the steel sphere splitting along a seam no one had noticed, and lays itself out flat. It becomes a curved plate, pitted and bright. Beside it another does the same, and another, overlapping, fitting edge to edge, riveting themselves with little threads of their own tin. Out on the mirror of the mere, in the dark, something low and banded is being assembled plate by plate. It is the shape of a barrel. It has a row of stubs along its spine that will one day be vents. Its sixteen legs are drawn up beneath it like a foal's.

The Seated say this is where the youngest stoker comes from, the bright near-new one whose iron has not yet seen weather. It is not born and it is not built, though on the Steppe the difference is not always kept. It is cast. The whole plateau has been sending it tin, one bead at a time, for longer than the herd has been circling.

You ask where the oldest stoker goes, the one with plates so eroded they look like rock.

It stops, they say. And when a thing stops on the Steppe, the Steppe comes to it.

The oldest stoker sits down. Its firebox cools. The ticks spin down and fall away. The powder drifts over it, and its banded back weathers round and low. After a very long time it is no longer a beast but a shallow basin, a broad pan a few hundred metres across, with a low rounded rim like the edge of a plate. Its ribs are the ridges. The verdigris grows on its north face.

Every pan on the Steppe is one of these. The whole tableland is a single animal lying down in its old age over and over, while it walks in its youth and prime, eating the rock of its own resting body and smelting it and leaving slag, and sending the tin downhill to cast itself again. Every grain has passed through a body, and it has always been the same one.

## VI. The Seated, Again

Now go back to your Seated in its gap, knowing all this. Look again at the way it sits, low and rounded, half drifted over, taking on the colours of the pan, running with silver threads in the hot hour, breathing a small dry fire. Put your hand against it and feel the footfalls going through.

It is stopping, the same way the old stoker stops.

The Seated do not hide this. The oldest have a crust at the base of the spine, the colour of verdigris. It advances a finger's breadth in a human lifetime, and the Seated have many lifetimes. They say, without distress, that in the end each of them will be a pan. It will be a small one, a few metres across, with a rim like the edge of a saucer. The beetles will roll over it and the tin will gather in its lowest point.

So you ask the question, and it takes a year to answer. *Are you the stoker too? Are you one of its moments, the one that sits, the one that holds the distance, the rest between its footsteps?*

The answer comes one word per whistle. The stokers call across the horizon and the Seated speaks, and the stokers call again.

*We are*

*where*

*it*

*is*

*not.*

*That*

*is*

*also*

*a*

*part*

*of*

*it.*

## VII. Night

At night the ground gives up its heat, and the seams of the great forges glow orange to the horizon, each breathing slowly. Between them, in the black pans, there are smaller glows, so faint you have to be sitting still to see them. They are the Seated, still humming with the moth hour's charge, each a dim ember in the exact centre of its emptiness, keeping the kilometres apart.

The drumming continues and the whistles call and answer. The footsteps go through the Seated one by one, as regular as a pulse, steps that have not yet been taken, crossing the places where their maker is not. Downhill, in the lowest place, the newest plates settle onto a young back in the silver dark.

If you have sat long enough to see all this, try to stand.

Your legs will be heavy. There will be a sheen on your knees, and a thread of grey metal running from your wrist to your palm, cooling where the shadow found it. Somewhere on the horizon a stoker will lift its leg, and you will feel the footfall arrive, a hand on your sternum, before the leg comes down.

You will find that you do not mind it.

The beetles are rolling past you toward home. The nearest one pauses at your ankle, clicks once, and stays.
