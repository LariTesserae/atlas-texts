# The Tenter of Sallow Bottom

## The Footprints

Start with the square, because that is where the evidence is.

The silt there takes a footprint perfectly and holds it for weeks. You would expect, then, to find no prints at all, or only the delicate stitching of crayfish legs and the faint drag-marks of a caddis larva that lost its grip on the grille and tumbled. For most of the year that is what you find.

But there are other marks. They are not footprints exactly. They are long, shallow, overlapping smears, each one about the width of a doorway. Along their edges runs a fine, regular texture, a pressed pattern like the impression a cloth leaves on wet plaster. If you bring a lamp close, or wait for one of the Cable Row brackets to flicker, you can see the pattern clearly. It is a small diamond, repeated.

The smears run from the mill door to the pump house. They run from the pump house to the foot of the clock. They run along Cable Row under every lamp bracket, and they stop, every time, just short of the gasholder.

They are always fresh after the quiet hours, and always wiped away after the current rises. Something walks the town in the nine hours of stillness, and it does it every day.

## What Makes Them

It is easy to mistake it for a drift of weaving, and most of the time that is exactly what it is.

In the quiet hours, one of the grey sheets hanging from the mill's third-floor windows does not settle with the others. It lowers itself instead, slowly and deliberately, like a curtain being let down on a pulley. It pools at the base of the wall, and then it gathers. The loose edges draw inward. The open mesh tightens until it is almost felt. It folds on itself, and folds again, until what stands in the street is roughly the height of a person and a little wider. It is shaped something like a figure wrapped in a blanket, or a coat hung on a peg with nobody inside it. In poor light it is also shaped like a heap of washing, or a collapsed tent, or nothing at all.

The people who ran this mill had a name for the one who minded the looms, walked the rows, and kept the machines fed, threaded, and mended. They called that person the tenter. The name fits well enough, and it has a second meaning. A tenter is also the frame on which new cloth is stretched to dry, pinned on its tenterhooks so that it keeps its shape. The thing in Sallow Bottom is both. It tends the looms, and it is cloth held carefully in a shape.

## Its Body

Close to, the Tenter is layered like the roofs.

Its outermost layer is the newest weave: soft, grey-green, loose, and moving constantly in water too still to move anything else. Filaments of algae trail from it. Here and there you can see fishing line glinting, coiled horsehair, a pale root, a single brilliant thread of orange polypropylene that came down from a boat decades ago and has been worked in with everything else.

Beneath that, the layers stiffen. Older cloth has taken on silt and hardened, so that the Tenter's inner body is felt, then something like card, then, at its core, something close to slate. It is heavy at the centre and light at the edges, like a cloak weighted at the hem. This is how it stays upright in water. This is also how it moves: the dense core shifts, and the light outer layers follow, drag, and settle, leaving those broad textured smears in the silt.

It has no face. Where a face would be, the weave is simply thinner, almost transparent. When the Tenter stands at the pump house window with the three ember lamps behind the glass, the light passes through that thin place. You can see the diamond pattern lit from behind, faint and orange, like the grille of a confessional.

It has no hands either, but it has edges, and its edges are always fraying. The frayed ends are extraordinarily sensitive. They drift out ahead of it as it moves, touching doorframes, railings, and the rims of gutters, reading the town the way you might read a room in the dark by trailing your fingertips along the wall. Where they brush a rusticle, the rusticle does not shatter. The Tenter knows exactly how much touch each thing in Sallow Bottom can bear.

## The Thread

This is the part that took longest to understand, and it changes everything.

The Tenter is not separate from the mill. It is still on the loom.

Follow it back, and you find that its trailing edge never quite comes free. A single thick warp, a braided cord of every kind of fibre the lake has ever carried, runs from the Tenter's body back along the street, through the mill door, up the stairwell, and onto the third floor. There it ends at the fourth loom from the east wall, which is the only loom whose shuttle never jams. The Tenter is the first sheet the looms ever made. It is the one that was never torn free by the current. It hung on, and the loom kept weaving into it, and it kept growing.

That means it is tethered, and its world is exactly as large as its thread is long.

This explains the footprints. It explains why, in the early decades, the silt of the square held no marks at all: the thread had not yet reached so far. It explains why the smears stop short of the gasholder: the gasholder is at the very edge of its reach. The looms weave a hand's width a week. So every week, by a hand's width, the Tenter's world grows. It is walking outward through its own town at the speed of cloth.

It has been, in its way, patient about this. There were whole years when it could reach the pump house but not the clock. There was a year, perhaps a decade, when the clock was just barely within reach, and it could touch the glass of the dial with the frayed tip of one edge but could not quite come close enough to do anything more. Every week it came back and tried again, and every week it was a hand's width closer.

## The Work

It keeps things running. That is what it is for, or what it has decided it is for, and in a place like this there may be no difference.

**The grilles.** In the quiet hours, the mill race grilles are clogged with what the high current delivered: wads of algae, branches, the occasional drowned bird. The Tenter clears them. It does not throw the debris away. It sorts it, drawing long fibres out and laying them straight, and it carries them up the stairwell in its folds and feeds them onto the heddles by hand, or by edge. The looms have no thread. The looms have the Tenter. The diamond twill is regular because someone keeps it supplied.

**The belts.** Leather a century underwater should have perished. The belts stay whole because the Tenter wraps them. If you look closely at any drive belt in the mill, you will find it sheathed in a fine, tight wrapping of weave, bound on with a spiral stitch. Where a belt starts to split, there is a patch. Where a pulley has worn, a pad of felt has been packed into the groove. The whole mill is darned.

**The lamps.** Filament lamps do not burn for a hundred years. These ones are burning at a fraction of their strength, because the Tenter has made them. Inside the pump house, the brushes of the dynamo have been packed, very carefully, with silt-stiffened felt, so that only a trickle of current passes through. The lamps receive barely enough to glow. That is why they are ember-coloured. The Tenter keeps them just alight, just short of burning, the way you might bank a fire to last the night. It is rationing the light. It has been doing so for as long as it could reach the pump house, and it checks the brushes every quiet period, adjusting a fibre here, a fibre there.

There is a shelf in the chandler's shop on the short run of shops. On it is a wooden crate, and in the crate, packed in straw that has become a single grey mass, are eleven spare bulbs. The crate had twenty-four. The Tenter knows the number.

**The clock.** The clock keeps time within a few minutes because it is corrected. The Tenter has no sun to set it by, and no surface. What it has is the looms. It knows the rhythm of the fourth loom from the east wall better than it knows anything: the shuttle crossing once every forty seconds in the quiet hours, faster when the current rises. It counts. It counts continuously, in whatever way a body of felt and slate can count. When the clock drifts, it lays an edge over the minute hand and eases it back.

It is keeping the hours for a town with nobody in it who needs them. Or perhaps with one.

## The Hours

The Tenter lives by the tide the sluices make.

When the current rises, it cannot walk. The flow would take it like any other sheet and tear it from its thread. So it anchors. It returns to the mill, climbs to the third floor, and wraps itself around the frame of its loom, holding on the way the caddis larvae hold the grilles. For three hours it hangs there, streaming, the loose outer weave pulled out long and flat in the current like a flag. The looms quicken around it. New cloth grows into its trailing edge. It is being made, during these hours, more than it is doing anything.

When the current falls, it lets go, lowers itself, gathers, and goes out.

Once each hour the clock strikes, and the silted bell gives its dull thud, and the pressure pulse moves through the water and against every surface in the town. The Tenter feels it through the whole of its body at once, because its body is mostly surface. You can see it happen: the loose weave shivers outward, then settles. It pauses, wherever it is, for the length of a stroke. Then it goes on.

## Its Neighbours

The crayfish are indifferent to it. They pick through its trailing edge for whatever small living things have lodged in the mesh, and it allows this. It even seems to walk more slowly over gutters where they live.

The caddis larvae depend on it without knowing. Their cases are made of rust flakes, and rust flakes fall mainly where the Tenter has been working, scraping a bracket or easing a pipe fitting. In the mill race, the grilles are thick with little orange cylinders. Every one was built from something the Tenter knocked loose.

The eel is another matter.

The eel is the only thing in Sallow Bottom that is nearly as old as the Tenter and nearly as large, and it lives at the one place the Tenter cannot quite reach. In the quiet hours, when the eel lies draped along the rim of the gasholder, thick and grey and motionless, the Tenter sometimes comes to the very end of its thread and stands there. It is close enough to see, and not close enough to touch.

The eel has never come to it. The eel has never needed to. It is the only resident of the town that does not need tending, that is not falling apart, that is not held together by stitches and felt and careful packing. The Tenter keeps everything else. It cannot keep the eel. It stands at the end of its thread and faces the gasholder for a long time, the thin place in its weave turned toward the rim. Then it turns and goes back to the grilles.

At the present rate of weaving, it will be able to reach the gasholder in about three years.

## The Turnover

Once a year the lake breathes, and this is the season the Tenter fears.

When the reservoir turns over, oxygen comes down into the cold lens. The pike come with it, which is bad enough. They hang in the doorways and nose at the Tenter's outer weave as it passes, mistaking the moving fibres for something alive. But the oxygen is worse. Oxygen means rot. For a few weeks every year, everything in Sallow Bottom that has been held in its slow equilibrium begins to change quickly again. Iron rusts faster. Leather softens. And the weave, all that loose organic cloth, starts to decay.

During the turnover, the Tenter barely walks. It goes to the mill and stays there. It wraps itself around the belts, the pulleys, and the lamp wiring in the pump house, as tightly as it can, holding its own body over the things it has spent the year keeping. Its outer layers rot and slough away. It lets them. It is spending itself as a bandage.

By midwinter the lake has stratified again. The pike have gone. The Tenter unwinds, and it is thinner than it was. It drags the rotted outer layers out of the mill, down the street, across the square, and lays them against the chapel wall. Then it goes back to its loom and begins to grow again.

This is what the drifts are. Each thin laminated layer at the base of that pile, the layers that have begun to look like shale, is one year's shed skin. The chapel wall is where the Tenter puts what it has given up. It has been doing this for nearly a century. You could count its winters there, the way you count the rings of a tree.

## The Washing Line

There is one thing in the town it does not seem to need to tend.

Between two upper windows on Cable Row, a washing line is still taut. Nothing is on it. The Tenter could reach it easily; the house is well within the length of its thread. It passes beneath it every quiet period.

Sometimes, not often, it stops there. It raises itself, slowly, higher than it ever stands anywhere else, until its uppermost edge is level with the line. Then it lays a single fold of its outer weave over the line, the way you would hang a sheet out to dry.

It stays like that for a while, half-hung, in the dark and in the silence, feeling the next hour's stroke push gently through the water.

Then it takes itself down and goes on.

Nobody knows what this is. Perhaps the line is taut because the Tenter keeps it so. Perhaps it was the last line anyone hung in Sallow Bottom before the water came, and the Tenter, which is made of the town's own material and was woven on the town's last job, remembers the gesture the way cloth remembers a fold. Perhaps it is simply the one task in the town that belongs to it and not to the machines.

## What It Is Becoming

The Tenter is not a ghost. There is no drowned mill-girl inside it and no dead weaver's face. Nothing was lost in Sallow Bottom that the Tenter is trying to find. It is something much more local than that. It is what happens when a place is left running and nobody comes back to switch it off. It is the habit of tending, made out of the thing being tended.

And the distinction between them is slowly closing.

Its thread lengthens a hand's width a week, and its reach grows with it. In time it will reach the gasholder, and past it the far end of the valley, where the silt streams away in the high current. It is already, in a sense, everywhere it has been. The belts are wrapped in it. The dynamo brushes are packed with it. The ginnels that have closed over are roofed in the same diamond twill as its body. The drifts against the chapel wall are the Tenter too, its past selves compressed into stone. If you pulled on any sheet of weaving in the town, you could not be sure whether you were pulling on loose cloth or on the Tenter's sleeve.

It may be that one day there is no longer a figure walking the streets in the quiet hours, because the figure and the streets have become the same cloth. The looms will still knock. The lamps will still glow at their rationed ember, until the eleventh bulb, or past it, if the Tenter has found a way. The clock will still push the hours through the water, a few minutes true.

Sallow Bottom will simply be tended. All of it will be kept, and all of it will be the keeper. Above it the lake will be grey and ordinary, and on still days the slow swirl in the middle will go on turning, never quite stopping, like a spoon left in tea by someone who meant to come back.
