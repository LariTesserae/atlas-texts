# Who Lives at Hallow Col

## The Tide

At first you see only a crowd.

You are on a landing halfway down the eastern stacks when the shift horn sounds. It is not really a horn. It is a low electric tone, felt in the ironwork before it is heard. Doors open below you and above you, and the stairways fill. For thirty minutes you cannot tell one person from another. You see grey wool in one continuous garment, pouring. Caps and hoods are beaded with fog. Hands rest on the rail, or on the shoulder of the person in front, and the whole mass moves at one pace, up and down at once, the rising shift threading through the falling one without collision, as if the stairs were a loom and the people were the weft.

Nobody pushes. On a stair this narrow and this wet, a push would kill someone three flights down. So the crowd moves the way the water moves, steadily and by gravity, finding the channel. When it is gone the stairs steam faintly with the warmth of all those bodies. Then the fog comes back in and cools them.

That is the first answer to the question of who lives here: tens of thousands of people who move like water because they have lived for two hundred years inside something built to move water.

## Faces

Look closer, at a single face, and it changes.

The people of Hallow Col are pale, and not the pale of the unwell. They are the colour of the undersides of things, the colour of a fern's new frond before it unrolls. The sun reaches the valley floor for two hours in summer. Children born on the lower levels have seen it perhaps a dozen times before they are grown, and many of them squint at it, when it comes, the way other children squint at a lamp held too close. Their eyes are large and dark-adapted. They see well in fog. They see well at dusk, which here lasts most of the day.

Their chests are deep from the thin air and the stairs. Everyone climbs. No one in Hallow Col lives at the level they work at. The smelters are low, the tenements are high, the kale boxes are higher, and the market is wherever the market has been rebuilt this year. An old woman of eighty will climb four hundred steps before noon without remarking on it, and she will have calves like a hill pony and a slight, permanent forward lean, as if walking into weather.

Their hands are the thing. Everyone's hands are rough. The mills do it, the wet does it, and the constant small labour of clearing gutters does it too. But the hands are also expressive in a way that strangers find unnerving, because they are always half-speaking. A woman listening to you will be signing, faintly, below the level of intention: *yes, go on, I see, careful*. It is like the moving lips of someone reading. Children's hands are never still at all.

Their skin is often marked. Burns from the smelters show as glossy white patches on forearms. Bluish-green stains from the copper runoff sink into the creases of the knuckles and never quite wash out; it is called *roof-hand*, and it marks someone who works the high gutters. There are old crush-scars from collapses. Nearly everyone over forty has one. They show them to each other the way people elsewhere might compare where they were born.

## The Signs

Their language is two languages braided.

Spoken, it is low and consonant-soft. A whole language has worn smooth in the fog the way stone wears smooth under a leat. Hard sounds echo badly off the stacked walls and come back from three directions, so over generations they have gone. Words blur into each other. A visitor hears murmuring and assumes secrecy. It is only courtesy. A raised voice in Hallow Col is shocking in a way that is hard to describe. It is like seeing someone block a drain.

Signed, it is exact. The mill signs came first: *stop*, *more*, *lift*, *hot*, *down*, *watch your hand*. Two hundred years have grown them into a full grammar, fast and spare, full of words that don't exist aloud. There is a sign that means *the fog is coming in from behind you*. There is a sign for the specific weight of a load that is just barely safe. There is a sign for *I heard it too*, used when a building comes down somewhere far off and two strangers on a stair both lift their heads at the dull roar. It is made by touching the flat of the hand briefly to the breastbone.

Everyone has a sign-name, given in childhood, usually by other children. It is almost never the same as the spoken name. A child is named for how they move: a quick flick of two fingers, or a hand drawn downward in a spiral, or the knuckles tapped twice against the temple. You can live beside someone for twenty years and know them only by the gesture. When someone dies, their sign-name is not made again for a year. People find their hands starting the shape and stopping.

## Trades

After the crowd and the faces come the particular people, because a place like this needs particular people.

**The leat-wardens** walk the channels. Every district has three or four, and they are the closest thing Hallow Col has to police, though they carry nothing but an iron hook, a lamp, and a book. They know their water the way a shepherd knows a flock. Put a leat-warden on a stair in the dark and she can tell you, by the sound of the grates, whether the channel beneath is running clear, running choked, or running too fast, which is worse, because it means something above has failed and is sending everything down at once. They are respected and a little feared. Their evidence is final in the water courts. They tend to be solitary, and they tend to talk to the water, quietly, the way you might talk to a horse.

There is one on the western stacks, Tegwen Harrow, sign-name a hand laid flat and slid forward like water over a lip, who has walked the same four kilometres of channel for thirty-one years. She has found eleven bodies in that time, and a great many lost things. She keeps a shelf of them in her room: a brass bell, a child's shoe, a set of false teeth, a glass eye, a porcelain figure of a shepherdess with the head worn off. She does not know who any of them belonged to. The water brings things down from levels that no longer exist.

**The surveyors** are always a year behind. They know it, and it shapes them. They are dry, patient, faintly melancholy men and women who spend their lives mapping a town that is not there anymore by the time the ink is dry. The guild keeps its master map in a vault near the col, drawn on linen and corrected so many times the linen is more paste than cloth. Young surveyors are sent out with a sheet of last year's map and told to find what's changed. Old surveyors don't need the sheet. They have stopped thinking of the town as a place and started thinking of it as a rate.

**The root-readers** are a branch of the surveyors that grew into its own guild. It is their word that decides whether a tree may be cut. They are strange people. They spend their days on roofs and in the vaults beneath roofs, following roots down through brick with their hands. They know how a birch grips and how a rowan grips and what it means when a root turns sideways instead of down. Some of them claim to be able to feel, by laying a palm on a wall, whether the building is being held or being pulled apart. Most people think this is a performance. Some people have watched a root-reader refuse to let a crew cut a single thin trunk, and then watched the terrace beside it, where the trees had been cleared the year before, slump into the street a week later.

**The ropeway keepers** live in the gantries. Some of them literally do. There are cabins bolted to the tops of the big towers, and the keepers sleep in them so as to be near the wheels. They listen to the cable all night. They know every bucket by its knock. They grease, they splice, they climb out along the line in the fog with their harnesses clipped to the running rope, and they do not stop it, because it does not stop. They are the people who carry out the hardest rule. When the ropeway will not wait, it is a keeper who stands at the gantry and signs to the waiting crowd, or the waiting mourners, or the waiting rescuers, *not yet, not yet, now*. They are not loved for it. They do not expect to be.

**The shorers, the breakers, and the raisers** are three crews, and they are the same people on different days. A crew that props a failing wall on Monday may pull it down on Thursday and frame its replacement the Monday after. They work fast and nearly silently, in hand signs, in the rain. They have a saying that every building in Hallow Col is either coming down, going up, or waiting. When they are asked which one a given building is, they rarely answer.

**The kale women** work the high boxes. Some are men, but the name holds. The high terraces are the only place clean enough to grow food, and whoever holds a sunny roof holds something like wealth. The kale women are brown-handed and loud by Hallow Col standards, which means they occasionally speak at a normal volume. They sell leeks and potatoes and kale by the armful on the stairways, and they send down baskets on string to the levels below, where nothing edible grows. They have strong opinions about the low town, and they are the ones who feed it.

## The Low Town

Below the fourth level, things change.

The water there is poisoned and the air is warm from the smelter flues, and the light is only lamps. The people who live there are paler even than the rest. They are taller, it's said, though that may only be the way they stand, stretched a little upward. The comparison is made by everyone above and is not meant kindly: they are like the leggy shade-plants that grow down there, reaching for the sodium light.

They are the smelter crews, the culvert crews, and the people who work the turbine halls at the valley's mouth. They live in the warmth. Their tenements are built into the flanks of the flues, and in winter the whole of Hallow Col envies them, and in summer the whole of Hallow Col pities them. They eat what is sent down, in baskets and on the conveyor galleries, and they pay for it in copper and in labour. Their signing is different: faster, more compressed, full of shapes the upper town doesn't know. Children from the top of the valley and children from the bottom can barely understand one another's hands.

They know the river better than anyone. They live beside its roof. Some of them claim they can hear it change its mind, hear the moment, days before a landslip, when the note of the water in the deep culvert shifts. The water courts do not accept this as evidence. The shorers listen to it anyway.

## The Under-Tenants

Here the description begins to become something else.

Old people in Hallow Col talk about neighbourhoods that are gone, stairways and squares and whole terraces taken by landslips or by collapse and then rubbled over and built across. This is a matter of record. What is not a matter of record, and what almost everyone believes regardless, is that not all of those places are empty.

When a terrace collapses and a new one is built on its rubble, the rubble is not solid. It is rooms. It is half-crushed rooms, voids between fallen slabs, stairwells that still descend a few flights before they end in stone. The builders don't clear it. There is no time, and no place to put it. They lay a new floor across the top and go on. And so beneath the living town there is a second town, a broken and discontinuous one, spaces sealed in the dark with water running through them, because water must be let through, and the law is kept even there.

People live in it. Or people did. Or people say they do.

The stories vary. Some say the under-tenants are the families of those crushed in collapses, who went down after their dead and did not come back up. Some say they are debtors, or people fleeing the water courts, or people who simply decided they had climbed enough stairs. Tegwen Harrow will tell you, if you ask quietly, that she has seen lamplight through grates where no lamp should be, and heard signing. You can hear signing, if you know what to listen for: the soft rasp of wool sleeves and the clap of palms. Once, she says, a hand came up through a drain cover in the dark of the western stacks and made the sign *I heard it too*, and went down again.

The surveyors do not map the under-tenants, because they have no level and no footprint and cannot be adjudicated. But in the vault at the col, on the master map, in the margins beside certain districts, someone generations ago began making a small mark, a circle with a line through it, and the mark has been copied forward through every revision since. No one knows who began it. It appears only above buried neighbourhoods. Surveyors will tell you it means *void, unsafe*. They will not quite meet your eyes.

## The Others

The people are not alone in it, though they are nearly alone.

**The snails** are the town's night-shift. On wet nights, which is most nights, the stairways shine with them, and the townspeople have learned to step around them without looking, the way you avoid a known loose tread. Children race them. Old people read them. They say that when the snails leave a stretch of wall, you should leave it too, because it has gone dry inside, and dry inside means the water has found another way through, and that means something is failing. It may be nonsense. The root-readers take it seriously.

**The ravens** live on the high scarps, the few bare faces where even the builders gave up. There are only a few pairs and they are old. People call them by name, and the names are passed down with the birds, so the same raven has been called Harrower for three raven-lifetimes. They come down to the refuse at the col and sometimes to the high kale boxes, where they are shouted at, one of the few things in Hallow Col that is. They are the only voices above the town that are loud. When a raven calls in the fog, people look up, briefly, as though they had been reminded that the sky exists.

**The herons** stand at the reservoir above the turbine intakes, grey on grey water in grey air. They are almost impossible to see. They are the patience of the place, made into a shape. When the builders erect a new crane on a rooftop, children call it a heron, and the crane operators call themselves heron-men, and there is a sign for both that is the same sign: one arm lifted, bent sharply at the wrist, still.

**The cats** live in the flue-warmth of the low town and belong to nobody, and nobody would dream of harming one. They are the only animals in Hallow Col that go everywhere: up and down, in and out of the gaps, through the grates. People say the cats go into the under-town and come back. People say you can tell a cat that has been down there by the dust on it, which is a different colour.

**The goats** are rarely seen, and only on the dam wall, the one piece of the town that looks like mountain. They look down on the valley from there. There is a feeling, when you see them, that you are being inspected by something that has decided, provisionally, that you are geology.

## What Is Actually Here

Stay long enough and the categories start to fail.

You begin to notice that the people clear the gutters but not the slopes. They cut back the moss so the water can pass, but they leave it on the roofs, because the moss holds the water and slows it, and slow water doesn't undermine. You notice that the birch on the old terraces is never cut, and the roots have gone so deep that some walls are more root than brick, and the root-readers lay their hands on them and speak of the terrace as *she*. You notice that people sign to the water, and that the leat-wardens talk to it, and that no one finds this strange.

You notice that when a building falls, the moss is already growing on the rubble before the dust has cleared, and the ferns are in the cracks by spring, and the new timber framing goes up through the ferns, and the ferns grow up through the timber. You notice that nothing here is quite built and nothing is quite grown. Every wall is shared.

And you start to think that you were asking the wrong question. You have been describing the things that live *in* Hallow Col, as if the town were a shell and the people and ravens and snails were the animals inside it. It isn't that kind of place.

There is a thing called lichen, which crusts the ironwork here in grey-green and rust-orange. It is not one creature. It is two, or more: a fungus that builds the structure, and an alga that lives inside it and makes the food, and neither could survive on bare rock without the other. Together they are something neither is alone. They grow very slowly and they live a very long time, and they can grow on stone that nothing else will touch.

Hallow Col is like that. The people are the fungus: they build the structure, endlessly, patching and propping and raising. The green is the alga: moss and fern and birch and kale, catching what light there is and holding the water and knitting the stones. Neither would survive here alone. The mountain would shrug off either one. Together they have held a glacial trough for two hundred years, falling and rising at the same rate. They are something that is not a town and not a forest, and is more alive than either.

## What Lives Inside

But that is still not quite it. Go down one more level.

In the cold rivers of the lowland to the west, the ones the cloud comes up from, there is an insect larva called the caddis. It lives on the stream bed, and it builds itself a case. It gathers grains of sand and fragments of shell and bits of leaf and twig, and it binds them together with silk into a tube, and it lives inside the tube. As it grows, it adds to the front of the case and lets the back wear away. The case is always the same shape and never the same material. It is a thing continuously repaired. From above you would take it for debris. You would not know, unless it moved, that anything lived inside.

Stand on the col again in the grey afternoon and look down.

Six kilometres of slate and iron and brick and concrete and copper and moss, gathered up out of the valley's own stone and the lowlands' coal and the ore of the upper workings, and bound together with cable and iron and roots, lying along the bottom of a trough. It is added to at one end and wearing away at the other. It is always the same shape and never the same material.

And inside it, enclosed, running in the dark four levels down: the river.

The whole town exists because of the river. Every law follows from the river. Every wall has a gutter at its foot and every gutter leads down to it. The people's language wore smooth in its fog. Their bodies are shaped by climbing its terraces. They are paid in the power its falling makes. They have built, over two hundred years, without ever planning it, a case of perfect intricacy around one living, moving, relentless thing. They let it through. They have always let it through. They call that the oldest law, as though it were their idea.

What lives in Hallow Col is the water. The rest of it is the case.

## And

A caddis larva does not live in its case forever.

There is a stage, late, when it seals the front of the tube and goes still inside. Then one day it cuts its way out, and swims to the surface, and splits its skin, and flies.

Nobody in Hallow Col talks about this. There is no sign for it. But the low-town crews say the note of the river in the deep culvert has been changing, very slowly, for years. The leat-wardens say the water runs a little faster every season, as though it were preparing for something. The old people say the fog has grown thicker, and it rises now in long grey braids that stream up the valley and over the col and away into the sky, as if the valley were exhaling. The snails have been leaving the lowest walls. The under-tenants have been signing in the drains.

The ravens, who are loud, have gone quiet.

And on the master map in the vault at the col, the small circle with a line through it, the mark no one admits to making, has begun to appear in districts where nothing has ever been buried. The surveyors copy it forward, a year behind as always, and do not ask what it means.

The town goes on. The ropeway does not stop. The shifts pour up and down the stairs like weft on a loom. The moss grows and the birches grip and the crews build on the rubble of what fell last week. Everything is falling and rising at the same rate, the way it always has, and the hiss of water is under everything. When two strangers on a stair stop and lift their heads at some far sound in the fog, one of them touches the flat of a hand to her breastbone.

*I heard it too.*
