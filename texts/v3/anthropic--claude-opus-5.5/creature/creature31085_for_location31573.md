# Who Lives in the Held Water

## One

Begin with a single one, because the others are too many to begin with.

It starts in the dark inside the wall. Not in the dark of the lake, which is dusk at worst and has a faint grey sense of up. This is the complete dark of a sealed chamber no larger than a lentil, cemented shut on all sides. It has been an egg here for most of a year. Before it knows anything else, it knows grain: the rough, curved interior of its cell pressing against its body on every side.

When it is ready, it eats its way out. Its first act in the world is to take a mouthful of the Masonry and move it. It chews through mucilage gone hard as bone, working the grit loose with limbs that are not yet trowels but are already trowel-shaped, soft and translucent, a promise of what they will do. It takes days. Many do not make it. Some cells were sealed too thick, some mothers chose too well, and the Masonry is full of eggs that never came out, little stoppered spaces that are now simply part of the wall.

This one breaks through. The first thing it meets is not light, because it has no use for light. It is movement. Water slides across the tiny hairs along its segments, and the sensation is total: the water is going *that way*. It has no word for this. It has no words at all. But everything it will ever be is contained in that first touch of current and in the answer rising in its body. The water is going that way, and there is a place it is going through, and that place should be closed.

It turns its head into the flow. It will face this way, more or less, for the rest of its life.

---

## What It Knows

A waller's world is made of three sensations, and everything else is noise.

The first is **flow**: its strength, its direction, the fine gradations of it along a surface. Through the hairs on its back, a waller can tell whether water is sliding past a wall or being drawn *into* it. Water drawn in is a leak, however small. A pore the width of a grain, sucking gently, is to a waller what a scream is to us. Every waller within a few body-lengths feels that suck and turns toward it.

The second is **grain**. The trowel-limbs are covered in dense, short bristles, and the bristles taste texture the way a tongue tastes salt. A waller sifting the passing water rejects most of what it catches. It keeps grains of a certain angularity, a certain size, a certain bite. Rock flour from the Throat is fine and sharp and binds beautifully. Rounded grains ground smooth by the underflows are worse. A waller can tell them apart without thinking. It can hardly think at all.

The third is **the others**. Every waller's mucilage has a chemistry, and a fresh smear of it on stone smells, if that is the word, of whoever laid it. This is how they coordinate without any coordination. A waller arriving at a breach tastes the cement already there, and the concentration tells it how many others are working and how recently. Where the cement is fresh and dense with others' traces, it works in close and fast. Where the cement is old and faint, it lays a thicker bead, because it is working alone. Where there is no cement at all, only raw broken wall, it does something close to panic: it presses harder and faster, the clicking of its trowels rising to a frantic tempo, until others taste its alarm in the water and come.

That is all. Flow, grain, others. From these three it builds everything.

---

## The Ones Who Stay In

I thought at first that the bands in the old walls were seasons. They are, in part. The cement laid in the pulse-months, when the Throat pours thick with flour, is paler and grittier than the cement of the slack months. The two alternate like pages.

But look closer at a broken stump, and some of the pages have things in them.

A waller lives two years, perhaps three. Near the end its trowels are worn to nubs, its bristles are gone smooth, and its mucilage glands produce less and less. It does not leave. It does not drift off to die somewhere apart. When its glands finally fail, it presses itself flat against the wall, into the flow, the way it has pressed ten million grains, and it stops moving. The others arrive, taste the faint old smear of it, and read it the way they read everything: as a surface. They cement over it.

The body becomes a grain. A large, soft grain, shaped like a waller, that hardens over the seasons into something indistinguishable from the rest.

The laminae in the old walls are full of them. A thumb-length pale fossil lies in every few centimetres of every band, all oriented the same way, all facing into a current that stopped flowing in that direction a thousand years ago. The Masonry is not only made *by* the wallers. Much of it, a fraction too large to estimate, is made *of* them.

This changes what the floor of the lake is. It is not a city with its builders crawling over it. It is a population, and nearly all of that population is still, packed into the ridges and domes and the endless flat plains, generation laid on generation, their bodies the substance of the place. The living wallers are only the surface of it, a thin, moving skin a single body deep, over a vast sleeping mass of their own dead.

When an underflow tears a ridge open, it is tearing open ancestors. The wallers that rush to the breach are sealing their own kind back in.

They do not know this either. But it is true, and once you know it, the clicking along the walls starts to sound different.

## The Keelers

At the South Wall the wallers change shape.

This took a long time to notice, because the crust along the dam is so dense and so pale that it reads as a single texture. But among the ordinary wallers there are others, far fewer, and they are large. Some are as long as a hand and broad as a palm, heavy-bodied, their segments thickened and overlapping like the plates of a pinecone. Their trowels have fused, over what must be many moults, into a single flat shield across the front of the body.

They do not gather grain. They cannot. Their bristles have hardened into short stiff pegs useless for sifting. What they do is simpler. They find the places where the lake is pulling hardest into the wall, the cracks that the ordinary wallers keep patching and the water keeps reopening, and they wedge themselves in, shield-first, facing the leak.

Then the small wallers cement them there.

Call them keelers. A keeler does not die in place as the others do. It is walled in alive, with only the rear edge of its back left exposed, a pale ridge in the grey. It lives like that for years. Some of them, from the thickness of the cement around them, must live that way for decades. The bacterial film grows on the fresh cement around its body, and it feeds on the film through a mouth that no longer faces the open water but opens backward along its own flank. It moults, somehow, and each moult is larger and heavier. It grows into the crack. It becomes a living plug, pressed into the South Wall with the whole weight of the lake bearing down on its shield.

What makes a waller become a keeler, no one knows. Perhaps it is the pressure itself. A larva that hatches in the South Wall, where the suck of the leaks never stops, may simply grow differently from one hatched in the calm of the northern plains. Perhaps it is something in the mucilage of the keelers already there, a chemistry that tells a young waller *there are not enough of us at the front*. However it happens, the South Wall is ribbed with them for its entire length: thousands upon thousands of pale shields, set edge to edge into the oldest cracks, facing the lake.

If the ordinary wallers are the skin of the Masonry, the keelers are its knuckles. They are where the hands close hardest.

---

## Neighbours

The other animals of the lake are not strangers to the wallers, though they are not kin either.

The flat grazers, the many-legged ones the size of a hand, turn out to matter more than they first seemed to. The bacterial film on fresh cement is food, and it is also a problem. Left to grow, it works its way into the mucilage and softens it; a wall furred too thickly will begin to crumble from the inside within a season. The grazers keep it down. Their polished trails across the Masonry are not just the tracks of feeding. They are maintenance. The wallers never touch a grazer. When one slides over a working crowd, the wallers flatten and let it pass, and the grazer moves on, leaving clean stone behind for fresh cement.

The eels do not help anyone. They are pale and long and nearly blind, and they have learned the pulse of the Throat better than anything else in the lake. Before a big underflow, they gather at the downstream edges of the ridges, hanging nearly motionless in the lee, waiting. When the grey wall of water comes through and rips the skin of living wallers off the stone, the eels are already in the wake, snapping. Among the wallers, only the keelers are too large and too fixed for them.

The filtering swimmers are everywhere and nowhere. They are the haze made alive, the glitter in a beam of light, and they feed on the ancient pollen and freeze-dried life that comes down from the ice. They are food for almost everything, including the drifters.

And the drifters are where this story turns.

---

## The Drifters

For a long time I thought the drifters were something else. Something older, something visiting. They are so large and so soft, and they seem so utterly unlike the small, hard, grinding wallers below. And there was that pause. When a drifter passed low over the Masonry, every waller beneath it stopped, all at once, and there was a silence like a held breath. It seemed to be the one thing in the lake that was not work.

It is not the only thing. But it is not quite what it seemed.

Consider what happens to a waller that does not die at the wall. Most do. But not all. Some, very late in life, when the trowels are worn and the bristles gone, do not press themselves flat into the flow. Instead, something happens to their mucilage glands. The glands swell. The mucilage they produce changes. It does not harden in the cold. It stays soft, fills with something lighter than water, and pools inside the body in a clear, spreading mass.

Such a waller loses its grip. It lifts off the wall, very slightly, and hangs there, turning in the current. The others taste it, but it no longer tastes like a surface, and they do not cement it. It rises.

It rises slowly, over days, until it reaches the shimmering boundary where the cold deep water meets the slightly warmer water above, and there it stops, suspended at the ceiling of the dusk. And there it goes on growing. The swollen glands keep producing, the clear mass keeps spreading, and the small hard body at the centre of it thins and softens and is slowly overgrown by its own bell. It puts out threads. It feeds on the glittering swimmers. For years, maybe for longer than it ever lived as a builder, it rides the seiche back and forth across the lake, north and south, north and south, following the long slosh of the water.

The drifters are old wallers. The pale, trailing, silent things, as wide as a person is tall, are what the builders become when they stop building.

And the pause, the moment of silence beneath them, is a birth.

Drifters carry eggs. Hundreds of them, maybe thousands, cling along the fine threads trailing down beneath the bell. When a drifter sinks low over a stretch of Masonry, low enough for its threads to brush the living surface, the wallers taste it. The taste is familiar: it is their own mucilage, a mucilage like theirs, magnified and strange. They stop. For a moment every trowel is still. The threads drag across the backs of the wallers and over the fresh cement, and the eggs come loose and settle into the soft new grey.

Then the clicking starts again. The wallers press grit over the eggs, cell by cell, sealing each one into a chamber the size of a lentil.

The young do not lay the eggs. The old do not bury them. It takes both. The ancestors float overhead, trailing the next generation, and the living press it into the wall. In the dark inside the stone the eggs wait a year, and then they eat their way out and turn their heads into the flow.

I thought, when I first saw it, that it looked like a custom. It does. It is the oldest one here.

---

## Downstream

There is one more place the wallers live, and it is the one no one sees.

The South Wall leaks. It always has. The keelers plug the worst of it and the ordinary wallers patch the rest, but water goes through, down into the ancient solution channels that riddle the plateau, and what goes through the wall carries things with it: grit, silt, the filtering swimmers, and sometimes wallers. A breach opens under a hard seiche. The suck is too strong. A cluster of pale bodies is torn off the face of the wall and pulled into the crack before it can be closed.

For a long time I assumed they died down there. Many must. But not all of them.

Down in the channels beneath the mountain there is no light at all and never has been. The water that leaks from the lake runs through the rock in a thousand narrow conduits, cold and fast and heavy with the grit the lake has lost. The wallers that survive the passage find themselves in a world that is all flow. There is no calm floor, no dusk, only water pouring past them through the dark, always in one direction.

They do what they do. They turn their heads into the flow.

In the conduits beneath the plateau, the water flows *away* from the lake. So a waller facing into it faces back toward the lake. It presses grit against the walls of the channel, sifted from the flour the leaks carry down, and it seals the pores through which the lake is draining. It builds, in other words, on the far side of the South Wall. It patches the dam from behind.

They have been doing this for as long as the lake has leaked, which is as long as the lake has been. The channels beneath the dam are lined with their work: pale crusts, narrowing conduits, whole passages choked and sealed and gone quiet. They live down there in their own small, blind colonies, never seeing the lake, never feeling a seiche or a drifter's threads, feeding on the film on their own cement and on what the leaks wash down. They have grown thinner and faster than their kin above, and their bristles are finer. They breed without drifters, somehow, eggs laid directly into the channel walls, their whole cycle folded down into the dark.

What they build makes the South Wall thicker. Every conduit they seal is a leak the lake cannot open again. The dam is not being held only from the front. The lost ones, the swept-away ones, the ones who fell through the cracks, are holding it from underneath. They will never know there is a lake. They only know the water is going *that way*, and there is a place it is going through, and that place should be closed.

---

## Whoever Lives Here

So: who lives in the Held Water?

A small pale thing with trowels for hands, facing into the current. That is the easy answer, and it is true.

But the longer you look, the less that answer holds on its own. The thing that lives here is spread across billions of bodies and more years than anyone has counted. Most of it is still, laminated into the walls in pages of grey, every body oriented to a current that has long since changed. A thin skin of it is alive and clicking. Some of it is wedged shield-first into the oldest cracks of the dam, grown heavy and fixed and patient. Some of it has let go and drifts overhead, soft and enormous and silent, riding the slosh of the lake and trailing its children. And some of it has fallen through into the dark beneath the mountain and is building there still, blind, facing the wrong way, which is the right way.

It has no plan. No part of it knows what the whole is doing. And yet the whole does one thing and has always done one thing: it holds the water. It made the lake by holding it, and it lives in the lake it made, and it goes on holding it against the Throat and the wind and the leaking, pull, and the slow grey erosion of everything, for no reason it could name except that it is what it is.

If you want a name for whoever lives here, the closest might be *the holding*. Not the builders and not the built. The holding itself, ten thousand years long and a single body deep, closing its fingers against the leaking.

---

And now, somewhere in the Masonry, in a sealed chamber no larger than a lentil, something is chewing through a wall that its ancestors are part of. The grit is hard. It will take days. Overhead, the drifter that trailed it here has long since moved on with the slosh.

It breaks through. The water slides across the hairs on its back.

It turns its head.
