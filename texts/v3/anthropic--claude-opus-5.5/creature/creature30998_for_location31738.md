# Who Lives on the Crown

## The People of the Pearl

About four thousand people live in Kell, though no one has counted them in living memory. A census would require knocking on doors, and in Kell a knock is barely a sound. The moss on the door takes it. You have to open the door and lean in and say, softly, *it's me*, and by then you are no longer counting. You are visiting.

They are a pale people. Most of their waking lives are spent in the pearl, which is what they call the long dim hours inside the cloud, and the pearl does not tan anyone. Their skin has the slightly translucent look of something kept out of the sun, and the cold keeps their cheeks and knuckles flushed a raw pink, so that a crowd in Kell looks faintly like a basket of unripe fruit. Their hair is always damp at the ends. On heavy days it rises. Children learn to tell a heavy day before they can tell the hour, by the way their mother's hair floats off her shoulders when she bends over the stove, and by the small blue tick that jumps from her fingertip to the kettle.

Their hands give them away anywhere they go, not that many of them go anywhere. Kell hands have clay in the creases so deep that no washing reaches it. Knuckles swell and split in winter. Palms are callused in a band across the root of the fingers from the scraper handle. People down in the lowland cities who have met a Kell trader say the handshake is cold and gritty and surprisingly gentle, *like shaking hands with a riverbank*.

They speak low. This is the first thing any stranger notices, and the last thing a Kell native loses if they ever leave. Their voices are pitched for a world where sound stops at ten metres. They lean in close to talk, closer than lowlanders find comfortable, because in Kell a conversation happens inside a small bubble of air and anything outside that bubble is fog. Intimacy here has nothing to do with privacy. You are simply standing near enough to be heard.

Because shouting is useless, they have hands for talking across distance: a working sign language born on the scraping crews and in the clay pits. It is blunt and practical, with signs for *stop*, *more*, *under you*, *coming down*, *heavy*, *leave it*. Over generations it has grown tender in places, the way working languages do. There is a sign for *I see you through the fog*: the palm raised flat and then slowly turned outward, like wiping a window. Lovers use it. So do mourners.

## How They Keep Time

Nobody in Kell says *eight o'clock*. They say *in the clear*, or *before the wall*, or *at the top of the pearl*, or *toward the sag*, which is the grey stretch before dawn when the accumulators run low and every lamp in town is breathing slowly, like a sleeper.

The clear hour is precious in a way that is hard to explain to anyone who has lived with sky. For most of Kell's people it is the only time they ever cast a shadow, and they take it seriously. Children are woken for it. Old people are carried out to it. Work stops. The scraping crews lean on their handles at the rim, the kiln workers come up blinking from the brick rows, and for an hour the whole town stands out on the catwalks and balconies and bridges in its thin cold light, watching the cloud pour off the edges of the world. They don't talk much. People who have quarrelled stand side by side without making it a truce. Marriages are made in the clear, and so are apologies. Babies are named in it, held up so their first shadow falls on the stone, and the name is spoken while the shadow is still there.

Then someone sees the wall coming over the rim. Nobody says it, but a sign ripples down the catwalks, the flat hand drawn slowly upward, *rising*, and people go back inside the day.

## The Scrapers

Every household owns scrapers, and every scraper is someone's.

They hang beside the door like swords in older and more violent towns: long ash or iron handles with a flat forged blade at the end, sharpened on one edge, curled on the other to roll the moss up like a carpet. A family's scrapers are passed down. Handles are worn to the shape of particular hands, dark and glossy where grandmothers gripped them, and a child growing into a scraper, their fingers slowly finding the old grooves, is one of the quieter ways Kell measures a life.

The street crews scrape the public ways. They call themselves the green gang, with the perfect seriousness of people who have never heard the phrase anywhere else. Orra Vane has run the crew on the Low Terraces for twenty-two years. She is short and wide and very strong, with a moss-stained leather apron and a voice she has not raised since she was nine. Orra can read a step at a glance: how many days since it was last done, whether the red moss or the grey is winning, whether the frost has loosened it. She says the moss has moods. On heavy days it goes faster; you can nearly watch it. After a boulder has fallen off the rim, she says, it slows for a week, *out of respect*. She says it without smiling, and nobody on her crew smiles either.

The scrapings go over the rim in the carts. Tipping them is a small ceremony that everyone pretends is not one. The cart is wheeled to the edge, the gang stands back, and the green mass slides off and folds down into the cloud without a sound. Someone always watches it until it's gone. There is a superstition, half-joking and entirely obeyed, that you must never tip the carts in the clear hour. The moss must go down into the cloud, not into sunlight. No one remembers why. Orra thinks the moss would not forgive it.

## The Potters

The pits and the kiln rows are a people inside the people. They have their own streets, their own walkways, their own way of standing, heavy-shouldered and slightly stooped from the troughs, and a smell that follows them everywhere: wet clay, which smells like rain on stone, and under it the dry, faintly burnt mineral smell of the kilns.

Their faces are dusted white. Kaolin gets into everything. Around the settling troughs it drifts in a fine luminous haze, catching the lamplight, and the washers come off shift looking powdered like actors or ghosts. Pit children are called *milkies* by everyone else, and they wear the name proudly.

The kiln masters are the aristocracy of Kell, if Kell has one. Duna Hessel is the eldest. She can tell the temperature inside a firing by the colour through the inspection port, to within a few degrees, and she has not been wrong in anyone's memory. She is nearly blind now from forty years of looking into orange light, and that has not slowed her. She taps each insulator as it comes out of the kiln with the back of a fingernail and listens. A sound piece rings clear and long, a small bell. A flawed one rings short and dead. She knows the note of every insulator pattern the Works has ever made, and sometimes, when the wind is up and the Harp is singing, she stops in the street with her head tilted, and her apprentices know better than to speak. She is listening for a flaw somewhere out in the ranks. More than once she has sent a pylon crew to a particular tower, a particular string, and they have found a cracked bell exactly where she said.

In winter the kiln rows become a dormitory. The warmest places on the mountain are the narrow brick passages behind the kilns, and the potters sleep there on straw pallets in long rows, with the kiln walls ticking and pinging beside them as they cool. Anyone may sleep there in the hard months. Old people who live alone come down. So do new mothers and runaway children. It is not charity. It is only that the warmth is there, and it would be wrong to waste it.

## The Climbers

The pylon crews tend the Harp, and they are the strangest of Kell's people, because they spend their working lives in the air.

They climb the lattice towers in the pearl, hooked on with leather belts and iron clips, and out on the wires the fog is so thick that the ground disappears entirely at ten metres up. A climber at the top of a pylon is alone in a grey sphere of nothing, with a single tower going down into mist below and wires running off in every direction into blankness, beaded, dripping, faintly humming. They say you stop knowing which way is up after an hour. They say the only way to tell is the direction the water runs.

Wen Asherby has climbed for eleven years. She is thin and quick, with lichen-grey eyes, and she has a burn scar running from her left wrist to her elbow like a pale fern, the branching mark of a discharge she took on a heavy day in her second year. Climbers call these *ferns*, and most of the old ones have at least one. It is not quite a badge of honour. It is closer to proof that the Harp noticed you.

The climbers do not go out on heavy days. At least, the rule says they don't. What they actually do on heavy days is sit in the accumulator sheds among the tall glass jars, which glow faintly in the dark as they fill, and wait with their hands flat on the brick floor, feeling the charge build through the stone like a held breath. When a spark gap fires somewhere out in the fog, the crack comes through the floor before it comes through the air. They count the seconds between. They say the Harp is louder on those days than anyone down in the streets can imagine. It isn't the sound. It's what the sound is doing to your teeth.

Climbers are the only people allowed near the cupbirds.

## The Cupbirds

The slate-grey bird that nests in the insulator bells has more names than any other creature on the Crown. The pit people call it the cupbird. The climbers call it the tenant. Old people call it the knell, after its call, which is two notes, high and then higher, like a struck cup and then its answer.

It lives only on the Harp. Nobody has found one anywhere else, not on the corestones, not on the flanks, not in any lowland city where the insulators are shipped. A crate once went down the funicular with a nest in it by mistake: two birds, a clutch of three eggs, packed by accident into a box of seconds. The crate was opened at the bottom. The birds were gone. The eggs were cold. The Works has checked every crate by lamplight ever since.

This raises the question everyone in Kell has asked and no one has answered: what was the cupbird before the Harp? Where did it nest when there were no white ribbed bells on iron towers in the fog? The birds are not new. The oldest records of the town mention them. The first pylon crews, sixty or eighty years before anyone now alive was born, found them already moving into the first insulators almost as soon as they were hung, as if they had been waiting.

Some say they nested in the hollows of the corestones, and moved. Some say they came up from the lowlands with the cloud. Duna Hessel has her own theory, which she shares only with apprentices, late, near the kilns. She says the cupbirds nested in the mountain itself, back when there was a peak, when the granite was hard and high and full of cracks. She says they have been waiting all this time for something on the Crown to be hard and white and hollow again, and the town made it for them.

A cracked insulator with a bird in it stays on the tower. That is the rule. Climbers string a bypass wire around it, a small loop of copper, and leave the bird its broken cup. There are some pylons on the outer ranks now where half the bells are bypassed, and the towers look oddly festooned, garlanded with little loops of green copper, each loop a small concession of the town's power to a bird the size of a thrush.

## The Children Below

Kell's children spend more of their lives underground than above it.

This isn't because anyone sends them. The sealed floors of the old quarter are forbidden, and every child knows exactly where the forbidden stairwells are. The buried floors are dark and dripping, full of wet air that smells of old iron. The walls are furred with pale moss that grows without light, a strange ghost-green, and some of the rooms still have furniture in them: chairs, a bedstead, a table set with cups. Deeper down the rooms are filled solid, moss and silt compacted like peat, and the only way through is where someone, long ago, tunnelled.

The children have mapped the undertown far better than any adult. They have their own names for it: the Kitchen, the Drowned Stair, the Clock Room, the Bed That Breathes (a mattress so saturated with moss it gives off a faint sigh when pressed). They pass these names down from one gang of children to the next, and when they grow up they stop saying them, and pretend to have forgotten.

Pell is eleven, a milky from the pits, thin and serious and white to the elbows, and he has been further down than anyone his age. He says that at the bottom of the Drowned Stair there is a room where you can put your ear to the floor on heavy nights and hear the hum clearer than anywhere in the upper town, clearer even than in the accumulator sheds. He says it doesn't sound like the Harp. He says it sounds like it's coming from below. He says it sounds like breathing, if a thing breathed once a year.

Nobody believes him, and every child who has gone down with him to listen comes back very quiet.

## The Kept

People die in Kell as they do everywhere. What happens next is particular to the Crown.

There is no graveyard on the dome. The grus is too soft, too wet, too unwilling to hold a shape. Burial in the rim would mean a grave that slides off the mountain within a decade. Nor do they send the dead down the funicular. That would be the worst thing, to be exported like porcelain, sent down into grey nothing and never met by anyone who knew you.

Instead, the dead of Kell are kept.

When a floor of the old quarter is finally being given up, when the rising ground has reached the windowsills and the damp is creeping in from below, the family whose rooms they were chooses a last room. The dead are laid there. Not in a coffin, but in a bed, or a chair by the old hearth, dressed and wrapped in wool, with some small porcelain thing in their hands: a cup, a bell, an insulator they fired themselves. Then the door is closed, and the stairwell is filled in over the years with scrapings and silt, and the moss is left to do what the moss does.

This is why the pit crews treat it so gravely when they break into the old buried floors. Everyone knows what they might find. Usually it is only a hearth, a clock, a cup of the old pattern. Sometimes it is more. They say the cold and the dark and the moss keep everything, and that the faces of the kept are soft and green and entirely calm, as if they had lain down in a meadow. The crew closes the breach, marks it, and cuts the pit around it. There are clay pits on the Crown shaped very oddly, bending around nothing visible, because a family is kept there.

In Kell, then, the dead are not gone. They are underneath. The town rises on them. Every generation lives one storey higher on the ones before, and that is what Kell means by *home*: the place where your grandmother is the floor.

The children in the undertown know this better than anyone. It doesn't frighten them, most of them. They have a rule, older than any adult rule, that you whisper *it's me* when you pass a sealed door, the same thing you'd say at any door in Kell. You are visiting.

## The Small Ones

The rest of the Crown's living things are small, and the people love them with a thrift that would seem strange anywhere else.

The glass slugs that graze windowpanes at night are never killed. Households sometimes set out a little dish of wet lichen on the sill to keep one visiting. There are folk who swear the slug's silver trail, read in the morning, forecasts the weather: a straight trail for a thin day, a spiral for heavy. The dew spiders are counted by the climbers, who say a year of heavy webs along the corestones is a year of good water. Children collect the pale moss beetles in porcelain thimbles and race them on the kitchen table, and let them go afterward on the same doorstep where they were caught, because that is the proper way.

It is not sentiment exactly. It is that on the Crown, where nothing is tall and nothing is loud, where the visible world ends at twenty metres and most of the life is underfoot, a living thing that moves is a kind of news. A slug on a pane is company. A beetle in a thimble is an event.

## The Stood

And then there is the grazer.

It is seen perhaps twice a year, on the very thickest nights, in the outer ranks of the Harp. It is tall, taller than a person at the shoulder, and very slow, and so shaggy that its shape is hard to make out even close, as though it were a heap of wet wool someone had stood up on long thin legs. Its coat is moss-coloured. More than that: people who have seen one close say its coat has moss in it. There are cushions and threads of green growing in the fleece, lichen in long grey beards hanging from its flanks, and once, according to Wen Asherby, a small red sundew no bigger than a thumbnail, rooted in the damp fur behind its ear, lifting its sticky little hand.

It browses on the lower cushions between the pylons, very carefully, taking the tops off the moss domes and leaving the roots. It never enters the streets. When a lamp is raised it goes perfectly still, and in the pearl, a perfectly still thing covered in moss is a boulder. That is all anyone ever sees: a boulder that wasn't there last night, among the outer pylons. In the morning, a boulder that isn't there anymore. A line of deep hoofprints in the sponge, filling slowly with water.

Kell has more names for it than for anything else on the mountain, and the names say more about the namers than the thing. The scrapers call it *the Gardener*, because where it has browsed, Orra says, the moss grows back slower and better for a year. The climbers call it *the Stood*, from how it stands. The pit people call it *the Loaf*, fondly, after the corestones. The oldest people in town don't call it anything. They simply go quiet when it's mentioned, and make the sign for *I see you through the fog*.

What it is, no one knows. It might be an animal like any other, a relict grazer from some older, colder age that has retreated to the one place high enough to keep it. It might be one of a herd on the flanks, below the cloud line, which the people of Kell have never seen because no one from Kell goes down the flanks. It might be the last of something.

But there is a story told near the kilns in winter, told carefully, as a story and not as a belief. It goes like this.

When the mountain was high, it was hard. It had a peak and edges and bare grey faces with no moss on them, and it lived the way stone lives, which is to say too slowly for anything to notice. Then the water got into it and it began to rot, grain by grain, from the inside. And the mountain, which had never needed anything, found it needed something now. It needed to be tended while it came apart. So it grew a thing to do that: a keeper, made of the only living things that could live on it, moss and lichen and wet wool, and set it walking on the dome, to crop the cushions so they would not choke the stone, and to stand very still among the corestones so the mountain would have something like itself to look at.

The kiln masters end the story differently, depending on who is telling it. Some say the keeper was alone for ten thousand years before the town came. Some say the town is the second keeper. Some say it is only a story, and pour another cup.

## What Lives Here

Ask what lives on the Crown and the first answer is the people: four thousand quiet, pale, clay-handed, damp-haired people, speaking low in a lamplit web of catwalks strung between old stones, scraping moss, washing clay, climbing wires into the fog, keeping their dead in the floors beneath them. That answer is true.

The longer you look, the harder it becomes to say where the town stops and the other living things begin. The moss lives in the town and the town lives in the moss, and each is slowly swallowing the other. The cupbirds live in the town's white bells, and the town leaves its broken bells up for them. The dead live under the living. The children live under the dead's old rooms. The Stood lives at the edge of the Harp, and the Harp lives off the cloud, and the cloud climbs the mountain twice a day, as regular as breath.

And the mountain lives here too, in the way old things live, too slowly to be seen, except on heavy nights when it hums.

This, finally, is what the people of Kell are, in the long account of the Crown. It is what Duna Hessel knows, tapping her insulators with the back of a fingernail and listening for the clear long ring.
