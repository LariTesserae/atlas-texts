# Who Lives in the Throat

## The Easy Answer

The first answer is the one the visitors give: the birds, the moss, the green skin on the shelves. These are real, and they matter. But they are tenants. They come and go, or they grow and are buried. They live *at* the Throat the way lichen lives on a gravestone.

Something else lives *in* it. To find it you have to lie on your belly at the lip of the fissure, where the stone is sharpest and newest, with the rising wall close enough to tear the cloth from your face. You have to look down past the white, into the part of the water that is not yet white, where it comes up clear and dark and hard as poured glass.

It is there, holding.

---

## The Ribbon

At first you think it is a flaw in the water, a streak of lighter current. Then it moves against the current, and you understand that it is a body.

It is flat and long, about the width of two hands. You can see perhaps an arm's length of it before it goes down into the dark, and no one has ever seen where it ends. It has no color of its own. The light that falls down the shaft goes into it and comes out the other side slightly changed, softened and bent, so that it looks less like flesh than like a place where the water has thickened. Along both edges runs a frill as fine as the edge of a leaf. The frill never stops moving. It ripples in a standing wave, always the same shape and always renewed, the way a flame keeps its shape by burning.

That movement is the whole secret of it.

In the Throat, stone takes whatever is still. The stick left on the floor, the handprint, the cord, the cairn, the ladder, the moss that slows down for one summer: anything that rests is claimed. The ribbon has never rested. It lives in the one spot where the water is most loaded with stone, the very mouth of the spring, and no crust has ever formed on it. Its skin is slick with something that refuses the mineral. Its frill sheds the pale grains as fast as they land. It holds its place in the torrent by moving exactly as hard as the water moves, never less. If it paused, it would begin to become glass.

The visitors say the room is *the place the water doesn't go*. The ribbon is the other half of that saying. It is the place the stone doesn't go. The room is a hollow in the water, and the ribbon is a hollow in the stone. The Throat is made of the two of them.

## What It Eats

What rises with the water from below is not nothing. Anyone who has held a cloth over the spring for an hour and then looked closely at it has seen specks caught in the weave. There are threads of pale growth, grains of something soft, and now and then a tiny curled creature no longer than an eyelash, colorless and eyeless like the ribbon itself. These are the small lives of the hot dark down there, torn loose and carried up.

The ribbon's frill combs them from the current. It feeds on the scraps of a world that no one above has ever seen. It is the only thing on the mountain that eats from below instead of from the sky.

It may also feed on warmth. The water is cold as a knife by the time it reaches the lip, but it was hot once. Lower down, where the ribbon's body goes, it must still be warm. Some visitors think the ribbon lies in the spring the way a person lies with their feet toward a fire and their face in the cold. Its tail is in the heat it came from. Its head is up here in the thin bright air, tasting.

## The Stutter

Everyone who comes learns the count. Every few breaths the pressure falters, the rising wall goes thin and glassy, and for a moment you can throw yourself through. The visitors call it the stutter. They say it is the Throat breathing.

They are nearly right.

Down in the fissure the crack is narrow, and it is growing narrower every year under its own deposits. The ribbon's body fills more of it than you would think. When the frill turns over, when the whole long body flexes to take a new grip on the water, it closes part of the channel for an instant. The pressure checks, the column falters, and the wall goes thin.

The stutter is the ribbon shifting its hold. It is the one gesture it has, repeated for longer than anyone's grandparents' grandparents have climbed the mountain. Every visitor who has ever come through the curtain came through in the gap the ribbon made. Every one who got back out went out the same way.

Whether it knows this is the oldest argument about the Throat. Some say it is blind in every sense, and that the stutter is nothing but a creature keeping its grip. Others point out that the stutters are not quite regular. When someone is waiting outside, soaked and counting, the gap tends to come a little sooner. When someone has lain down in the hollow on the shelf and closed their eyes, the gaps grow long and uneven, as if the thing below had forgotten them, or had decided to let them stay.

And the ones who lose the count and try the wall without the gap, the ones who go through and never come out on the other side: the arguers disagree about them too. Some say they are lifted in the rising sheet and thrown out over the lip onto the rocks below, and that no one has ever found the bodies because the stone gets there first. Others say that when there is no gap, it is because the ribbon has not given one. They say it is not cruelty, only a different kind of attention.

## The Word

Here is where it becomes more than an animal.

Lie at the lip long enough and you notice that the frill does not move in one pattern. The standing wave changes. It lengthens and shortens, doubles, falls into a slow triple beat and then out of it. Each change alters the stutter a little, and each altered stutter changes the way the tones of the chamber slide against each other: the low floor, the shear, the collapse. That split-second overlap of sounds is where visitors swear they hear something almost like a word.

The ribbon is shaping the water the way a tongue shapes breath. The fissure is the throat and the ribbon is the tongue. The whole column, rising and falling, the curtain and the room and the booming lip, is the mouth held open to the sky.

What it is saying, no one knows. But people who have stayed the full day and night, and heard the stutters line up into rhythm and repeat, come out with a certain feeling. It doesn't sound like a prayer or a warning. It sounds more like a description, as if something that came up from the deep dark is naming, very slowly, everything it has found up here: cold, then light, then the small dark bodies that pierce the curtain like darts and return, then the green, then the soft hands pressed into the new floor, one on top of another, generation on generation. The shape of each of those things goes into the pattern and becomes a syllable.

It is not talking to anyone. It is talking the way the visitors tie their cords, the way you'd say something aloud in an empty room.

## The Old Throats

The visitors believe that when the Throat closes, the ribbon will be sealed in the glass at the heart of the spire. The mountain will open somewhere else and a new Throat will begin, empty.

They are half right, and the proof is on the mountain for anyone willing to look.

The mountain is not bare of spires. Higher up, and to the east, and in a line curving round the shoulder below the snow, stand pale green-white needles of stone, some as tall as three people, some worn down to stumps. They are old Throats, long closed. Most people take them for strange rocks. Break one open, and a few foolish people have, and near the core, sealed in glass, you will find a long flat shape, colorless and frilled at the edges, going down.

It is always only part of a body. It is never the whole length, and it never has a head.

The ribbon sheds. When a Throat narrows to its end and the needle of water grows too thin to live in, the ribbon lets its outermost length go still, and the stone takes it. The living part, the part that was always deeper and warmer, draws down and away through cracks no one will ever see, following the pressure to wherever the mountain is about to split. When the rock opens and the water comes up cold and new, the ribbon comes up with it, already holding.

So *some say it's the same one that was there when the rock split* is true, but it is too small a truth. It was there when this rock split, and the one before, and the one before that. The line of spires across the mountain is a trail of its old skins, or its old tongues. Each one was stopped mid-syllable and left standing in the weather.

## The Long Word

If each Throat is one breath, and each stutter within it is one small sound, then the word the ribbon is saying is longer than any Throat. It was begun in the first spire, whose stump is now little more than a pale knob in the scree, and it has been carried forward through every one since. The visitors hear a fragment and come out quiet. They are quiet because they have understood something without being able to say it: they have heard the middle of a sentence that began before there were people on the mountain and will end, if it ends, long after.

That is why they come back. It isn't to hear the whole word, which no one will. They come back to hear the next part.

## What Else Lives There

There is one more inhabitant, and it is the hardest to see.

When someone climbs the stone ladder that can no longer be climbed, or once could, and curls in the shallow hollow on the first shelf, and stays until the stutters begin to rhyme, something happens to them that the visitors have no word for. They stop being a guest. They lose their own voice and forget they had one, and for that stretch of time they are something the Throat is holding the way it holds the moss and the cairns and the cords. They are still, and it is slowly making them into the room. They leave before the stone can finish.

Every one of them leaves something behind: a handprint, a cord, a small stone on a pile. None of it stays theirs. All of it ends up inside the glass, and so all of it, in the end, goes into the word.

So the answer to *who lives in the Throat* is this. A pale thing with no eyes lives in the throat of the spring. It holds itself against the whole weight of the mountain's water by never once being still. It came up out of a heat no one has touched, and it makes, without meaning to or perhaps meaning to, the only doors in and out. It is tasting the upper world a little at a time and saying what it tastes. And for as long as each of them can bear the cold, it lives there with every person who ever learned the count, lay on the lip, looked down into the clear dark water, and saw it turn, very slightly, toward the shadow of their face.
