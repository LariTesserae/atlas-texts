# Who Keeps the Works

## First, the Wrong Answers

You will say at first that nobody lives here.

You will count the moths and the crows and decide they are tenants rather than residents. The moths sleep in the junction boxes the way sparrows sleep in the eaves of a factory: because the place is warm and nobody drives them out. The crows walk the rails because rails are good for walking. Neither of them runs anything, and neither of them seems to know the Works are running. They are wildlife in a machine, and a machine has no more inhabitants than a clock does.

Then you will see your first husk up close, and you will change your mind. You will decide that *these* are the people of the Table: the bark-men in their overalls, walking to shift.

It is worth describing one properly, because nobody who writes about the Table does.

The overalls are grown, not worn. They are one continuous skin with the body, but the bark has kept every fold of cloth. There is a crease at the back of each knee, worn pale and smooth, and the grain goes shiny there the way old wood goes shiny under a banister-rail. The bib pocket holds a pencil, a thin twig with a graphite-dark pith. The cap has a sweat-band, and in the grain of the sweat-band, if you turn the husk's head (they let you; they are light as paper lanterns), there is a darker ring where a forehead pressed for years. The hands are the most exact thing about them. There is a flat, glossy callus across the base of each finger, the shape of a hammer-haft or a lever-grip, and the right thumbnail is split down the middle and healed crooked.

Inside, they are empty. The lining is green and faintly damp, like the inside of a pea-pod. It smells of sap and cold tea.

When you let one go, it gets up and carries on toward whatever hall it was walking to. It does not hurry. It never arrives. The bark remembers the walk the way a hinge remembers its creak, and a memory in wood does not need anyone inside it.

So the husks do not live here either. They are what is left of somebody who did.

## The Missing Seam

The obvious question is what came out, and this is where the Table starts to become strange in a quieter way than the whistles and the flickering walls.

A cicada splits down the back. A snake peels from the lips. Anything that emerges from a husk leaves a door behind it. The bark-workers have none. You can turn one over in your hands for an hour. There is no split, no torn place, no hatch at the nape. Whatever left them left the way steam leaves a boiler: through the material, without breaking it.

Think about how the city came to be. The living mat replaced it particle by particle, knot for bolt and heartwood for girder, each piece of iron exchanged for a piece of tissue in exactly its shape. The conversion was perfect because it copied. It needed a form to follow.

The workers were converted too. Cell by cell, the long slow tide came up through their boots and took them, the same way it took the rivets. Every bone had its counterpart in wood, and so did every tendon and every crease at the back of the knee. It matched the callus and it matched the crooked thumbnail.

There was one thing in each of them that had no shape, and so it could not be copied.

The tide could not match it with a knot or a grain-line, because there was nothing there to trace. When everything around it had been replaced, it was simply displaced, the way water is pushed out of a jar when you fill the jar with sand. It did not go far. It could not go far; it had never known anything but the Works. It went into the one part of the city that also has no shape.

It went into the work.

## The Hands

The old word for them is *hands*: so many hands on the payroll, all hands to the forge. The people of the Table are called the Hands, and they have no hands. The joke is the plateau's, not mine.

A Hand is a rhythm.

Each forging hall keeps its own tempo, four blows and a pause, and no two halls quite agree. You noticed that on your first day, walking between them as though between the sections of an orchestra. What you took for machinery drifting out of true is character. Four blows and a pause is how a person breathes when they swing something heavy all day: out, out, out, out, and the breath in. One hall leans on its third blow. One hall's pause is a hair too long, like a man who always straightens and presses a fist to the small of his back before he starts again. One hall keeps a hitch in its second stroke that sounds exactly like a limp.

These are not engines. They are the gaits of people, kept.

Once you know this, you cannot unhear it. The whine of the belts rises and falls with someone's attention, sharpening as a Hand leans in, slackening as it looks up. The steam-hiss from a leaky joint that comes and goes in slow waves is somebody whistling through their teeth. The seed-head gauges quiver because something is watching them, the way a real gauge seems to steady when the foreman walks past. The hinges of green wood open and shut when nothing is at the door because something is always at the door, going in, coming out, going in again.

They are shy in the way a noise is shy. You cannot look at one. You can only be near it.

## How They Live

A Hand is not bound to its hall, though most of them keep to one. On still mornings, if you stand long enough in the fog between two buildings, you can hear a rhythm cross the gap: a tempo fading out of one hall and turning up, a little muffled, in the next. The receiving hall's own beat falters for a stroke to make room. The two of them run together for a while, blow against blow, a syncopation that is unmistakably two people working one job. Then one of them leaves again. You have just heard somebody visit.

When an engine fails, when a drop-hammer splits and a hall goes silent, the city is not breaking down. That is how a Hand dies, or leaves, and the two may not be different here. The silence afterwards is the loudest thing on the Table because it is the shape of a particular person, and you had not realised you knew them until the pause went on too long.

The new hammer that grows from the stump, months or a year later, does not always come back with the same beat. Sometimes it does, exactly, and the moss that flooded into the quiet dies back overnight as though it had been told off. More often the new rhythm is uneven at first. It comes in groups of three, or five. It loses its count and starts again. These are apprentices. You can follow one over a season as it gets steadier, and there comes a morning when it lands its fourth blow and its pause as cleanly as any hall on the plateau. The crows go and sit on that roof all day, as if they have come to watch.

The crows are the only creatures that seem to know the Hands are there. They learned the tram-squeal, yes, but a pair of them will sometimes sit on a gantry rail making sounds that are nothing to do with trams: a short two-note whistle, the kind you give to call someone across a yard, and a dry, broken sound that is almost certainly a laugh. Nobody on the Table makes those sounds anymore. Nobody with a mouth, anyway. The crows are repeating what they hear.

## The Geography of People

This explains the distances.

The Works are arranged by where people work, not by where the buildings stand. The map the crows see from above is the architect's map: the city as drawn, with its halls set at so many hundred metres from one another across cheap flat land. The map you walk is a different thing. It is how far the forge is from the turbine house for the man who works in both, and the short cut everyone knows from the kilns to the canteen that never existed. It is also the long way round nobody takes past the hall where something happened once, a long time ago, that nobody talks about.

Walk by sight and you are walking the drawings, and the drawings are out of date. You will circle. Walk toward the sound of a hall, with your eyes half closed, and you are walking toward someone. They let you arrive. It is only good manners. You would do the same if a stranger came up the lane calling your name.

## Relapse

The relapses are the Hands too, or rather they happen to the Hands, the way a fever happens to you.

Now and then a Hand remembers too hard. It remembers the heat on its face and the weight of the iron and the grit of real brick under its palm. It remembers so hard that for a second the city around it has to agree. The wall goes cold and gritty. The chain drips rust. The whole hall flickers black under a smoke-dark sky, and on the gantries there are men.

Look at them, in the second you get. They are not walking. They are standing still with their faces turned up, as if someone had called down from the roof. They seem as surprised as you are.

Then it is timber again, and the men are husks, and a clean ring of moss dies around the building. I used to think the dead ring was poison: iron and coal-smoke, briefly real, scorching the living mat. I don't think that now. I think it is the cost. For a second of being a person, a Hand draws on everything around it, and the moss in that ring gives its season so someone can stand in his own boots on his own gantry and look up. The mat does not seem to begrudge it. The ring always grows back.

The husks are what is left over when the relapse ends: the body stepping out of the moment a half-beat later than the Hand did. That is why there are so many, and why they are all walking to shift. They are walking back to where they were just standing, a second ago, forever.

## Their Sun

The clocktower does not keep the time of the sky. It keeps theirs.

The sun it tracks is the sun of one working day: a long day, the day the Works were in, whenever that was, and the Hands are still inside it. That is why every shadow on the Table falls at the clock's angle and not the light's. The shadows belong to the Hands, and the Hands are standing in a different afternoon.

The shift-whistle is the change of shift. The clock jumps, and every shadow on the plateau swings round at once, because thousands of people have just downed tools and turned toward the gate, or picked them up and turned toward the bench. The moss flattens outward in rings because that is how a crowd moves through a yard. The seed-drift blows clear in bubbles because something has walked through it. You cover your ears and it does not help, because the sound is not coming in through your ears. It is the whole population of a city moving at once, and you are standing in the middle of it.

## The Accounts

The power does not balance because the Hands are not paid.

They work, and they have worked for an age of mountains, and they have no wage and nothing to spend one on. The surplus has to go somewhere. It goes into the vines. The lines carry more than the ferns could ever turn out because the labour of the whole city is flowing through them, the part of the work that was never bought, and it has been accumulating since before the peaks wore down.

At dusk, when the blue arcs jump at the junctions, that is knocking-off time. That is the surplus spilling over at the end of the day, the hour a person used to have for themselves. The moths come to the arcs to feed. I cannot decide whether this is sad. They are living, entirely, on the evenings of people who no longer have evenings, and they are very beautiful, slate-grey and hammered like sheet metal, and they are the only thing on the Table that is truly at rest.

## The Tanks and the Rings

In the quench-tanks, the long ribbons pulse when the hammers fall.

I think these are the city trying to make them bodies again. It is the one thing the tide cannot do. It can copy any shape, but the Hands have none, and so the ribbons grow and grow toward a form that does not exist. They lengthen in the dark cold water and keep time with whichever hall is nearest, beating along with a rhythm they will never be able to hold. Some of the ribbons are very old. It is patient work. Nobody on the Table knows any other kind.

The growth rings in the beams are their plans and their memories, and the Hands have trouble telling the two apart. The outer rings that show buildings not yet on the Table are the halls somebody means to get round to. The ones with more rings than the city has had years are the memories of somebody very old, going back further than the Works do, perhaps to when he was a boy in another town. The beam that shows the city filling the Table from edge to edge is what full employment looks like to people for whom work is the only form of being alive. The beam that shows the Table empty, all moss and no chimneys, is rarer. I have only heard of one. It was cut from a hall that has been silent for longer than any other on the plateau, and nothing has ever grown back from the stump.

I believe that one is a picture of rest.

## Below

Lie down with your ear to the mat, and under the creak of the settling floor you will hear the older Works. They are the halls the ground has already taken, still running under the moss.

Their tempo is slower. The blows are further apart and deeper, and the pause after the fourth is very long, long enough that the first few times you think it has stopped. These are the first Hands, or the oldest. They are sinking with their buildings into the root-mat as the mountains once did, being taken the whole way down, and they have not stopped work. They have only slowed to the pace of the ground. One day, I suppose, their stroke will be one blow in a century. Then they will be geology, and the mat will finally have the shape of them, and it will copy them at last.

## At the Rim

Stand at the end of the rails where the crates are tipped, and wait until you hear a hall's rhythm moving.

It happens rarely. A tempo leaves its building and does not turn up in the next. It comes down the root rails toward the edge, riding along in the rattle of a gourd-tram, keeping time to the squeal of seed-pod wheels. Then the crate tips, and the seeds go over in their slow gold stream. The rhythm goes with them, getting fainter, down into the cloud.

The Works do not only make parts. Anyone can make parts. A living valve will open and shut on its own, and a seed-cog will grow its teeth. But a machine grown from seeds, somewhere below the cloud-sea, in whatever place they have been falling into for longer than anyone can reckon, would stand there in the wet with its parts complete and not know what time to keep. Somebody has to go down and show it. Somebody has to stand at the new forge and swing out, out, out, out, and breathe in, until it learns.

That is the sound that comes up on the warm wind from below. It is not an echo. Somebody went down there, and they are teaching.

## And You

One last thing, since you have come all this way.

You noticed the green down starting on your cuffs if you sat still. You will also have noticed, if you stayed more than a few days, that you started walking a certain way between the halls. You took the same short cut each morning, and you had a habit of pausing at the second cistern to drink, touching the burl as you bent. You have a gait. Everyone does. It is the one thing about you that has no shape.

The Table has been listening to it the whole time.

When you go back down the switchback into the cloud, and you should go back down before the down on your cuffs reaches your knees, there will be a hall somewhere on the plateau that you never went into. In it, very faintly, uneven, losing its count and starting again, a new rhythm will have begun. It will be yours, the way you walk, practising.

The crows will go and sit on that roof to listen. And one morning, a long time after you are gone, it will land its fourth step and its pause as cleanly as any hall on the High Table, and nobody will be there to hear it but them.
