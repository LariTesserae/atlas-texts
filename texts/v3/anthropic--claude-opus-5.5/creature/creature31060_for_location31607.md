# Those Who Live in the Lowland of the First Crust

## The Obvious Answer

The first answer is the one already given. The crabs live here, and the frogs, the skinks, the rails, the blind white shrimp in the tube-water. Anyone standing on the warm glass could name a hundred residents before finishing a breath.

But that answers the wrong question. The crabs live here the way rain lives here. They are part of the weather of the place. The real question is who lives here the way a person lives in a house, knowing where the doors are and keeping them shut.

For a long time you wouldn't notice them. That is deliberate.

## A Shape at the Crack Mouth

You'd see one first as a disturbance in the pattern: a mound of moss at the lip of a fissure, a little too regular, breathing slightly out of rhythm with the steam. Then it lifts its head. It had been lying with its ear pressed to the rock.

They are small, roughly the height of a child of eight or nine, though nothing about them is childlike once you look closely. Their skin is damp and soft, nearly translucent at the throat and wrists, and moss grows on them. It grows on purpose, cultivated and tended. It covers their scalps and shoulders and runs in a fine green nap down their spines, so a group of them lying together on a flow lobe looks like one more swelling of the carpet. Their eyes are very large and dark, made for pewter light, with no need to squint because nothing here is ever bright. Their fingertips are padded and faintly sticky, like the tree frogs', and so are their soles. They can walk up a fern trunk as easily as across a pool edge.

They have no nails and no claws. Their teeth are flat and dull, made for fern-fruit and snail and the soft white pith of the root bundles. Nothing about them is hard, and you'll come to understand that nothing about them is permitted to be.

They breathe partly through their skin. That is why they lie so long at the crack mouths. The warm exhalation from below is nourishment of a kind, and it is something else too.

## What They Call Themselves

Their word for themselves is the same as their word for moss. Translated as closely as it goes, it means *the thing that holds without gripping*.

This is the center of their lives. Moss covers the rock without entering it. It pads every coil of pāhoehoe and wears nothing away. It takes the drip and holds it, gives it back slowly, and never lets it run. The people regard moss as the most virtuous thing in the world, and they try to be moss.

## The Rule of No Groove

Every people has a first law. Theirs is this: **make no path for water.**

They understand something about this place that its geology only hints at. Water here does not run. It sits, seeps, rises as steam, and drips back down, a closed circuit with no slope. But they believe water *wants* to run. Every pool is patient, and every drip is looking. If you gave it a single groove, one scratch across a flow lobe with a slight fall to it, the water would find it. It would follow it and deepen it a hair. The next drip would follow the deepened line and deepen it again. In time there would be a channel, and then a gully, and then a fan of broken stone at its foot, the first rubble the world has ever seen. After that there would be before and after, and the world would have begun to get old.

So they do not scratch. They carry nothing that could. Their tools are fiber and pith and plaited frond: nets for mayflies, cups of folded filmy fern, slings of root-bark for carrying the young. They never knap stone, because there is no loose stone to knap and they would sooner die than make some. Where they must cross bare lobe, they walk only where the crabs have walked, in the grazed lanes, and they walk softly, padded feet spreading their weight.

When they find the crabs have scoured a lobe too thoroughly, stripping moss down to glass along some line that might, given a million years, become a slope, they bring moss from elsewhere and press it back on with their palms. They sing to it until it takes. You'll see them doing this endlessly, everywhere: kneeling on the rock, patting green into place like people smoothing a sleeper's blanket.

## The Count

They do not grow in number, and they do not shrink.

There is a fixed set of names. No one knows exactly how many. Several hundred, perhaps, or a few thousand scattered across the whole unseeable plain. Every name is always held. When someone dies, their body is laid out in a glade by a pool for the crabs, who take it in a day and a night that are not a day and a night. Within the same span, somewhere, someone gives birth, and the child receives the name.

It isn't that the dead one is reborn. The people don't think that way, or at least not in those words. It's that the name is a place, like a pool or a tumulus. The pool doesn't change when different water sits in it. Neither does the name.

This is where the whoop comes from, the large unseen calling in the haze, answered from far away and then from farther. It is them. Somewhere a name-holder cups their hands and lifts their voice in a single rising note: *still held*. Another answers from past the gray-blue ridges: *still held*. The call moves outward like a ripple, name to name, across distances no one has ever walked, until it has gone around the whole count and come back. Then someone else begins it again. It has never stopped. Like the frog chorus, like the drip, it is simply always going.

If a call ever went unanswered, it would mean a name was empty. No one alive has heard that happen. No one who ever held any name has heard it.

## The Tongue With One Tense

Their language has no past and no future. It has something closer to a single, endlessly extended present. It is not a present of *now* as opposed to *then*. It is a present of *this*, as opposed to nothing at all.

They can say *the frog calls*. They cannot say *the frog called* or *the frog will call*. When pressed, they explain patiently, as to a slow child, that there is no difference. The frog is calling. Some frog is always calling. That is what a frog is.

They have stories, but the stories don't happen. They are descriptions of how things are, told the way you'd describe the grain of a trunk. *Crab eats what falls. Moss holds what drips. The breath comes up.* A good storyteller is one who can describe the world so completely and so evenly that nothing in the telling stands out, a story as level as the plain.

The worst thing you can say of someone is a word that means *they made a mark*. It is almost never said.

## The Breath

They listen to the rock the way other people pray.

Every name-holder spends part of the endless afternoon lying at a crack mouth with an ear to the warm glass, hearing the low hiss beneath the chorus. They do not agree on what it is. Some say it is the land breathing in its sleep. Some say it is something under the land, something large and warm and curled, and that the whole plain is its skin, or its shell. Some say, more quietly, that the plain is not a skin but a lid.

On one point they all agree: the breathing is slow and even, and it must stay that way. A sleeper whose skin is never scratched never wakes. They are careful because they love the world as it is, and also because they are not certain what the world is resting on.

Listening, they breathe with it. Their exhalations slow to match. Lying there, moss-furred, damp, warm, one of them is hard to tell apart from a tumulus: a small swelling of the crust, breathing.

## The One Who Holds the Edge

This is the part they don't tell easily. It is the part where the level story develops a single ridge.

The tumuli crack open across their crowns. Everyone knows this, and it is allowed: the cracks were always there, made in the cooling, before anything. But once, and the people have no tense for *once*, so they say *always, at one place*, a crack in a tumulus crown let go of a flake.

It is a sliver of black glass about the length of a finger joint, iridescent blue-violet at its edges. It is the only loose piece of stone on the entire plain. Its edge is sharper than anything else in the world.

The people did not destroy it, because they cannot. There is no way to unmake stone, and throwing it into a deep pool would only put it somewhere else. Nor did they bury it, because burying would mean digging. So among the fixed names there is one name whose holder carries the edge.

That name-holder carries it in a closed hand. Always. They eat one-handed, climb one-handed, press moss back onto scoured rock one-handed. Their other hand stays shut around the only thing in the world that could make a groove. The people have a whole grammar of gestures for speaking to this person without asking them to open it.

When that holder dies, the next holder is chosen before the crabs arrive. They kneel by the body, and the dead fingers are eased open against the living palm, so the edge passes from skin to skin and never once touches rock, never lies in open air, never has the chance to fall. The old holder's hand is empty. The new holder's hand closes. The call goes out: *still held*.

They say the Edge-Holder is the only one of them who can dream. Maybe it is only a saying. But it is true that the Edge-Holder lies at the crack mouths longer than anyone. When they lift their head, the others sometimes see an expression on their face that has no word in a language with one tense. Perhaps it would mean *what if*. Perhaps it would mean *before*.

## What Lives Here, Then

So here is what lives in the Lowland of the First Crust, set out as evenly as they would want it told:

A soft people who hold without gripping. They wear moss as skin and walk where the crabs have walked. They mend the green wherever it thins, because they believe the world's youth depends on water never finding a way downhill. They keep a fixed count of names and call it endlessly across a haze no one has seen through: *still held, still held*. They have no word for yesterday. They lie with their ears to the warm glass and breathe in time with something they cannot see and do not fully trust to stay asleep.

Among them, always, one of them walks with a closed hand. Inside it is the one sharp thing in the world, the single possibility of a beginning, carried carefully and never set down. It is kept exactly as the whole plain is kept: warm, wet, whole, and not yet.

And beneath them, if the listeners are right, something else lives here too. Its breath comes up through ten thousand fissures, even and slow. It has never been scratched.

It is listening back.
