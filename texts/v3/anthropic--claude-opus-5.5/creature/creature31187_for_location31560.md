# Who Lives in the Kiln

## I. The obvious answer

You would say the herons. Anyone would.

On the first morning they look like a single substance, a pale crust that shifts and settles. By the second morning you can tell them apart, and the colony stops being a texture and becomes a population.

A kiln-heron hatches blind into a cup of warm mud. The cup is warm because its parents build it facing the vents, the rim of each nest tilted toward the nearest thread of steam the way a sunflower tilts toward light. The egg is incubated as much by the mountain as by the bird. Some parents have learned that they barely need to sit at all. They leave the egg on the steam pipe, where the insulation has worn through and the metal beneath holds a steady, forgiving heat, and they spend their days in the pools. The chicks on the pipe hatch earlier and grow faster, and their parents are thinner and more often taken by the raptor, because they spend more time out in the open. Nobody designed this trade. The colony is working it out one generation at a time.

The chicks are born with a voice. For the first few weeks they cheep, a thin high sound that carries almost nowhere in the flattened air, so that a nest a metre away cannot hear it. Then, sometime in their second month, they stop. Nobody teaches them to stop. The cheeping simply gets them nothing: no food comes to the one who calls, and food comes to the one who lifts its wings and shows the pink. Every kiln-heron makes this passage from sound to gesture, one by one, the way every child in a loud city learns to shout.

What replaces the voice is enormous. Watch two neighbours on the rim at dawn. One lowers her head and half-opens her left wing. The other bobs twice, slowly, and turns so that his pink underside faces the sun. There are variations in timing that you cannot read and they obviously can: the length of a pause, the depth of a bow, whether the wing opens from the wrist or from the shoulder. Along the terrace edges the lines of dancers perform something that goes on for an hour, bodies rippling in sequences that repeat with small deliberate changes, like a rhyme being reworked. You begin to suspect it is not about mating, or not only. It may be closer to news.

So yes, the herons live here. But if you stay longer than a day, the answer starts to feel too simple.

## II. The ones who fall

On the third day you watch the ropeway.

The young herons gather at the concrete terminal in the late morning, before the heat drives everyone into shade. They wait in a loose, fidgeting queue, which is the least organised thing in the colony. The empty buckets come up out of the cloud, swing around the great iron wheel, and pass the platform at walking pace. One by one the young birds step in. Some do it neatly. Some misjudge, flap, and fall back onto the concrete while the others watch with what you can only call patience.

The bucket carries the bird out over the rim, over the talus, and down. At first you can see it perched on the lip, neck extended, looking at the white. Then the cloud takes it.

You wait, and after a while they come back. They rise out of the cloud sea on the vent-thermals one at a time, wings spread and barely moving, the pink flush showing as they bank. They come up in long spirals and land on the rim, and they are different when they land. It shows in the way they hold themselves. The older birds come to them and there is a dance, short and specific, that you never see performed at any other time.

It takes you most of a week to understand, and you might be wrong, but here is what you come to believe. The cloud sea is the one thing a kiln-heron must eventually learn. When the colony moves, and colonies do move when a vent field cools or a pool goes sterile, it moves through cloud. Below the white there is no sun to steer by and no rim to see, only the feel of rising warm air and the slope of the mountain. A bird that has never been inside the cloud panics in it. A bird that has ridden down in the slow iron bucket, tipped out into blindness, and found its way back up by the warmth has learned something it cannot learn any other way.

Before the ropeway there must have been some other way to teach this, a crueller one. Perhaps the young simply jumped, and the ones who could not find the thermals did not come back. The machine made the lesson gentler. It does not know this about itself.

And the buckets are not always empty. You notice it on the fifth day. A young heron comes up out of the cloud holding something in its bill: a sprig of something green and broad-leaved that has never grown on this mountain. It drops it at the feet of an old bird, who looks at it for a long time. Later you find more of them, tucked into the mud walls of certain nests. There are pieces of leaf and a feather from a bird you don't recognise. There is a fragment of blue plastic, and a pebble polished smooth by a river. The young carry news back up from below in the only form the colony can read.

## III. The one who keeps the lamp

On the sixth night you realise the lamp above the turbine hall door has been changed.

It is a small thing. On your first night the bulb was orange and slightly flickering. Tonight it is orange and steady. The bracket has been wiped, and the rust on the housing has a clean thumb-shaped mark in it.

In the morning you look properly for the first time. The hut with the collapsed roof really is empty. Its hearth is cold and its floor is thick with guano and dust. But the turbine hall is not empty. At the back, beyond the generator, in the space where the hot air from the machine pools against the iron wall, there is a pallet of folded sacking. There is a tin cup, a coil of wire, and a row of glass bulbs of different sizes, wrapped in cloth and lined up on a beam like eggs. Heron nests fill the rafters directly above the pallet, so thickly that droppings should be falling on it constantly, and they are not. Someone has strung a canvas sheet under the rafters, angled so it sheds everything into a trough. The trough drains into a bucket, and the bucket's contents have been spread on the one patch of cushion-moss that is noticeably greener than all the others.

You do not see her for two more days. When you do, you almost miss her, because she is standing among the herons in a pink pool at mid-morning. She is grey-white with sinter dust from her hair to her boots and bent at exactly their angle, her hands in the water, and she is sieving shrimp through a square of cloth.

She is old. She was a sulphur cutter, you think, from the way her hands are ruined, yellowed and cracked at the knuckles in the particular way the old photos show. She stayed when the company left, or she came later, or she was born to someone who stayed. You never find out, because she does not speak. You try. She looks at you with mild interest and says nothing, and after a moment she lifts her forearm and turns it slowly outward, palm up. You realise, with a shock that goes all the way down, that it is a heron gesture. You have seen it a hundred times on the rim. You do not know what it means. It seems kind.

She has lived here long enough that the air has taken her voice the way it takes theirs. She may still be able to speak and have simply found no use for it. There was nobody to hear, and what she needed to say could be said with the body.

Over the following days you piece together her life. She keeps the lamp because in the afternoon fog, when steam and cloud fill the caldera for two hours and visibility falls to nothing, the young herons coming back up from the ropeway steer by it. You see this for yourself: shapes emerging from the white, low over the pipes, homing on the smear of orange light above the door. On the day the old bulb flickered, you now remember, the fog was full of lost birds landing at random, walking back to the rim across the vents. She keeps the lamp for them.

She keeps the crusher's intake clear of nests, gently, moving the half-built cups a metre to the side, because a jammed crusher trips the circuit and a tripped circuit stops the ropeway. She oils the great wheel at the terminal once a week. She does not care about the sulphur. It goes down into the cloud to no one, and when you mime the question at her she shrugs a whole-body shrug that includes the mountain. She does care that the buckets keep moving.

At night she sleeps on the terrace with everyone else. You see her there on the ninth night, lying on the warmest stretch of sinter below the turbine hall. Herons are tucked against her on both sides, a sulphur-lizard is flattened on her shin, and three metres away the fox-thing is curled with its thick-soled paws folded under it. Its eyes are open and on her, but only the way anyone's eyes are open when they are too warm to bother moving.

## IV. The fox, the hawk, and the arithmetic

You had thought of the predators as the colony's enemies. The longer you watch, the more they look like part of its regulation, the way the temperature of a pool decides which colour of microbe can live in it.

The raptor takes the birds that stray to the colony's edges, and it takes more of them in the years when the colony grows too large for its pools. You work this out from the old woman, who scratches tally marks on the turbine hall wall. There are columns of them going back further than you can easily count: marks for nests, marks for strikes, and a symbol that might be a pool going cold. Her records show the raptors thickening in the sky whenever the herons outgrow their food, and thinning again when the colony crashes. She has been keeping the mountain's accounts.

The fox-thing is the colony's night mind. It works the rows of nests along the rim, and each morning there are gaps. But the gaps are not random. It takes chicks from nests on the cold outer face of the rim, the ones built by young pairs who have not yet claimed a spot near the steam. Those chicks would have died in the first hard frost anyway. The fox is taking them a few weeks early. It is lean and does not waste anything, and its den is under the slumped part of the rim, in a crack that exhales warmth. Its kits are raised in a nursery heated by the same interior fire that warms every egg on the mountain.

Everything here eats the heat eventually. The predators just eat it once removed.

## V. What actually lives here

By the second week the question has changed shape in your head, and you cannot get it back into its old form.

You came asking who lives in the kiln, and you have counted a great many answers. There are microbes in bands of orange and green, and brine flies drifting like smoke. There are sulphur lizards flickering into cracks. There are half a million silent herons, one raptor riding the thermals and one fox working the rim at dusk. There is an old woman with a heron's gestures, sleeping between the generator and the wall.

But lie on the terrace at night among the others and feel the ground. There is a rhythm in it. Part of it is the turbine's hum, and part is something slower and deeper. You start to think the proper answer is singular.

Something lives here whose body is twelve kilometres across. Its heart is below, a chamber of molten rock you will never see, beating once every few thousand years. Its veins are the fissures that carry hot water up and the old iron pipe that carries steam. Its skin is the sinter, laid down a millimetre a century, scalloped and white. Its breath rises from a thousand vents and falls back each afternoon as fog. It eats nothing and it spends constantly, and what it spends becomes the green mats and the pink shrimp and the grey birds.

The herons are how it moves. They are the part of it that can walk, fly and choose, pieces of the mountain's heat made mobile and sent out as far as the clouds. The dances along the terraces are the closest it comes to thinking, a slow circulation of information from bird to bird, each bow and wing-flash passing on news of which pool is warming and which vent is failing and where the raptor struck. The young riding the ropeway down into the white are how it learns about the world below, and the green leaves they carry back are what it has learned.

The machine was built to rob it. Some company drilled a well to steal its heat and ran a cable to carry away its yellow crystals. And the creature did what large, patient things do with an intrusion: it grew around it. The pipe became a nursery. The pylons became cliffs. The crushing shed became a roost, and the ropeway became a school. The lamp became a beacon in the fog. The boot sole fused into the sinter by the well is the same event on a smaller scale. The ground tried to take in something foreign and got halfway through.

And the old woman is the part of it that remembers what the machines are for. You could call her a keeper, a last worker, a hermit. But you watched her standing in the pool among the others, bent at their angle, her hands in the water, and you think the kiln has taken her in the way it took the pipe. It has grown around her and given her a use. She has become the one cell in the body that knows how to change a bulb.

## VI. After

When you leave, you ride the ropeway.

It is the only way down that doesn't involve two days of scrambling on talus, and she mimes it to you with obvious amusement. You fold yourself into an empty returning bucket at the terminal, knees to chin, and the iron wheel turns you out over the edge.

For a minute the whole caldera lies below and behind you: the pale crust of the colony, the white terraces, the steam threads standing straight up in the morning, the small rusted hall with its orange lamp. You look for her and cannot find her among the grey shapes in the pools.

Then the cloud comes up and closes over you. It is white, wet, and total. The only sounds are the creak of the cable and your own breath, which you can hear again as the air thickens. Something flutters at the rim of the bucket. A young heron has stepped in behind you from the platform and is perched on the lip, neck extended, looking down into the blind white.

It looks at you once. Then it lifts its forearm, slowly, and turns it outward, palm up.

It tips forward into the cloud and is gone. You are left descending alone through the white toward a mountain of sulphur growing somewhere in the rain. Above you, the thing that lives in the kiln goes on breathing.
