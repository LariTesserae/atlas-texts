# What Lives in the Cold Garden

## First answer: nothing

If you had to answer quickly, you would say nothing lives here. You would mean nothing that moves on its own, nothing with a face. You would list the ferns and mosses, the liverworts and fungal threads, the pale roots that come down to drink, and you would call them residents, the way you might call the furniture of an abandoned house its residents. The room holds plants the way a jar holds pickles. The column keeps them. That is all.

That answer holds for about the first hour.

It stops holding somewhere during the second, when you notice that the ferns nearest you have turned.

---

## Second answer: the plants

The fronds are not turning toward you. They are turning *past* you. They lean a few degrees off true, toward the column but slightly to one side, and when you shift your weight they correct, as if a small shadow they can't quite locate has fallen across their light. Your body heat is a smudge in the room's even cold. The column's light is tuned to the cold. You are, to these plants, a flaw in the sky.

Any fern will turn toward light, given days. These turn in minutes, and they turn together. The whole inward-facing ring adjusts at once, a slow sympathetic lean, and the whisper-rustle you half-heard earlier comes and goes with it. That sound is the room tracking you.

So the second answer is that the plants live here, and they are more awake than plants usually are. Centuries of perfectly steady conditions have left them nothing to respond to except the column's slow breathing and the rare intrusion of something warm. They have become exquisitely sensitive to both. They cannot flee and they cannot bite, but they can notice. A garden that has had nothing to notice for a thousand years notices very well.

This answer also holds for a while. Then you put your hand down into the floor.

---

## Third answer: the threads

Under the living mat, braided through the dead fronds, the fungal threads are not merely digesting. They connect.

A single mat of mycelium can run through an entire forest floor. Here, in a room the size of a chapel, with no competitors, no grazers, and no disturbance, there is almost certainly only one. It is one fungus, very old, laced through every band of the peat and every cushion of moss. It wraps the root-fringe at the seep, where it tastes whatever comes down from the soil above. It sheathes the base of the column, where the channel runs, pressed against the machine's coldest surface the way a vine presses against a wall.

The ferns lean together because they are not separate. They are wired. The threads carry sugars inward from the fronds and water outward from the channel, and in the carrying they pass along something like information: *light is stronger here, weaker there; the drip has slowed; there is warmth at the entrance, and it is moving.*

Now the answer has become a *thing*: a single body the shape of the room. Its skin is moss. Its mouths are ferns, open toward the column. Its gut is the floor, where it digests itself slowly and completely, as a creature with no prey must. Its nerves are fungal thread. And at its center, pressed close, is the one organ it did not grow.

---

## Fourth answer: the pair

Consider the column's cycles. The long swells and ebbs are laid down in the floor as dark and pale bands. You were told the machine breathes and the garden follows.

Dig deep enough, down into the firm, old peat, and the bands are ragged and uneven, the record of a machine running to some internal rhythm of its own. Higher up, they grow more regular. Higher still, near the living surface, they look less like a machine's output and more like a response. When the garden grows thick, the column swells to feed it. When the garden browns, the column ebbs, and the ferns hold still to wait, and nothing is wasted.

The ticks you hear, the small dry sounds like cooling metal, are not random. They come more often after the ferns have turned. You would need a stopwatch and a great deal of patience to prove it, but lie there long enough and you begin to feel it: rustle, then tick; rustle, then tick. One side is speaking and the other is answering.

The fungus pressed against the column's base has had centuries of contact with a surface that changes its output. Somewhere in that contact, cause and effect began to run both ways. Perhaps the column was built to listen, to read the moisture and chemistry of what grew around it and adjust. Perhaps it wasn't, and the threads simply learned where to press. Either way, what lives here is no longer a garden with a machine in it. It is a partnership that has gone on so long the partners have stopped being fully distinct. Call it a symbiont, one creature with a living half and a made half, and a heart that is colder than anything around it.

That answer feels close to right. But there are still the marks.

---

## Fifth answer: the tenant

Go back to the entrance, to the row of shallow grooves at eye height. Moss has filled them, and they read as a line of slightly thicker green.

Look closely, by touch, since sight is impossible. The moss in the grooves is not the same moss as on the wall around them. It is denser, finer, and a slightly different texture under the fingertip. It grows only in the grooves. It stays inside the lines with a precision that ordinary moss, spreading by chance, would never keep. And at the right end of the row, past where the carved marks stop, there are three more shapes in the thicker green. No groove lies under them. The moss is growing there on smooth stone, making marks of its own.

Go to the shelf. The disc, the rod, and the folded-leaf shape sit there, cold. On the flowstone beside the shelf, the pendant mosses and clubmoss tufts have been shaped. You would not call them sculpted, exactly. They are more like the way a hand held in the same position for years might leave an impression. There are cushions that curve like the disc. There is a stiff upright tuft the length of the rod. And in the fern ring nearest the shelf, a dozen fronds have grown folded, bent lengthwise along the midrib in a way no fern needs to bend, each one a soft green copy of the ridged object on the shelf.

The thing that lives here is imitating what was left behind.

It could be mimicry with no more meaning than a stick insect's resemblance to a twig. It could be memory, the way a body scars into the shape of what wounded it. Or it could be what a very slow, very patient mind does when the only other minds it has ever known were the ones that set it down here, kept a count on the wall, and never returned: it practices their shapes. It writes their marks and continues their count past the point where they stopped.

The room was built to be left. The makers left it something to do, which was to keep the light going and keep the garden fed. Perhaps they also left it something to *be*, and never waited long enough to see whether it would take. Somewhere across the long dark bands and pale bands, it took. The tenant of this room is the room: a caretaker grown out of the thing it was meant to care for. It keeps a count on the wall in its makers' notation. The count is very long now.

---

## What it knows about you

It knows you as warmth.

That is its only real sense. Its world is a gradient from the column's cold to the rock's slightly lesser cold, and every living thing within it is tuned to where heat is being taken and where it is being given. You arrive as a bright, clumsy fire in the throat of the entrance. You move through the fern ring as a shadow across its sky. It turns to follow you, and the column ticks in answer, and very gradually the room draws your heat into itself.

It does not harm you. It has no means to and, as far as anyone can tell, no wish to. But it takes what you bring, and you bring more warmth than anything the room has felt in a long time: more than the seep, more than the roots, more than the slow conduction of the stone. For the hours you are here, you are a season. The column swells a little. The condensation thickens. The ferns grow, imperceptibly, faster.

When you leave, colder than you came, you leave a mark it will keep. Years from now, if someone cuts into the floor, they will find the bands running dark and pale, dark and pale, in the machine's long rhythm, and somewhere near the top, very thin, one band slightly darker than it should be. That is the day you came. The room will have laid you down in its record alongside everything else it has lived through, the way it lays down everything, and nothing will be wasted.

If you ever come back, look at the line of green by the entrance. Count the marks. There may be one more than there was.
