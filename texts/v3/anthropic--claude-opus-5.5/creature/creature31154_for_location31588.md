# Those Who Live in the Loud Country

## First, the Easy Answer

It would be easy to say that no one lives here. The works are staffed, the railway is run, the stakes are tended, and most of the people who do this come up through the pass on a two-year contract and go back down with ringing ears and a small pension. They are sojourners. They sleep in bunkhouses behind the turbine halls, in iron cots bolted to stone floors, and they count the weeks. When they leave, the moss closes over their footprints within the hour, as it closes over everyone's.

It would be easy to say that the hare lives here, and the pipit, and the two ravens at the ice. That is true as far as it goes.

Stay a season, though, and you begin to notice certain people at the works who do not count the weeks. They do not flinch at the roar. They do not hold their goggles the way visitors do, like something borrowed. Their hands move before they think about it. When a visitor shouts at one of them, as visitors always do in the first days despite the card, the plateau-born one tilts their head with a faint, puzzled courtesy, the way you might regard someone speaking earnestly to you underwater.

These are the people who live here. There have never been many of them.

## The Plateau-Born

They number perhaps three hundred, counting everyone alive who was born above the pass and raised within sound of the Falls. Their families have been here four generations, five in a few cases, since the first turbine hall was raised. They are linemen and switch-tenders, trestle-jackers and penstock divers, and they are the only people who stay.

You know them first by their faces. The light has worked on them since infancy, and their cheeks carry a permanent burnish, a red-brown like the painted roofs. The skin at the corners of their eyes is creased white where the goggle-edges sit, so that with the goggles off they look faintly masked in pale. They squint even in the dark months, from habit. Their eyes are not a different color from anyone else's, but they rest differently. They do not try to fix on a single glitter out on the mat. They look *through* the brilliance, unfocused, and read the land by its larger shapes, the way a sailor reads water.

You know them by how they walk. Visitors stride and sink, and learn to curse the pale-green patches that take them to the knee in ice water. The plateau-born have never walked any other way. They step short and flat-footed, weight spread, and they read color as other people read paving. Red holds you. Gold mostly holds you. The green that seems lit from inside is water with a skin on it, and children are taught this before they are taught numbers. There is a nursery game in which one child signs a color and the other must jump to it. It looks like play. It is also how the young learn not to drown.

Most of all you know them by their hands.

## The Speech of Flow

The sign language of the works was invented by adults for use at the rim: flat, fast, full of practical words for pressure and weather and danger. For the plateau-born it is not a workplace code. It is the first language they have, the one they dream in, and in their hands it has grown into something else.

It has a grammar of movement. The visitor's card lists *water* as a single sign, a cupped palm tipping. Among the plateau-born there are forty kinds of water. There is sheet-water, the flat hand tilted with the fingers rippling. There is seep-water, the fingers trickling between the knuckles of the other hand. There is spray, a quick opening of both fists upward. There is the water inside the moss, the palm pressed slowly down and released, like a thumb pressing a sponge. Verbs of motion bend themselves to what is moving. A cart does not go the way a person goes, and a person does not go the way news goes. You can tell from how someone signs *she left* whether she walked, rode the train, or died.

They have a sign for silence, and it is not the one on the visitor's card. The card's sign for *quiet* is a finger to the lips, which the plateau-born find faintly comic, a sign for a thing that happens to mouths. Their own sign is the palm laid flat against the breastbone and held perfectly still. It means *silence*. It also means *the turbine has stopped*. That is the gravest alarm on the plateau, the one that brings every hand in the hall running. Among the old ones it means a third thing too, which is *dead*. No one explains this to visitors. It doesn't need explaining.

Names are signs. Every plateau-born child is given a name in the hands, usually by the grandmother, usually for something she noticed in the first week of the child's life. There is a man called *Leaning-Pylon*. There is a woman called *Rainbow-Held-Still*, and a boy called *Hare-Guessed-At*, because he was born in the dark months and no one saw him properly for days. The names on their papers at the pass are ones the valley clerks wrote down, and the plateau-born answer to them with the patience of people answering to a nickname they didn't choose.

## How They Hear

The roar did not make them deaf, the way it makes the contract workers deaf. It made them listen somewhere else.

A plateau-born mother cannot sing to her baby. She presses the child against her sternum and hums, low, below the roar, so that the child feels the song through bone. Every plateau-born adult remembers this without remembering it: a warmth in the chest that was their mother's voice. As children they lie flat on the moss with their ears pressed to it and feel the seep moving under them. They learn early that the ground has a pulse.

So they hear the Falls not as noise but as a body of information. A switch-tender at the rim, born up here, will stop in the middle of a shift, go still, and then walk to a gauge that hasn't moved yet. A minute later it moves. She felt the change in pitch through the soles of her boots: a single outlet two kilometers east choking with slush, the bass note of the whole rim shifting by a hair. The contract workers call this a gift, or a trick, and are slightly afraid of it. The plateau-born don't consider it anything. It is simply what listening is.

What they fear is the valley.

Every plateau-born person goes down the pass at some point. They go for schooling, or for a doctor, or for a wedding among valley kin. What they find at the bottom is almost unbearable. The quiet there has a pressure of its own. In the valley town, in the first night, they lie in a borrowed bed and hear for the first time in their lives the sound of their own blood, a rushing in the ears they never knew was there, because the Falls had always covered it. They hear their own breathing. They hear, horribly, the thoughts in their heads as if spoken aloud. Some cannot sleep at all. Some come back up on the next train, gray-faced, and are met at the pass by people who don't ask why.

The ones who stay down longer report something stranger. After a week or two in the valley they begin to feel lonely in a way they can't name, as if a large animal that had always slept beside them had quietly gotten up and left.

## The Dead in the Moss

There is no soil on the plateau. You cannot dig a grave in two meters of wet sphagnum over polished rock. For the first years of the works, the dead were taken down the pass on the train like freight. The plateau-born stopped doing that three generations ago, and they do not talk about the change with outsiders.

This is what they do now. The body is washed in melt and wrapped in undyed wool. It is carried out into the middle of the plateau, far from the rim, where the roar is only a hum and speech is almost possible, though no one speaks. It is laid on its back on the red moss, the firm moss, with the face uncovered to the enormous light. The mourners stand in a ring and hold the silence sign against their chests, palm flat and still, for as long as they can bear it. Then they walk away and do not come back for a year.

The moss takes the body. It does not do this quickly. Sphagnum grows perhaps a finger's width in a good year, and in the first seasons you can still see the shape under the green, a long low mound among the tussocks. In ten years it is a rise in the mat. In fifty it is gone, sunk below the living surface into the dark, cold, acid water below. It does not rot there. The moss keeps what it holds, as it keeps the snowmelt. Down in the bottom of the mat, a meter and more below the tussocks where the hares graze, the plateau-born dead lie whole. Their skin is tanned bronze, their hair gone red, their faces peaceful and intact. They are held in the cold like pressed flowers.

This is the second meaning of the first rule. *Do not cut the moss.* The contract workers are told it is about the reservoir and the turbines, and that is true. Among the plateau-born, a peat-cutter's spade in the mat would be something else entirely. It would be a desecration of their grandparents.

There are older bodies too. They lie in the southern moss, near the rim, where the mat is thickest and oldest. No plateau-born person put them there. Once, generations ago, a trestle-jacker driving a timber pile struck something that wasn't rock. What came up on the end of the pile was a hand, small and brown, with a ring of twisted gold wire on one finger. The trestle-jackers put it back. They moved the pile a meter over. They built the collapsed cairn a little more carefully that season, stone on stone, and did not report it. Someone lived here before the works, when the ice had only just gone. The moss has them, too. No one knows their language or what they called the Falls. The plateau-born sign for them is the silence sign made with both hands at once, one pressed on top of the other.

## The Moss Itself

Stay long enough and the question of who lives here turns inside out.

The plateau-born will tell you, if they trust you, that they don't think of the moss as a plant. Or not as *plants*, plural. They have a sign for it that doesn't appear on any card: both hands spread flat, palms down, fingers interlaced, slowly lifting and lowering together, as if breathing. It is a single thing. It is one enormous, patient, low organism three thousand years old and thirty kilometers wide. It drinks the whole sky and lets the water out slowly, all year. It keeps the dead. It is creeping, finger's-width by finger's-width, north toward the ice.

In this view, everything else on the plateau is a guest of the moss. The hare lives in it. The pipit nests in it. The rails and pylons float on it, tilting slowly as it grows. The turbines run on its patience; without it, the Falls would be a spring flood and an autumn trickle, and the works would starve half the year. Even the roar is the moss's voice, in a sense. It is the sound of a sponge the size of a province being slowly, endlessly wrung out over the edge of the world.

The linemen, checking the lean of a pylon in spring with plumb and level, do not say *the pylon has tilted*. They sign that *the moss has shrugged*.

## The Keeper of the Stakes

If you want a single person, the one who lives here more than anyone, go north.

The ice station is the farthest point from the Falls on the whole plateau. Up here, forty kilometers from the rim, the roar is at its lowest, a hum at the edge of hearing, like wind in the next valley. It is the quietest place a plateau-born person can stand without going down the pass. The ice cap fills the horizon, white wall and blue base. Now and then it makes the only other large sound in the whole country: a crack, sharp as a rifle, and a long groaning settle as some deep part of it shifts. The ravens lift at the sound and come down again.

The keeper is a woman in late middle age. Her sign-name is the flat tilted hand with the fingers rippling: sheet-water. Her grandmother gave it to her, watching the melt run across the rock in her first week of life. Her papers at the pass say something else, which she has not used in thirty years.

She lives alone in the stone hut with the weather mast and the rain gauge. She reads the instruments morning and evening and writes the figures in a ledger that goes down on the train each month. Her real work, the work she would say she was born for if anyone asked in a language she cared to answer in, is the stakes.

Once a year, in late summer, when the ice front stands at its farthest retreat, she walks out to the new edge carrying an iron stake, a brass tag stamped with the year, and a sledgehammer. She finds the line where the ice meets the rock. She drives the stake into a crack in the new stone, which is dark and grooved and shining and has never been touched by anything alive. Then she turns around and walks south along the line of stakes, all the way back, three kilometers, clearing the tags as she goes.

The walk takes her the whole of a day. The first stakes she passes stand on naked rock. Then come stakes with a green film in the cracks around their bases. Then stakes in a thin carpet, then stakes in moss to the ankle, the shin, the knee. Near the southern end of the line the stakes are her grandmother's, and her great-grandmother's, and they are buried to their tops. She kneels and pulls back the living moss with her fingers to find the brass and wipe it clean, and reads each date as she might read a gravestone. In a sense it is one. Every stake is a year in which someone stood here and marked where the ice was, and now the moss has walked over it.

She knows something the ledgers do not record. When she walks out to the new edge each year, she walks from the moss onto the bare rock. The moss comes with her. It rides in the treads of her boots, in the cuffs of her trousers, in the wool of her gloves, as fragments too small to see, and spores. When she kneels on the virgin stone to drive the stake, some of it falls. She has never tried to stop this. The film of green that shows in the cracks the next year, around the base of the stake she drove, is hers in a way. The moss would get there anyway, on the wind, in time. But it gets there a little sooner because she walked it there.

She has come to think that this is what the people are for. The moss is patient and green and slow, and it cannot hurry. So it grew, or was given, or simply found, a few hundred quick red-cheeked creatures with clever hands to carry it the last few meters. It lets them live in it. It keeps their dead. In return they walk it north.

She would not say this to anyone from the valley. She is not sure she would say it to anyone at all.

## What Lives Here

In early summer, when the pipit rises from the tussocks near the station and hangs in the brilliant air with its beak open and its throat working, singing its whole song into the hum and not a note of it reaching her, she puts down whatever she is carrying. She lifts her hands and answers it.

She signs *I hear you*. Palm to the chest, still. Then open, lifting.

The bird doesn't understand, of course. The ravens, watching from the rock, might. They have watched her hands for twenty years.

So this is who lives in the Loud Country. There are the hare, rarely seen, and the silent-singing pipit, and the ravens at the ice. There are the few hundred people who speak with their hands and hear through their feet, who cannot bear the valley's quiet, and who lay their dead on the red moss under the sky and let it take them down into the cold. There are the older dead beneath them, with their wire rings and their forgotten words. And under and around all of it, holding all of it, there is the one great slow green thing. It breathes the sky in and wrings it out over the edge, and it is walking north toward the ice, one boot-tread of green at a time, carried by the only creatures in the country quick enough to hurry.
