# Who Lives in the Exhale

## The First Answer

The easy answer is the swifts, and it is not wrong.

Take one of them. She is eleven years old, which is old for her kind, and she has touched solid ground perhaps a few hundred times in her life. Every one of those times was here, in the same slot in the fourth course of brick above the east doorway, where the mortar washed out in a shape like a thumbprint. She eats in the air, drinks in the air, mates in the air. On summer nights she climbs in spirals until the plain is a grey sheet under starlight and sleeps up there in snatches of a second or two, one half of her brain at a time, drifting on the high cold currents. If you added up her flying it would reach the moon and come back, and most of it was over the plain, which she has crossed ten thousand times without ever landing on it. To her the plain is not a place. It is a gap between the sky and the slot.

She knows the green country. She has never set foot in it and never will, but she has eaten it all her life. She knows it as a taste that arrives on warm fronts from the south: aphids fat with sap from leaves she will never see, winged ants lifted out of soil she cannot imagine, spiderlings that let out silk threads from some meadow and rose on the wind until the wind gave them to her. She strains the sky with her open mouth, packs what she catches into a pellet in her throat, and carries it forty miles to the thumbprint slot. There she gives it to two chicks who have never seen anything but brick and the ragged square of light overhead.

So she lives here. So do her neighbours, several thousand of them, and the bats in the shaft, and the beetles, and the rest of the census. You could stop there. Most would.

## The Second Answer

Spend long enough in the fan house, though, and the census stops adding up.

The swifts leave in autumn. One morning the walls are simply quiet, the slots empty, and the birds are somewhere unimaginably far away, sleeping on the wing over another continent. The bats go torpid in winter and hang in the shaft like dried fruit. Sometimes they wake and fly out on a warm night, mostly not. The beetles slow. The crickets go deeper. For months the fan house holds almost nothing that moves by its own will.

The fan still turns. The plate still hums. The mine still breathes out at night and in at noon. In the deep cold hours the lamp still comes up to its orange ember, even when no moths are crossing the plain and no bats are awake to hunt them. It burns for no one, and in spring everything comes back to it.

Once you notice that, you start to suspect that the swifts are not the inhabitant. They are tenants, like the bats and the beetles. Something else holds the lease.

## In the Casing

Start small, because it starts small.

Open the inspection hatch on the motor housing, if you can get the rusted dogs to turn, and hold a light inside. The windings are the colour of old blood. The brushes are worn to nubs of carbon, still pressed against the commutator by springs that have long since lost most of their temper. By any sensible reckoning this machine should have fused, shorted, or ground itself to powder decades ago. The air in the fan house is thick with dust: guano, feather scurf, the powdered shells of insects. Dust like that should have packed the commutator solid.

It hasn't, because something lives in there.

There is a population of beetles in the casing that does not look quite like the ones on the drifts. They are smaller and paler, with the faint band on their backs worn almost to nothing. They came in, presumably, after bat hair and feather sheath drawn into the housing on the draught, because hair and feather are what their kind eats. They stayed because the casing is the one place in the fan house that is faintly warm on cold nights, warmed by the windings when the dynamo runs. Their generations have been going in and out through the vent slots for longer than anyone has been counting. Along the way they became the machine's housekeepers without meaning anything by it. They eat the organic film off the copper. They carry the fibres away. Their constant small traffic keeps the gap between brush and commutator open, a hair's width of clean metal in a building made of dust.

A spider lives on the lamp cage. Always one, never two. When one dies, another takes the post within days, because the cage is the best hunting ground in fifty miles: the only place where moths come to you. Her web runs from the wire to the casing flange. It is so fine and so often rebuilt that it never gathers the grey dust of the corner webs. On lit nights it glows orange, a small halo around the small light. Very probably her web is part of why the bulb survives. It catches the moths before they batter the glass, and the few shocks and cracks that would end a filament kept at the edge of burning never come.

Neither of these is the inhabitant either. They are closer to it, though. They are what it uses for hands.

## The Third Answer

Here is what the fan house has, if you count it as one thing.

It has lungs: a mile of drifts and stopes in the old evaporite, connected through collapsed workings to other shafts miles off. They hold the deep ground's temperature, and every day they take one long breath in and one long breath out. The breath is the oldest thing about it that moves. It began the moment the first shaft broke the surface, and it will go on until the last one chokes shut.

It has a throat: the upcast shaft, brick for a hundred feet, then raw rock, furred with bats.

It has a voice. The swifts scream and the bats chitter, but under them is the fan's clack and the plate's hum, and that part of the voice never goes away. Listen to it through a whole day and it stops sounding like a broken machine. Before dawn it is fast and hard, like someone running. At noon it stutters, catches, and stops. Then comes a turn forward, the wrong way, like a word taken back. In the evening it slowly resumes. It is the only speech the place has that belongs to the place.

It has a heart, or something that does a heart's work: the ember in the wire cage. It is lit by the breath, beats faster when the breath comes harder, and draws the night's scarce food across the plain toward the hunters in the throat.

It has a gut, spread thin over every ledge and drift. There the beetles and crickets and mites take everything that falls, and turn it into more beetles and crickets and mites, and into the fine white lace of bone.

It has memory. The draught sorts that bone into windrows along the floor, and the windrows are not random. Each season lays down a line: chick bones in summer, bat bones in the hard end of winter, a thin band of moth scales wherever the lamp burned often. Dig a trench across the floor of the fan house and you would find decades pressed into the dust in layers, the way a lake keeps its years in mud. Nobody reads it. The place keeps it anyway, and it keeps it perfectly, because the air is too dry for anything to rot and forget.

It has eyes, and they are what gives it away. It sends them out every morning. Thousands of swifts pour through the broken roof and spread across forty miles of sky, and every evening they come back full of the world. A creature that could not move had to find some way to look at the horizon, and it found swifts. The bats are its night sight. The kestrels in the chimney stub are the sharp, cruel edge of its attention, which keeps the eyes quick. The owl that comes at dusk is its one visitor from outside, something it did not grow. It tolerates the owl the way a body tolerates a single foreign stone.

So what lives in the Exhale is the Exhale.

It is not a ghost and not a spirit. It has no mind in any sense a person would recognise, and it wants nothing. It is simply a living thing made from the leftovers of other living things and a dead machine, arranged around a breath. Each of its parts could die and be replaced, and every part does, every season. Swift by swift, beetle by beetle, spider by spider on the lamp cage, the whole of it changes. The shape stays, the way a flame stays while the wax goes, or a whirlpool stays while the water runs through.

## How It Was Born

It was not always here. There was a time, before the mine closed, when the fan was driven, and the breath of the ground was not the ground's own. A motor pulled the air out hard and fast, day and night, and the fan roared, and nothing could nest in that wind. The upcast carried the mine's poison: gas, dust, and the stale air of men working half a mile down. It was a wound in the plain, kept open by force.

Then the town left. The motor stopped. For a while the fan was just still, and the shaft just breathed, and nothing happened.

The first swift to find the eroded mortar would not have known what she was starting. She wanted a slot out of the wind with a clear drop beneath it for launching. The first bats wanted a shaft with a constant temperature. The first beetles wanted the corpses those two would leave behind. None of it was planned. But the fan began to turn backward on the ground's own exhale. One night, for the first time, the old motor sent a thread of current up the dead wiring and the filament flushed dull orange. From then on the thing was complete. Breath, voice, heart, gut, memory and eyes were all in place, and the pieces began to hold each other up.

The bats learned the lamp and fed the gut, and the gut fed the beetles. Some of the beetles went into the casing and kept the dynamo clean, so the lamp stayed lit. The swifts' spit and feathers packed the joints of the brick. That slowed the weathering, so the walls stood longer and held more swifts. None of them knows it is doing anything but living.

A body is only that: many things living, each for itself, in a shape that happens to keep them all alive.

## What It Knows

If it knows anything, it knows its edges.

It knows the exact line of the lip of the hollow, where the breath dies away into the plain's dry stillness and nothing lives. It knows the forty-mile radius of the swifts' reach, and the thinner, closer circle of the bats. It knows, in its guts and its bones, the rhythm of the seasons: a long fullness, a long emptying, a return. It knows the warm fronts from the south the way a person knows their own hunger.

It knows the stillness. Twice a day the pressure inside and out comes into balance and the fan stops. For a few minutes there is no clack and no hum, only the separate sound of individual wings. If the Exhale fears anything, it fears those minutes. Nothing in it can fear, so perhaps "fear" is the wrong word. Say instead that those minutes are the closest it comes to dying. The breath has stopped, the voice is gone, the heart would go dark. And every time, so far, the ground has changed its mind and the fan has twitched and turned again. The whole thing starts back up like someone waking with a gasp from a dream of drowning.

It will die one day, properly. A collapse far down the workings will seal the last connecting shaft, the breath will weaken and stop, the fan will seize. The last filament will go cold in its cage, and the swifts will come home one spring to a silent house and find it still good enough to nest in. They will not notice the difference. The bats might. The beetles in the casing will starve slowly on hair and dust and never know what they were keeping. The gut and the eyes and the memory will go on for a while, a body without its breath, until the brick finally goes to sand and the plain closes over the hollow as if nothing had ever lived there.

That hasn't happened yet.

## What It Does to You

Walk across the plain toward it and for a long time there is nothing at all: gravel pavement, varnished dark, and the bands of old mountains polished on the whalebacks.

Then, before you can see the hollow, before you can hear the screaming, a cool pressure rises against your face. It smells of salt and old iron. It is faint and dry as paper, and it is coming from below.

It does not know you are there. It has no way of knowing anything. Yet the first thing this place does to anyone who finds it is the same thing it does all night, every night, to the cold sky and the thin moths and its thousands of sleeping tenants. It breathes on them.

Stand at the lip long enough and you start to feel accounted for. You too have come from somewhere green beyond the horizon. You carry the world in your body, and if you stayed and died here you would end up as a fine white lace in the windrows, sorted by the draught into the year you came.
