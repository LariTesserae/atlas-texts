# Those Who Live in the Seam

## The Tenders

Start with the hands, because in the Seam the hands are where a person lives.

A bridge-tender's palms are ridged like the underside of a fern. Decades of running cords through their fingers in the dark have worn some places smooth and built others into hard little pads. Those pads are more sensitive than the rest of the skin, because they are where the reading happens. An old tender can close her hand around a railing-cord and know, without looking, how many seasons since it was spliced, whether the snail-thread is new or old, and whether the tension is right. She knows the tension the way you know the weight of your own arm. The fingertips go pale and slightly flattened. The nails are kept very short. At night, sleeping, the fingers still move a little, working knots no one else can see.

The people of the Seam are small. That isn't a rule, just what the place has made of them over the generations. Big bodies need more warmth, more food, and more room on a narrow stair. They walk with their weight low and forward, one hand always trailing along the wall or the rail. They seem to lean into the rock the way the black-barked trees do, shaped by the fog rather than the sun. Their skin is pale from the dimness and faintly grey. The grey isn't pigment. It is moth-dust, the fine scaling of a million wings, worked into the pores over a lifetime until it no longer washes out. A child is born pink and turns grey slowly, like a moss-bed going gold on top. The old ones shine very faintly where the dust is thickest, at the temples and the backs of the hands, the way a moth's wing shines.

Their eyes are large and dark in their faces, and they narrow them almost constantly: against the dusk flight, against the midday blade of sun, against the drip. When they are thinking, they close them entirely. It is not rude. Among people who speak with touch, closing the eyes means *I am listening with the rest of me.*

## Names

A name in the Seam is not a sound. It is a pattern pressed into the palm or the inside of the forearm.

When a child is born, its parents take its tiny hand and press into the centre of the palm some small arrangement of their own: two quick touches and a drawn line, or a circle traced and then a single firm press at its heart. The same pattern is knotted into a short cord and tied to the long bridge, among the other names. That is how a person is introduced: you take their hand and give them yourself.

Some of the names are old enough that the pattern has worn into the grammar of the place. The bridge-tender of the lower quarter carries a name that is three presses along the heel of the hand, then a slow drag of the thumb toward the wrist. It means nothing, the way names mean nothing. But the gesture is also how you test whether a splice will hold: three short pulls, then a long steady draw. So everyone who has ever touched her name has, without meaning to, asked a question about strength.

Call her Draw, for the thumb-stroke at the end. She is the oldest person in the Seam.

## Draw

She lives on the steeper wall, on the fourth tier, in a room her grandmother's grandmother cut. Its sleeping-niche is shaped around a body no one alive remembers. Her doorway curtain is woven grass gone brittle and brown. It rustles when the moths settle against it at dawn, the loudest sound in her day.

She remembers when the long bridge had three poles. She will tell you so if you take her hand: three presses, and then her fingers spread flat across your palm, which means *long ago* or *wide*, depending on context. In the Seam those are nearly the same word.

Every morning in the grey hour she walks the bridges of the lower quarter. She does not hurry. She lays her whole forearm along a railing-cord and closes her eyes and stays there, sometimes for as long as a hundred drips, feeling. The widening doesn't pull evenly. A bridge will go slack at one anchor and bowstring-tight at the other, and the wrongness travels along the cord like a held breath. When she finds it she loosens a lashing and lets the tension ease through her hands. Then she pays out a length of snail-thread from the coil at her hip, splices it in with a knot that has no name because it is simply *the* knot, the one every tender learns first, and ties a small record-knot beside it. The record-knot is a promise and a confession at once: *I let this bridge out. The walls moved. I followed them.*

The railings of the old bridges are furred with these knots, thousands of them, each one a season. The oldest stretches of cord are so dense with record that they are more knot than cord. They are stiff and lumpy, and running your hand along them feels like reading a spine.

## The Two Walls

There is a thing about the town that the stone doesn't tell you and the people won't unless you ask with your hands.

Families are split.

Not all of them, and not cruelly. But when the town was built it was one town, and people chose their rooms by the ordinary reasons: a better ledge, a cousin nearby, a shaft of midday sun. Over the generations the widening has taken those ordinary choices and made them into distances. A sister's room that was once a short hop across a three-pole bridge is now a crossing of seven poles, sagging, on a rope that must be tended daily. The crossing can still be made. But people don't make it lightly, and not at dusk, and not when the tremor is due.

So there are pairs in the Seam who live across from each other. A couple who were married on the long bridge, at its middle, forty years ago. A mother and a grown son. Two old friends who used to share a terrace of snail-beds on the gentler wall until one of them, for reasons neither would explain, moved across.

These pairs have a cord between them. Not a bridge, just a single line of snail-thread, strung doorway to doorway across the gap, too thin to walk and never meant to be. In the evening, before the flight, each of them sits in their doorway with the cord across their lap. One of them presses a pattern into it, a pull, a pull, a slow draw, and the other feels it arrive through twenty paces of dim air. They answer. This is how they say goodnight. It's how they say *the fire caught*, *my knee is bad today*, *I dreamt of you*. It is the most intimate speech in a place where all speech is intimate, and its grammar is so private that no one else can read it.

Every season these cords, too, have to be let out.

You begin to see that everything here is let out. The bridges, the cords, the marriages. The people aren't fighting the widening; you cannot fight a mountain. They are *following* it, a handspan at a time, keeping the reach intact as the distance grows. Their whole lives are an argument that a thing can come apart and still stay joined, if only someone is willing to tend the join every single day.

## The Children

There are four children in the Seam. There were five.

They have never heard a voice from farther than the length of their own arm. When they are small their mothers hum to them, mouth pressed against the crown of the head, so the sound goes into the skull and down the spine rather than into the air. A Seam child learns early that sound is a thing you feel in the bones of someone you are holding. It does not travel. It does not escape. It's kept.

They play with cords the way children elsewhere play with sticks. They tie their own names into things, which is not allowed, and are gently scolded with a squeeze of the wrist. They lie flat on the terraces during the midday blade, still as the lizards, and let the translucent little bodies run across their bellies. They collect snail-shells, the dull red ones, and line them along the ledges of empty rooms, whole abandoned tiers colonised by their small private orders.

And they lean over edges. Every child in the Seam, at some age, is caught lying on a bridge with their face over the side, looking down into the blue that never resolves. The adults pull them back and press into their palms the pattern that means *not yet*. It is an odd thing to say to a child about a drop. Not *never*. Not *danger*. *Not yet.*

## The Others

Understand that the people are not the only ones who live here. They may not even be the main ones.

The moths are the Seam's great body. By day their sheets on the walls are a kind of skin. Touch the stone and it moves, ripples, breathes. At dusk they rise in their silent millions and the rift is filled, wall to wall, with soft grey matter moving upward. There is no better way to put it than this: the Seam exhales. And it is a sewing motion, if you watch from the lip at the top. Each moth goes up through the fog, out over the high grass, and back down before dawn, over and over, a billion small stitches passed through the eye of the crack every night.

The spiders spin across the small gaps every dawn, and every dusk the flight tears through what they made, and every dawn they spin it again. They are never discouraged. They have been doing this for as long as the crack has been here.

The snails lay down their trails in the moss-beds. The slime hardens into glass-thread. The people gather it, twist it, and splice it into bridges. The newest crossings in the Seam shine faintly in the dimness because they are made from the patient leavings of a creature that has never once, in its whole life, moved fast.

And the moths' fur eats the sound, and the quiet forces the people's speech into cords, and the cords are strung across every gap, knotted and re-knotted, name after name after name.

Everything that lives here makes thread. Everything that lives here spans.

Once you see it you can't stop seeing it. The Seam isn't a place with a town in it and some animals besides. It's a single living practice. The mountain opens, and the life that has gathered in the opening responds, instantly and without being asked, by trying to close it. Not with stone, which would be useless, but with line, with lashing, with every soft binding material that bodies can make. Web and trail and fibre and knot. The crack widens a handspan a year, and a handspan a year the Seam is sewn.

It is losing. Of course it is losing. The town was built for hundreds and there are forty left. But it is losing slowly, and beautifully, and it has not stopped.

## The Owls

The owls are the exception, and the people know it.

They sit on the highest ledges, pale and heavy, faces turned downward. They are the only creatures that don't make thread and don't span. When the moths rise, the owls go against them: white shapes folding their wings and dropping into the rising grey, down past the lowest dwellings, past the last sheets of settled moths, past the bare black walls, into the blue, and out of sight.

They come back. Not always the same night. Sometimes days later, a white shape rising up out of the depths at dawn with the last of the moths, feathers damp and warm to the touch and smelling of wet iron.

The people don't hunt the owls or name them. Draw, when she was young, asked her grandmother what the owls went down for. Her grandmother took her hand and pressed a pattern into it that Draw had never felt before and has never felt since. It was not a word. It was the knot every tender learns first, *the* knot, the splice, pressed flat into the skin of a palm.

## Below

Here is where it becomes more than it seemed.

Cracks in stone are not like trenches. They don't have a floor. They are widest at the top, where the rock has been pulled apart the most, and they narrow as they go down, until at the very bottom, somewhere far below, the two walls still touch. The widening happens from above. Down there, at the root, the mountain has not yet opened. It is still whole.

The first builders knew this. That is why the oldest carvings face downward. That is why the figures lean over and look. They weren't watching the depths for a monster, or a god, or a treasure. They were watching the place where the seam was still sealed, where the two halves of the carved hand and the thing it reaches for were still, far below, one piece of stone.

The warm air comes from there.

Sometimes the tenders lower cords. It isn't a ritual exactly. It's more like a habit, the way you might put a hand on a sleeping person's back to feel them breathe. A long line of snail-thread paid out hand over hand into the blue until the coil runs out, then held a while, then drawn up. It comes back warm. It comes back damp. Sometimes there is a white feather in it. Sometimes a scrap of leaf from a plant that does not grow in the upper Seam: broad, pale, veined like the back of an old hand, still soft.

And once there was the knotted cord.

The story is told with the hands, so it's told differently every time. But the knots were real. Draw has touched them; they hang now on the long bridge, at the middle, among the oldest names. They are tied in the grammar of the most ancient railing-cords, the lumpy ones that no one living can fully read. But some of it can be read. The record-knot is there, the one every tender ties after a splice: *I let this out. The walls moved. I followed them.* It is the same knot, repeated, over and over, for the entire length of the cord.

Only it is tied backwards. Mirrored. As though from the other side.

## Who Lives There

So who lives in the Seam?

The tenders, yes. Draw with her ridged palms and her three-pole memory, laying her forearm on a cord at dawn and feeling for the wrongness. The pairs across the gap, pulling goodnight through twenty paces of dark. The four children with their faces over the edge, being told *not yet*.

The moths, the great grey breath of the place. The spiders, who have never once been discouraged. The snails, who make bridges out of patience. The lizards and the silent bats and the white owls falling against the current.

But there is another answer, and the people of the Seam have known it for as long as there have been people there. It is why the ones who remain have stayed, when the rest climbed out onto the cold slopes and went away to lives with voices in them.

Down at the root, where the walls still touch, someone is tending the other end.

No one has seen them. No one will say what they look like. They may be the first builders, who went down to live in the place they came to watch. They may be the ones who "left," who went down rather than up, into the warmth, following the owls. They may be something that was never people at all: the join itself, the part of the mountain that has not yet come apart, aware in some slow stone way that it is being pulled, and answering the only way anything in the Seam knows how, with knots.

It doesn't matter. What matters is that the seam is being sewn from both sides.

Up here, the tenders let the bridges out a handspan every year and splice the gap with snail-thread. Down there, where the black rock is still one piece, something ties the same knot in mirror. The first builders carved a hand reaching across the gap. They carved it before the gap was there, on a single wall, and they must have known the stone would open and carry the two halves apart. Perhaps that was the point. You carve the reaching first. Then you let the world pull it wide, and you spend every generation after that keeping the reach alive across whatever distance opens.

## Dusk

The fog comes. It pours over the lip of the rift and sinks, cool and heavy, and the drip begins.

Draw sits in her doorway on the fourth tier with a single cord across her lap. It doesn't go across the gap. It goes down. She lowered it this morning, paid out the whole coil into the blue, and tied it to the peg beside her curtain. Now she sits with her hand resting on it, lightly, the way you rest a hand on a sleeping back.

The moths rise. The air fills wall to wall with soft grey wings, brushing her eyelids and her lips, carrying the whole quiet of the world upward in a slow spiral. An owl drops past her, white against the rising, wings folded, and is gone.

The quiet becomes total. She cannot hear her own breath. She closes her eyes, which means *I am listening with the rest of me.*

Down the cord, from very far below, from the warm place where the mountain is still whole, comes a touch.

Three short pulls. Then a long, slow draw.

Her name. Or the question every tender asks of a splice: *Will it hold?*

She takes the cord between her ridged fingers and answers the only way anyone in the Seam has ever answered: the first knot, *the* knot, pressed into the line and sent back down into the dark.

*Yes. For now. I'll let it out tomorrow.*

The two walls lean, very slightly, farther apart. And on both sides of the widening, in the warm dark and the grey, something keeps on sewing.
