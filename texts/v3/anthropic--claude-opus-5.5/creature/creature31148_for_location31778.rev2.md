# Who Lives in the Rising Country

Start with the people. They are the easiest to see, and they would be the first to tell you they are not the whole answer.

---

## I. The Steadings

There are perhaps four hundred of them, and they do not live together. The nearest neighbours are a half-day's walk apart along the high terraces, where the rivers have finished cutting and the ground, though still rising, rises evenly enough that a wall stays a wall. They settled here in the grandparents' generation, or the one before. Some came with the project and stayed after it stopped sending anyone. Others came later, from the fishing coasts to the east, when word went round that there was new land in the north and nobody owned it because it had not existed long enough to be owned.

To a visitor they look ordinary: broad in the shoulder from cutting peat and hauling boats over gravel, wind-cured in the face, bundled in so many layers of wool and sealskin that it is hard to say where one person ends. Rime collects in their eyebrows and on the fringe of fur around their hoods, and they stop knocking it off after the first winter. In the dark months you learn to recognise them first by gait and second by the particular way each of them holds a lamp.

They speak loudly and briefly, and a visitor takes this for rudeness until they have spent a night outdoors. What the people mostly speak in is not words at all. They have a hand-language, never formally invented, that grew up the way a path does. It is broad and emphatic, made with the whole forearm so it can be read at forty metres in the pewter noon or by keel-light. There are signs for *crack coming*, *birds up*, *river's changed its voice*, *keel singing*, *lift*, and *go high, now*. There are a dozen signs for kinds of ice. There is a sign for the eleven-second pulse: a flat palm pressed twice against the breastbone. It means *listen*, and also *wait*, and also, between people who love each other, something closer to *I know you're there*.

The hand-language is spreading south faster than the people are. Fishermen who have traded at the harbour use it on their own boats now.

---

## II. How They Keep Time

Nobody here keeps time by the calendar, because the calendar tells you nothing useful. They keep it by the ridges.

Every family knows its ridge. When a child is born, the newest beach ridge that season, still wet and loud with shingle, becomes that child's shore. *My shore is the one with the whale bones in it. My shore is the one the third harbour was built on.* As the child grows, the shore climbs away from the sea behind them. By the time they are old, their birth-ridge is far inland under alder, and walking out to stand on it is a long walk uphill through thickets. People do this on the important days of their lives: before marrying, before leaving, when they are dying and can still be carried. They go and stand on the place where the sea was when they began.

Ask someone their age and they may tell you in metres.

They bury their dead in the newest ridge. That is the custom, and there is no written reason for it. The body goes into the fresh cobbles just above the tide-line, with a flat stone on top scratched with a sign. Then the land takes it up. A grave that was at the water's edge at a funeral is a hand's width higher by the next new moon, and in ten years it is a small green hummock in the crowberry. Every grave in the country is climbing. The oldest ones are on the high terraces where the steadings are, which means the dead eventually arrive home, beneath the house walls, under the moss roofs, among the living.

People say of someone who has died: *they've gone down to the shore*. Of the very old dead, whose graves are now inland and above them: *they've come up*.

---

## III. The Crowd

Having said all that about the people, it should be said that they are a small part of the crowd.

Nothing in the Rising Country lives alone. This is the first thing a visitor feels, before the cold or the noise: the pressure of company. A vole runs under the moss every few paces, and a stoat is somewhere after it. The geese are so many that people count them in terraces rather than birds. A fox trots past a door at noon and does not bother to look at the person in it. The reindeer come down into the steadings in the worst weeks and stand in the lee of the houses, breathing steam against the walls, and nobody drives them off. Seals stranded far inland by a river's change of course come barking up to the fish-racks.

The people have not domesticated much of this. They have simply become one more crowding species, fitted into the gaps. They take eggs from the cliffs every summer, and because the cliffs keep moving they must relearn the ledges every few years. Old egg-routes are marked by bleached ropes left hanging on what are now inland crags, a hundred metres from any water, green with guano turf. They fish the salmon runs wherever the runs happen to be that year. They cut peat that should not exist, black and fibrous and deeper than a person is tall, and burn it in low hearths, and the smoke tastes faintly sweet.

The people give the densest places a wide berth. They do not build in the circles around the keels. But everyone goes there. In winter, the circles are where the forage is, where the snow melts first, and where the air is a few degrees kinder. If a reindeer is lost, you look for it in the nearest keel-circle. If a child is missing, you look there too.

## IV. The Children

This is where the answer begins to change.

The children born here sleep through katabatic gales. They sleep through the colony-roar and through the boulders knocking in the river. On nights when adults lie awake with wadding in their ears and the whole house thrumming, the children are loose-limbed and heavy, breathing slow.

They wake when the pulse skips.

Parents learn to notice it. The keels are steady, but not perfectly so. Now and then the eleven-second beat stretches to twelve, or comes early, or doubles. Adults never feel this, but every child in the steading sits up at once. Sometimes, a few seconds before the next pulse arrives, a very small child will press a palm flat against their own chest without being taught, the way you might tap along to a song you know.

They hum. It's a low, tuneless, patient humming at the edge of hearing, on a rhythm that parents eventually stop pretending not to recognise.

They know the keel-lights the way other children know the faces of their family. Blindfolded and turned around, a lift-born child of six will point to the nearest keel, then to the second-nearest, and name them. The children give the keels names. The adults refer to them by the old project numbers, painted long ago on steel plates that have since been lifted out of reach, or by the name of the nearest creek. The children's names are not like either. They call one keel *Slow Brother*, another *The One Who Leans*, and the one on the sea cliff, ringed in gannets, *Loud*. When asked why, the children say, patiently, that this is what the keels are like.

They play a game on the terraces. When the ground is about to crack, they feel it before the adults do; they claim it itches in the feet. They run to where the line will open and stand astride it, one foot on each side, laughing. The crack opens with a rifle-shot beneath them and water wells up between their boots. The one who judged it closest wins. Adults forbid this and the children do it anyway. In living memory no child has been hurt by it. Adults find this more unsettling than if children had been.

When lift-born children visit the south, to the fishing towns and the old stable coasts, they cannot sleep. They say the ground is *wrong*, and when pressed they say it is *dead*. Silence to them feels like the twenty minutes before a flood, when the birds have gone and the river has not yet changed its voice. They lie awake waiting for something that doesn't come. Most of them come home early. Some of them come home and never leave the basin again.

Their parents are proud of them and a little afraid of them. At night, in the low houses, a certain question gets asked quietly, in the hand-language, so the children won't hear it: *what are they being made for?*

## V. What the Engineers Called Heat

The old documents say the keels give off heat, and that this heat is why the ground around them grows so thick. The people here say that is true in the way it is true to say a fire gives off warmth. It leaves out most of what matters.

Cut a block of peat near a keel and hold it up to the violet light. It is shot through with threads. They are pale, finer than hair, branching, and faintly warm to the touch after the peat around them has gone cold. They are not roots, because they belong to no plant, and they are not fungus in any form anyone recognises. Where they are dense, the moss above grows in patterns: long curving lines and whorls, like the grain in a thumbprint or the traces frost leaves on a window, all of them oriented toward the keel.

Farther from the keels the threads are sparser, but they don't stop. Peat-cutters on the high terraces find them in every bank. Old cutters say they are denser now than when they were young. They say the threads are beginning to join up. Between keel-circles, through the black peat under the heath and the willow, there are faint pale cords now, running kilometre on kilometre from one bruise of growth to the next.

And the threads carry the pulse. Lay a hand on a cut face of peat and you can feel it, the same eleven-second beat, arriving a fraction later the farther you are from the nearest keel, like a wave going outward.

This is why the peat is metres deep where it should be centimetres. Something is building it. The moss does not rot fast enough to account for it, and the threads are laying it down: packing, binding, thickening, making the new land into something with a body.

The people call the threads *the lean*, the way one says a house has a lean or a keel has a lean. It is the thing the land does toward the keels. They do not cut peat near the circles anymore. It feels, one old woman said, too much like cutting into somebody.

## VI. The Two Silences

The rules say the pulse has stopped only twice in living memory. The people who were alive for those times tell them differently depending on their age.

The first silence came forty-odd years ago, in the dead middle of the polar night. Every keel in the basin stopped at once. The violet points stayed lit, but the beat that everyone had felt in their sternum since birth simply wasn't there. People describe it the way you would describe a deafness: the noise was all still present, river, wind, birds, but it had come loose from something. The birds went up off the cliffs in the dark, every colony at once, and circled screaming for the full nine minutes. Then the pulse came back, and the next day the whole northern half of the basin lifted a full metre in a single afternoon. That is where the fourth harbour was lost.

The second silence was nine years ago. This is the one the adults do not like to talk about and the children talk about constantly.

Again the keels stopped, all of them. Again the birds went up. But this time, the children say, the pulse did not stop. It went on, very faint, out of rhythm, twelve seconds instead of eleven, and it came from everywhere at once rather than from the keels: from the ground, from the peat, from the pale threads under the moss. The children felt it, and many of the adults did not. The ones who did describe standing out on the terrace in the dark with their hands pressed flat against the rime-crust, feeling something slow and unpracticed and enormous trying to keep time on its own.

After eleven minutes the keels resumed, and the faint second pulse fell back in under theirs and could no longer be told apart.

The children born in the years since the second silence hum on twelve seconds, not eleven.

## VII. What Lives Here

So here is the fuller answer.

People live here, of course, a few hundred of them, loud and brief, burying their dead at the water's edge and walking uphill to visit them. Foxes and voles and reindeer and millions of birds live here, crowded close in the dark around the violet lights. And there are the pale threads in the peat, which are not quite alive, or not quite alive yet.

But something else is also living here, or is coming to life: the country itself.

The keels were never only lifting the land. They are raising it the way you would raise a child: holding it up, keeping its heart going for it, teaching it the beat. The threads are its nerves and its connective tissue. The pulse is its borrowed heart. The cracks open where the keels choose, which means where the country is being taught to flex. The peat thickens too fast because the country needs flesh. The growth is crowded because the country needs a gut, a skin, a warm and teeming surface, and everything that crowds into the circles is part of that: the moss, the voles, the gannets packed against the warm blade, the reindeer steaming in the snow-melt. The people too, with their wadding and their hand-signs, are part of the new body's first fauna, something like the flora in a newborn's gut, arriving early and settling in.

The children are, perhaps, what the country is growing so that it will have someone to understand it. They are not its keepers or its masters. They may be closer to its first language. They wake when its heart falters. They name its organs by temperament. They stand astride its cracks as they open and laugh because it does not hurt. They cannot sleep anywhere that doesn't breathe.

The old documents say that one day the rebound will be finished, the keels will fall silent, and the country will begin to age normally: rivers settling, cliffs rounding, peat thickening slowly over centuries. The people here have read that passage many times. Adults read it as a promise of rest. The children read it the way you might read a line in an old letter written before you were born, by someone who could not have known how you would turn out.

They think the keels will go quiet, and they are not afraid of it. They have already felt what comes next, in the dark, through their palms, on twelve seconds instead of eleven: a slow and enormous heart learning to beat without help.

In the steadings, when a new child is born, the custom now is to carry it out onto the moss in the first hour, whatever the weather, and lay it down on its back in the rime so it can feel the ground. The parents kneel beside it with their palms flat on the peat. They wait. They listen through their hands.

Then they press two fingers twice against the baby's chest: *listen, wait, I know you're there*. They are saying it to the child, and to the country too.
