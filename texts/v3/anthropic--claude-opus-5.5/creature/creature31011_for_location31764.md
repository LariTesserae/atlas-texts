# The One Who Keeps the Level

## Nothing, at First

The first honest answer is that nothing lives here.

Look at the place and try to imagine a body that could take it: the hammering layers, the upward drownings, the roar that shakes the long bones, the surface of the sea passing through you like a cold thought. Anything soft would be torn loose, and anything hard would be ground down. Anything that breathed only air would drown, and anything that breathed only water would suffocate in the minutes when the membrane-surface sinks below it. The reasonable conclusion is that the shelf is uninhabited. The fish and the bird are only passing through, tenants of the column rather than residents of the rock.

The second answer takes longer to reach. You would have to stand in the doorway for a while, out of the violence, with your eyes adjusting to the green, and count.

Near the base of the leaning slabs there is a cluster of pillow basalts, the bulbous loaf-shaped stones that are everywhere here. You count seven. You wipe your eyes, which does nothing, since everything is water, and count again. There are eight.

One of them is not where it was.

## The Shape

It is about the size of a person crouched against the cold. From above it is a dome of black glass, the same glassy rind as the pillow basalt, cracked and crazed in the same way, so like the rock around it that the eye slides off it. It is not stone, though. It is shell, grown over a lifetime from the same minerals the mountain is made of, so that it matches. That may be a disguise, or it may be a family resemblance.

The dome carries its history in bands. Near the crown, the oldest growth is pale and smooth and ridged with fine regular lines, the growth of an animal living in a calm, warm lagoon, laying down shell a little at a time with the easy rhythm of good tides. Dead barnacles are scattered across this part, their cones empty and bleached. They settled there when the shelf was a sea floor, and they died when it stopped being one.

Below that, toward the rim, the shell changes. The newer bands are darker, thicker, rough as a knuckle, laid down hurriedly and unevenly in the years since the land rose. Each band is a season of falling water. Along the underside of the rim a fringe of pink coralline algae grows, the same crust that survives beneath the overhangs. It is still alive because the creature carries it, the way someone who has lost a house might keep carrying a single potted plant from it.

When it moves, it moves from underneath. A broad muscular foot, grey-violet and faintly luminous at its edges, ripples against the stone. Arms come out from under the rim, four of them, maybe five, long and soft and many-jointed, ending in flattened pads that grip wet rock the way a gecko grips glass. A head extends last and only partway, like a turtle's. It is small for the body. The mouth underneath holds a ribbon of fine teeth for rasping rock. The eyes are large, black, and wide-set, and each has a second clear lid that slides across when the sea's surface passes through. Along the throat, gill-slits flutter and seal and open again. It breathes whatever it happens to be in. It had to learn that.

## What It Was

It did not come here. It was here already, before *here* was a place with a name like *shelf*.

When this was the floor of a shallow warm sea, it lived on the reef flat where the tide came and went twice a day. It was an intertidal creature, a thing of the in-between, built for the place where water gives way to air and gives way back. Its kind were grazers. They rasped the film of life off sunlit rock, clamped down when the waves came, and waited out the dry hours in crevices full of trapped water. Its kind lived alongside a great many others: cowries and conches, pale clams, worms that built white tubes, a thousand kinds of small thing that clicked and fed and hid in the reef.

Then the ground rose, in a geological heartbeat, over what was for the creature a few terrible seasons. The tide went out and did not come back. The rock tipped toward the sky, and the water that had held everything fell away downhill into cloud. Most of the reef died quickly, of air. The creature did not die, because it was already built for the hours when the tide is out. It simply had to learn to make those hours last for the rest of its life.

Then the sea began to come back, from above, confused, trying to find the floor it remembered. The creature, which had spent its whole existence at the edge between sea and not-sea, found itself living inside that confusion.

It adapted and it held on. Holding on is the one thing its kind have always been perfect at.

## How It Lives

It keeps close to the stone. When the upward rain comes and everything loose rises, it clamps. The foot seals flat, the rim of the shell grinds down against the rock, and it becomes, for all practical purposes, part of the mountain. The lifting water pulls at it, finds nothing to take hold of, and moves on.

It eats at the warm fissures. The sulfur breath there feeds a thin film of pale bacteria on the stone, and the creature grazes it slowly, rasping in long sweeps. It leaves faint pale tracks across the black rock like handwriting, washed away within the hour. It eats the tubeworms too, sometimes, and apologizes to them in whatever way it apologizes.

It tends the weed. After each upward surge, when the green-gold mats have been flung skyward and have fallen back tangled, the creature comes and combs them flat with its pads, one blade at a time. It is not clear the weed needs this. It is clear the creature does.

It knows the layers. It hears them coming. On this shelf the echo arrives before the crash, and the creature has lived for a long time with that loose tooth of time. It listens ahead, to the boom of a layer folding a heartbeat before the layer folds. Just before the shear-seam screams across the shelf, you will see it pull its head in and seal, already braced, already somewhere else inside its own shell. The transparent fish does the same thing. Perhaps one learned from the other. Perhaps they both learned from the rock.

## What It Built

The stones are its work.

**The wall** along the open edge was the first thing. It did not build the wall to keep anything in. It built the wall at the place where the old reef crest used to be, the lip where waves broke white when this was a lagoon. It carried each stone there in its arms, slowly, over many years, and set each one in place. Beneath each one, where no one would think to look, it laid a seam of its own cement, the same glue its kind have always used to hold fast. That is why the wall does not lift when everything else does. The stones are held down the way the creature holds itself down, by something it made out of its own body.

The wall marks where the edge of the sea is supposed to be. It is not a barrier. It is a statement.

**The doorway** was harder. The creature made it in the shape of a thing it remembered: the mouth of a reef crevice, the narrow cleft its kind used to shelter in during the tides, where water moved gently in and out no matter what the open sea was doing. It leaned the two slabs together at the angle the old cleft had, as nearly as its memory could get it.

The water there behaves. The creature does not know why, or it knows in the way animals know things. Perhaps the sea recognizes the shape. The sea is falling here because it remembers, and in the doorway it finds something it remembers too, and for that one narrow place the two memories agree. Inside it, the water falls as ordinary rain on an ordinary day, like the gentle surge in a sheltering crack of reef.

The creature sleeps in the doorway, when it sleeps. The transparent fish sometimes rests there too, hanging in the calm with its blue line glowing faintly. The dark bird has never followed it in. There seems to be an understanding that nobody hunts in the doorway. No one agreed to it. It is simply true.

**The cairns** are still being built.

There are twelve, or thirteen, or eleven, depending on the year. Each one holds a shell the creature found in the old barnacle bands high on the cliffs, climbing to reach it, pad over pad, while the column hammered its back. Each shell is from a different kind of creature: a cowrie, a whorled conch, a pale clam, a tiny turret, a limpet that is not its own kind but a cousin. Each cairn is one kind of neighbor, one voice from the reef.

It is a census of the dead. The creature is trying to remember everyone who lived here, and it has found twelve. The reef held thousands. It keeps looking.

The shells face upward, toward the place the water comes from, because it wants the sea to see them. *These are the ones you knew. These are the ones who lived in you. They are gone. I have kept them. You can stop looking for them.*
