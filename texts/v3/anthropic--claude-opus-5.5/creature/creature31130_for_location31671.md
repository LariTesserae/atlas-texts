# Who Lives on the Walled Shelf

## Nothing, at First

The first answer is that nothing lives here. That is almost right, and the part that is wrong is worth taking slowly.

Put a light into the water of an enclosure in the dark season and it shows you brown straps standing in still water, pink crust on grey stone, and a floor of settled silk. Nothing darts out of the beam, and nothing folds itself into a crack. On most coasts a light brings small glittering eyes to the edges of the dark. Here the beam shows only surfaces.

The second answer is the kelp. That answer is so obvious that it hides what is actually happening. The forest is not one tenant. It is thousands of them, it has been here a very long time, and it has taken on the shape of the rooms.

## In the Joints

Begin with the smallest residents, since they are the ones who actually live *in* the walls.

Kelp has two lives. The large brown plant, the one with stipe, blade, and holdfast, is only half of it. In autumn the fertile patches on the blades darken, and then, in the dark, they release spores. Each spore is a single swimming cell with two threads to pull it along. It swims weakly for a day or two and then settles. It does not grow into kelp. It grows into a gametophyte, a filament a few cells long, too small to see: a speck of brown thread lying on stone.

Gametophytes can wait. They can stay dormant for months or years, kept in darkness and cold, until the light is right. They need shelter to do it, somewhere the brine currents won't scour them off and the silt won't bury them.

The builders provided that shelter everywhere. A dry-stone wall is made as much of gaps as of stone, and every wall on the shelf is threaded through with narrow dark joints that no mortar ever filled. Those joints are where the waiting generation lives. If you prise two stones apart, which is hard now because the coralline crust has welded most of them, the faces are velvety with invisible filaments. There are millions of them in every course, packed into crevices the width of a fingernail, the whole height of the wall, along every wall on the shelf.

The walls are occupied in the way a dovecote wall is occupied, row on row of niches. A lintel over an old doorway holds a nursery in its underside. The bored disc-stones, whatever they were for, have filaments crowded around the inner rims of their holes. Nobody lives in the beehive cells, but their inward-leaning courses are the most thickly settled stonework on the shelf. The corbelling makes a lattice of overhangs, and every overhang shelters a seed bank.

When the green season arrives and light filters down, some of these filaments wake. They make eggs and sperm and are fertilized, and a new sporophyte, a young kelp, pushes out of the joint. Holdfasts do not grip the wall only from the outside. Many of them started growing *from inside it*.

## Households

Kelp spores do not travel far. Most settle within a few meters of the parent. On an open coast that barely matters, because currents mix everything and each bay's forest blurs into the next.

Here there are walls every few paces, and the water inside an enclosure is still enough that spores settle almost where they fell. Across a span of time long enough to wear every edge from the stone, this has had a consequence. Each enclosure has been breeding mostly with itself. The rooms have become households.

A diver who knew what to look for would begin to see it. In one long narrow strip, the old field kind, the kelp is dark, nearly black-brown, with blades cut deeply into ribbons, and every plant has the same fine ruffling along the margin. Over the wall, in a square room of the same size, the blades are broad, smooth-edged, and a lighter tan, and the stipes are thicker and shorter. The two rooms stand side by side under the same ice and in the same water, with a meter of tumbled stone between them. They are different families.

Walk the lattice long enough and the differences add up to a patchwork. Some rooms release their spores a few weeks before their neighbors, and their blades darken with fertile patches while the next enclosure is still plain. Some rooms put out new blade earlier in the dark, spending their reserves sooner and more boldly. In some rooms the kelp grows tall and spindly and in others short and dense. Some have frayed and splitting blade tips, others clean ones. The honeycomb of stone carries a second honeycomb over it, made of lineages. Each cell holds its own kind of forest, much as a map of old field strips shows which crop grew where.

The strangest households are in the beehive cells. Inside the low corbelled chambers, under stone roofs that have not fully fallen, almost no light comes in even in the green season. Kelp grows there anyway, having grown up out of the joints of the chamber's own walls. It is pale, with long thin stipes and blades the color of weak tea, nearly translucent, the tissue thin and starved. It lives almost entirely on the dark-growth way of life, eking out a tiny share of light each year and storing it with enormous thrift. These families have adapted to the rooms they were born into. Put a beehive plant out in the open and it bleaches and fails in the green light it has never had. The chambers have become its whole world.

Some querns lie at the base of walls, their grinding hollows still faintly dished. Where one sits in the right spot, a single kelp has anchored in the hollow, its holdfast pooled in the curve like water settled in a bowl. Often there is only one plant per quern, but it is often a *particular* plant. Its spores fall into the same hollow, and the hollow fills again with its own kind.

## The Roads

The channels are the exception. They are also where the forest's families meet.

Brine moves along them all winter, and in the green season meltwater and upwelling move along them too. Spores that fall into the channels do not stay put. They are carried downslope along the builders' gradients, sometimes for kilometers, and settle along the channel walls and in the slots where sluice-boards used to slide. So the channels are lined with a mixed kelp, less distinct and more like a crowd, with lineages flowing into one another along the current. The rope-kelp in the channels, the longest on the shelf, combed flat and running for meters, draws on many households at once.

Where a channel passes an enclosure through a gap or a culvert mouth, the room's family and the road's crowd mix at the threshold. There are zones like this at every doorway. Just inside a gap into a room of dark ribboned kelp, the plants are half-ribboned. Just outside, a few ribbon-edged plants lean with the current, on their way somewhere else.

The channels have one more resident of their own. In the lowest reaches, where brine pools for weeks behind a collapsed sluice or a drift-blocked culvert, only kelp that can bear dense, cold, salty water survives. These are squat plants with thick-walled blades, and they stand with their lower halves in one water and their upper halves in another, with the pale boundary of the halocline across their stipes like a tideline drawn in the middle of the sea. They are the brine-folk. They live only where the old water-courses lead the heaviest water, so their range follows the builders' plumbing exactly. Any map of them would also be a plan of the drains.

## The Undercroft

Only one kind of resident here makes a living from anything other than light, and it lives below.

When the drifts of shed blades pile up in the lower rooms and pack down over centuries, and when brine sits still for weeks on the paving, the oxygen runs out. At the bottom of the heaviest pools and inside the peat, the water turns airless and faintly sour. Bacteria live there, as they must: slow, cold-adapted ones that break down kelp that nothing ever grazed, a molecule at a time. Some of them breathe sulfate from the seawater in place of oxygen and give off sulfide as they work.

Where that sulfide seeps up and meets the last traces of oxygen, a different kind of bacterium spreads out. It is the only white thing on the shelf.

In the deepest brine enclosures, a light shows veils: soft grey-white fleece lying over the peat and the paving, over the fallen blades in the corners, over the base courses of the walls. It is made of bacterial filaments, each cell carrying small bright beads of sulfur that gleam in the beam like frost. The veils reach no higher than the brine boundary. Above it they end, as cleanly as if trimmed. In some rooms the white fills to exactly the same height on all four walls, a level white floor inside stone, as though the room had been flooded with milk and the milk had set.

These veils are the closest thing the shelf has to an eater. They eat what the forest drops, or more precisely, they eat what the first eaters leave. They do it at a pace hard to recognize as life. A veil might spread a few centimeters across a room in a year and pull back when a storm stirs the brine and brings oxygen down. They live in the rooms the forest is filling, and they are the ones doing the filling, turning the drifts to peat and the peat to something denser. If anything here is burying the walls, it is these white threads.

They also keep to the plan. The brine pools where the gradients take it, the drifts gather in corners where walls meet, and the veils grow where the brine and the drifts are. Their distribution follows the architecture: the low rooms and the corners, and never the high strips or the channel tops where the water moves.

## The Crust

The oldest residents here are not the kelp.

The coralline algae that coat the stones in pink-grey plaster are alive. They are red algae that build skeletons of calcite in their cell walls, grow outward over stone a fraction of a millimeter a year, and never stop. On a coast with grazers, urchins and limpets scrape them back. Here nothing ever has, and some plaques are enormously old. A crust on a lintel might be as thick as a hand. Cut through it and it is banded like a tree: pale bands laid down in the green seasons, dark bands for the long dark, one pair for every year.

In that sense the coralline crusts are the shelf's record-keepers, though they keep the record without knowing it. Every good light year leaves a slightly thicker band, and every year of heavy ice leaves a thinner one. The crust on a wall in the middle of the shelf holds more years of this place's weather than any other object there. It is growing *on* the builders' work, so its innermost band marks the moment the sea came in and it first took hold on the stone. Somewhere under the oldest crust, pressed against the rock, is the first layer that grew after the last dry year.

The crusts do one other thing. Where they have filled a joint between two stones, they seal it, and the gametophytes that were waiting there are closed in. Some joints on the shelf have been closed for a very long time. The seed bank in the walls is sealed in as well as waiting. Nobody knows how long a kelp filament can wait. Probably not that long. But the walls are full of sealed niches, and each one holds a small brown spark from some season long past.

## The Crowd

For a few weeks each year there is a crowd.

When the ice thins and light comes through, it also reaches the diatoms, single-celled algae in glass shells that have been dormant in the silt and on the blades through the dark. They bloom. Over days, the surfaces of every kelp blade on the shelf grow a fine golden-brown fur, and the water turns faintly cloudy, full of drifting cells. Seen through the green light it looks almost like dust in a sunbeam, or like pollen. In a sense it is the shelf's only population, a real multitude, too many to count. For those weeks every surface is crowded, and the forest wears a coat of them.

Then the light fades, the bloom runs out, the cells sink, and the silt takes them back. The crowd lies dormant in the courtyard floors, a pale layer in the fine sediment, waiting for the next year.

## What the Forest Never Learned

One more fact about the kelp here changes everything else about it.

On every other coast kelp defends itself. It makes bitter compounds and tannins, and it toughens its tissues. It sheds and replaces blades as grazers wear them down. Much of what kelp is, anywhere, is shaped by being eaten.

Nothing has ever bitten this forest. Over the long ages it has not needed its defenses. Its blades are soft and faintly sweet and slick, and they tear easily. Its holdfasts are loose. On an open coast it would last a season. Here it is the only kind of kelp that could ever have been. The forest is gentle in the plain physical sense: soft all the way through, never having needed to be anything else.

The softness is part of why the drifts pile so deep. The blades break easily under the ice-storms and fall easily, and nothing clears them, so the rooms fill. The forest's gentleness is also what is slowly burying the walls it grew out of.

## Who, Then

It is time to answer plainly. Whoever or whatever lives here?

The kelp lives here, as households kept apart by walls, each with a lineage suited to its room, some bred for darkness inside stone chambers and some for brine along the drains. The waiting generation lives here, filament by filament, inside the stones in joints the builders left. Bacteria live in the lowest rooms as a white fleece that fills them only to the brine line. Coralline crust lives here, the oldest living thing on the stone, laying down one band a year and holding the record of the seasons. For a few weeks, a crowd of glass-shelled cells lives here too, before sinking back into the courtyard floors.

There is also a larger answer, and it came together while the rest was being written, so it belongs at the end.

Everything alive here lives according to the plan. The kelp is divided by the walls into families, one per room. The waiting generation is housed in the gaps of the stonework. The brine-folk grow along the drains, and their range matches the plumbing. The white veils fill the lowest rooms to the level the gradients set. The crowd settles in the courtyards. None of these things knows anything about the builders, and none of them is building anything. Yet each has taken up the builders' pattern and organized its life by it, in the same shapes the builders gave the ground.

So the builders' design is inhabited. The occupants are just not the ones who laid the stones. They store up light in a short bright season and live off it through a long dark one, which is also what people with saddle-querns would have done with grain. They keep to their rooms. They move along the channels. The shelf is still being lived in according to its plan, room by room, by occupants that could not have imagined the plan and follow it anyway.

This is also why the slow burial will not end things as simply as it seems. When the drifts finally fill the rooms and the walls vanish into brown peat, and the shelf is a smooth plain with the stonework hidden inside it, the kelp growing on that plain will still be divided. For a long time, possibly longer than it took the walls to fall, the families will keep their boundaries. The ribboned lineage will stay where its room was and the broad-bladed one where the next room was. The line between them will run where the wall ran, even after nothing is left of the wall. The pattern outlasted the structure once, when the walls slumped but kept their lines, and it will do so again, in the lineages of the kelp.

Out in the dark the ice creaks and a stone settles. A pale plant in a beehive cell pushes out a finger's-width of new blade in darkness, living off light it stored months ago.
