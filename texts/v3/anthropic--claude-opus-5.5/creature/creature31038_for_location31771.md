# Who Lives in the Hollow Wards

## First Answer: The Colony

You would say the bats, and you would be right in the way a census is right.

Up close the great-hall colony is not a population. It is a single surface, a pelt that breathes. When you shine a light on it, it ripples outward from the beam like water around a thrown stone. Each body shifts a finger's width, and the whole ceiling seems to flinch. It has moods. On cold mornings it hangs dense and still, and the chittering drops into a low simmer. On hot evenings before the emergence it seethes. Patches loosen and drop, catch themselves in the air, and flutter back. The floor beneath gets a fine, steady rain of fur and droppings, a sound like sand hissing through fingers.

If anything here has a mind, you might think, it is this. A million small heads thinking the same thought: *out, out, the air is warm, out.* The emergence is a decision the colony makes all at once.

But the colony leaves every night and every winter. It lives *in* the city the way weather lives in a valley. It passes through. It isn't what I mean.

## Second Answer: The Fig

So try the fig. Try the oldest one, in the court the plateau people call the Well, though it holds no water.

Its trunk is not large; the dryness has kept it small. It never needed to be large above ground. All its ambition went down. Its roots leave the court through eleven separate doorways. They cross galleries, climb over drifts of dust, and enter chambers that have been dark since before the plateau had a language. One root, thicker than a thigh, has taken the place of a failed column. The column's two stumps are still there, one above and one below, and the root runs between them, holding up the ceiling in a slow grey embrace.

Follow the roots far enough and you realize the fig has been *reading* the city. It follows every carved channel, every niche, every seam the diggers cut. It has taken the diggers' map of the place and memorized it by touch, and it is still searching for the one thing the diggers must have known how to find: water, somewhere below.

That is closer. The fig lives here. It is patient in the way the city is patient.

But the fig doesn't know it is here. It is devoted without being a dweller.

## Third Answer: The Ones Below

The plateau people have a word, *hemmet*, which is their oldest word for the diggers. It means something like *the ones below* or *those who went down*.

The word is also in use now, and it means something else. Or it means the same thing, and the diggers have simply been forgotten out of it.

On the plateau, when a person's hearing goes, whether from age, fever, or the birth-deafness that runs in some families like a seam in stone, people begin to say of them, gently, that they are *becoming hemmet*. It is not an insult. It is closer to what a grandmother says when a child starts walking toward the door on her own.

Most never go. Some do.

The city is the one place on earth that cannot be lived in by those who hear, and it is not uninhabitable to those who don't. The wall of sound that would break a hearing person in a week reaches the deaf as something else: a pressure, a trembling in the floor, a buzz in the teeth and breastbone. It is loud to them the way a warm wind is loud. It is not a wall. It is a climate.

There are never many of them. In a given generation there might be nine, or twenty, or three. They do not form a village. They are scattered the way the courts are scattered, each living near one opening, in the first chambers past the edge of the light, where the green fades to grey.

## One of Them

Let me go further down. Let this become one person.

She lives in the ward off the court with the three leaning figs. She has lived there for twenty-six years. She was forty-one when she came down, deaf since a fever in her thirties, and she came down the collapse on a rope her brother held. She has not been back up the rope. She has been up the ramps, by the long way, to trade, and she has always returned.

Her room is a digger's room, one of the thousands on the lower tier. The doorway has rounded to an oval. She sleeps in the ceiling.

This needs explaining. At the back of her room, where bats roosted for ten thousand years before the colony drifted toward the great hall, the ceiling is pocked with the smooth domes their clustered bodies wore into the rock. One of the domes is the size of a curled person. She built a platform of fig-wood beneath it and packed it with dry grass, and she sleeps with her face a hand's width from stone polished by millennia of small warm bodies. On winter nights, she says in her hands, it is still faintly warm. It isn't. But she says it.

**The dew hour.** She wakes before dawn because the floor tells her the bats are coming back. The long rumble of their descent comes up through the platform legs, and she lies still and feels it: first a distant thrum, then a tightening, then a trembling so fine it makes the hairs on her arms lift. By the time the last of them are in, she is out in the court with a cloth in each hand.

For forty minutes the rock faces sweat, and she wipes them. She moves along the walls in the grey light, pressing the cloth flat, wringing it into a clay bowl, pressing again. The resurrection ferns unroll around her fingers as she works. She has learned exactly which faces bead first, which rocks are slow, where the dew pools in the old tool-grooves. She drinks what she gathers and gives some to the capers. By the time the ferns go green all the way across the court, she has perhaps a cup and a half. It is enough. It has been enough for twenty-six years.

**The day.** She gathers. Capers from the upper tiers, before the buds open. Thyme and the pepper-silver herb. Fig fruit in season, fought over with the swiftlets and the goats. Most of all she gathers guano. She digs it from the older beds, dry and crumbling, sacks it, and hauls it up the long ramps to the plateau, where it becomes fields. This is the trade. The plateau grows its barley in what the hemmet carry up. In the plateau's accounting, the deaf are the ones who keep the fields fed.

She eats well. Nothing here is rare except water and people.

**The walls.** Here is the part that matters. When she walks the dark galleries between courts, she walks with one hand on the wall.

She does not do this to find her way; she knows the way. She does it to listen. The tuff carries sound the way it carries everything, and it carries it into her palm. Each gallery hums at its own pitch, and under her hand she feels which ones ring and which are dead. She can feel the cricket-drone in the lower wards as a fine grinding, like a millstone turning far away. She can tell by touch alone when the great hall is restless. She has learned to feel a ceiling that is getting ready to fall. It gives a particular hollowness, a flatness in the vibration, a place where the stone no longer answers. She has warned the others twice. Both times it came down within the decade, and both times no one was under it.

Her fingers have found the ghost-grooves of the digger's tools a thousand times. She knows the difference between a wall cut by a right-handed digger and one cut by a left. She has found a place in the fourth ward where one digger's strokes change angle halfway across a wall, as if someone else picked up the tool and finished the job. She thinks about this often.

**The niches.** There are thousands of them at shoulder height, rounded into shallow cups. No one knows what they held.

She has a guess. When the hemmet speak to one another, they sign, and signing in the dark requires touch. You put your hand in the other person's hand and shape the words into their palm. Or, if you are sitting side by side along a gallery wall, you rest your forearm in the niche beside you, because that is exactly the height where a tired arm wants to rest. From there you can sign to the person next to you without lifting your elbow for an hour.

She has sat in the galleries with another hemmet, both of them with an arm in a niche, talking by hand for half a night while the bats roared overhead and neither of them heard a thing.

She does not claim the diggers were deaf. She claims only that the niches fit the arm exactly, and that the city is too loud now and must have been loud even then, and that *hemmet* is a very old word.

## The Emergence

At dusk, she goes to the gallery with the parted column.

It is a place she found in her third year. Two stumps, one rising from the floor, one hanging from the ceiling, with a hand's width of air between them. When the emergence begins, the wind of wings pours through that gallery toward the nearest collapse. She sits on the lower stump and puts her open palm into the gap.

She feels the city exhale through her hand.

It lasts more than an hour. The air moves steadily past her fingers, warm with the bodies that made it. The stone under her trembles with a million wingbeats, and the dust lifts off the floor and flows past her ankles like a shallow river. She has tried to describe it to her brother, on the plateau, and he said it sounded terrible. She said no, it is the only time the city is gentle. It is the only time it touches you.

## Winter

In the cold months, the colonies go south, and the city goes nearly still.

For the hearing, this would be the only livable season. For her it is the hardest. The floor stops trembling. The walls under her palm go dead. She walks the galleries and feels nothing, and it is like walking through the rooms of a house where someone has just died. She sleeps badly. She sits up in the ceiling dome and presses her palm to the stone and waits for it to hum.

The other hemmet visit each other more in winter. They sit in the niches and talk by hand, because the stone won't talk to them. They say this is when the diggers left. Not because of the noise. Because of the silence. Because one year the bats didn't come back soon enough.

In spring the first tremor comes up through the floor at night, faint and far off, and she wakes and lies still with her hands flat on the fig-wood, and then she weeps. She does this every year.

## What Lives Here

So: who lives in the Hollow Wards?

The bats, by count. The fig, by patience. The beetles, by the billion, turning the floor over grain by grain.

And a few people who cannot hear the city and who therefore can bear to live in it, who know it better than anything alive, because they know it through their skin. They wipe dew off the walls at dawn. They carry the floor of the city up to the fields. They sleep in the hollows the bats wore and sit in the niches the diggers cut, and they put their hands into the gaps the ages made.

That last thing is what they have in common with everything else here, from the goats to the harvestmen. The city is shaped by occupancy. The ceiling domes are the shapes of bodies. The ramps are the shapes of feet. The polish on the ledges is the shape of a grip held for ten thousand years. Everything that lives here leaves its outline pressed into the soft stone, and the stone keeps it.

She knows this. On certain walls, in the ward with the three leaning figs, there is a place at shoulder height where the tuff has begun, very faintly, to shine. It is the height of a palm and the width of a palm, next to the door she walks through every morning with her hand out, feeling for the hum.

It is twenty-six years of touching. Given enough time, it will be a hollow. Someone after her will find it with their own hand, fit their fingers into it, and wonder what it was for.
