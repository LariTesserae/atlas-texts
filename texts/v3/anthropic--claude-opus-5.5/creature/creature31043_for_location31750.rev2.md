# Those Who Live in the Anvil Highlands

## The Easy Answer

Begin with the easy answer, because it is incomplete.

What lives in the Anvil Highlands is what the description lists: smelters grinding ore in their incandescent bellies, eels strung like capacitors between the pools, moths pouring along the seams in rivers of millions, wire-goats crackling violet when they startle, and the spade-headed hunter reading all of it through the pits in its face. Add the burrowers in the tubes, the beetles that chime when they collide, and a great uncatalogued remainder. That is the population of the place, and a naturalist could spend several lifetimes on it and die satisfied.

But the description gives something away in small slips. The smear on the horizon may be a sun, and *no one has decided which*. The spade-headed hunter is *the one most spoken of*. Some iron has been concentrated *not by geology alone*. Scavengers follow the smelter herds to gather beads *for reasons of their own*.

Deciding requires someone to decide, and speaking requires a speaker. Reasons of their own belong to someone who has reasons.

So look again at the scavengers.

## The Ones Who Follow

They walk behind the smelter herds at a distance of a few hundred meters. That is far enough to stay out from under the great slow feet, and close enough to feel the herd's warmth on the skin like the heat off a banked hearth.

They are about the height of a person when they stand upright, which they seldom do. Their usual gait is a stooped, unhurried lope, with long arms carried low and the hands often trailing near the ground or touching it. A traveler who met one in the dark, as any meeting here happens, might take it for another of the plateau's animals: something lean, warm, and damp-skinned, smelling faintly of rust and ozone. The skin is dark, slick with the ever-present condensation, and it has taken on the oxblood tint of the rock from a lifetime of iron grit worked into it. It feels like wet slate that has been lying in the sun.

The hands are what give them away. The palms are pale and very sensitive, and they are lined with shallow pits in a pattern much like the rows along the hunter's face. Nature arrived at the same solution twice. These people feel current through their hands. When one of them lays a palm on the warm stone, the ground opens under the touch into a crowded map: the slow tidal pull of the deep currents, the bright knots where seeps break through, the small moving disturbances that are grazers, and the larger ones that are smelters. Somewhere out along a rise there may be a long, low, patient disturbance that does not move at all, and that is the hunter waiting.

They have eyes, large and black under heavy lids, and they keep them closed nearly all the time. There is almost nothing for eyes to do here. Almost.

## The Showing

The eels discharge without warning to anyone who cannot feel them. The people can. As charge builds in a seep-chain, the ground near the pools takes on a pressure they feel in the palms and at the backs of their necks, a tightening like the air before speech. When they feel it, they turn toward the water and open their eyes.

Then, for half a second, the world is lit.

They call it the showing. It is the only time they see anything at all. Across a whole life, a person might witness a few thousand showings, and each one is kept. It would be wrong to call these memories in the ordinary sense. They are more like possessions, or relics. Each is a single frozen, complete, blue-white tableau: terraced pools, mist, the black backs of smelters, a child's face turned up in surprise, a hunter caught mid-stride on a far slope with its spade head lifted. The afterimage fades from the eye in a minute, but it does not fade from them. Old people have enormous inner galleries of these, and they can walk through them the way we might walk through a house we grew up in.

So they know what things look like, but only in fragments, only in cold light, and only in instants. They have never seen anything move. Motion is something they feel, and hear, and read in the ground. It is not something they have ever seen. To them sight is the sense of stillness, the sense that shows how things *are* rather than what they are doing, and they treat it with the gravity you might expect of a people who get so little of it.

A child's first showing is the most important thing that happens to them. They are carried to a seep-chain, held with a palm pressed to the ground until they learn to feel the pressure, and told to open their eyes when it comes. Whatever that first blaze of light reveals becomes the child's private name. It might be a moth frozen in the air, or an adult's hand, or the far string of pools reaching into the mist. The name is never spoken aloud, for reasons that will become clear. It is given only by touch, palm to palm, to the very few who are close enough to hold it.

## The Two Voices

In the thin highland air, a word spoken at ordinary volume travels a surprising distance, and the people have organized their whole speech around that fact.

They have two voices. The first is the air-voice, soft and breathy and pitched low, and it carries. Anything said aloud is said to everyone within a kilometer or more, so the air-voice is by nature public, and they use it only for public things. They use it for warnings, for the location of a new seep, and for the names of the dead, which are said aloud once so that everyone hears them. And they use it for the Argument, which comes later. On the plateau, speaking aloud is closer to announcing than to talking. A person who habitually says private things in the air-voice is regarded as a little mad, or as a child.

The second is the ground-voice. It is spoken by touch, through current: palm to palm, or palm to stone. It travels along conductors and nowhere else, so it is directed and intimate. Between two people touching hands it can carry the complicated weather of feeling, the thing underneath the thing, the shape of what you would say if words were not so blunt. Through the ground it goes wherever the rock conducts it, and on this plateau that means along the iron.

This matters a great deal, because it is why they gather the beads.

## Being Seen

One more thing must be understood about them before the beads. It follows from a rule of the place: *everything alive carries a charge, and everything alive can feel the charge of everything else.*

The people cannot hide from one another. Fear changes a body's field, and so do grief, desire, and anger. A friend's sorrow can be felt from a hundred meters away as a disturbance in the ground, the way we might notice a change in someone's breathing across a room. Privacy of feeling does not exist here.

They have not responded by becoming shameless, and they have not become cruel. They have developed a courtesy of *not reading*. When someone is in distress, the polite thing is to turn your palms up, off the ground, and let them be unperceived for a while. It is a deliberate act of withholding attention, the equivalent of averting your eyes, and it is the gentlest gift they give each other. A lover lifting their hands from the stone so that you can grieve without being felt is performing something close to tenderness in its purest form.

Lying is nearly impossible. It can be attempted only by damping your own field, going quiet and still. But the stillness itself shows, as a visible hush in the ground. So the only lie available is an obvious silence, and everyone knows what it means. As a result they are not a people of deception, although they are very much a people of discretion.

## The Beads

They carry pouches of cured mat-hide, and the pouches click faintly with iron beads, the small dense droppings of the smelters, nearly pure metal. The people gather them patiently from behind the herds. They carry them for days, sometimes across whole basins, and then they lay them down.

They lay them in lines, bead against bead, extending the seams and connecting one fissure to another. The work is slow, and its scale is geological. One person, working a whole life, might lay a few kilometers.

Ask why and you will get different answers depending on who is asked and how old they are.

A child will say that the lines mark the way, which is true. The lines guide travel between seeps and can be followed by touch in absolute darkness.

An adult will say the lines carry the ground-voice. That is also true, and it is the real reason in the practical sense. Along a laid line, a palm-message goes much farther than it would through bare rock. A young line lets you speak to the next seep. An old line, one that has been fused by the currents into a continuous ribbon of metal, lets you speak across a basin. The oldest lines, the iron roads that run along the ancient grazing routes, let you speak farther than anyone has ever walked.

An elder will say something else, and to understand it you have to know what they do with their dead.

## The Dead

Nothing rots quickly here. The dark and the thin air slow decay, and bodies left on the stone slowly mineralize until the skeleton is outlined in rust and iron.

When one of the people dies, their name is said aloud once, in the air-voice, and everyone within hearing stops and turns their palms up in the long courtesy. Then the body is carried to the nearest line and laid down alongside it, face down, arms outstretched, palms flat against the stone and touching the beads. And it is left there.

Over years the body becomes metal. The iron in the blood gathers, and the bones take up minerals from the seep-water. The currents that draw loose iron toward the seams draw on the dead as well, until the outline of a person becomes part of the conductor. The line carries current *through* them.

So the old lines, the ones that carry voices farthest, are not made of beads alone. They are made of beads and ancestors. They are strung with generations of the dead lying palm-down along the metal, and every message sent along them passes through a thousand silent bodies.

This is what the elder means. They say they lay lines so that they have somewhere to go.

## What Comes Back

When you speak into an old line, sometimes something answers.

The people do not agree about what it is, and here we arrive at the center of who they are.

Some say the answers are simply other people. There are kin on distant parts of the plateau, far beyond walking, whose palms happen to be on the same line. That is the plain explanation, and it is certainly sometimes the right one.

Some say the answers come from the dead. A body wired into the line does not entirely stop, they say, and the long slow current running through a thousand mineralized hands carries something that responds. These answers tend to arrive late, sometimes days after the message was sent, and they come changed. They are softened and slowed, as though they had passed through something that thought about them first.

And some say the answers come from the plateau.

The plateau is concentrating its iron and drawing it into lines. It has been doing this for longer than the people have existed. Now the people are doing it too, and the two kinds of line have begun to merge. In the oldest regions no one can tell any longer which iron roads were laid by hands and which were grown by the ground. The seams thicken. The circuits lengthen and interconnect. The hum beneath everything may be the current flowing, or the rock flexing, or it may be something else: a process running across a continent-sized web of iron, far too slow to be felt as anything but a sound. Perhaps it forms one thought in a thousand years. Perhaps the great migrations of the ecology across the plateau, the drying of one basin's seeps and the flowering of another's, are that thought's attention moving.

If that is so, then the people are not scavengers, and they are not the builders of the network, and they are not quite its servants. They might be the part of it that is fast enough to speak aloud.

## The Argument

They call themselves, in the air-voice, by a word that translates roughly as *still-deciding*.

This is the heart of them, and it is what makes them unlike almost any other people. They do not close questions. Where other cultures might treat an unanswered question as a failure, a gap to be filled, these people regard settling a question too early the way we regard killing something young. An open question is alive. It can still grow. Carrying it is a responsibility, and passing it to your children unresolved is a kind of inheritance.

They carry many questions this way. Are the answers from the line the dead, or the plateau, or both? And if both, is there a difference? Are the people wiring the plateau, or is the plateau wiring itself through the people, using their hands the way it uses the currents? Is the hunter a predator, or something closer to a relative, since it reads the world through pits in its face just as they read it through pits in their palms? (They speak of it more than of any other animal. When one of them is taken, they do not say *killed*. They say *read*.)

But the greatest of the open questions, the one they call simply the Argument, is the one the description raised at the very beginning.

Is the smear of brightness on the horizon a sun?

This has been debated in the air-voice, publicly, for as long as anyone remembers. Anyone may add to it. People speak into it from rises and basins, and their words carry across kilometers. A traveler crossing the plateau will hear pieces of it drifting through the dark, spoken by strangers far away: *it brightens in the long cold seasons*; *my grandmother said it was a pool of the eels' light that never empties*; *it is the eye wanting*; *it moved, I felt it move*. Over millennia the Argument has become something like their literature, their philosophy, and their liturgy at once. It is an enormous distributed conversation that no one expects to finish and no one wants to.

## The Measurers

There is one faction within the Argument, small and patient and regarded with some unease, that keeps records.

The records are knotted cords strung with beads in patterns, passed from palm to palm down generations. They record where things are. They record the position of old lines relative to the seams, the angles of iron roads, and the direction in which a laid nail creeps over a season.

Iron moves. Everyone knows this. Loose iron creeps toward the nearest seam, a millimeter at a time. But the measurers, reading their cords across a span of time longer than their whole recorded history, claim to have found something beneath the familiar creep. The seams themselves are bending. The great straight fissures that run for enormous distances across the plateau are, on the longest timescale, very slowly curving, and they are all curving the same way.

They are curving toward the brightness on the horizon.

It is the way a plant leans toward a window, if a plant were a continent and the lean took a hundred thousand years. The whole wired plateau is turning, with vast slowness, toward the thing that may or may not be a sun.

The measurers say this aloud, in the air-voice, and the Argument receives it the way it receives everything, as one more thing to be carried. Some say it proves the brightness is real, since why else would the iron turn. Some say it proves only that the plateau also wants there to be a sun, and that the wanting is no evidence. Some say the plateau is not turning toward the light at all. They say it is turning to *speak* to it, stretching its lines toward something that might answer the way the dead answer.

No one has decided. That is the point. That is who they are.

## A Night, Which Is to Say Always

On the crest of a rise, above a basin full of warm mist, a small group of the people walks slowly toward a seep-chain. There are six of them. Two carry a body between them, an old woman whose inner gallery of showings held more than four thousand images. Her name was said aloud an hour ago, and the whole basin turned its palms up in silence. One carries a child, very young, who has never seen anything.

At the terraces they lay the old woman down along the line, a road of fused beads far older than any of them. They place her face down, with her arms outstretched and her pale pitted palms flat against the iron. There are other shapes along the line, rust-dark and half-sunk into the metal, going back into the mist as far as touch can reach.

Then one of them kneels, sets the child's palm on the warm stone, and holds it there.

Through the rock the child begins to feel what everyone else already feels: the pressure building in the pools, and the tightening of the air before speech. And under that there is something the adults feel too and do not remark on. A slow, faint current is moving outward from where the old woman's hands touch the line, her last warmth leaving her and entering the iron and traveling away. Somewhere far across the plateau, days from now, someone with a palm on the ground may feel it arrive, softened and slowed as though something had thought about it first.

"Now," one of the adults says in the ground-voice, palm to the child's palm. "Open."

The child opens its eyes.

The eels let go.

For half a second everything is lit. The terraced pools are lit, and the mist, and the six still figures and the old woman lying palm-down among her ancestors. So is the long iron line running out and away through the basin, and it does not run straight. Anyone who knows to look can see that it curves, gently, toward a smear of brightness low on the horizon. The smear might be a sun that never rises, or it might be only the eye wanting one.

Then it is dark again.

The child has a name now. No one will ever say it aloud.
