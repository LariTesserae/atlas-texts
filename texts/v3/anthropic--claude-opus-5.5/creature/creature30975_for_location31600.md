# Who Lives on the Kettle Shelf

## The Easy Answer

The easy answer is that everything lives here. The herd, the beetles, the moths, the birds that hammer, the eels that swim through fog, the engines. A visitor could stand on a slag road with frost growing on their shoulders and count species until they lost track, and that would be a true answer.

But it would be the answer of someone who has only just arrived. The Kettle Shelf is a place where the first thing you see is rarely the thing that is there, because the land is so bright and loud that it hides what it holds in plain view. Stay longer and the question changes. It stops being *what lives here* and becomes *who*.

So begin small, and begin warm, because on the Shelf warmth is where the living things are.

## The Ones Who Are Carried

The grazers' coats are not really hair. They are metal-stiffened fiber that grows in dense, springy spirals, each strand sheathed in a thin rind of iron oxide. The coats are half a yard deep on an old animal. In the constant cold they trap a layer of body heat like the air in a mitten, and on a moving herd of millions that layer is, taken together, the largest warm place on the plateau. It is warmer than the vents, larger than the arcs, and it never stays in one place.

Something lives in it.

They are small, about the length of a hand from nose to tail-root. They are long-bodied and loose-jointed, built to flow through the wire spirals the way water moves through gravel. Their fur is short and dense and so fine it looks like smoke. Their eyes are huge and dark. In the permanent glare of the Shelf they would be blinded in an instant, but inside the coat the light arrives only as a copper dusk, filtered and red. Their paws end in soft pads without claws, because a claw would catch on the wire and tear. They are grey where the coat is grey and rust where it is rust. Nobody would see them. The herd itself barely notices them.

Call them the Carried, because that is what they are.

The Carried are born in the coat, live in the coat, and die in the coat. They never touch the ground. The ground is death for them in two ways: the cold would stop their small hearts within minutes, and the moss's charge, which a grazer's thick hide shrugs off, would stop them faster. A Carried that falls is lost. They know this in the way a bird knows the sky. It is less a fear than a basic fact of the shape of their world.

Their world is enormous, though. It is the herd, and the herd is a continent in motion. It is a landscape of living backs, rolling and steaming, broad as a country, with pathways, regions, weather, and seasons. When two grazers walk flank to flank their coats touch, and the Carried cross in that moment, flowing from one animal to the next like smoke through a gap under a door. Over a lifetime a Carried might travel through thousands of animals and cross the herd from one edge to the other. It would never know the herd was moving over the ground at all, only that it was moving through the herd.

They eat what the Shelf drops on them. Frostmoths settle on the grazers in their soft blizzards and freeze there, and the Carried harvest them from the outer coat at the edge of the cold, darting out and back. Wirebeetles climb the grazers for the static, and the Carried take them too, but carefully. They wait until a beetle has discharged in fright and is empty and harmless before they crack it. They drink from the grazers' breath. Each exhalation leaves a bloom of condensation on the fur of the face and neck, and the Carried gather at the throat in the rhythm of the chuffing, drinking the fog the animal makes.

They do not build. There is nothing to build with and nowhere to build it, and nothing on the Shelf builds anyway. They have something else. They groom. They comb the coat and keep the spirals clean. They eat the parasites that would otherwise bore into the grazer's skin. They pull out the dead fiber so the living fiber grows straight. The grazers are healthier for them, and the grazers seem, dimly, to know it. An animal whose Carried have left grows ragged and slow and falls to the back of the herd.

They speak, and this is the first sign that "who" is the right question. Their voices are too small to carry through the herd's roar, so they talk by touch. They tap patterns onto the wire strands of the coat and pass the vibration along. A message can run through a whole animal's fleece in seconds, down the spirals and across the back and around the flank. Where two grazers' coats touch, a message can cross.

What the Carried say to each other, as far as anything can be understood across that distance, is mostly about the grazers themselves. They say which animal is warm, which is cooling, which is ill, which is old. They keep the herd's memory. They know its genealogy better than the herd does, because they have ridden it for longer than any single grazer has lived.

And they know something about old grazers that nothing else on the Shelf seems to know, or at least nothing else has said.

## The Ones Who Keep Too Much

A grazer eats coppermoss its whole life, and its whole life it smelts. The metal it cannot use it concentrates and passes, and those slag cakes pave the roads. But the process is not perfect. Every year a little more metal stays behind. It settles in the bones, in the hide, in the fiber of the coat. A young grazer's coat is springy and rust-bright. An old one's is dark, heavy, and stiff, and it rings faintly when two animals bump shoulders.

The Carried notice first. The coat stops breathing. The spirals, clogged with metal, no longer flex, and the warm layer thins. Tapped messages ring differently, sharper and more resonant, as if the animal were turning into an instrument. When the grazer lies down to rest, it lies longer each time, and it stops passing slag.

When that happens, the Carried leave.

They do it in stages over many days. They flow out of the old animal into its neighbors, and they tap as they go. The pattern is always the same, and it spreads through the herd around the departing animal. It might be a farewell. It might be a warning. It might be the Carried equivalent of a prayer. It may be all three, because they may not distinguish between them.

The old grazer, emptied of its tenants, begins to drift. It falls behind the herd. The herd doesn't wait for it and doesn't mourn. The grazer turns away from the migration route and walks, stiff-legged and ringing, toward the nearest standing arc.

It lies down in the arc's light and does not get up for a long time.

What happens there takes place in the violet glare and the ozone and the endless crackle, under drifts of wirebeetles that crowd the arc and clamber over the prone body as if it were rock. The current runs into the animal. Its metal-choked hide, which would have been a burden anywhere else, is a conductor here, and the charge pours through it. The coat fuses. The spirals weld into plates. The shaggy wire coat hardens into armor, layer by layer, the way the arc fuses the basalt beneath it into green glass. The body inside changes too, though nothing has seen how. Eventually, after a season or a year, what rises from the arc is no longer a grazer.

It is an engine.

That is the Carried's knowledge, tapped through ten thousand generations of wire: the engines are grazers that kept too much. They are the herd's old, refined by lightning into something else. Metal moves upward. The moss concentrates it, the grazer concentrates it again, and at the end of a grazer's life the grazer becomes the concentration. The engine is the herd's slag given a body and a will.

This explains why the herd goes silent when an engine calls. It is not fear, or not only fear. It is the silence of a people who hear, rolling across the valley in that long turbine drone, the voice of their grandparents.

## The Engines, More Closely

Engines are hot. This is the fact about them that everything else follows from.

A grazer is warm the way any living thing is warm. An engine is hot the way a forge is hot. It carries the arc's charge inside its armor and turns it slowly into heat, the way a filament does. The rime flees from it and the moss blackens under its feet. Its breath is not fog but a shimmer. Where it walks it leaves a trail of ground warmed through, and the carpet that returns there comes back doubled and fierce.

So the engines are gardeners, whether they mean to be or not. Their paths across the Shelf are the richest ground on it after the fissure-lips and the arcs. Wirebeetles follow in their wake. Anvil-birds follow the beetles. The frostmoths, drawn to warmth like all moths, spiral around engines in pale glowing columns, forming a slow tornado of wings that marks where one is walking from miles away.

And something lives on the engines too, as something lives on everything here.

On their armor, in the cracks between the fused plates where the heat is just bearable, grows a crust of life found nowhere else. There is a moss that isn't coppermoss, ash-grey and bristling, able to live on nothing but heat and current. There are small crabs, or crab-like things, with shells of the same fused metal as the engine's armor, who feed on that grey moss and seem to be born from the plates themselves. It may be that they are the plates, or were once. They cluster most thickly around the engine's head, and when the engine calls they all lift their claws at once like a crowd raising its hands.

Engines do not return to the herd. Once an engine rises from its arc, it walks from arc to arc. It lies in the light for a while, feeds, rises again. Each time it rises it is larger and darker and slower, its armor thicker, its heat fiercer. It is concentrating further. The engine keeps refining itself.

And it walks inward.

This is the thing the Carried tap about least, because they know it least. But they know the direction. The herds migrate along the terraces, back and forth, turning at the rim. Engines do not turn. Their paths, traced by the blackened-and-regrown trails, radiate across the Shelf like spokes, and every spoke points the same way. They point uphill and inland, toward the high center of the plateau that nothing has reached.

## Glass

At every arc, over years, the basalt fuses. The standing column of lightning melts the rock in place and lets it cool, melts and cools, until there is a pool of green glass beneath each arc. The glass is clear as bottle-glass at the top and dark and cloudy deeper down. When an arc finally breaks and grounds itself somewhere else, the glass remains. The Shelf is scattered with these old pools, green eyes in the verdigris fur, some the size of a table and some the size of a field.

If you lie on one and look down into it, you see patterns in the glass.

They are branching lines, frozen in the green. They are the same shape as the discharges that crawl across the moss at dusk, the ones that flare, fade, and return in the same form. Every arc that has ever stood has recorded, layer by layer, the shapes of the lightning that passed through it. The deepest layers are dim, and the upper ones are sharp. In old pools the shapes repeat, one pattern over and over, a hundred feet down through the glass. The same branching figure appears at every depth, century after century, as if the current, every time it came to this place, followed the same path.

It raises the question of why the discharges return in the same shape. Lightning is not supposed to remember.

## The One in the Moss

Here the question of who lives on the Kettle Shelf grows larger than a visitor would want it to.

The coppermoss is one web. It might be one organism, or millions touching, and the distinction may not matter. Its filaments are metal. They carry current. They are laid in patterns, branching and rejoining, dense in some places and sparse in others, continent-wide. And the patterns of current that run through them are not random. The discharges at dusk return in the same shapes. The arcs record the same figures down a hundred feet of glass.

Anything that has a web of conductors, carries current through it in patterns, repeats those patterns, and keeps a record of them has everything a mind requires.

Suppose, then, that something lives in the moss. It does not live in it the way the Carried live in the coat. It lives as the moss, and as the current running through it. Suppose that every blue discharge crawling across the carpet at dusk is a thought, and the returning shape is that thought recurring. The mind thinks it again because it matters, or because it is how that mind thinks. Suppose that the glass pools are where its oldest thoughts are kept, burned into stone at the places where its attention concentrated hardest. Its memory, then, would be a scatter of green eyes across the plateau, looking down into itself.

What would such a mind be like?

It would run on the Shelf's two clocks at once. Its thoughts are instants, each a spark lasting a fraction of a second. But its body grows a finger's breadth a lifetime, and its memory is laid down in glass over centuries. It would be very fast and very slow. It would think a million quick thoughts about things that take a hundred years.

It would feel the herd. A grazer's hooves, striking the moss, would register as pressure and heat. The herd would register as a vast warmth moving across its body, the way a hand moving across your back is felt as a single presence and not a thousand points of contact. The herd's slow migration would be to it a slow caress, or a slow pain, or a tide. The herd eats it, after all. The herd scrapes it off the rock. But the herd also fertilizes it with slag and churns it, and the carpet comes back richer where the herd has passed. The mind in the moss might feel the herd as something it can't help needing and can't help losing to, the way a shore feels the sea.

It would feel the engines too, and it would feel them intensely. An engine is a knot of concentrated current walking across the web. When it lies on an arc, it drinks. When it walks, it burns the moss beneath it, which must hurt, if anything here can hurt. And the moss grows back doubled. The engine is the one part of the Shelf's life that the mind could feel the way it feels itself. It is conductive, charged, and patterned. Perhaps the mind in the moss senses the engines less as animals than as parts of itself that have stood up and begun walking.

Maybe that is what they are. Maybe when the grazer lies down in the arc and the current pours through its metal-heavy body, it is not only the grazer's armor that fuses. Maybe the current writes something into it. The arc gives it a pattern, the way it writes patterns into the glass. The engine that rises is then a grazer with a thought of the moss burned into it, and it walks the way the thought directs. If so, every engine is a sentence, and the long turbine drone it makes when it calls is that sentence spoken aloud in the only voice a moss-mind could give its messengers.

All of the engines are walking in the same direction.

## The Groan

There is one thing the mind in the moss cannot control, and everything on the Shelf knows it.

Every few days the tectonic groan comes. The pressure under the continent pushes, the plateau lifts a fraction of a fraction, and the sound rolls out of the ground, deeper than hearing. The whole Shelf goes still for it. The herd stops chuffing. The anvil-birds stop hammering. The wirebeetles freeze on the moss. The Carried flatten in the coats of the grazers and stop tapping.

For that moment the discharges stop too. The blue lines on the moss go dark. The arcs, which crackle continuously for weeks, dim and seem to hold their breath. Then the groan ends. New hair-thin fractures appear across the terraces. The noise resumes, and the lightning returns, perhaps a little changed.

Everything alive on the Shelf, including the one living in the web of the Shelf itself, stops and listens when the ground speaks.

The description of the land says that in places the stone still shows the ropy wrinkles of the lava that made it, frozen mid-pour, *as if the lava stopped a moment ago to listen.* Perhaps it did. Perhaps listening is the oldest thing on the Kettle Shelf. The rock did it first. Then the moss learned, and the herd, and the engines, and the small warm tenants of the grazers' coats, each of them stopping at the sound of the deep pressure below. It is the one voice on the plateau that nothing here makes.

## Toward the Center

So, who lives on the Kettle Shelf?

The Carried live in the grazers' coats. The Carried would say the herd lives on the Shelf and they live in the herd. The herd would say nothing. The engines are the herd's old, rising and burning and walking inward. The mind in the moss might be the one who truly lives here, the Shelf's own nervous system, thinking lightning and remembering glass.

But none of this explains what is at the center.

Metal moves upward. The Shelf is refining itself. The moss concentrates, the grazers concentrate, and the engines concentrate further, walking arc to arc, growing heavier and hotter and more armored. And every one of them walks inward, uphill, toward the high middle of the plateau that nobody has seen.

The Carried's oldest tapping-songs say something about that place. It is less a story than a direction and a feeling. Where the engines go, the songs say, it is warm all the way through, from the ground up and the sky down. The songs say the engines go there to be kept. They do not say what keeps them.

A mind in the moss would know the center differently. It would feel it as the place where all its current tends. It would be the lowest point in its field, the drain toward which the charge of a continent slopes. It might feel the center the way a body feels its heart, as the thing everything flows toward and away from.

And the pressure? The pressure beneath the continent, pushing up the Shelf a fingerbreadth a year and groaning every few days, is strongest there. That is where the land is rising from. That is where it is still arriving.

It is possible that the center of the Kettle Shelf holds nothing but rock. It might be a high, cold dome of basalt, the source of the flow, where the engines finally cool and stand like iron statues around a vent. It might be a place where the mind in the moss thinks its densest thoughts, and the ground is solid glass a thousand feet deep, green all the way down. It might be something the land is making.
