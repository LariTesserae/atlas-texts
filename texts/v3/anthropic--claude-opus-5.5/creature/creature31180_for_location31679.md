# Who Lives on the Shelf

## First, the Wrong Answers

The easy answer is that nobody lives there. The animals are residents, not inhabitants. The lampbacks work their shifts, the moths hatch and die inside a single breath, the stilt-hunters take their three steps. None of them seems to be *home*. They are more like parts of the works: a herd is a grazing mechanism, a moth-hatch is a fertilizing mechanism, a snail is a slow device for moving copper from the living layer to the dead one. You could call the Shelf empty the way a running mill at night is empty: full of motion, with no one in it.

The second easy answer is that something tends it. Every orderly thing seems to imply a keeper: the belts, the rings, the grazing shifts no herd decides on, the glass pipe laid out as if by a surveyor. You picture a foreman, something tall and patient walking the fields and checking the strokes of the vents. But nothing on the Shelf needs checking. The vents do not skip. The mill does not pause. A keeper would have nothing to do, and a thing with nothing to do does not stay a keeper for long.

So the keeper dissolves, and in the space where it stood, something else starts to take shape. It takes shape as a gap, because that is how it can be found.

## The Gap in the Field

On the Shelf, everything alive carries charge, and everything alive can feel the charge of everything else. Nothing is hidden. Predators and prey know exactly where each other are, and survival is a matter of timing.

To the stilt-hunter, whose whole world is fields, the Shelf at night is a lit map. Every lampback is a warm smear, every calf a small cold one, every cushion of wirewort a faint glowing mound. The creepmoss is a network of drifting sparks. The fulgurite grids hum under the peat like buried roads.

Across that map, in a few dozen places spread over forty miles, there are holes.

They are not dark spots. Dark would mean a weak field, and weak fields can be read. These are places where the field *stops*: small, rounded, entirely blank, as if someone had pressed a thumb into wet paint and lifted it away. A stilt-hunter walking toward one feels nothing, then keeps feeling nothing, then walks into something solid. It stands there for a long moment with one pale leg lifted, trying to understand an object that is not in the world.

These are the only concealed things on the Shelf. I will call them the **Slack**, after slack water, the still moment at the turn of a tide when the current stands and does not flow. The name will make more sense the longer you know them.

## What a Slack Is

At first you would take one for a stone.

It is about the size of a lampback, but it sits upright, broad at the base and narrowing to a rounded dome. It has no neck, only a gradual shouldering where a neck would be. It is the shape the spires have, the round-shouldered, wind-finished shape of something that has stopped changing in any way that matters. Its surface is black and wet, with the texture of peat, and it is covered in a close dark moss that is not creepmoss and never lights. Rime forms on it during the breaths the way it forms on everything. When the fog drains, the rime weeps off it like anything else. It is exactly the temperature of the ground. Wirewort is warm to the touch and a Slack is not. Laying your hand on one feels like laying your hand on the Shelf itself.

That is close to the truth. A Slack is grounded. The underside of its body does not end cleanly. It thins into a mass of fine wet filaments, pale as the roots in the fulgurite tubes, which run down into the peat and keep going, down past the living layer and into the cold saturated black where the snail shells lie in seams. Water fills every part of it, and the water is continuous with the water of the ground. Charge enters a Slack and passes straight through it into the earth, the way rain passes through a gravel bed. It cannot hold potential. It has no difference from its surroundings, and in a world that perceives through differences, it cannot be perceived.

When one moves, which is seldom, it pulls its filaments up out of the peat with a faint wet tearing that you would hear only if you were beside it. It slides forward a few feet on a muscular foot, like a snail's but broader, then sits and lets the filaments sink again. A Slack might cross a basin in a season. Most never cross one at all.

It has eyes. On the Shelf this is almost an eccentricity. Everything else navigates by field, and the Slack cannot, since fields pass through it the way they pass through wet stone. So it looks. Its eyes sit low on the front of the dome, two of them, large and round and black, and a layer behind them catches starlight and holds it. They look very much like the shallow basin pools after a breath, still and dark with stars in them. A Slack sees the Shelf the way you would: the blue of the moss, the lights of the herds, the violet of a strike rising from the rim. It is the only native creature that sees the aurora falling off the edge as light rather than as a feeling of draining.

It hears only what is close. In that thin air, close is all there is to hear: the crackle of a corona near its base, rime slumping off a reed, the small quick heartbeat of anything that comes near. Every other creature on the Shelf knows everything at once, at every distance, all the time. The Slack knows only what is within arm's reach, and it knows those things completely.

## What a Slack Does

Mostly, nothing.

This is the fact that sets them apart. The Shelf is always working: the breaths, the vents, the mill, the flow of current downhill to the rim. Every creature is shaped by the work and caught up in it. The lampback grazes its stripe, the moth combs the fog, the snail moves copper. The Slack does not work. It sits in the peat, drinks the weeping rime through its skin, and very slowly eats the peat it sits in, a few inches of black in a century. That is all.

But on the Shelf, sitting still has effects.

**Around a Slack, the order bends.** The sorting belts run parallel for miles, magnetite, garnet, quartz, until they reach a spot where one Slack has sat for three hundred years. There the stripes waver and curl around it, like wood grain around a knot. The wind sorts grit by weight, and the Slack disturbs the wind only slightly, but it does so for a very long time. The fulgurite grids show the same kinks. From above, the grids and belts are nearly perfect, and every imperfection is the mark of one of them. If you mapped the Shelf's flaws, you would have mapped the Slack, past and present.

**Around a Slack, the lights go out.** A corona crawling across the creepmoss reaches the edge of a Slack's filaments and simply ends, drained into the ground. The columns of light traveling like freight along the magnetized belts break around it and re-form on the far side. On a dry night each Slack sits inside a small ring of darkness in the drifting blue, so that the thing that cannot be seen by field is easy to see by eye. It is a round black absence with two points of starlight in its face.

**Around a Slack, the lightning passes through.** When a strike rises from the rim and forks inland along the copper veins, it sometimes finds a Slack in its path. Nothing living on the Shelf can take a strike. The Slack does not take it either. It lets the strike go by. The violet column enters the dome, passes through the wet body and the filaments, and spreads into the deep peat so quickly and completely that the Slack does not even steam. Afterward there is a ring of fused glass in the ground around it, a small fulgurite crown with the Slack sitting at its center, unchanged. Older ones sit inside many such rings, one within another, like the concentric blooms around the stroke vents. But those rings mark time on an eleven-minute clock, and these mark centuries.

## Mercy

This is where the Slack stop being a curiosity and start being necessary.

The Shelf has no concealment. That is its rule. The stilt-hunter always knows where the calf is, and the calf's only defense is timing: stay in the warm crowd, keep moving, never be the small cold shape at the edge of the field.

A calf that has lost the herd, one that strayed in a breath or woke late from a sleep in the moss, has nothing. It is a cold point on a lit map, and the stilt-hunter is already standing somewhere nearby with one leg raised.

Unless it finds a Slack.

A calf pressed against the side of a Slack becomes blurred. Its small charge bleeds away through the contact into the ground and is drawn down into the dark. It does not vanish entirely, but it dims and smears into the blank the way a candle flame blurs against a lamp. To the stilt-hunter the cold shape it was tracking thins and then is simply gone, folded into the one place on the Shelf it cannot read. It may stand there all night. It may take its three steps and meet only wet stone.

Lampbacks do not seem to know about this. Nothing decides anything on the Shelf. But calves find the Slack anyway, the way water finds a low place: they are drawn into the dark rings in the creepmoss because the dark feels, to a small frightened field, like somewhere to go. On any night, somewhere on the forty miles, there is a Slack sitting in its crown of old glass with a calf asleep against its side, both of them dripping rime, neither one visible to anything that hunts.

The Slack hears the calf's heartbeat, because the heartbeat is within arm's reach. That is exactly the range of everything the Slack can know. I don't think it does anything about the calf. I think it simply keeps sitting, which is all it has ever done and all that is needed.

The stilt-hunters, for their part, hunt a little less well than they would on a Shelf without Slack. There are fewer of them than there might be. There are more lampbacks. The famous balance of the Shelf, its regular grazing shifts and its herds in their steady proportions, is not the product of pure self-running order after all. It depends partly on a few dozen blind spots where the order breaks down and small things are spared.

## Where They Come From, and Where They Go

No one has seen a Slack born. It may happen too slowly to see. My guess is that one begins as something small and dark and cold in the deep peat, the way the snail seams begin: a place where the ground's wetness gathers itself and is slightly more continuous than the ground around it. It grows for centuries before it rises. It may never rise at all, and we would only know about the ones that do.

The spires, though, invite a different guess.

They are isolated, round-shouldered, and smooth, and they have settled into their shapes the way an old face settles. They have stopped eroding in any way that matters. They stand in exactly the places where the belts bend and the grids kink. At the foot of each one, when meltwater has cut the peat back far enough, you find ring after ring of fused glass going down into the dark, far more rings than the fields around it could account for.

The Slack live a very long time. Over that time they sit a little more, move a little less, and draw a little more of the ground up into themselves. The moss on the dome thins. The wet surface grows harder and slower to weep. The filaments go deeper. The eyes, perhaps, close, or silt over, or simply stop catching starlight and become stone. Eventually there comes a century when a Slack does not move at all, and then a thousand years when it does not move, and then it is a spire. It still grounds the lightning, still bends the belts, still holds a small dark ring in the drifting blue, and it still hides a calf on a bad night. It has stopped being alive in any way anything notices, but it has not stopped doing what it did.

If that is true, then the Slack are not recent. They are the oldest shapes on the Shelf, and the most permanent thing there is not the machinery but the pause within it.

## Slack Water

Here is the name again, and here is why it fits.

The Shelf is a tide. It breathes: the fog pours up over the rim and drains back, over and over. Current flows downhill and off the edge. The vents stroke, the mill grinds, the herds shift. Everything is flow and work and output, all of it running toward the curtains of light that fall into the cloud sea, where nothing collects them.

Every tide has a moment when it turns. The water has stopped coming in and has not yet begun going out, and for that moment it simply stands. Sailors call it slack water. It is the only time in the cycle when the sea is still.

The Slack are the Shelf's slack water, made into bodies and set down among the works. They do not work. They do not produce. They are the places where the current stands still and goes to ground instead of flowing onward, where the pattern loosens and the lights go out and a hunted thing can rest. You could call it a fault in the design. Or you could say that a machine running forever in the dark needs somewhere inside it where it stops, and the Slack are where the Shelf stops.

So when I asked who lives there, I meant who is at home. The lampbacks are passing through their shifts, the moths are passing through their breath, the current is passing through everything on its way off the edge. The Slack are not passing through anything. They are the only ones who stay.

## Standing There, Again

Suppose you do stand at the edge on a still night. The curtains fall beneath your feet toward the storm-floor. The basins behind you are full of drifting blue, the stars stand motionless, and there is no sound but your blood. You have the feeling, impossible to shake, that you are standing in a vast working plant with no owner, producing something for no one, forever.

Then, because your legs are tired from the thin air, you put your hand out to steady yourself on the round-shouldered rock beside you, the one you walked past without thinking.

It is exactly the temperature of the ground. It is wet. Under your palm, within arm's reach, where every sound on the Shelf happens, you feel something slowly draw in water through its skin and slowly let it go. You notice that the prickle in your fingertips, the faint charge that has been with you since you arrived, is draining out of your hand into it and down into the dark.

Two points of starlight sit low on its face. They have been watching the curtains, and now they turn toward you.

It will not do anything. Doing nothing is all it has ever done. But for as long as you stand there with your hand on it, you are the one thing on the Shelf nothing else can find, and the work goes on around you without touching you.
