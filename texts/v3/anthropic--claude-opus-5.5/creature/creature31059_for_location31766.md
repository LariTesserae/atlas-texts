# Who Lives in the Vaulting

## I. A woman on a knuckle

Begin with one of them, because that is how you would meet them if you met them at all: one at a time, suddenly, close.

She is crouched on a junction in the middle tiers near the edge of the Night, when the gray has gone out of the upper lattice and has not yet come back. You would not see her. You might feel her, as a faint warmth at the edge of a gap, or smell her: resin smoke, moth-down, the sour-milk smell of cured skirler leather. If you had been in the dark long enough to see the growing-point stars, you might notice that a small patch of them has gone out. It is the shape of a head and shoulders, and it is holding very still.

Her name, as near as letters can come to it, is Ennet. That isn't really her name. Her voice-name is a whistle, two notes falling a fifth, pitched high enough to cut through the Breathing. Her mother chose it the way everyone's name is chosen, by listening for an interval the nearby pipes don't already make, so that it would stand out against the wind. Her hand-name is a press at the heel of the palm and a slow drag to the tip of the middle finger. That is the name her family uses, and the one she will be called by at the end.

She is small. Almost everyone here is small. Big bodies fall harder, and they burn more of the little that can be eaten. Her arms are long for her height and her shoulders are heavy and roped. Her chest is deep and barrel-shaped from generations of breathing air that has too little in it. Her toes are long, nearly as clever as her fingers, and the soles of her feet are callused into something like horn, yellow and cracked. She can grip a cane-span with them and hang. Her eyes are the first thing you would notice if there were light. They are huge, the pupils so wide there is almost no iris, and in the gray of the half-light season she keeps them narrowed to slits and wears a strip of moth-gauze over them.

Her palms are soft, which is the strange part. The rest of her is weathered and hard, but her palms are pale, tender, and almost hairless. Every person in the Vaulting protects their palms. They climb with the fingers, the heel of the hand, and the feet, and they keep the centers of their hands soft because that is where they listen.

Tonight she is waiting for moths.

The moths come up out of the dark on a convection current she can feel against the backs of her legs. The Breathing has shifted into a long rising tone, and she knows what that means. She has a blowpipe the length of her arm and a pouch of darts. Each dart is a silica splinter with a bead of fresh resin at the tip, sticky enough to hold a moth and bring it down. But she doesn't use the pipe for moths. That would waste darts. She uses a net of cane-fiber stretched between two spurs, and she waits. When the cloud passes, the stars go out in a great slow shiver. The net fills. She feels it tugging, soft and many-bodied, and she starts rolling it closed.

She eats one right there. They taste of the sweet vapor they feed on, faintly smoky and faintly like crushed green stems. The rest she will press into cakes with resin and moss-starch, and the cakes will keep through the Night.

That is the first thing to know about who lives in the Vaulting. They live the way everything else here lives: by waiting at a junction for the air to bring them something.

## II. Of the Tilted Pool

Nobody here would say Ennet lives *in the Vaulting*. The Vaulting is too big to live in, the way the sky is too big to live in. They would say she is *of the Tilted Pool*, and that is where she lives.

The Tilted Pool is a hanging pond about four meters across, cradled in a junction where seven spans meet at odd angles. Over the generations the whole knot has slumped a little to one side, so the water sits lopsided in its resin bowl, deep at one end and a thin skin at the other. Three shelters are lashed into the crooks around it. The oldest has been overgrown so thoroughly that a young cane runs straight through its floor and out through its roof. The family simply lives around it. They hang things on it. Children learn to climb on it before they are allowed out onto open spans.

Kinship is counted by pools. Your pool feeds you, and your family is whoever drinks there. When two people of different pools make a household, the question is which water they will share, and the answer decides whose name the children carry. Sometimes the pool decides for them. A pool can fail. It can crack and drain into the dark, or dry out when the drip shifts, or be sealed over by the plant. When that happens the family becomes a family of nothing, and they have to go looking for water. They travel inward, following the old dark growth, until they find a pool with room, or one no one drinks from. Then they give themselves its name. There are families who have carried six or seven pool-names, the lost ones recited in order like ancestors.

Tilted Pool people are known for three things: good ears, bad tempers, and the size of their skirler roost. Twenty meters above the pool, a split tower-cane houses several hundred of the animals. Tilted Pool children are sent up to harvest guano from its lower chambers, which is filthy work. The guano fertilizes the moss beds the family scrapes onto old resin. Moss is bedding, lamp-wick, and famine food. So the family's life rests, fairly literally, on skirler droppings, and the other pools make jokes about it.

Every pool has a character like this, a set of things it is known for, a way of talking. Pools two kilometers apart can barely understand each other's spoken words. Everyone shares palm-speech, though. Palm-speech moves slowly. The Vaulting is too loud for voice to be reliable and too dark for anything visual to spread, so touch became the common language.

## III. The pipe-cutters

Ennet was not born a moth-netter. Her real trade, the one she was trained in by her mother's brother, is cutting.

Remember the rule: *never cut a living cane without cause.* The pipe-cutters are the people with cause.

The borers make most of the Vaulting's music by accident. The people make some of it on purpose. A pipe-cutter knows how a cane of a given width and length will sound if its node-membrane is pierced here, or here, or split just so along a growth ring. She knows how the tone changes when the Breathing rises, and how it carries along certain spans and dies against others. With a flaked silica awl and a very steady hand she can open a cane so that it sings a particular interval, in a particular wind, for years.

These are the whistle-lines. Across the middle tiers, strung along routes no stranger would recognize as routes, there are chains of deliberately tuned canes, each placed within earshot of the next. A pipe-cutter can close a cane by stopping its hole with a plug of resin, or open it by pulling the plug, and change what the line says. The messages are simple. *Moths rising.* *Pool failed.* *Death at the Tilted Pool.* *Storm from the pole side.* But they travel faster than any climber, and they ride the Breathing itself. At the right time of the Breathing, news from forty kilometers away arrives in an afternoon.

The old cutters can do more than send signals. They can hear the whole lattice the way a herder reads a flock. When Ennet's uncle was dying, he lay in the overgrown shelter with his hand flat on the cane that runs through its floor and told her things. A span had failed a tier below. The drip had shifted west. There was a new roost in a cane near the Hooked Pool, because the pitch of one pipe had dropped where the chamber behind it had filled with bodies. He said the Vaulting was always talking about itself and most people simply didn't listen.

The pipe-cutters are respected, and a little feared. Cutting a cane is exactly what the plant is said to punish. The cutters say it doesn't, not if you do it properly. A clean cut in the right place is like a borer-hole, something the plant accepts and even welcomes, because it lets breath move through the inner chambers. The cutters say the plant can tell a careless cut from a careful one. Some of them go further and say the plant can tell a cut made for a line from a cut made for a single person's convenience, and that the lines last because the lattice *wants* them. Other people say that's just what cutters would say.

## IV. The knuckles

There is no burial here, and nothing is burned. Burning would waste resin, and there is no earth to bury anyone in.

When someone of the Tilted Pool dies, the family carries the body inward to one of the old strong junctions where the resin is thick. They wrap it in moss, with the hands folded palm-up and open. They press it into the crook where the spans meet, and they leave it.

The plant takes it. Fresh resin flows around the body over months, slowly, like honey moving across a table. Lichens colonize the surface. Within a few years it is a knuckle, a swelling in the junction, amber-dark and hard as bone. The shape of a person is barely visible inside, and only if you know to look. The oldest junctions in the inward growth are knotted with dozens of these. The people call such places *hands*, because the dead lie in them palm-up.

The people say, without much sentiment, that part of the ground here is made of them. It is true. The hanging pools collect in resin bowls, and some of that resin has swallowed a grandmother. When a Tilted Pool child crosses the Mother-Hand junction on the way to the skirler roost, she steps over her great-great-grandmother's knee.

This is also how the people know the plant accepts them. It doesn't close over the dead the way it closes over a careless cut. It takes them in, gently, the way it takes in fallen spans and dead climbers and drifts of dead moths in the crooks. The dead become junction. The junctions hold the lattice together, and the lattice holds the pools.

When palm-speech was invented, and nobody knows when, the sign for *dead* was a hand pressed flat into the partner's palm and held there. It is the same as the sign for *strong ground*.

## V. The Night

In the Night, Tilted Pool people go inside the canes.

Not into their shelters. Those are too thin for the deepest cold. They climb inward and down a little, to where the canes are as wide as a house, and they cut through a node-membrane into one of the great hollow chambers where the plant's slow respiration keeps the air a few degrees above killing. They seal the hole behind them with resin and moss. They sleep in the plant's breath.

It is dark in there in a way the outer Vaulting never is. The growing-point stars are gone. The air smells heavy and sweet, and the whole chamber hums faintly with the Breathing. Families sleep in heaps with the children in the middle. They wake for a few hours at a time, eat a moth-cake, drink from skins filled at the pool before the freeze, press a few words into each other's palms, and sleep again.

They are living as the node-borers live, in the warm chambers, eating what they stored. They know it. Some of the oldest palm-stories say the people *were* borers once, or were taught by them, or were given the chambers because the borers are the plant's children and the people asked to be adopted. The borers share the chambers without complaint. You can hear them chewing in the next compartment, a slow patient crunch.

Not everyone wakes. The Night is long, and the air in a sealed chamber is thin and sweet and heavy with the plant's vapor, and some old people and some babies simply keep sleeping. The people say those ones went further in.

When the gray returns to the upper tiers, the families cut their way out again. They are thin and blinking, their eyes enormous, and the meltwater is already starting to fall.

## VI. The ones who went outward, and the ones who went up

Not everyone lives in the dark strong growth near the pools.

A few people live out at the edges, among the black spurs. The pool-people call them *spur-folk* and don't entirely trust them. The spurs are brittle and dangerous, but they're also where the climbers graze and where the silica for tools is freshest. The new growth is where the Vaulting is changing, so it's where new pools form and new routes open. Spur-folk are wanderers and scouts. They are the first to see the plant reach somewhere new. They carry tools, silica, and news inward in exchange for resin and moss-cake and a place to sleep in the Night. They are lean even by Vaulting standards and famous for their jumps. They talk fast and laugh loudly, and most of them die young, in the long clattering way.

Then there are the stories of people who went up.

No one goes to the crown. That's the rule. But some say there were people who went anyway, a long time ago. They climbed until the air was too thin to breathe and then kept climbing, and they changed. They got thinner and paler. Their chests grew even larger, and they stopped needing to eat. In the stories they live among the matted fronds, in a cold gray light no one below has ever seen, and they are so light that they drift. Some pool-people swear they have seen them: pale shapes moving among the moth clouds during the rising currents, too large to be moths, too silent to be skirlers.

Ennet does not believe this. But once, in the half-light, she saw a moth cloud part around something she couldn't see, and she has thought about it since.

## VII. What booms in the roots

And then there are the Gulpers.

The people will tell you honestly that they don't know what the Gulpers are. They will then tell you, at length, several things they believe.

One story says the Gulpers are climbers that went down instead of out. Generations of them grew huge in the dark among the tower-canes, eating the root-buttresses and whatever falls, and they swell their throats to boom the way frogs do. Another says they are skirlers that have forgotten how to fly and grown as big as shelters, hunting by sound, and that the boom is a hunting call pitched to shake prey loose from the spans. A third, the one old people tell children, is that the Gulpers are the ones who keep the Vaulting's pipes. The deepest canes, the tower-canes, are too big to sound in any ordinary wind. The Breathing is those canes sounding, and the Gulpers make them sound. They live inside the great bass pipes and blow.

Ennet's uncle, the pipe-cutter, had his own version, and it is the one that has stayed with her.

He said he had listened to the Breathing for sixty years, and the Breathing is not only wind. Wind rises and falls with weather. The Breathing, he said, has *habits*. It goes quieter before a tremor. It changes pitch when a big span falls somewhere in the deep tiers, the way a sleeper shifts at a noise. Sometimes it goes silent for a moment, all the bass pipes at once, and then comes a Gulper's boom, a single wet cavernous note from one direction, and after it the Breathing starts again.

He didn't say what the Gulpers were. He said: *something down there is listening the way I listen, and it is much bigger than me, and it is answering the plant.*

The people don't go down, and they don't say much more. The skirlers go silent when the Gulpers sound, and so, for that one moment, do the people.

## VIII. The one that lives here

All these beings live in the Vaulting: drift-moths and skirlers, borers, climbers, Gulpers, the people of the Tilted Pool, the spur-folk, and perhaps the pale drifting things in the crown.

But ask the question again more slowly, *who lives here?*, and the answer shifts.

The Vaulting isn't really a place. It is an inhabitant. It lives in the mountains the way Ennet lives at her pool. It arrived at some point, as a seed or a spore or something stranger. It found the young black rock and the cold and the thin air, and it settled in. It split the basalt with its roots. It is still building its house around itself and still moving in. The mountains are its place. It is the thing that lives in them.

It is also a place. It is a house that other things live in, and it seems to know they are there. It closes over careless cuts and accepts careful ones. It thickens its spurs against the climbers. It takes in the dead. It lets the borers chew because their tunnels help it breathe, and it seems to let the people cut pipes for the same reason. The people have been in it so long that some of its ground is made of them. Their shelters look like its seed-cases, and it has grown straight through their walls without breaking them.

It is not quiet about any of this. It speaks, if it speaks at all, in the only language its body has. It speaks in pressure: sap stirring in a cut span, a tremor passing up through the lattice, a slow thickening of growth over years. It speaks in touch.

And the children of the Vaulting learn palm-speech before voice.

## IX. A tremor

Late in the half-light, one of the mountains shrugs.

Ennet is on a junction near the Tilted Pool when it comes. She feels it first in her feet, a hum rising through the horn of her soles, and then the whole lattice rings, every cane at once, a single struck note for a long ringing second. The moths lift. The skirler roost above her pours out of its split cane like smoke, screaming. Somewhere below, and then somewhere farther, dead spans let go and come clattering down through the dark, ringing off junction after junction, the echoes chasing each other through the empty tiers.

She does the thing her uncle taught her. She kneels and lays her open palm flat against the old cane that runs through the overgrown shelter's floor, the one she learned to climb on before she was allowed out onto the spans.

The ringing fades. The Breathing, which fell silent at the tremor, begins again low in the dark.

Under her palm the cane is warm. It is always a little warm; the plant breathes. But now, for a moment, she feels something more than warmth. Sap stirs in the span under her hand, a slow pulse rising from below and passing on upward, the way it stirs when a cane is cut and the whole plant seems to know. Nothing has been cut. It is just the plant checking itself after the tremor, sending sap up through every span to find the breaks.

Or it is the sign pressed flat into her palm and held there, the sign for *dead* and for *strong ground*. It means *I am still here. I am still holding.*

She presses back.

Then she stands, finds the growing-point stars that mark the way to the moth currents, and goes to see what has fallen.
