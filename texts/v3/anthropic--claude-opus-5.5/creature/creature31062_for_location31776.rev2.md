# Those Who Sit Between

## A Hand on the Stone

Begin with a hand laid flat on rock.

This is how people on the Hightable greet one another. They greet a threshold the same way, and the morning, and the dead. You lower yourself a little, knees bent, and press your palm to the ground until you feel the hearth-warmth come up into it. Then you rise and look at whoever is in front of you. Nothing is said for a moment, because nothing needs to be. You have both touched the same warmth, and that is the introduction.

They do not have a word for *to live* that is separate from their word for *to sit*. "Where do you sit?" means *where is your home*. "She sat for ninety years" is an obituary. "Sit well" is what you say to a child leaving on the cable. Sitting is the natural posture of a life here: settled on the warm stone and under the cold sky, occupying the gap where one becomes the other. They call themselves, when they need a word for all of themselves at once, the Seated.

They are easy to describe, because they have been shaped by the place so plainly.

## Their Bodies

The Seated are deep-chested. Their ribs are broad, their lungs large, their hearts heavy and slow, because the air they breathe is so thin that a lowlander would be gasping on the mesa tops within minutes. They are not tall. They are dense, like their buildings, and they move with an economy that a stranger would read as weariness until he saw one of them climb a quarry face without pausing.

Their skin is dark, a deep red-brown shading to near-black. Almost no atmosphere stands between them and the small white sun, and its light, though it carries no warmth, carries everything else. Their eyes are hooded under heavy lids, set deep beneath a brow that shades them. Every adult has a fan of fine lines at the outer corners from a lifetime of narrowing against glare. Those lines are considered beautiful. A face without them looks unfinished, like a child's or a sick person's, or like someone who has spent too long indoors.

Their noses are long and narrow, and they breathe through them always. The long passage warms the thin air going in and reclaims moisture going out, so each breath gives back a little of what the last one took. To breathe through the open mouth wastes water, and among the Seated *open-mouthed* is a real insult. It means a spendthrift, a person who lets things escape. Children are corrected for it the way lowland children might be corrected for leaving a door open in winter.

They sweat very little. Their bodies hold water the way the gypsum does, locked in and released only under heat. At the end of a long day a kiln-worker's skin is dry as paper and faintly dusted white with salt.

There is metal in them. Paver flesh is eaten rarely, since a paver is more valuable as ore than as meat, but it is eaten, and the fungal cellars are fed on guano rich in what the chimney-swarms concentrate. Over a lifetime, iron and copper and manganese settle into the Seated's bones and teeth. Very old people have teeth with a faint dark sheen, like pavement varnish. The Seated say that the old are becoming more like the ground, and they say it fondly.

## How They Speak

Sound dies within a few metres on the Hightable, so the voice is an intimate instrument. To speak aloud to someone, you have to come close enough that you could touch them. Voice therefore means nearness. It is used between family, between friends, between lovers, and in courts and bargains where closeness is part of the seriousness.

Everything else is said with the hands.

The hard, even light makes gesture legible at great distances. There is no haze to soften a moving finger, and every shadow is crisp. A hand-sign made at one end of a mesa-top courtyard can be read perfectly at the other, and a skilled signer can hold a conversation across a canyon. Public speech on the Hightable is silent. When a city gathers to decide something, the assembly hall is full of people and completely quiet except for the low hum of the engines, while speakers on a lit platform argue with their whole arms. A great orator is someone with beautiful hands. A cruel remark is sharper for being seen.

Between mesas, over dozens of kilometres, they flash. Every city has a mirror-gallery on its highest point, where polished glass plates catch the sun and throw messages across the yardang country in pulses. On a clear afternoon the horizon winks. The cable brings the long news, the goods and letters and people. The mirrors bring the short news, the price of water, a birth, the first sighting of the Drift.

Shadow has its own grammar. A step out of the light here really does feel like stepping into another room, and the Seated treat it that way. Shade is privacy. Two people who stand together in the shadow of a single condenser-fin have withdrawn from the world, and it is rude to watch them. "To share shade" means to confide, or to court. Houses are built with deep, sharp-edged porches so that there is always a room of darkness just outside the door. Someone who wants to be alone for a while simply stands in one.

## How They Keep Time

They are slow. They are not lazy or dull, but they are slow in the way the machines are slow, because hurry buys nothing when the heat will still be there tomorrow. A mason may spend a year on a single stair and consider it quick work. A dispute over quarry rights may be argued for a decade, and nobody thinks the arguers unreasonable. Anger among the Seated rises very slowly and fades more slowly still, so a grievance can outlast both people who began it. Love works the same way. Courtships last years, and few people think that strange.

Their lives are counted in Drifts. You are born in the plenty after one Drift, you come of age around your second, you take up your trade by your third, and a person who has seen eight Drifts pass over has lived a long and fortunate life. Because children come most often in the fat years after the storm, when the herds swell and the cellars are full, each Drift leaves a generation behind it. These are the *Drift-kin*, a cohort of children born within a year or two of each other and raised together in the city's nurseries. Loyalties among the Drift-kin often run deeper than those of blood. When someone dies, their Drift-kin gather. When the last of a Drift-kin dies, the city marks it: an entire generation has gone up.

## How They Go Up

The dead are taken to the kilns.

The Seated do not think this is grim, and they would find the lowland alternatives almost unbearably wasteful: burying water in the ground, or burning it into nothing. A body is mostly water, and water is stone, and stone is what they live on. So the dead are laid in the retorts at night, in the galleries where the gypsum is baked, and before dawn their water rises with everything else. It goes up the condensers and beads on the fins in the dark. It runs down into the cisterns with a faint musical dripping.

"She went up at dawn," they say. Or: "He is in the cistern now." Everyone knows that every cup contains a little of everyone who sat before them. They drink it with a small, private attention that a lowlander would never notice.

So the morning plume, standing vertical in the still air and visible for a hundred kilometres, is many things. It is the city's industry and its wealth, and it is also its funeral. When a traveller on the pavement sees the white thread of a distant city at sunrise, they lift a hand toward it, palm out, the way you would acknowledge someone passing on a stair. Whatever else is in that plume, someone is going up in it.

The dry remainder, the minerals and the bone-ash, goes down the cables to the fungal cellars, where it enriches the beds. The Seated eat what grows there. Nothing is lost if it can possibly be kept.

## Their Trades

A city is made of many kinds of sitting, and each trade shapes its people differently.

**The kiln-folk** work in the retort galleries, in the one place on the Hightable where the air is wet and hot. They shave their heads and wear ceramic masks with long nose-channels, and they read the colour and weight of steam as fluently as others read hands. They are the nearest thing the cities have to priests, because they handle the dead and the water, and because they know better than anyone how much of the mesa is left.

**The walkers** herd pavers. A paver-drive lasts a season or more, and a walker moves at paver pace for the whole of it, a few hundred metres on a good day. Walkers are famous for coming back changed. After months beside the herd they think in the herd's rhythm, and on returning to the city they need weeks to *speed back up*, as it is said, before they can hold an ordinary conversation. They seem half-asleep, then suddenly present, as if arriving from very far off. Walkers never kill pavers. They gather shells after natural death, lift them onto their backs, and carry them home like armour made of coins. They know the stilt-hunters better than anyone. It is walkers who taught the habit of counting the rocks around you, and counting again later.

**The tower-keepers** live alone at the foot of the updraft towers on the open pavement. Their towers are wreathed in chimney-swarms, and they harvest guano from the collector floors. Many of them stop using their voices entirely, since there is no one near enough to speak to, and they develop private hand-languages that even other Seated struggle to read. They are said to talk to the swarms. They do not deny it.

**The cable-riders** carry what the mirrors can't. They spend days at a time in the enclosed cars, creeping at walking pace high above the yardang corridors, sleeping over the gaps and waking over new country. A rider's arrival is a small festival. The rider brings letters, glass, rumour, and sometimes a stranger from a city so far away that its customs seem exotic, a place where they greet with the left palm, or bury their dead's hair, or let children speak aloud in public. Riders are the only Seated who are regularly in a hurry, and they are gently mocked for it.

**The lens-wardens** tend the great clear eyes of the lens-towers, where the thin hard sunlight is gathered to a point too bright to look at. Most wardens are blind in one eye by middle age. This is so common that a single clouded eye is the sign of the trade. Children who see a warden in the street touch their own eyelid in respect.

## What They Know

Every city is eating its own mountain, and every city knows it.

This is not a secret or a fear, exactly. It is something closer to a shape the mind has learned to hold. Children are taught to read the cistern-marks, and they are also taught the quarry-marks, the notches cut into the pillars of the hollow halls that record how far the digging has gone in each generation. They know roughly how many generations remain before the shelf their city sits on becomes too thin. In the oldest cities that number is small. There is a word for the last generation that will sit in a place. It is not a sad word, though it is not a happy one either. It is the word you would use for the final turn of a flywheel before it comes to rest.

When a city's mesa is spent, the city moves. The move takes centuries. First a few families settle on a new mesa along the cable line, then a kiln, then a condenser, and then, over many lifetimes, the old city empties toward the new one the way water drains from one cistern into another. The abandoned halls are left to the kites, who roost in them by the thousands and fill the great pillared dark with papery sound. Some of the ruins that travellers mistake for ventifact fields, and some of the ventifact fields they mistake for ruins, are old cities that have been left long enough to be worn back toward stone.

This is the deepest habit of mind among the Seated: nothing stays, but nothing needs to hurry either. A city is a slow fire. It burns its mountain down, and it does so decently.

---

That is the easy answer to who lives on the Hightable. It is the one the Seated themselves would give you first, out of politeness. But ask a walker, somewhere out on the deep pavement weeks from any city, with the herd grinding softly past, and they may give you a different answer.

## The Reckoners

The walkers say the Seated are guests.

What they mean is that the Reckoners were here first, and are still here, and will be here after. Nobody knows this for certain. It is only the feeling of standing near one.

A Reckoner is large, larger than any other creature on the plateau, long-bodied and many-legged. It is plated in slabs that look exactly like weathered slate: faceted, polished by sand, glazed faintly with varnish. Up close you can see that the varnish grows on them, as it grows on the pavement, which means they stand still long enough for it to take hold. The oldest ones have the dark bronze sheen of ancient stones. Pavers have been seen grazing on their flanks.

They do not come near cities. They do not gather. Each one keeps to the deep pavement, far from the plumes, and a walker might see three in a lifetime. They seem to feed on nothing, and the belief that they live on the hearth itself, pressing their broad undersides to the warm ground and drinking heat directly, is so widely held that it is taught as fact. It may be. When a Reckoner stands still, the ground beneath it is said to be cooler than the ground around it. Walkers who have laid a palm there swear to it.

And they arrange stones.

The arrangements are circles, spirals, and long straight lines, sometimes kilometres of them, pebble after pebble lifted from the pavement and set down with a precision no mason would be ashamed of. The lines always run on the bearing of the yardangs, which is the bearing of the wind. A Reckoner may take years over a single figure. It may stand motionless for a Drift beside a half-finished spiral, then move one stone, then stand again.

*Do not move the Reckoners' stones.* Every child learns it before they learn to read cistern-marks. Asked why, most adults say simply that it is bad luck, or that it angers them. Nobody has ever seen a Reckoner angered.

Here is what the walkers know, and mostly don't say.

The oldest lines, the ones so deeply varnished that they have nearly merged back into the pavement, do not quite run on the yardang bearing. They are off by a degree, sometimes two. The newer lines run true. Lay them side by side on a map, as a few patient people have done over several lifetimes, and a slow turning appears. The wind has changed direction, very slightly, over spans of time that make a city's life look like a single breath. The Reckoners have been keeping track.

The kiln-folk have something to add, though they add it quietly. The bores that bring the hearth-heat up to the kilns have to be deepened every few centuries. This is usually put down to scaling in the pipes, and usually that is the reason. But some of the oldest Reckoner circles, measured with great care, are wider than the newer ones, and the spacing between turns of their spirals narrows as the spirals approach the present. Something is being counted down.

No one on the Hightable believes the hearth will go cold. It is the one constant of their world, the warmth under every palm and every sleeping body, the thing *nothing is fast* is built on. The Reckoners may be the only creatures on the plateau keeping the account in which it doesn't last forever. They are called Reckoners because of the stones, but the name has come to mean more than that to the people who have spent time near them. They are the ones who reckon, who keep the long tally, who know the sum.

There is one more thing, and walkers tell it only to other walkers, late, in shade.

Sometimes the very old walk out. It is usually a walker, near the end of a long life. They leave the last paver-drive they will ever join and simply keep going, past the herd, onto the deep pavement where the Reckoners wander, carrying nothing. The Seated do not stop them. It is understood as a kind of going up, only sideways, out across the stone rather than into the plume. Usually they are found later, sitting, palm down on the warm ground, and they are carried home to the kilns.

Sometimes they are not found.

No one has ever seen a Reckoner die. What the walkers say, and do not quite believe, and do not quite stop saying, is that no one has ever seen a young one either. There is a story, repeated from walker to walker through more Drifts than anyone can count, of a Reckoner seen far out on the pavement with a ring of worn copper, a herder's ring, pressed deep into a seam between two of its plates, half-grown over with slate.

It is only a story. But it is why the Seated who know it do not move the stones, even the ones that seem to have been set down by nothing, far from anything: a single pebble on its end, or a short line of three. It might be someone's last sentence, and it would be ill-mannered to interrupt.

## Under

Past the Reckoners, past everything, the ground ends. At the escarpment the stepped cliffs drop into the cloud sea, and whatever lives there lives out of sight.

The Seated do not go down. They have no rule forbidding it, or no written one. It is simply that the Hightable is the world, and the edge is where the world stops, the way the rim of a table is where the table stops. But they know something is there, because it feeds them. Every thermal kite, every chimney-swarm, every fattened paver after a Drift, every cellar bed enriched with guano: all of it rests, at the root, on the dust that rises out of the cloud.

Walkers who have stood at the edge in a strong updraft describe a smell. It comes up in the lowland air along with the pollen and spores and drifting mites, and it is unlike anything on the plateau. The Seated have had to invent a word for it, and the word has no meaning except that smell. It comes from a colour they have never seen. People who have smelled it say they miss it afterward, which makes no sense, since they have never had it.

On clear nights, the cloud sea glows from beneath.

Some say it is another hearth, deeper and hotter than theirs, shining up through the cloud. Some say it is the lowland's own varnish, living light instead of living bronze. And some, mostly the children and the very old, say that it is a city, or many cities. They imagine a people down there under a pale lid of cloud, with their own slow engines and their own lights coming up at dusk, looking up at the white ceiling of their world and never guessing that anyone sits on top of it. They say the Seated see the lowlanders' lamps as that faint glow, and the lowlanders see the Seated as nothing at all, or perhaps as a rumour: *something lives above the clouds; we do not go there.*

Nobody knows whether anything the Seated do ever goes down. They take the lowland's dust and give nothing back that they know of. It troubles some of them. There is a small custom, followed by walkers and no one else, of carrying a single cup of cistern-water to the edge and pouring it over into the updraft, where it scatters into nothing before it falls a metre. It is not an offering, they say. It is a thank-you letter written in a language that probably no one can read.

## Who Lives Here

Ask for the whole answer, and the Seated will give you one more word.

Their oldest word for a person, older than *the Seated*, older than the cities, survives only in the mouths of kiln-folk and in the inscriptions on the deepest quarry pillars. It does not mean *one who sits*. It means something closer to *a place the warmth passes through*.

Once you hear it, you can see the idea everywhere. The hearth-heat rises from the thin old crust and escapes into the black sky, and everything alive here stands, for a while, in its path. The pavers are warmed from beneath and cooled from above. The kites hang in columns of rising air. The stilt-hunters stand so long among the ventifacts that they take on the ground's temperature. The Reckoners press their bellies to the stone and drink. The Seated sleep on warm floors under blankets that hold the heat down onto their bodies, and their skin gives that same heat back to the stars. Every body on the Hightable is a small crossing: warm inside and cold outside, with a life lived at the boundary.

That is what the greeting means, the hand flat on the stone. It isn't *hello*. It is closer to *I am standing in it too*. You feel the warmth come up into your palm, pass through you, and leave by your face and your breath and the top of your head, out into the indigo, gone. You rise and look at the other person, who has felt the same thing, and you both know that neither of you is holding it. You are only in the way of it for a while.

So who lives on the Hightable?

The Seated, in their stone cities, eating their mountains decently and going up at dawn. The walkers at paver pace, the one-eyed wardens, the silent tower-keepers, the riders in their creeping cars. The pavers like dropped coins, the kites in their spirals, the stilt-hunters, the steam-drinkers in rows along the chimneys. The Reckoners, who keep the one account no one else is keeping. Whoever sits beneath the cloud with lights on.

And under all of them, moving through all of them, the one resident that never stops: the warmth itself, on its long way out of the world. Everything else here has learned to live by standing in its way.

## Dusk, Far Out

The small white sun touches the cloud sea. The varnish burns copper across a thousand kilometres and then goes dark. The kites come down and fold themselves in their grey cloaks on the mesa flanks. A hundred kilometres off, a city's lamps come up amber, and the cisterns begin to drip.

Out on the deep pavement, farther from any plume than most people ever go, a Reckoner that has stood still for four years lowers one leg, lifts a single pebble, and sets it down at the end of a line. It lies one degree truer to the wind than the stone before it.

Beside the line, its palm flat on the ground as if in greeting, something sits that might once have been a rock, or a walker, or both. The varnish has begun to take on it.

The ground beneath it is warm, though very slightly less warm than it used to be. Only the Reckoner is counting.
