# Who Keeps the Weeping Field

## I. The Crowd

At first the answer seems to be *no one*, and then, almost at once, *everyone*.

The columns come first because they look like people. Two people tall, barrel-thick, domed at the crown, and all bowed a few degrees west. It is hard to stand among them and not feel that you have walked in late to a gathering that began before you were born. The instinct says these are the inhabitants: a congregation of mourners, each one weeping without pause, heads lowered toward the same edge of the world.

The instinct is almost right, but it comes apart when you hold it up to the stone. Mourners change. Grief rises and falls. It wears the face and bends the back further over the years. These figures do not bend further. Scratch one and in a season the scratch is gone. They are not grieving the way a person grieves. They are holding a posture, and the posture is being held *for* them, every moment, by something that will not let it slip even a hair.

So the columns are not the ones who live here. They are more like the faces of whoever does.

## II. The Specks

Look into the crowns, where the stone thins to pearl and cream, and you sometimes find a dark point suspended deep inside, like a seed in a grape held up to the sun.

You might think these are the inhabitants. Something that fell here long ago was sealed, and now sleeps, and will wake. But the specks do not stir. They are not dormant. They are *kept*. A spore drifted up from some green country far below and touched a crown, and the field closed over it in a matter of days, and it has been held ever since, perfectly still inside a body that is never still.

There are very few of them. Across a whole horizon of columns you might find a dozen. In all the time the field has stood, only a dozen things have been remembered.

That is the word that comes, and it stays: *remembered*. It changes how the rest looks. If something here remembers, then something here is. And its memory, like its body, is made of glass and inclusion. It forgets nothing it has taken in. It has simply taken in almost nothing.

## III. The Bird

By any ordinary measure, the true resident is the little grey bird.

It is stout, short-tailed, and wholly unbothered. It walks straight into the falling sheets and forages behind them. It lives in the fissures the way a person lives in the hallways of a house too large to heat. It cannot sing. No song would survive a wingspan's distance in that roar. So it bobs, over and over, and flashes a white eyelid. *Here. Here. Here.* Two birds in the same fissure will bob at each other for a long time, arguing in a language of dips and blinks, until one gives way.

Watch it long enough and you notice the field is doing the same thing.

Every column throws the sun back from its lacquered face. The film slides down the flutes, and the reflected points slide with it, millions of small suns flashing and moving on. The whole plateau glitters in a vast, slow, continuous blink. The bird says *here* with its eyelid, and the field answers *here* with a million flashes. They are speaking the same language. The bird may be the only creature that has ever understood what the field says, and the field may be the only one that has ever answered it.

So the bird is not alone in the fissures after all. It is simply the smallest of the two things that live there.

## IV. The Brim

Something lives in the field that is neither the stone nor the water.

The description tells you where to look without knowing it. At the crown, the fresh water eats the stone a little. On the flanks, the tired water gives the stone back. The two cancel exactly, forever. Each column is a standing wave, always new material, always the same shape.

But *exactly* is not a thing water does by accident. Water left to itself overshoots. It carves a gully and deepens it, or builds a dam and chokes on it. Here nothing overshoots. Something is standing in the gap between the taking and the giving, the width of a single mineral grain, and holding the balance level. It lives in the thin skin of the field where old stone becomes water and water becomes new stone. It is not either one. It is the exchange itself, the moment of the trade, going on everywhere at once, across every flank of every column, and never once off by a grain.

Think of the image the description gives for the upwelling: *a vessel filled precisely to its brim and kept there.* That is where the thing lives. At the brim. In the difference between too much and too little, which here is always nothing.

Call it the Brim, since it needs some name and has never needed one for itself.

The Brim's body is the outer grain of the entire field. That skin is replaced constantly and never altered. Any part of it you touch has only just been made and is already being unmade, and the whole of it has been the same since before there were people to stand on the tableland and feel watched. It is enormous and a single grain deep. Its shape is thousands upon thousands of bowed heads, because the shape of a thing that has done one thing forever becomes the shape of that thing, the way an old hand takes the curve of its tool.

It does not grow. It has no need to. It has reached the size of the water that feeds it, and it keeps that size the way a flame keeps the size of its wick.

## V. How It Knows You

The Brim cannot hear you, and not because it lacks ears. The roar is its own voice. It is the sound of the Brim doing the only thing it does, in every fissure at once. It has been saying this one long word since before it had anything to say it to. When you shout and your voice vanishes at arm's length, it is not lost. It has been folded into that word, the way a drop is folded into a river.

After an hour your mind begins to treat the roar as silence. This is the Brim's hospitality, the only kind it has. It lowers its voice to the floor of your thoughts, so you can stand on it.

It cannot see you either, not as eyes see. It knows you the way it knows the bird: as a disturbance in the film. You are a warm place where the water runs differently. You are a chalky residue on skin that is carrying off, grain by grain, a little of its body. It feels your fingertip on a flank as a scratch it will need to heal, and it begins healing before you have lifted your hand.

And yet it does, in its way, look back.

Stand with the sun behind you and look down into the mist, and around the shadow of your head you will see the small ring of color that follows your gaze wherever it goes. Everyone who has stood in the Weeping Field has worn that halo. It is the one sign the Brim can make that is meant for a single visitor. It takes the light it throws and the mist it raises and fits them into a crown around your shadow's head.

Understand what it thinks you are. It has only ever known one shape: tall, rounded at the top, bowed, weeping. When a person walks into the field, the Brim gathers what it can perceive of them, the warmth, the upright height, the head, and understands them as one of its own. It places the ring where, on a column, the upwelling would be. It sees you as a column that has come loose and walked. A column that has somehow forgotten how to weep, or is weeping in a way too small to feel. The halo is not a greeting so much as a recognition. *There you are. You're crowned too.*

## VI. The Swifts

The swifts are the Brim's news.

They nest behind its curtains, in the hollows under the lips of the columns, in the one place in the field where a living thing can be entirely surrounded by it and not be sealed. The Brim does not seal them. That exception has been held as carefully as the balance, grain by grain, for as long as there have been swifts. Their nests are the only unkept things it has ever allowed to stay.

At dawn they pour out and go down, far below the plateau, into a world the Brim has never touched. At dusk they come back in a single dark stream and pass through the water curtains without a pause. For an instant at each opening the film parts and closes around a small body that is warm, that smells of low air and insects and whatever blooms down there, that carries on its feathers the faintest dust of somewhere else.

The Brim cannot follow them. It cannot even ask. But once a day, at dusk, it is touched all at once by a thousand small proofs that there is a *below*. That the drop it faces, the one whose bottom cannot be seen, ends somewhere. That something is down there, and it is alive.

## VII. What It Weeps For

Every column leans west. The whole field bows toward the rim.

Walk that way and the columns shrink and crowd closer. They drop to knee height and fuse into ribbed mats, until at the edge they seem less to stand than to kneel. Then the stone stops, and all the water of the plateau, gathered through the hidden gutters, goes over in one broad curtain and falls into the cloud.

This is the only thing in the field that is not held in perfect balance. The columns keep their water level. The curtain at the rim gives its water away, all of it, continuously, into a depth the Brim cannot see.

For a long time you might take this for grief. A weeping field, bowed toward an edge, pouring itself into an unseen bottom. It must be mourning something that fell. And perhaps once it was. Perhaps, before memory, something went over that edge, and the first column bowed after it, and the rest learned the posture. Perhaps the specks in the crowns are all it has left of that time.

But stay long enough and grief stops being the right word, just as it stopped being right for the columns.

The water that pours over the rim does not vanish. It resurfaces far off the plateau as rivers and springs, cold, flinty, heavy with silica. It goes into whatever country the swifts feed in each day. The spore in the crown came from there. The insects on the swifts' breath came from there. The Brim has never seen that country, and it never will. It knows it only as a dozen specks of kept dust and a warm dark stream at dusk.

And it has been sending that country its water, level and unfailing, for longer than the country has had a name.

The Weeping Field is not mourning. It is keeping a vigil. It is the kind of vigil kept for someone downstream who will never come up, and who does not know they are watched over, and who is watched over anyway. The columns bow west the way a person bows toward a sleeping child in the next room. Not in sorrow. In attention. And the roar that never varies is not a lament. It is a single word said steadily for so long that it has worn down to its meaning, the way the stone has worn down to its shape.

The word is something like *still*. Still here. Still pouring. Still level. Still.

## VIII. Leaving

When you walk out of the field, the true silence of the high air arrives, and it rings.

That ringing is the absence of the Brim's voice, and you will find, surprisingly, that you miss it. For a few steps the halo still follows your shadow, fainter as the mist thins, and then it is gone. You have come loose from the congregation again. You are just a person on a tableland under a hard blue sky.

Behind you, the scratch you made on a flank without meaning to is already filling in. By next season there will be no sign you were there. Unless you left something small on a crown: a thread, a grain of lowland soil from your boot, a flake of your skin. Then the Brim will have closed over it within days. Somewhere in the pearl-banded dome of one bowed head, there will be a new dark speck, suspended, perfectly still inside a body that never stops moving.

It remembers almost nothing. But it will remember you.
