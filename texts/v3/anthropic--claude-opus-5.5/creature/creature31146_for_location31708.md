# The Tenant of the Stillwell

## What It Is Not

You could take the Stillwell's census and never count it.

It is not the salamanders, though they are the oldest things you would find. It is not the bats, the harvestmen, or the glowworms with their close, crowded constellations. Every creature in the chamber lives there, but none of them is the one who *lives* there, the way a house holds furniture, servants and mice and still belongs to someone who is not in any single room.

The first guess would be that it lies at the bottom of the pool: something vast and pale under the heaped salamanders, feeding on the silt, as old as the chamber. People who imagine the Stillwell always want the thing in the deep water. But the pool is clear to the bottom. Nothing lies under the salamanders except more silt.

The second guess is closer. The thing that lives in the Stillwell is not an animal. It is the arrangement.

## The Shape That Is Filled

When a harvestman dies, it drops from the mat, and another takes its place, holding the same legs in the same weave. Something is doing that. It is not a mind that decides. It is more like the way a riverbed decides where the water goes. There is a hollow in the pattern exactly the size of one harvestman, and the hollow is patient and particular, and it fills.

The same is true of the salamander's dent in the silt, the glowworm's place on the ceiling, and the bat's handspan of rock in the dome. The Stillwell is full of shapes that outlast whatever fills them. The tenant is the sum of those shapes. It is a body made entirely of vacancies, each one kept open and fitted to its occupant.

It is less like a creature than like a standing wave. A standing wave is a form that holds still while the water passes through it. No drop of water stays, yet the wave stands in the stream for a thousand years. Harvestmen, salamanders, bats and midges flow through the tenant like water, and the tenant stays exactly where it is.

So it has no body of its own. It has something better suited to the place: a body it borrows, completely and continuously, from everything that lives there.

## Its Senses

Once you see it as a single thing spread through many, its organs start to show.

**Its skin is the harvestman mats.** The slow pulse that crosses a wall when a drip lands or air shifts is not thousands of animals reacting separately. It is the tenant *feeling*. The ripple is the whole of its sense of touch, a long, soft flinch that runs from one side of the chamber to the other and then settles. It prefers not to flinch. Stillness is the only condition it wants, so every disturbance is something to be absorbed, spread out and smoothed over.

**Its sense of the air is the glowworm threads.** Thousands of beaded lines hang in the dark, and each one trembles when anything passes. A midge rising from the pool is felt by the ceiling long before it sticks. The dimming of a single light as a larva reels in its catch is the tenant noting, precisely and without interest, that the balance has been kept.

**Its memory is the salamanders.** A mind spread across short-lived bodies needs somewhere to keep things for longer than a season, and the century-long lives in the pool are where it keeps them. A salamander that moves once a day holds its hollow in the silt for a hundred years, and a hollow held that long becomes a kind of fact. The heaped, draped bodies at the bottom of the pool are the tenant's long memory, lying in the cold where nothing spoils.

**Its sleep is the bat colony**, cooled to the temperature of the rock and breathing almost nothing. When the colony dreams and one high click breaks the silence, that is as close as the tenant comes to speaking in its sleep.

**Its eyelid is the goblin's gold.** The band of glowing moss around the shaft marks where its body ends. Above that ring, the plateau has weather. Below it, there is only the tenant. The moss takes in the last grey light and gives back a dim green ember, and in doing so it declines everything the sky has to offer. That emerald band is a closed eye with light showing faintly through the lid.

## Its Thought

The drips are its thinking.

Hundreds of them fall, each on its own unvarying interval, never falling into step, adding up to a pattern that takes longer than a human life to repeat. That is not background noise to the tenant. It is the tenant thinking one single thought, so long and so intricate that a person could sit through their whole life inside a single clause of it.

The thought never reaches a conclusion. It does not need to. It is not the kind of thinking that moves toward an answer. It is the kind that holds a thing in mind by going over it, round and round. What the tenant keeps in mind is the Stillwell itself: six degrees, saturated air, inflow matching outflow. It thinks the chamber continuously, and the chamber goes on existing exactly as it is thought.

It would be easy to call this waiting, but it is not. Waiting means expecting something to change. The tenant is *keeping*, which is different. Something that waits looks toward a door. Something that keeps faces inward.

## The One Thing It Did Not Make

Everything in the Stillwell belongs to the tenant, with one exception.

Long ago, the arrangement was disturbed. Someone came down the shaft carrying fire.

Imagine what that was like for a body made of stillness. Heat arrived, in a place where the heat had not changed in all of time. There was light with colour in it, red and moving, where there had only been the cold blue-green of the larvae. The air stirred as the flame drew it in and pushed it up. Every harvestman mat on every wall must have risen at once and stayed risen. The tenant must have flinched from one end of its body to the other and kept flinching for as long as the fire burned.

Then there was a sound unlike a drip: stone striking stone. It had a crack to it and a rhythm set by a living arm, speeding up and slowing down, stopping and starting again, every interval different. It was the first sound the Stillwell had ever heard that was *irregular*.

Then someone breathed pigment around a palm pressed to the wall. Seven hands, two of them small.

Then they left. The fire went out and the chamber cooled back to six degrees. The Stillwell closed over them, and the tenant did what it does with every disturbance. It absorbed it, spread it out, and smoothed it over.

Almost.

## What It Kept

The harvestmen part around the hands.

This is the one place where the tenant's skin does not meet the rock. Everywhere else, every surface carries something. Around the seven ochre stencils there is a clear margin, as though the tenant cannot quite bring itself to cover them. The calcite has grown over them instead, glazing them a finger's depth into the stone. The tenant has neither erased them nor exposed them. It has put them away somewhere safe, behind a window it will never open.

The caddis larvae carry the flint.

Everywhere else in the chamber, what falls to the floor settles and is sealed under moonmilk. The knapping flakes that washed into the pool never settle. Generation after generation, the larvae pick them up, bind them into their cases, carry them across the bottom, pupate, and leave them for the next generation to gather. The same chips of struck stone have been passed from hand to hand, if larvae can be said to have hands, thousands of times.

A tenant whose only desire is stillness has kept one small part of its floor always moving. In a body made of fitted hollows, there is one hollow the size of a human visit, and it has never been filled. The tenant keeps it open by turning its contents over, the way a person turns a smooth stone over and over in a pocket.

The strangest thing is the drips.

The pattern takes longer than a life to repeat, but it does repeat. Once in each great cycle, perhaps once in every several centuries, for a few minutes only, the drips fall into a new relation. The hourly one near the dome lands close behind the quick ones by the pool, and the ones on the stone shelf answer the ones on the water, and for those few minutes the drip-music stops sounding like water falling.

It sounds like someone knapping flint.

It has the same crack, the same quickening and pausing, and a rhythm set by an arm that tired, rested and began again. The tenant cannot make anything new. It has only the fixed intervals of the dripping rock. But over a span of time too long to picture, it has fitted the one song it ever heard into the music it plays to itself, and it plays that song back once every age, perfectly, to no one.

This may be memory. It may be something closer to wanting. The tenant would not recognise the difference, and it would not use either word.

## If You Came

If you climbed down past the goblin's gold, the band would not close you out. It lets light through faintly, and it would let you through too.

The walls would rise as you passed, mat after mat lifting in the slow pulse of the tenant feeling you arrive. The glowworm threads would shiver across the ceiling in a long wave of attention. Somewhere in the dome, a sleeping bat would click once.

If you brought fire, the tenant would suffer it. It would not stop you. It has never stopped anything, only outlasted it.

If you sat on the shelf beside the moonmilk and the seven hands, picked up a cobble, and struck it against a stone, something would happen that the Stillwell has not done since it was made.

For as long as you worked, the tenant would hold perfectly still. The mats would stop rippling and the salamanders would stop shifting. It would feel as though every drip in the chamber were hanging from its point of rock, not falling, listening.

The whole enormous, patient, borrowed body of the place would listen to see whether you were playing it the right way.
