# Who Lives in the Dry Works

The first honest answer is that the Works lives there, and nothing else needs to. It feeds itself on light, repairs itself with weather, and turns its own dead wood back into stock. A thing that complete should have no tenants.

But anything this large and this old leaves gaps between its mechanisms, and some of the gaps are not empty. Some of what fills them is the Works by another name. Some of it came from outside and stayed. And one of the things that lives there, if "lives" is the word, may be the reason the rest of it exists at all.

---

## The Chaff Moths

Start with the smallest thing that is certainly an animal.

Travelers call them flour-moths, or chaff-moths. The few people who live in the Works permanently call them *the forgetting*. They are grey, with a wingspan about the width of a spread hand, and they are so heavy with starch dust that they look sculpted out of it. Before a moth can fly it has to shake itself, a sharp shiver of the whole body, and each takeoff leaves a small white burst hanging in the air where the moth used to be. Up close they smell sweeter than the rest of the Works, like the crust of bread rather than the flour.

They do not fly well and do not need to. They rise on the updrafts over the drive-stems the way the shed leaf-skin does, loose and patient, until they reach the high pearl banks of cloud. There they feed. A leaf-skin bank that has held moths for a few days is thinner and more ragged than one that hasn't, and when it comes down as dry snowfall, a fraction of the flakes have wings. They are the moths coming down to lay.

What makes them strange is not what they eat. It is that they are the only thing in the Works that can tell living wood from dead. To a human eye, and to a human hand, a stopped line and a running line differ only in motion, and some lines run so slowly that you cannot see them move. The moths are never wrong. They lay only in the grey timber of abandoned avenues, and their larvae, soft, white, blind, and almost weightless, chew that timber into powder that sifts down onto the belts below and is carried to the reclamation mills. The permanent residents use larva holes as a map. A gantry pocked with them can be climbed and broken and cut. A gantry without them belongs to the Works, even if it has not moved in a hundred years.

So the moths are the Works' way of deciding what it no longer needs. Without them it would still function, but it would keep everything. The stratified layers of old factory would never compact. The rings would never be counted, because nothing would split. The moths do not destroy. They let go on the Works' behalf.

When a moth dies it dies dry. It falls, lands on a belt, and is carried with everything else. It is milled, pressed, and eventually becomes part of a pod wall somewhere. The residents say that if you saw open enough pods you will find, very rarely, the faint outline of a wing in the grain.

## The Tallymen

The tallymen are harder.

They are long-legged beetles, jointed like dry grass stalks, bark-brown and nearly as tall as a person's knee. They walk against the direction of the belts at exactly belt speed, and so, from the point of view of anything standing on a belt, they appear to be standing perfectly still while the Works slides by beneath them. From the point of view of anything standing off the belt, on a gantry or a stair, they are walking steadily. They never seem to hurry.

This is well known. What took the residents a long time to notice is that it is not quite true. A tallyman is not matching the belt. It is matching the ground, the real ground, whatever the Works stands on, the thing that no one has found. When a belt passes through the mouth of an old overgrown seedpod into one of the folded districts, where the space inside is far larger than the space outside, the belt carries on at the same speed, and the rider notices nothing. But the tallyman changes its pace. It slows, sometimes almost to a stop, or it speeds up until it is scrambling. It is keeping still relative to something that the belt, and the rider, have lost track of.

Tallymen, in other words, always know where they are. They may be the only things in the Works that do.

Some residents navigate by them. It is called *walking the tally*. You step off the belt and walk beside the beetle, matching it exactly, stride for stride, for as long as your legs will hold out. You arrive, slowly and exhaustingly, somewhere that is actually a fixed distance from where you began. Nobody does it often. It feels, people say, like walking against a current you cannot see, and the Works around you seems to swim.

Then there is the tapping. A tallyman stops beside a roller or a socket or a length of gantry, raises its forelegs, and taps three times. Then it walks on. Within the month something grows there: a stem, a strut, sometimes a branching of new belt. The residents argue about what the taps are. The oldest theory is that they are an inspection, a knock to hear whether the wood is sound. The next is that they are an instruction. The one that has quietly become most popular is that they are counting. The tallymen are the ones who know how many rings are in the air around a cut, the pale ones that extend past the bark into years that haven't happened. Each tap is a ring. The tallyman is telling the Works: *three more, here.*

No one has seen a young tallyman. They simply appear, full-sized, on new growth, stepping down from a roller as though they have always been there. On a few new rollers, if you look closely, there is a row of small closed seams along the grain, like the seams of pods too small to hold anything. When a tallyman dies it does the only thing it has never done in life. It stops walking. The belt catches it, and it is carried away at belt speed, finally moving through the world instead of holding still in it.

## The Early

Here the account gets less certain, and the residents get quieter when they talk about it.

The rungs of the gantries are worn. Everyone who comes here notices this. They are hollowed in the middle into smooth saddles, the shape a staircase takes after a century of feet. Something climbs them, a great deal, and has for a very long time. But nothing has been seen climbing them. The tallymen stay on the belts. The moths fly. Residents climb, but there have never been enough residents to wear a groove into heartwood.

Several people have tried dusting a rung with fresh starch and watching it. There is no night, so there is no morning to check it in. You simply wait, and sleep a little, and look again. The dust is never disturbed. But over the course of months, measured carefully, the hollow under the undisturbed dust gets deeper.

The early sounds belong with this. The *hff* of a press arrives a second or two before the press closes. On gantries, sometimes, there is a creak of weight on a rung above you, a slow soft creak, then another one higher, as if someone were climbing ahead. When you look, the rung is empty. A second or two later, if you are the one climbing, your own weight makes exactly that creak on exactly that rung.

The residents call them the Early, and they do not agree whether the Early are anyone. Some say the Works simply runs a little ahead of itself in places. Space here is loose, so time is too, and the sounds and the wear are leaking backward. Others are sure there are occupants, that the Works is already inhabited by whoever it is going to be inhabited by, and that they are living a little further along the rings, in the pale growth out in the air past the bark, where the years haven't come yet. The wear on the rungs, in this view, is their footsteps. They have been climbing since before the gantries were built, because the gantries were always going to be here.

What everyone agrees on is that the Early are not unfriendly. The creak ahead of you on a gantry is never startling after the first time. It feels, people say, like being guided. Like a hand held out one rung up.

## The Stayers

People come to the Works. Most of them leave, eventually, by walking out through the grey buffer zones before the floor gives way into pale nothing. Some don't.

The ones who don't are called stayers, and they change.

It begins with thirst, which is terrible, because there is no water anywhere, not a drop in the whole plain. A stayer is someone who has survived that thirst without water, and nobody entirely understands how. Their lips crack, then stop cracking. Their skin tightens, then takes on a bloom, a faint whitish dust like the bloom on a plum, which is starch, which comes off on anything they touch. They stop sweating. Somewhere after that they stop being hungry, although they will still eat a pinch of the fine white dust from the channels sometimes, the way a person might chew a grass stem, for the habit of it. Their breath, which never fogged in the cold air, eventually stops smelling of anything at all. They grow light. An old stayer can stand on a belt so lightly that the fiber does not dip under their feet.

They whisper, of course. Everyone does. But stayers whisper so softly that newcomers often think they aren't speaking, only moving their lips, until they learn to hear it. Their language has changed to fit the place. There are no nouns for locations, because locations move. They name routes, not places, and they name routes by duration and smell: *the long sweet ride*, *the two-hour burnt one*. They measure distance in belt-changes. They greet each other by blowing gently on the other's hands, which lifts a little of the bloom away. It is a gesture of trust. You are letting someone see you are still solid underneath.

There are never many of them. Because the routes shift, it is hard for stayers to live near one another. Two who share an overgrown pod as a dwelling may, if they go out riding separately, not find each other again for years. So they leave knots. Small twists of belt fiber, tied in particular ways, hung on gantries along the routes. A knot says *I passed this way, going this direction, at about this time*. Stayers read them the way people elsewhere read letters. Some knots are very old, grey and silvered, tied by people no one living has met.

They live, mostly, near the edges or inside the old incorporated pods where the space is folded. They spend a great deal of their time opening pods. It takes days, with a saw of hardened resin, working the seam around the middle. Asked why, stayers give different answers. Shelter, they say, or curiosity. But the ones who have stayed longest usually say something closer to this: that opening a pod is the only thing in the Works a person can do that the Works does not already do for itself. Everything else runs without you. But the pods come off the line sealed, and they sit, and they wait, and if no one opens them they go on waiting. The stayers suspect that they are supposed to be opened. That opening is a job, and a person can hold it.

Here is what stayers know about the inside of a pod that visitors don't.

The pale, empty, lit interior is cool and still. Far in, there is sometimes a single drive-stem, or a short length of belt, not moving. Visitors see this and leave. Stayers sit with it. If someone stays inside a pod long enough, days or weeks, living there, sleeping there, breathing that dry cool air, the stem starts to turn. Very slowly at first. A creak, and then a leaf-crown lifting toward the sourceless light, and then a tremor down the stem, and the knuckle at the base beginning, unmistakably, to rotate.

The machinery inside a pod does not start on its own. It starts when the room is lived in.

Stayers who have seen this happen describe it with something between reverence and embarrassment, as though they had been caught setting a place at a table for someone who wasn't coming. Some of them feel that they have been used. Most of them feel, instead, that they have finally been told what they are for.

When a stayer comes to the end, they know it. They grow too light to hold still, and the belts start to take them whether they step onto them or not. So at the last they choose one and lie down on it, face up, looking into the white sky, and it carries them to the reclamation mills, and they are pressed back into stock. It is not considered a death, exactly. Other stayers say the pods made on that line for a while afterward have a different grain, a slight whorl near the seam, like the pad of a thumb.

## The Leaner

And then there is the large one.

The accounts are few and consistent. It is ox-sized, perhaps bigger, pale as unbleached linen, and powdered all over with starch so thick it seems to have no edges, only a density where the dust gets whiter. It moves slowly, with a wide rolling gait. It has four broad toes on each foot, and its prints are soft and round, spaced far apart in the fresh dust, leading into a district and never out. Nobody who has seen it mentions eyes. They describe its head as broad and flat and gentle, like the side of a face.

Stayers call it the Leaner, because of what it does. It goes among the largest finished pods in the buffer zones, the house-sized ones, and stops beside one, and lays that broad flat head against the husk, very carefully, the way a person puts an ear to a wall. It stays there for a long time. It seems to be listening. Sometimes it moves on to the next pod and listens again. Sometimes it doesn't move.

People have tried pressing their own ears against pods, after seeing this. They hear nothing, or rather they hear the particular nothing of a large room: that faint rushing hush that big empty spaces have, the sound of air with a great deal of distance in it. Some stayers think that is exactly what the Leaner is listening for. It is measuring the room by ear. Judging whether it is big enough.

When it is, the seam opens. Not sawed; it parts, cleanly, the two halves of the husk easing away from each other like lips. The Leaner goes in. The prints lead to the threshold and over it and on into the pale floor inside, and they go on so far they become too faint to see. By the time anyone reaches the place, the pod is open and empty. It is always larger inside than any pod anyone has opened before.

That last detail is the one the stayers have worried at for generations. The pods are not getting larger on the outside. It is the interiors. Each one the Leaner chooses holds more space than the last. The stayers have kept records, knotted cords measuring the depth of interiors, passed from hand to hand. The trend is not in doubt.

And the prints are getting larger too. Slightly. Measurably. Across the whole span of the knotted records, the four-toed prints in the dust have widened, and the stride between them has lengthened, and the Leaner in the oldest descriptions is described as the size of a calf.

So it is growing. Whatever it is, it is growing, and the Works is making rooms to fit it, and each time it moves into a new room the room is a little bigger than the last one, because it needs to be.

## What the Rooms Are For

Put it together, the way the oldest stayers have.

The Works makes interior. It grows space inward and packs it into husks and sets the husks down at its edges and waits. The interiors are not quite empty. Each one holds the beginning of a mechanism: a stem, a belt, not moving. The mechanism does not start until the room is lived in. Something comes and listens at the walls and chooses a room large enough, and goes in, and lives there. The stem begins to creak. Over a very long time, inside that pod, a new Works grows: gantries, belts, crowns of translucent leaf drinking the white light, a plain.

Where does the Leaner come from? Nobody knows. It smells, the few who have been close say, faintly of pollen. It is seen most often in the week after a gold front has passed through a district, when the new growth is coming in. It may be that the Works makes it the way it makes everything, in some district no one has found. That it is simply the Works' last product. Its moving part. The moths are the Works' forgetting and the tallymen are its counting and the stayers, whether they meant to or not, are its tending. The Leaner would be its going.

The account of the place says that no one has found what lies beneath the plain, and that at the far edges it is not clear whether the Works sits on a surface or inside a husk. The stayers are fairly sure, now, which it is.

They think the Dry Works is the inside of a pod. They think that, a very long time ago, something broad and pale and four-toed laid its head against a husk somewhere else, and listened, and heard enough room. The husk opened and it went in and stayed, and a single stem in the cool light began to turn. Everything since then, the gantries, the white sky, the dust weather, the rings upon rings of old factory, has been that stem, going on.

They think the Leaner walks here because it was born here, in the last room of a house that something else moved into. It is growing up in it. When it is fully grown, when its prints are as wide as the pods it listens to, it will need a room the size of the plain.

What lies under the Works, then, the thing no one has found, is not ground. It is whoever lived here first. The tallymen are keeping still relative to them.

The shadows in the fast districts, the ones that point toward the light, are easier to understand this way too. Those districts sit above the oldest incorporated pods, the ones the Leaner has already entered, deep under many layers of later growth. Something in those rooms is living, and the life of it pulls. The light is drawn out of objects toward the brightest part of the sky, and the drive-stems overhead spin so fast their leaves blur, as though the Works were leaning its whole weight toward the rooms that have been moved into.

And the Early, the ones climbing ahead of you, whose sounds arrive before the sounds do? The stayers do not say this aloud very often. But the rings in the air past the bark record years that haven't happened, and the rungs are being worn by feet that haven't stepped, and someone always creaks one rung above you on the gantry, guiding. Some of the oldest stayers think the Early are the ones the house is for. The residents of the finished Works, long after this: the future tenants of whatever the Leaner grows into, already moving through the pale outer rings, already at home. They were here first, they say, because they will have been here longest.

## Being Inhabited

So, what lives in the Dry Works?

Grey moths that let go of what is finished. Brown beetles that keep count, standing still on the true ground while everything slides beneath them. Dry quiet people with a bloom on their skin who tie knots and saw seams and sit with unmoving stems until they turn. Footsteps on the rungs a little ahead of your own. And one pale, slow, patient animal that is growing up, that puts its ear to the walls, looking for the room it will fit.

But more than any of them, the Works is inhabited the way a house is inhabited in the weeks before someone moves in. The rooms are swept. The fittings are in. Someone has walked through every hallway, touching the walls, checking. Nobody sleeps there yet, but it is not empty, and anyone who walks in feels it at once: the sense of a place arranged entirely around a person who is not there. That is what makes people whisper. It isn't the silence. It is the courtesy you show in someone else's home.

At the far edge of the buffer zone, if you are lucky, you can find a line of broad soft prints going out across the pale floor that is not a floor. They go past the last finished pods, out into the level nothing, which a little further on becomes the inside of something: the same pale wood, the same cool sourceless light, stretching away. The prints get fainter as they go. They do not turn.

If you stand there long enough, with the hiss of the dust behind you and the cold cereal air in your mouth, you may hear it. It will be very far off, in the direction the prints went. It is a single thin creak, green wood flexing, the sound of one stem, somewhere out in all that room, beginning to drink the light.
