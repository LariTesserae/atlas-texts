# Those Who Keep the Quiet

## The Sign

Ask one of the herders who still bring their animals up the passes in the warm season who lives in the Hushlands, and they won't answer aloud, even far out on the open rock where a voice would carry. They will cup their hands together in front of them, palms up, the way you would to drink from a spring.

It takes most outsiders a while to understand that the gesture has two meanings. On the plateau it means *water*. Raised to the side of the head, the same cupped hands mean *listening*. The herders use one shape for both. When they make it in answer to your question, they mean both, and they mean something more. Their hands, held that way, are exactly the size of one cell of a comb.

At first you assume they mean the animals: the rock rabbits in their honeycomb hollows, the camelids asleep in the noon shade, the falcon on the dome crests. Those live here, certainly. But the herders will shake their heads, cup their hands again, and point toward the nearest grey lattice standing among the domes.

## The Tappers

The first thing you find, if you go looking, is small.

Stand at a large comb at midday and look closely at the beads of water standing on the lattice under that impossible sun. They aren't randomly placed. Each bead sits at the mouth of a small grey animal clinging to the comb, and there are thousands of them, sometimes hundreds of thousands on the largest combs. They're the color of the lattice and nearly flat against it, and your eye slides off them until you learn the shape.

The herders call them tappers. They're something like a cricket and something like a woodlouse: segmented, armored, about as long as a fingernail. Under the abdomen each one carries a hard, domed plate. They spend the day pressed against the comb, drumming that plate against the lattice, over and over, a few beats a second.

You hear nothing. The comb takes every tap before it can become sound. The comb turns it into cold, and the cold gathers right where the tapper is drumming, and a bead of water grows on the lattice an eyelash away from its mouthparts. The tapper drinks. It drums again.

The whole comb is covered in this at noon, all of it invisible and all of it silent, tens of thousands of tiny animals paying for water with noise.

Pry one loose and carry it twenty paces out into the open, and it will keep drumming in your palm. The sound is shocking: a hard, dry, frantic ticking, far louder than anything that size should make. It has never once heard itself. It has no idea how loud it is. Its whole lineage evolved in a place where sound is currency, and loudness costs nothing because the comb takes all of it. Set it back on the lattice and the ticking stops mid-beat, swallowed, and a bead begins to form.

The tappers are the first hint of what the herders mean. The combs aren't only silence-engines and dew-engines. They trade. Sound goes in and water comes out, and anything on the plateau that learns this can drink in the middle of the day.

## The Seedlings

The herders' second answer is in the tafoni.

Almost every hollow in the high domes is occupied, and the rock rabbits in them are only the most obvious tenants. Crawl into one of the larger hollows, the ones deep enough to take your shoulders, and you'll notice two things. The stone at the back is cold, far colder than shade alone would explain. And the rabbits' breathing, which you should be able to hear in so small a space, is inaudible.

Pressed into the back of the hollow, usually in a crack or a shallow cup in the rock, there is a tiny comb. Some are the size of a thumbnail, a cluster of six or seven hexagons. Some are as big as a fist. They're the same smoke-grey, with the same oily depth when a slant of light gets in, and they're cold enough to ache.

These are young combs. The herders call them *kits*, the same word they use for young rabbits, and the choice isn't careless. Where you find a kit, you find a rabbit colony curled around it.

The arrangement works like this. At noon, when the plateau is unbearable, the kit keeps the burrow cool, and the moisture it draws out of the air feeds the ferns that grow wiry and green at the mouths of the hollows, and the rabbits eat the ferns. At night the rabbits sleep pressed around the kit in a warm grey heap. A kit is too small to feed on wind. It feeds on them: on their breathing, the small sounds of their sleep, their heartbeats. Lay your palm against the rock beside a kit with a colony asleep around it and you can feel, very faintly, the cold pulse in time with their hearts.

A rabbit colony can nurse a kit for generations. The herders say a kit leaves its hollow when it grows too big to be fed by breathing. It begins to reach toward the mouth of the tafone, toward the wind, and one year the rabbits move out and the young comb is standing in the open, the height of a child, taking the wind for itself.

No one is sure how the kits get into the hollows. The herders point to the mirror-winged birds, the ones that nest in the broken cells of the dead combs and still sing there. The birds carry grit in their feathers, and grit from a dead comb is not always entirely dead. The herders also admit, when pressed, that sometimes they carry a kit themselves: prised gently from a crowded hollow and cradled between cupped hands to a new one, where a rabbit colony has no comb and its young die in the noon heat. They don't talk about this much. It feels presumptuous, they say, like moving someone else's child.

## The Ones in the Grey

Here the herders' answer starts to come into focus.

The combs are not built things that happen to be convenient to animals. Or not only that. They're alive, or they're inhabited so thoroughly that the difference stops mattering.
