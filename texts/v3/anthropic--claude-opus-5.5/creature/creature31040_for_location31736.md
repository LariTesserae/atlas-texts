# Who Lives in the Drowned Terraces

## I. The Obvious Answer

Start with what anyone would see.

A newt is climbing the east wall of the round house on the second terrace. It moves the way newts move, one foot and then a long consideration and then another foot. It has climbed this wall before. There is a path worn into the moss, not deep, a darker line where the cushion has been pressed flat by small bellies over many seasons. It goes up the wall, along the top, down the far side, and across the lane to the next house. The newts did not agree on this path. It simply turned out to be the good way, and the good way wore in.

In the lane below, a caddis larva drags its case across a fallen lintel. The case is a tube of schist flakes and one grain of charcoal. The charcoal is black and glossy and was once part of a fire that cooked someone's supper. The larva doesn't know that. It chose the grain for its weight and its fit.

The moss cushions swarm with shrimp. A leech ripples across a floor. A diving beetle hangs head-down among the milfoil with a bubble of silver air held under its wings.

All of this is true, and all of it is too scattered to be an answer. These are creatures that happen to be here. Look longer and the town begins to sort them. It has always been a place that sorted: lanes and houses, garden and byre, the square and the edges. The animals have fallen into its categories without knowing it, the same way the newts' path wore in. Something like a population has formed. It has elders and masons, visitors and sweepers. And, it turns out, it has citizens.

## II. The Elders in the Gardens

The mussels were the first to settle, and they will be the last to leave. They cannot leave.

They sit in the open ground between the houses, where the gardens were, half-sunk in marl with only the gape of their shells showing. There are hundreds of them. Some are no bigger than a thumb. Some are as long as a hand, the shells eroded pale at the hinge and flaked like old bark. Freshwater pearl mussels, in water this cold and clean, can live more than a century. The oldest here may have been children when the last person to hear the town's spring chorus in its present pitch was still alive.

They do almost nothing. They open a little and draw water in, and they strain it and let it go. Over a summer a single large mussel filters enough water to fill one of the drowned houses to the lintel several times over. The clarity of the lake, which lets anyone look down from the rim and see streets, is partly their work. They are why the town can be seen.

They also listen, in their way. A mussel has no ears, but it feels vibration through its foot and its mantle edge. When the frogs sing in spring, the whole garden ground trembles faintly, and the mussels close by degrees, a millimeter, then two, like old people half-shutting their eyes against a noise they have heard many times before. When a stone falls from the cirque wall and the boom travels down through the lanes, every shell in the town snaps shut at once. For a few seconds the gardens go silent, a clicking wave that crosses the whole town. Then, one by one, they open again.

They are rooted, and that is the problem at the heart of their lives. Something that cannot move has to get its children out into the world somehow.

## III. The Char, Who Carry Them

In late summer the mussels release their young, and the young do not stay.

Mussel larvae are tiny: clamped half-shells, hundreds of thousands of them, loosed into the water in faint drifting clouds. Almost all of them die within hours. The few that survive do so by being breathed in by a fish. They snap shut on the fish's gills, and there they live for most of a year, embedded in the tissue, carried.

The only fish here is the char.

So every char in the tarn is, through the autumn and winter and into spring, a vessel. They carry the mussels' children on their gills through the lanes and around the square, down to the deepest terraces and up to the shallows. When the young mussels are ready, they drop off wherever the fish happens to be. Where the char linger is where the next generation of mussels settles. The char linger in the open ground. They always have. So the old gardens fill with new elders, and the pattern holds.

Watch the char with that in mind, and they stop looking like fish that happen to be in a flooded town. They look like its people.

They hold station in the lanes, facing into a current too slow to see, which is exactly what a person standing in a doorway looks like from a distance. They patrol the square. They pass through the low doors, which are just large enough for them. There are paths they use and paths they don't, and there are houses a given char will enter and houses it won't. Territories form and are defended in small sudden rushes, and then relax again. In spawning season their bellies go red as coals, the only warm color in the whole green-and-white town.

When they spawn, they sweep the streets.

The upper terraces have old paving under a skin of gravel. The source of that clear gravel isn't only the inflow. Every autumn the female char turn on their sides over the paving and beat their tails against it, again and again. They fan out the silt and lift away the fallen needles of stonewort and the drifted caddis cases, until the gravel shines and the old stones beneath show their worn edges. They lay their eggs there, the males cover them, and the stones are kept clean for one more year.

Of all the town's inhabitants, only the char keep any part of it the way it was kept when it was lived in. The marl is burying everything else. The upper lanes stay open because the fish sweep them.

## IV. The Masons

The caddis larvae are something different. They aren't citizens. They are closer to the town's memory of its own craft.

The people who built here worked in drystone: flat slabs fitted without mortar, chinked with smaller stones. Look closely at a caddis case made of schist and you will see the same technique. Flat pieces are laid overlapping and fitted edge to edge, with smaller grains wedged into the gaps. The larva binds them with silk instead of weight, but the logic is the same. Take what the place gives you, fit it close, and make a shelter.

Each case is a single house, carried. Across the whole town, at any moment, thousands of small drystone houses are crawling over the ruins of fifty large ones. When the larvae mature they seal their cases, pupate, and rise to the surface as winged adults. They fly out over the tarn for a few days in summer, brief and papery, and die. The empty cases fall.

So in the sheltered doorways the drifts accumulate: little tubes of schist and marl and charcoal, heaped. The marl buries them in turn, and they become part of the walls they were quarried from. The town is being disassembled and reassembled at a scale too small for anyone who built it to have noticed. The stone keeps passing through the hands, or the mandibles, of builders.

## V. The Visitors

And then, every spring, the crowd.

The frogs don't live in the town. They live on the scree slopes and in the thin grass above the waterline, under stones, in the lee of the snow gullies. For most of the year they are scattered across the cirque, cold and solitary. When the ice goes out they come down, all of them at once, the way people come down from the high pastures for a fair.

They fill the houses. They dive into the roofless rooms and call from inside them, and each room gives back its own note. The round house on the second terrace hums deep, and the narrow building by the square buzzes high. For a few weeks the town is packed, louder and more crowded than it ever was when its hearths were warm. The mussels half-close. The char drift uneasily at the edges of the square. The newts, who live here year-round, go on climbing their walls with the patience of residents waiting out a festival.

Then the frogs leave. Their spawn hangs in the shallows in clouds of black-dotted jelly, and in time the tadpoles scatter, grow legs, and climb out onto the scree. The town is left to the people who actually live there.

## VI. The One Who Has Been Here Longest

There is one char, and it might be her you meet first if you come in winter.

Char in cold, clear, food-poor water grow slowly and live a long time: twenty years, thirty, sometimes more. There is a large one in this tarn, larger than the others, dark-backed and heavy, with spots faded almost to nothing. Her jaw is hooked slightly with age. She holds station in the square by the standing stone. She is always there, facing the stone as though reading it.

She has spawned on the upper paving more autumns than any other fish in the lake, which means she has swept those lanes more times than anyone. She has carried the mussels' children on her gills for decades, so some of the elders now filtering water in the gardens rode into those gardens on her. She was in the lake the autumn the big stone fell from the eastern wall. It sent every mussel in the town snapping shut, and the marl it dislodged is still visible as a slump at the corner of the lowest lane.

And she has heard the frogs every spring of her life.

Fish hear well, much better than mussels. The sound comes to her through the water and through her body, and through the line of sense organs running down her flanks. She was a fingerling once, and she heard the round house on the second terrace sing. She has heard it every spring since. It is higher now than it was. Not by much, perhaps a few hairs' breadth of pitch, because the marl in that floor has risen by a finger's width in her lifetime. But the note has changed, and she has heard all of it.

She is the only thing in the town that has listened to its voice rise. She doesn't know that is what she has done. She holds her station.

## VII. What Actually Lives There

Put it all together and the answer comes into focus.

The people walked out over the pass, and the town was not left empty. It was taken up, piece by piece, by creatures that fit its shapes. Rooted elders settled in the gardens. Walkers keep the lanes and sweep the stones, and they carry the elders' children from house to house. Masons rebuild it in miniature and give their buildings back to its walls. Visitors fill it once a year until it rings. Residents climb the same paths season after season until the paths are worn in.

Nobody planned any of this. It happened because a town is a shape that sorts things, and the life that came in with the water sorted itself along the old lines. There are still gardens, streets, festivals, houses, masons, and the old. The only thing that changed was who.

Underneath all of them, filling the rooms from the floor up, is the one inhabitant that will outlast them: the stonewort meadow. It is really one slow thing, continuous across every terrace, precipitating lime and dying and being buried and growing again. It doesn't live in the town the way the char do. It is turning into the town. It moves in centimeters per century. It is closing the doors, and it will close the rooms, and in the end it will close the frogs' song.

## VIII. After

But the char won't leave.

When the town is a white limestone hill under green water, entombed to the tip of its standing stone, the lake will still be cold and clear and full of oxygen. The char will still hold station over the place where the square was. In autumn they will still turn on their sides and beat their tails against whatever gravel lies on top of the hill, sweeping the one clean patch. By then nothing will remember that anything was built there. The mussels will still sit where the char linger. The frogs will still come down in spring and call, even if they have nowhere left to call from but open water.

People built the town, and the char kept it. When it is gone, the char will go on doing what they have done in it all along: keeping one small patch of stones clean, year after year, in the cold.

Up on the rim, where the path comes over the pass, the cairn stands in the wind with snow in its lee. It is the only part of the town that stayed dry and the only part nobody lives in. Everything that lives in this place now lives below it, in the water.
