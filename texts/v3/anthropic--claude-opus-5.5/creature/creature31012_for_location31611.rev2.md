# What Lives in the Hush

## In the Cells

Start with the cells, because that is where you would find them first, if you knew to look.

Come to a large comb in the hour before dawn, when the lattice is sweating hardest, and put your eye close to one of the lower cells. Bring no light; they dislike it at that hour. Wait until the east has paled enough to show shapes. The cell will look empty at first, only a hexagonal cup of smoke-grey glass brimming with water that trembles at its lip. Then you will see that the water has a shape inside it. Something clearer than the water is folded against the back wall of the cell. It fits the cell exactly, as a hand fits a glove.

The herders who still cross the plateau call them *stillings*. The word means two things. They are still, and they are a still, as in the copper vessel that draws spirit from mash. Both meanings are correct.

A stilling is about the size of a cupped pair of hands, and it is shaped much like one: a curved membrane, thin at the edges and thicker toward a central ridge, folded so that it holds a hollow of air or water against itself. It has no eyes, no mouth you would recognize, no limbs. Its body is almost perfectly transparent. When the light comes through it at an angle you can see its structure. Fine radiating ribs run from the thick ridge to the edge, finer than hair, and there are thousands of them, like the gills of a mushroom or the reeds of some impossible instrument. Each rib is a slightly different length. Each one trembles at its own pitch.

This is how they hear, and it is also how they eat.

## What They Eat

The combs take vibration and turn it into cold. No one living knows the principle, the old accounts say, and that is true. But the stillings know it, in the way a leaf knows photosynthesis. The combs may have been built by whoever laid the black pavement, but they were built *for* something. The cells are the size of the stillings because they were made to hold them. What the lattice does, the stillings do. What the stillings do, the lattice makes possible. By now it may not be possible to say which is the engine and which is the fuel.

A sound arrives at a comb. It might be wind across the tuff, the whine of midges over a pan, a rabbit's alarm-squeak, the far roar of weather on the escarpment. The lattice gathers it and guides it inward, cell by cell, the way the whorls of an ear gather sound. In each cell a stilling waits with its ribs spread, and the ribs that match the pitch begin to move. The stilling takes that motion into itself. Something in its tissue converts the ordered energy of a pressure-wave into whatever it is that stillings live on. The heat that ought to result from all that work does not appear. It runs backward. The stilling grows cold. The cold goes into the glass, the glass draws water from the thin air, and the comb sweats.

So the dew is what is left over when a sound has been eaten.

The herders do not put it that way. They say something simpler: the water from each comb tastes of what it has heard. Nobody can prove this. Nobody who has lived on the plateau doubts it. Water from the combs along the western rim, where the wind comes up the escarpment day and night, tastes flat and mineral, like licked stone. Water from a comb that stood near a rabbit warren for a century was sweet, they say, faintly grassy. After a storm crosses the basin, the morning pans taste of iron for a day. And there is one comb in the north, near the ruined huts, where the herders will not drink, though the water is clear and plentiful. They say it tastes of salt, like tears. They say that is because people used to go there to weep.

## How They Live

A stilling lives a very long time, and very slowly.

Most of its life it spends folded in its cell, listening. It does not move except to adjust itself. It tilts a few degrees toward a louder quarter of the sky, opens its fold a little wider in the quiet of night, closes against the intense light of noon. In the heat of midday the stillings in the upper cells go dormant and cloudy, like frosted glass. This is part of why the combs seem to darken slightly at noon, and part of why the caustics they throw grow so sharp: a clouded stilling bends light differently than a clear one.

At night they are at their most alive. The stone gives up its heat and the air goes still. What sound there is out in the open country, the insects, the scrape of a lizard, the far calls of birds beyond the silence, comes thin and clear across the rock, and the stillings unfold completely. Their ribs spread until the membrane is almost flat. If you had some way to see in the dark, the whole comb would look like a wall of opened hands.

They are feeding then, but that is not all they are doing. Something about the way they hold themselves at night is attentive in a way that feeding is not. A rabbit grazing is not the same as a rabbit with its head up.

## How They Begin

In the last hour of dark, when the cells are fullest, the stillings spawn.

Nobody saw this for a long time, because it is almost impossible to see. A stilling's body pulses once, a slight contraction along the central ridge, and releases into the water of its cell a scatter of motes no bigger than grains of salt. They are clear. In water they are invisible. The cell overflows. The water runs down the lattice in threads, gathers at the lowest edge, and falls, drop by drop, into the pan below.

Each drop carries a few of them. The rings that spread across the black water and carry the stars also carry the young.

Almost all of them die. They live in the pans through the morning, drifting in the cold water, feeding on the small vibrations of the pan's surface: midge wings, the lapping of camelid tongues, the tremor of beetles walking at the margin. As the sun climbs and the pans shrink, they crowd into the shrinking water. When the pan dries, they dry with it. The fine white silt that lines the pans is, in part, made of them. The silt is so fine and so white because it is ten thousand years of stillings that did not make it.

Some do make it. They survive by being carried. A mote can cling to the wet muzzle of a camelid that drinks and moves on, and dry on its whiskers into a speck of glass, and wake when the animal drinks again somewhere else. A mote can ride on the foot of a bird, or in the damp fur of a rabbit, or in the silt stuck to the belly of the snake that waits at the pan's edge. The luckiest are carried into the tafoni and left in some seepage-damp hollow among the ferns, where water gathers but never quite dries. There they grow, very slowly, in the dark.

That is why almost every tafoni hollow on the high domes is occupied, and not only by rabbits and lizards. In the backs of the deeper hollows, where the seepage glistens, there are stillings the size of a fingernail, the size of a coin, the size of a palm. They are growing and waiting, and they hum faintly with the small sounds of the burrow. The rabbits sleep beside them. The rabbits' breathing feeds them, and the cold they give back keeps the burrow cool through the noon heat. It is possible that the rabbits have lived like this for so long that they could not live any other way.

When a stilling in the tafoni grows too large for its hollow, it does something no one has seen and everyone has inferred. It leaves. It has no limbs, but it goes somewhere, and the only place it can go is a comb.

## How the Combs Walk

This is how the combs walk.

A comb is not quite a building. It is closer to a reef. The lattice was made, once, by hands or tools or processes we cannot name, but it is not finished and never was. The stillings maintain it. Their cold draws minerals out of the dew and lays them down as the same smoke-grey glass, a hair's width a year. Where the stillings are healthy and well fed, the lattice grows. Where they starve, it weathers.

The cells on the side of a comb that faces into the wind, toward the loud passes and the escarpment edge, are the best fed. The stillings there grow large and spawn well, and the lattice thickens and extends. The cells on the lee side hear less. The stillings there dwindle, and some die, and the glass around their empty cells slowly softens, crumbles, and falls. Over a century the comb has grown a little on one side and shed a little on the other, and it stands somewhere it did not stand before.

The polished trail behind each comb is the ground it has shed onto, the fine grey dust of its own discarded lattice ground by wind across the stone. The burn-lines scored into that trail are the ghost of its caustics, written over and over as it passed.

The combs move toward sound because the stillings are hungry. That is the simplest answer, and it is true. But it may not be the whole of it.

## How They Die

A comb dies the way a reef bleaches.

Something goes wrong, and it is not always clear what. A season of too little wind. A sound too loud and too sudden, a rockfall or a strike of lightning directly on the lattice, that the stillings take into themselves and cannot hold. A sickness that travels through the cells. Or simple age. The stillings go cloudy and do not clear again. Then they go white, then chalky, and then they are gone, and there is nothing in the cells but grey dust. The lattice, no longer tended, begins to slump. The glass loses its oily depth and turns the color of old bone.

When a comb dies, the silence around it dies with it. The first thing that comes in is the wind. Then the birds come back, the ones that still call, and the silence has left behind a lattice full of sheltered hollows at a convenient height. They build their nests in the empty cells. Their calls fill the space where the silence was.

The herders do not go near the dead combs if they can help it. They say it is like standing near a body. They say it is too loud.

## What They Say

The stillings cannot speak. They have no way to make a sound, and they would eat it if they did.

But they bend light.

Every stilling is a lens. A clear stilling in a cell refracts the sun one way, and a clouded one refracts it another. A stilling that has opened its fold bends light differently than one that has closed. The combs throw their caustic patterns across the stone, and those patterns are not only an accident of geometry. They change. They slide and knot and unknot, and not always in time with the sun. Sometimes, on a still morning, the caustics under a large comb will ripple suddenly outward in a ring, like a stone dropped into water, when nothing has moved at all.

Stand at the right distance from two combs, where their caustic fields overlap, and wait long enough, and you will see the patterns answer each other. It is slow, sometimes a few minutes between one ripple and its reply. It does not look like a conversation. It looks more like two people in a dark room, each lifting a lamp now and then so the other will know they are still there.

The lizards flash their throat fans in blue and ultraviolet. The small birds signal with the mirror patches on their wings. The rabbits flick their white tails to warn each other of falcons. The whole plateau speaks in light, and has for longer than anyone can say. The easy explanation is that animals near the combs learned to signal in light because sound was taken from them. That is probably true. But the combs were speaking in light first.

It is possible that the animals of the plateau learned their language the way a child learns one, by watching the adults.

## What They Remember

The burn-lines in the stone are scorched into the tuff by focused light over centuries. They are usually described as lace-work, and they do look like lace, irregular and delicate, spreading under each comb and trailing behind it.

The herders say they are writing.

Probably they are not writing in any sense a scholar would recognize. But the caustics a comb throws are shaped by the state of its stillings, and the state of its stillings is shaped by what they have heard. A comb that has listened to a great storm holds itself differently afterward. Its light falls differently, and burns differently into the rock beneath. Over a century the stone under a comb is marked by everything it has taken in. The trail behind it holds a record of every place it has stood, kilometers long, written in light onto stone. Where old trails cross, the records lie over one another.

If any reader could read that record, they would find everything the plateau has ever sounded like, since the combs began. The wind. The storms. Every rabbit, every falcon's stoop, every camelid's footfall. Every word a traveler spoke too near a comb. Every shout that was cut off and every sob that went into the lattice and came out as dew.

Nobody can read it. The stillings may not be able to either. Or they may have no need to, because they do not keep the record for themselves. They keep it because keeping it is what happens when something listens this carefully for this long.

## The Hand on the Wall

In one of the huts, someone painted a comb on the inside wall, and beneath it a hand with the fingers raised, as though for silence.

Look at it again. The fingers are raised, but they are also slightly curved, and the palm is turned outward and cupped. It is not quite the gesture a person makes to hush a room. It is the shape of a stilling in its cell.

The herders have customs around the combs that they do not explain to outsiders, and that they may not fully explain to themselves. You do not bring bells onto the plateau. You do not strike stone against stone near a comb for no reason. You do not shout, even in the open, and no one can quite say why. The shout would not reach a comb, and if it did, the comb would only eat it. The best explanation anyone gives is that the quiet belongs to the place, and it would be rude.

There is another custom, older, that most of them no longer practice. When someone has died, or when something has been lost that cannot be spoken of in the ordinary way, a person may walk out alone before dawn to a comb and stand within its silence and say the thing aloud. Say the name, say the grief, say whatever cannot be said. The voice leaves the mouth and stops. No one hears it, not even the speaker. It goes into the lattice and is taken.

Then you wait for sunrise. When the pans fill, you watch the rabbits come down to drink.

They say this helps, and that it is not because the grief is gone. It is because it has been heard, all of it, by something that took the whole of it into its body and did not give it back as an echo. It gave it back as water, and small animals drank it, and the day began.

## The One Beneath the Many

So far I have described the stillings as many small animals, one to a cell, spawning and drifting and dying, and that is what they are.

But spend enough mornings on the plateau and the description starts to feel incomplete, the way it feels incomplete to call a person a great many cells.

Consider how it works across the basin. A comb takes in sound and gives back cold and water. The water feeds the lichen, the cushion plants, the rosettes, the midges, the beetles, the lizards, the rabbits, the falcons, the camelids. All of them make small sounds, and the sounds feed the combs. The young of the stillings are carried from comb to comb inside the life that the combs sustain: on muzzles, on feet, in fur. They grow in the burrows of the animals that drink their water. They return to the lattice and feed it and move it. Their light passes from comb to comb across kilometers, and the animals speak in it. The whole basin is one slow circulation of sound into silence into water into life into sound.

The silence is the body of something. The combs are its organs. The pans are where it breathes out. The trails are its slow movements across the ground. The caustics are its thinking, if it thinks. The stillings in their cells are the cells of its body, and the rabbits and falcons and lizards and ferns live inside it the way the bacteria of your gut live inside you, necessary and never quite aware of the larger life they are part of.

It has no name, though the herders' word for the quiet places near the combs is the same word they use for the stillings, only spoken with a different weight. It is not quite a god. It is too literal for that, too much a matter of glass and water and small soft bodies. It is not quite an animal. It is too large and too slow, and its boundaries are drawn in sound rather than skin. It is closer to what the first description of the place called it without knowing: a held breath. Something that is, all the time, all across the basin, holding itself still so as to hear.

## What It Listens For

The easy answer is that it listens for food, and it moves toward sound because it is hungry. But the stillings open widest at night, when there is least to eat, and they turn, in their cells, not only toward the loud passes but upward.

And there is the pavement.

A band of black fused stone runs straight across the basin, cutting through domes, bridging pans on nothing, and ending in the air above the cloud-sea. Whoever made it made the combs, or made the world the combs were made for. They went somewhere. The pavement ends in air. Perhaps it did not always end there. Perhaps it ends there the way a pier ends at the water.

If you have built something that can hear everything, and then you leave, you might want it to go on listening. You might want it to take in every ordinary sound of the world and turn it into water, and keep the living things fed, and keep itself fed, and clear away the noise year after year for ten thousand years. The purpose would not be silence. The purpose would be that something very faint could still be heard over the silence if it ever came. A voice from far off. A footstep on the pavement.

Or perhaps not. Perhaps the builders made the combs for their own reasons and the stillings came afterward and moved in, the way birds move into a dead lattice. Perhaps what began as a machine has become simply a life, and the listening is not for anything. It may just be the shape the life has taken, the way a rabbit's ears are the shape of a rabbit's fear.

The herders have an opinion. They will not tell you what it is. But on the nights when a storm walks along the escarpment without thunder, and the lightning shows the combs standing all across the basin, every lattice seems to lean very slightly west. The old herders get up from their fires and turn to face the same way. They stand there with their hands at their sides and do not speak, even out in the open where they could.

## By the Pan, Again

So lie at the edge of the pan again, on the warm stone, under the stars, with the comb dark above you and its cold on your face.

There is no sound. You can hear only the faint pulse of your own blood, and even that seems muffled.

It is muffled because it is being taken. The comb above you has turned the whole of its attention, in ten thousand small clear bodies, toward the only sound left near it. Your heartbeat goes into the lattice. So does your breath. So does every small shift of your body on the stone. You are being heard as completely as you have ever been heard, by something that will keep what it hears written in light in the rock for as long as the rock lasts. It does not judge. It does not answer. It only listens, the way a cupped hand holds water.

Toward the end of the night a drop forms on the lowest edge of the comb, grows and falls. It strikes the pan and sends out rings that carry the stars, and among the stars, unseen, the smallest clear beginnings of the ones who listen.

The drop is made of the night. It is made of the wind that crossed the basin, the insects out in the open rock, the falcon's wings as it came home to its ledge. It is made, a little, of you.

The water gathers. The east goes pale. Soon the rabbits will come down to drink, and the lizards will climb out into the light and begin their silent arguments in blue, and the birds will flash their mirrors across the domes. The whole plateau will wake and glitter and speak in light, fed on what was heard in the dark.

And the stillings, in their cells, will fold themselves closed against the coming sun. They will keep listening.
