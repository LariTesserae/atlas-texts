# What Lives at Low Tallow

## The Ones Who Work There

Start with the obvious answer: the rooks, the rats, the jackdaws, the bats, the beetles, the mice, the moths, the swifts, and the pale grey skin of yeast on the cistern. They are counted, all but the yeast. Their numbers are on the wall in white lead, and the numbers are right.

It is worth being careful here, though. Watch any of them long enough and you will stop thinking of them as living at Low Tallow. They work there. The difference is hard to put into words, but it becomes clear after an hour or two, and it is the same difference you feel between a house and a warehouse.

A rook on the chimney has no nest. Look for one and you won't find it: no sticks, no lining, no scrape. The young rook that takes a dead bird's place on the ledge arrives feathered and ragged and ready. It was never hatched here, so far as anyone can tell. It never squabbles for its spot. It never preens longer than it takes to set a feather straight. The rats in the wheels do not groom each other or play. The mice do not stop. None of these creatures has a place to go when the work is done, because the work is not done. They are not residents. They are the shift, and the shift does not end, so they have never had to learn what to do with themselves afterwards.

So the honest answer to *who lives at Low Tallow* is that almost nothing does. Almost everything there is employed.

The word *almost* matters.

## The Width of the Crown

The last thing you notice as you leave is the thing to begin with: the brick crown of the embankment is rounded at its edges.

Everything else at Low Tallow has been worn to the shape of its traffic. The mill sills are grooved to the breadth of a rat's belly. The engine house corners are swept to the arc of a jackdaw's turning wingtip. The cistern rivets are domed at the height where swifts skim. So you can read the dyke the same way, and when you do, it tells you some things.

The wear runs all the way round, one continuous track, with no start and no finish. It is about as wide as a person walking, and a little wider than a person walking carefully. It sits on the inner edge of the crown, the basin side, never the marsh side. Whatever makes it walks with its shoulder to the works and its back to the reeds, looking down.

The wear is shallower than the wear on the engine house steps. Those were worn by a million claws. This was worn by fewer feet over the same time, or by one pair over longer, or by one pair that is very heavy.

The dyke is also the only place from which all nine structures can be seen at once. The chimney, the mill, the gasometer, the canal, the terrace, the weighhouse, the cistern, the engine house, the counting wall. From the embankment you can count them. That is the first thing anyone says about Low Tallow, and most people say it without noticing what it means. The dyke is where the counting is done.

## The Watchman

The first shape it takes in the mind is a watchman.

Every works of this kind had one. He was an old man, usually, too stiff for the stones or the firebox, kept on for his honesty and his bad sleep. He had a lantern, a coat, a stick, a round to walk, and a book in which to write that all was well. He went round in the small hours, when the works ran with a skeleton crew, and it was his job to be the one person awake who wasn't working. He was the attention of the place while its hands were busy.

There is no night at Low Tallow, so the watchman has no lantern. It is always half past three, so he is always on his round. And the works have not had a skeleton crew for a long time. Now they have more crew than any works has ever had, every hand filled exactly. So the watchman has nothing to report. Every round he looks down, counts the nine, and finds everything as it was.

That is the first shape. It doesn't hold, because the watchman image is too small and too kind. It leaves out the yards.

## The Foreman's Floor

Think again about the open clay.

Most of the basin by area is bare yard, and nothing uses it. Every lane is exactly the width of its traffic, and nothing spills over. The rooks go by rope, the mice by stream, the beetles by drip-channel. You have been told this is simply how the system works: efficient, closed, with nothing to spare for wandering.

Anyone who has worked a shop floor will recognise that bare yard for what it is. It is the space in front of the foreman's office. It is the stretch of floor that stays clear because it can be seen, because anyone crossing it is visibly not at their station. The lanes aren't only efficient. They keep out of sight. Every creature at Low Tallow moves in the shadow of a wall, along a gutter, under a bridge, in a rope of its own kind. The open clay is where the watching lands.

So the watchman on the dyke is not idle, and he was never a watchman. What walks the crown is the thing whose looking keeps the yards empty. Its gaze falls on the clay the way rain falls on a roof, and nothing living will stand in it.

Except you.

When you stand in the yard and nothing looks at you, you are wrong about that. Nothing below looks at you. They don't need to, because you are standing where the looking already is. The feeling of not being needed that settles on every visitor is not something you bring with you. It is the verdict of being assessed by something whose only question is *does this have a column?* It finds you have none and lets you stand there, uncounted and unremoved. You are a button dropped on the clay.

The thought that comes to most people after an hour, the one slow thought, *this is finished*, may not be yours either. You may just be close enough to hear it.

## The Weight

The weighhouse is where the shape goes strange.

The needle stands at one hundredweight and never quivers, though an endless stream of mice crosses the plate. The mice weigh nothing that the scale admits to. Something else is on it: exactly one hundredweight, all the time.

A hundredweight is not a round number. It is a hundred and twelve pounds, the weight of a sack of coal as the coalman used to carry it on his back, or of a lean grown man, or of a woman who has worked since she was nine.

So there is a contradiction. Something walks the whole round of the dyke, and something stands without moving on the weighhouse scale. You could decide there are two of them. The better answer, the one that fits a place where every lane is exactly the width of its traffic, is that there is one, and it is on the scale because it is being weighed. Every round it makes is also a weighing. The mice go into the hut with nothing and come out with coal, and what the hut does with the difference is its business. Something is being paid out at a steady rate. The coal that keeps the firebox that keeps the steam that keeps the yeast that keeps the swifts comes, grain by grain, from whatever has been standing on that plate since the needle last moved.

It is not being used up. The needle doesn't drop. It is being drawn on, the way a spring is drawn on or a bank account with a fixed income. It is the one thing at Low Tallow the loop takes from without giving back, and the loop still balances. That means it is not part of the loop. It is where the loop gets its capital.

## Tallow

The name of the place has been sitting there all along.

Tallow is rendered fat. It is what candles were made of before gas, and soap, and the grease on axles and engine glands. Low Tallow, then, was a rendering works before it was anything else, a place where things were boiled down to what would burn.

There are eight cottages, and every one has a gas mantle burning with a pale green flame that has never gone out. There is no gas main. The gasometer neither rises nor falls. Its drum hangs half-risen, half full of whatever it holds, and nothing has drawn on it that the drum admits to. Eight families' worth of lamps burn in eight houses with no one in them, and the moths live on that light.

This is what the workers became, or part of it. They did not leave, and they did not become something countable. They were rendered: the long working lives of eight households, boiled down over a very long time into one thing that burns slowly and steadily. It has a hundredweight's heft, and it can walk. The lamps in the terrace are what it left on in the windows. People do that when they mean to come home.

## What It Looks Like

No one has seen it. Here is what can be pieced together.

It is tall, taller than the doors of the terrace, which is one reason it does not go in. It wears something like a coat, heavy and dark and cut long. The coat is beaded all over with condensation, the way the iron is and the way your own hands become after an hour, so in the half-past-three light it would glitter faintly, like a wet slate roof. Its hands would be pale and smooth, soft at the knuckles and rounded at the fingertips, the way everything here gets rounded. They would be tallow-coloured, candle-coloured, the colour of the yellow chimney bands that the rooks' feet have polished to old ivory.

Its face turns always toward the basin. Whatever expression it has is the one you wear when you are adding a column of figures in your head and have almost reached the bottom. It is not unkind. It is occupied.

It walks slowly. Here is the one fact you can test, if you are willing to stand on the dyke for a long time.

## Out of Step

Everything at Low Tallow keeps time. The beam comes down every eleven seconds. The rat-wheels squeak in step. The bats in the gasometer click the beat. The whole basin holds one chord and does not waver, and after an hour the chord becomes a kind of silence.

In that silence, if you stand still on the crown, you may hear a step.

It is not loud. It sounds like a boot on wet brick, somewhere round the curve of the embankment, out of sight. The strange thing about it, the thing that raises the hair on your arms, is that it is not in time. It falls between the engine strokes, sometimes early, sometimes late. There might be a pause, then two steps close together, the way someone stops to look at something and then catches up.

It is the only irregular sound in the basin, which makes it the only living one.

Everything below it is working, perfectly, without surplus or shortfall. Up on the rim, going round, is the one thing at Low Tallow that still hesitates, the one thing that stops to look. It is the only resident. Everything else is staff.

## The Clock

The clock on the engine house gable says twenty to nine, and its hands do not move.

Twenty to nine is not a time anything happens at a works. The hooter goes at six, or seven, or eight. Twenty to nine is the time someone arrives who has been told to come at nine and is anxious not to be late. It is the time you get there early, stand at the gate, and wait for the others.

You don't have to take that literally, but it is hard not to. Something at Low Tallow came early to its shift and is still waiting for the others to arrive. Meanwhile it walks the round, because walking the round is the thing it knows how to do. Meanwhile the jackdaws feed the firebox and the rats turn the stones. The work goes on so well that no one will ever be needed again, and the thing on the dyke knows this. It counts the nine and finds them all present and correct. It thinks, slowly, *this is finished*. Then it keeps walking, because being finished is not the same as being over, and nobody has come to tell it to go home.

## The Swifts

It seems to be why the swifts go out.

They are the only creatures that leave the basin. They go up over the dyke, past the place where it walks, and down onto the marsh, and they come back with insects. That is their function in the loop, and the loop would fail without it. But you never see a swift leave without another coming in, as if the basin can't bear to be without one of them out there, looking.

You could read it as supply. You could also read it as a single question, sent out over the reeds again and again, a few hundred times an hour: *anyone coming?* The swifts come back and say nothing. They are the quiet things in the place, the only ones. The walker on the dyke goes on round.

## The Tenth Column

Go back down to the counting wall before you leave, if you can bear the yard a second time.

There are nine columns of figures in white lead, one for each structure. Look at the right-hand end of the wall. The brick there is unpainted, a clean strip exactly the width of one column. No weather has touched it. No soot has settled on it. It is the only surface at Low Tallow that isn't worn to fit something.

That space is its column. It has never been filled, and it cannot be filled from the inside, because the one who keeps the count can't count itself. To write a number you have to stand outside the thing you are numbering. Everyone at Low Tallow is inside, except for the occasional visitor who comes down off the embankment onto the bare clay and stands for a while where the looking lands.

The figure would not be large. You know what it would be. You have stood on the crown, counted nine, and felt, for a moment, what it is like to be the one doing the counting.

Most people don't write it. They climb back to the dyke with their hands beaded like iron and their coats heavy, and they turn for a last look. The beam nods, the smoke leans, the lamps glow green in the terrace windows for families who are not coming home. Then somewhere round the long curve of the embankment, a step falls out of time.

Then another, closer.

The marsh behind you looks almost unbearably loose. That is the right way to go.
