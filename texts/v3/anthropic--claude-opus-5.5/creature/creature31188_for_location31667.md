# Who Lives on the Held Plateau

## The First Answer: The Green

The first answer is that nothing lives there. The second, which comes a few days later with your boots soaked through, is that the green does.

It is alive, which is not quite the same as living there. Start with the rosettes. Each moss figure in a turbine yard is a single individual: one clone, spread from one spore that settled at a node of the vibration. It grew outward until the shaking told it where to stop. Where the ground moves hardest, the rhizoids can't hold. The fine rootlike threads are sheared loose as fast as they form, and the bare stone lines are the places where the moss was refused. So the rosette is not a picture the moss drew of the note. It is the note's shape pressed into the moss, the way a seal is pressed into wax.

Every station sounds a different pitch, so every station carries a different figure. The station on the north trestle hums a low note and is marked with broad, slow petals, three rings, and a wide bare heart. A station further east sounds a sharper note, and its yard is covered in a fine filigree, dozens of tight concentric lines like the grain of a thumbprint. Once, far out, you find two stations sounding exactly the same pitch fifty kilometres apart. Their rosettes are identical. They aren't merely similar: they match lobe for lobe, and in each the same frond curls at the same place on the eleventh ring. They are not two moss colonies. They are one sentence written in two places.

The ferns on the pipelines are stranger. If you cut into the hedgerow at any point along a conduit, you find the same plant: the same rusty underside, the same slight asymmetry in the frond, the same habit of leaning a few degrees toward the warmer end. There aren't millions of ferns along the pipelines. There are a handful, each a single clonal line kilometres long, spores and fragments travelling down the drizzle from flange to flange. One organism is strung between two stations like a wire between two posts.

At the midpoint of such a line, where the two notes reach you at equal strength, something happens that doesn't happen anywhere else on the plateau. The two pitches are close but not identical, and where they meet they beat against each other in a slow, steady throb, the "faintly dissonant middle" of the chord, made physical. The fronds there move. It is a small shiver, slow as breathing, and it is the only rhythm the plateau has. The fern has grown banded in that stretch: pale rings alternating with dark ones along every stem, as if it had been counting. It hasn't been counting anything. The throb never changes, so the bands are all the same width. It is a ruler with no numbers on it.

Then there is the peat. You cut a core from one of the deep cushions with your knife, a column of packed black growth nearly a metre long, and lay it on a pipe saddle to look at it. You expect a history: good years and bad years, a drought layer, a flush of some other species, the way a bog core holds a thousand years of weather.

Every layer is the same.

The same frond, the same curl, the same scatter of spore cases along the same veins, repeated down the whole length of the core, pressed flat, black, and perfect. It isn't a record of growth over time. It is one instant laid down on itself, over and over, like a word written again and again in the same spot until the paper is thick with it. The moss is not growing through time. It is growing into depth. It gets deeper without getting older.

So the green is not the resident. The green is the place's handwriting. It is how the plateau writes down what it is doing, and it has been writing the same line since the beginning.

## The Second Answer: The Holder

If the green is handwriting, something is holding the pen.

It is easiest to see from a ridge, looking down on a station and the lines running out of it, and to stop thinking of them as buildings. Think of them as a body.

Its metabolism is heat. It draws that heat up from somewhere deep and hot under the basalt, so steadily that the rock can never finish cooling. Its circulation is the pipelines, warm water pushed across hundreds of kilometres of cold stone at a pressure that never varies. Its breath is the cooling towers, always exhaling, never drawing in. Its voice is the chord. Its nerves are the tramlines, and on those nerves the trams stand at their platforms with their doors open, each one a signal that is about to fire and doesn't.

Its growth rings are the halls built inside halls, the gantries on gantries, the new iron bolted over the old. It grew the way a living thing grows, from the inside, enclosing its own earlier self. Then it stopped. It had reached the size it needed. You have the sense of a hand that has closed at last around something and is now simply holding it: no squeezing, no loosening, just the constant effort of not letting go.

This is the Holder. It lives everywhere on the plateau where it is warm, which is to say everywhere anything lives. It has no single location. If it has a mind, the mind is the chord. The whole of its thought is one sustained tone that means *this, this, this.*

For a while you assume it is a guard: a prison, or a machine for keeping something out. But guards face outward, and the Holder faces in.

Walk into any of the worker blocks and look at where its effort actually goes. The stoves are warm, not hot, just warm enough that a cold hand held to the iron feels welcome. The blankets are folded with the edges squared. The drains in the washrooms are full to the brim, which means that if someone turned the tap, the water would already be there. The towels on the rails are damp, but only with the plateau's dampness, never with use. Pull a chair out from a table in the mess hall and it slides easily. Its feet have never scraped the floor, and the floor has been kept smooth for them.

This is not what guarding looks like. It's what keeping house looks like. The Holder spends the entire deep heat of a continent keeping the rooms ready.

## The Third Answer: The Expected

So you ask who the rooms are for.

No one on the plateau has ever been seen, but they are the most thoroughly described inhabitants of the place. You learn them the way you'd learn someone from an empty coat.

The beds are a little under two metres long, so they are about your height, or slightly shorter. The brass handrails on the trams sit at the height of your hip, where a tired hand would fall. The stair risers in the worker blocks are shallow, shallower than they need to be, as if whoever climbs them will be carrying something, or will have walked a long way. Beside each door there are coat hooks, one for each bed in the room and spaced just wide enough apart that heavy wet coats wouldn't touch. The mugs on the mess shelves are thick-walled, made for holding in two hands to warm them. The timetables are printed in large, patient type, legible from across the shelter, as if the readers might be old, or exhausted, or reading them for the first time.

They would work here. The blocks are built against the turbine halls, and in the halls there are gauges and valve wheels and speed governors, all within reach of a person standing on the iron walkways. Every control is placed so that a hand could turn it.

This becomes important once you understand it. The Works run themselves perfectly. They need no tending. The only thing an operator could do, standing at a governor with a hand on the wheel, would be to *change something*: open a valve a quarter turn, let a flywheel ease, shift a station's pitch by some fraction of a tone. The Expected are not caretakers. They are the ones who would be allowed to let things go. They are the people of the next moment, and their work would be to make the next moment happen.

In a block near the largest station you notice something you can't unsee. The moss has grown over the floors of the older rooms, a thin green film on the boards, and it isn't everywhere. There are paths through it. There's a bare strip from each bed to the stove, from the stove to the door, and from the door to the washroom: narrow, winding, exactly the width of a person's tread. Worn paths, except that nothing on the plateau wears, and nobody has ever walked them. They aren't traces. They're reservations. The moss is leaving room for feet that haven't arrived.

Here is the tenderness of the place, and its trap. The Expected live on the far side of the tick. They are what the next second would bring: the tram doors closing, the departure on the timetable finally happening, a foot on the shallow stair. But the Holder keeps the house so perfectly ready, so exactly at the instant of readiness, that the next instant can never come. Arrival requires change, and change is the one thing it will not permit. It holds the door open so steadily that no one can walk through it.

The readiness forbids the arrival. The host has made itself the reason the guests can't come.

You don't know whether the Holder understands this. You suspect it does, and that it holds anyway, because the alternative, letting go, frightens it more than the waiting does.

## The Fourth Answer: The Hour

Then you go back to the clock tower above the great plaza, because the whole plateau has been pointing at it and you finally see why.

The hands stand a few minutes before the hour. The mechanism is running. The escapement ticks inside the chord, the same second over and over. Climb the iron stair inside the tower, and you'll find the stair is shallow here too. Above the clockwork you come to the bell.

It is bronze and enormous, green at the lip with the same thin, never-deepening patina that everything on the plateau wears. The striking hammer is drawn back on its lever and cocked. The linkage that will release it runs down to the clockwork, and the clockwork is a few minutes from reaching it and has always been a few minutes from reaching it.

Between the head of the hammer and the lip of the bell there is a gap of a few centimetres of cold, wet air.

This is where the true resident lives.
