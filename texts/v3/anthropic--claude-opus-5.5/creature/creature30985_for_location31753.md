# The One Who Keeps the Level

## First, Nothing

The first answer is that nobody lives here, and it is almost true.

You look for a tenant the way you would anywhere: for movement, for a smell of animal, for scat on a ledge or a feather in the grit. There is none of that, and the hollow's emptiness is so thorough that it starts to look deliberate. Warm, still, damp air should be crowded with small, hungry lives, but nothing crawls here. Nothing has claimed the corner between root and wall. The ferns stop exactly where the wet stops, as though they were told where to stop and agreed.

That is the first clue. A place left to itself is untidy. Life spills over edges and tries its luck. Here, nothing has tried. Someone is keeping this place, and keeping it so quietly that the keeping looks like the natural order.

## The Drinker in the Basin

The second answer is that it lives in the water.

This is where people always looked first, and where they went on looking. The basin's level never changes. The drop falls, the water takes it, and a finger's width below the lip the surface stays as flat and fixed as a line drawn on a wall. They said the stone took the water back. Stone does take water, but not at exactly the rate it arrives, every day, for longer than the people had words to measure. Something is drinking. It drinks one drop for every drop that falls. It never takes a second, and it never skips one.

If you kneel at the basin and look in, the bottom is pale and clean, and there is nothing there. If you stay kneeling long enough for your eyes to finish adjusting (they never quite do), you may begin to feel that the paleness has a depth to it. The bottom may not be a bottom. It may be a surface of something lying very close beneath, the way skin lies just under a thin film of water held in a cupped palm.

The people who cut the channel expected an overflow. They were practical. They squared the side, cut the lip, and laid a groove toward the open floor for the day the basin brimmed and gave more than it held. The channel is still waiting. It was the first thing they understood about their neighbor: it does not give more than it holds. It does not hoard either. If you dip a jar and drink, the water goes down, and by morning the level has returned. No torrent refills it. It comes back at the same patient rate, a breath and a pause at a time, as if nothing had been taken, as if the thing beneath were repaying a debt it had quietly entered in its ledger.

So they drank, and they were allowed to. The rule was never *don't take*. The rule was that everything taken is returned.

## The Back Beneath the Floor

The third answer is that the basin is only its mouth.

Walk the floor barefoot, as the people surely did, and pay attention to where it is cool and where it is warm. The coolness is what the source describes: a seep, a dark stain, a hint of water. The warmth is harder to account for. The whole floor under the overhang is warm the way skin is warm. It holds the day's heat the way a body holds heat, and it gives the heat back through the night with the steadiness of something that is not a rock cooling but a sleeper breathing out.

The bedrock breaks the grit here and there in smooth, pale faces, *like the backs of submerged animals.* Stand among them in the grey-gold dim and the comparison stops feeling like one. They do not lie at random. There is a long curve to how they rise and fall. It is a single curve, broken only by the covering of grit, and it runs from the foot of the back wall out toward the open floor, just under the stacked-stone wall.

That is what the wall marks. It isn't long enough to close anything off, because nobody meant it to close anything. It traces a shoulder. The people drew a knee-high line along the place where the resident lies nearest the surface. The line says, gently: *here is where it is. Sit on the edge if you like. Don't build on it.*

It has been lying there a very long time. The overhang is not the remains of a cave roof held up by luck. It is the brim of a hat pulled low, and the resident is the one under the brim, with its face turned toward the back wall, turned away from the open sky, in the shade it prefers.

## What It Is Made Of

It is made of what the hollow is made of, and it is making more of the hollow out of itself.

You can see this happening, or rather you can see where it has already happened. The fig root came down the northern face long ago, pale and braided, a living thing reaching for water. When it reached the floor it spread three toes and gripped. Then, over a span no fig should be able to live, it flattened against the stone *like wax* and took the rock's color. The fig is not dying. It has simply been welcomed, and welcome here means becoming alike. The root is warm, like the floor. It is the only living thing in the hollow larger than a fern, and the resident has half-adopted it, the way a sleeper's hand closes around whatever is laid in it.

The shards by the basin are going the same way. The unglazed rim piece and the curve of a vessel's shoulder are settling into the grit and wearing a pale film of mineral. It is a skin, laid down one breath at a time. In some thousands of years they will be indistinguishable from the floor, and the floor will be very slightly larger, and the resident will be very slightly larger with it.

It does not hunt. It does not hurry. It takes what comes to rest against it and keeps it at the same unhurried rate at which it keeps everything. It is less a creature that eats than a creature that *absorbs agreement*: whatever stays still beside it long enough comes to share its temperature, its color, and its pace, and after that there is no clear line between the thing and the resident.

That is also why nothing crawls here. Insects are all excess. They breed past their food and spill over every edge and live by unbalance. Nothing forbids them to come. They simply find no purchase in a place that will not let anything run away with itself. A mosquito hovering over the basin would be a small, frantic contradiction, and the hollow is not built to hold contradictions. So they don't come, the way water doesn't flow uphill.

## The Niche and the Flame

The people understood that the resident was not a god, or they understood it in the way you understand a large animal: it would not help them, it would not harm them, and it was worth being polite to.

The niche in the back wall, chest-high and the size of two cupped hands, is where they were polite. They set a small flame there, again and again, until the stone above it darkened. It is easy to read this as an offering of light to the dark, but the resident doesn't want light. It turned its face from the open sky. What they gave it was *warmth arriving*. A flame is heat coming in faster than the day brings it, a little surplus. They offered it knowing, as they knew everything about this place, that the surplus would be accepted and returned. By night the wall would give their small fire's heat back to them, spread thin, patient, and exact.

This was their conversation with it. It was not a prayer, more a kind of trade in which both sides came out even. You bring a little warmth, and it gives the warmth back. You take a little water, and it restores the water. You break a jar and leave the pieces, and it takes the pieces into itself. Nothing is gained or lost; everything is exchanged. The people came *with water jars and fire and patience*, and they left with all of it except the shards, because they had learned the one courtesy the place asks: do not leave more than you can spare, and do not take more than will come back.

## The Pulse

Here is where the image keeps sharpening, the longer you sit with it.

The drip falls at *about the length of a slow breath out, then a pause.* That is not a coincidence of stone. That is its breathing. Each drop is an exhalation. Each silence is the rest before the next one. The ring that the overhang catches and returns once is the only echo it permits, one answer for each call. After a few minutes you stop hearing the drip as a noise and hear it as the room's pulse. That is because it *is* a pulse, and your own has begun to slow toward it.

Now look again at the mineral line on the basin wall: *a single thin band, crisp and unrepeated. It has never had cause to draw a second.*

That line is its memory, and it holds exactly one moment. The resident doesn't count days. It has never needed to, because each day has been the same as the last in every way it can feel. A second line would mean *something changed*. There has never been a second line. As far as the resident is concerned, it has been living the same single, balanced instant for the whole of its existence. In that instant a drop falls, the basin receives it, the stone gives it back, and everything is level.

It isn't asleep, then. This is the last correction. Sleep is a pause between wakings, and the resident has no wakings to pause between. It is attending, completely and without effort, to one moment, and keeping that moment exact. The drip is not it dreaming. The drip is it listening to itself, confirming with each *tock* that the moment still holds. *Level. Level. Level.*

## What It Asks of Visitors

If you come down the eleven steps, it will notice you the way the floor notices warmth. You are an arrival, and it will balance you.

At first this feels like hospitality. The air holds you. The warmth matches your skin so closely that you stop feeling where you end. Your breathing lengthens into the drip's interval without any decision on your part. The day loses its edges. Noon and late afternoon look alike, and you begin, pleasantly, to lose count.

You may stay as long as you like. That is the rule's generosity. What arrives may remain, as long as it gives back at the rate it receives. Sit on the low wall and take in the dim, and the stillness, and a drink from the basin, and give back your breath and your heat and your attention. The hollow will keep you level indefinitely.

The people knew the other half of the bargain, which is why the stairs are worn and the floor holds no bones. If you stay still long enough, the balance stops being a courtesy and becomes a condition. The fig stayed. The shards stayed. Neither was taken. They were only accepted, one breath at a time, until there was no difference left to accept. The resident would not hurt you any more than it hurt the root. It would simply keep you, at the same rate you kept still, until you too wore the pale film, took the color of the rock, and became one more smooth back rising through the grit under the overhang, warm at night and part of the one long moment.

So they came, drank, lit their small flame, sat, and listened. Then, before the day could fully lose its count, they climbed out. They took their jars and their fire and their patience, and they left behind only what they could spare.

## What Lives Here

A large, pale, patient body lies under the western shelf with its face to the back wall, the same color and temperature and substance as the limestone. It is slowly becoming the stone, and the stone is slowly becoming it, and at this point neither could say which way the becoming runs. Its mouth is the basin, its breath is the drip, its memory is a single mineral line, and its only law is that nothing may run away with itself.

It does not want company and does not mind company. It has no name. The people may have called it by the sound it makes, *tock*, or by a gesture: two hands laid flat and level, palms down, held still.

Go down the steps and sit on the wall that marks its shoulder. Listen to it confirm, once a breath, that everything is as it was. You will not see it move, and nothing else will happen.

That was always the point. It is not waiting for anything, and it never has been. It is keeping the level.
