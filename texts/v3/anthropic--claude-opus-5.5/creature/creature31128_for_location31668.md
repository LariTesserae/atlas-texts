# Who Lives in the Header Tank

## The First Answer

Ask what lives in the Header Tank and the honest first answer is: *the shrimp*. There are more of them than of anything else. At any moment perhaps forty thousand hang in the weed, and on every upstroke the room fills with them the way the air over a summer field fills with seed.

A shrimp lives about four hundred days. That comes to a little over three million strokes. It hatches at dawn, in the milky hour when frost covers the window and the light is soft enough for the eggs to lie open to it. Its mother carries the clutch under her tail until the moment is right, and the right moment is always the top of an upstroke. She lets go, and the bulge of warm water from below lifts the eggs into the open room like a hand opening. On the downstroke they are pulled a little way toward the grilles, and then the weed catches them. Most of them. The rest go down the mountain.

So the first thing a shrimp ever knows is the stroke, and it knows it as pleasure. The upstroke is warmth and food and being lifted. The downstroke is the flick back into cover, the thrill of the pull going past. A shrimp does not experience the stroke as danger any more than you experience your own breathing as danger. It is the shape of the day, repeated two hundred and fifty times an hour. The clicking of their tails is not fear. It is closer to applause.

They are small, clear, flecked with orange, and they are not who this place belongs to. But they are its joy, and any account of what lives here that left them out would be describing a machine.

## The Second Answer

Look more slowly and the answer changes: *the snails*.

A snail in the Header Tank lives for years. Nobody is here to count how many, but the shells keep the count themselves. Each is built from the rust the snail grazes, laid down in thin bands, and the bands differ in color with what the snail was eating. Iron from the dome wall lays down a deep, almost purple red. Iron from near the piston, where the moss has already half-digested it, lays down something warmer, nearly orange. A snail's shell is a map of where it has been, wound into a spiral.

The snails travel their lanes. Those are the polished paths the description mentions, the ones cut deeper than the rivet valleys in the oldest parts of the tank. The paths are not random. Each snail follows the slime of the ones before it, because the slime marks where the grazing has been good. Over uncounted generations the lanes have become roads, and the roads have become a kind of settled opinion about the room: this way, and then this way, and then down this seam.

If you traced every lane, you would find that more of them converge on the small rectangular plate beside the ladder than on anything else in the tank.

That plate was stamped iron, and letters stamped into iron hold rust in their grooves longer than smooth metal does. For a very long time the plate was the richest grazing in the room. The snails came to it in procession. They ate the words.

Whatever the builders wrote there, whether it was a name, a warning, a date, or a number to check against, it did not simply wear away. It was eaten, a letter at a time, and laid down again in thin red bands inside a thousand spiral shells. Then those snails died. The downstroke took their shells through the grilles and sent them down the mountain. The builders' last message went out through the conduits after all, in pieces too small to read, and some of it may still be tumbling in a lowland gutter.

The snails are the tank's writing now. They write slowly and in a script only they follow, and what they are writing is *here, and then here, and then down this seam*. They are closer to the answer than the shrimp. But they are not who lives here either.

## The Third Answer

The eels know something the rest do not.

They live in the gap between the piston crown and the sleeve, and in that gap you cannot be wrong about the stroke even once. A shrimp that mistimes a flick loses a meal. An eel that mistimes a descent is simply gone, pressed into the wall and taken downward with the moss. So the eels do not just keep time with the stroke. They feel it the way you feel a step in the dark, with their whole long bodies pressed to the moving iron.

Because of that, they know what nothing else in the room could know: **the stroke is not perfect.**

It is not exactly eleven seconds. Some strokes run long by the width of a heartbeat. Some come a hair early. On the coldest nights, when frost covers the window's outer face so thickly that it stays half-grey into mid-morning, the stroke slows. Perhaps the far engines in the lowlands are sluggish with cold, or the water in the long conduits has thickened. The downstroke lags. The eels feel the lag in the crown against their bellies and adjust without thinking.

The eels also go down. They are the only creatures in the tank that regularly do. In the breeding season they let the downstroke take them, slipping through the grilles on purpose, and they travel a long way into the conduits, into branches no light has ever reached. Some of them come back up on the long return. The pale ones that drape themselves over the lip of the sleeve and taste the room are often these returners. They lie there for one upstroke with their faces lifted, as though checking that the top of the world is still where they left it.

What they check, specifically, is the fish.

## The Fish

It hangs just below the window, dark green and heavy, fins barely moving. It does not feed. The other animals give it room.

Watch it long enough and a few small things begin to add up.

Its scales are worn smooth, like the rivets. Some of them have the same faint blistered roundness, and a few look less like scales than like plates. Along its flank, under the green, there is a seam.

It does not feed, and a fish that does not feed for as long as this one has would starve. It has not. Something else keeps it.

It holds its position. Everything else in the room sways with the stroke: the weed leans, the shrimp are lofted, even the lily-plants tremble on their roots. The fish holds its place against the push and the pull with small, exact movements of its fins, and it holds it within a finger's width, every stroke, all day.

Its fins move a fraction of a second before the stroke, not after.

Everything else in the tank reacts. The eels are the most precise reactors in the room, but they are still reacting. The fish moves slightly ahead. It braces for the push before the push begins. Watching it is like watching someone lean into a wave they can see coming, when nothing in the room can see the wave coming.

Then look above it. A fine dark filament rises from its lower jaw, so thin that the silvery bubbles collecting under the glass hide it. It climbs to a small housing set into the dome beside the window. The housing has a pivot and a short arm, and it is the one thing in the whole room whose edges are still sharp. It has not been worn round. It has hardly moved in a very long time.

A header tank, in the plumbing of the builders, is the highest vessel in a system. Its job is to hold the level. Every header tank has a float. When the water is low, the float sinks and the arm drops, and far below a valve opens and the pumps work. When the water is high enough, the float rises, the arm lifts, and the pumps are told: *enough. Rest.*

The fish is where the float was.

It may be the float itself, a hollow thing of plate and seam that the moss and the weed and a very long time have made into something that is no longer only a thing. It may be a real fish that swallowed the float's function along with its chain, long ago, and was changed by carrying it. It may be both, and the difference may have stopped mattering several ages back. What matters is what it does.

A float in a tank that is completely full, with no surface anywhere, would simply rise. It would press up against the underside of the dome, the arm would lift, and the signal would go down the mountain: *full. Enough. Rest.* And the stroke would stop.

The fish does not rise.

It holds itself a finger's width below the glass, exactly neutral, neither sinking nor rising. It holds the arm at the one angle that says *not yet*. It does not feed, because feeding would change its weight. A single shrimp in its belly would tip the balance by some tiny amount, the arm would twitch, and somewhere in the lowlands a valve the size of a house would begin to close. So it takes nothing. It lives on the light, the way the weed does. It adjusts its fins a moment before each push, because it cannot afford to be moved.

Every eleven seconds, it decides again to stay.

## What the Shells Remember

Sometimes it has not been perfect.

Among the snail shells cycling through the tank there is, now and then, a pale band. It is not red, not purple, not orange, but a thin line of almost grey, the color that shells lay down when a snail stops eating for a while. It is the color of a snail that was frightened.

The oldest snails all have it at the same depth in their spirals.

Nothing living remembers directly. No shrimp lives that long, and the eels keep their knowledge in their bodies, not in stories. But the shells record it, and the record says that at some point, once, the stroke did not come. There was a stillness longer than eleven seconds. The weed stood upright in water that was not being pulled. The shrimp hung in the open with no current to carry them home. For the first time in the history of the room there was silence, and the silence was, as the description says, the loudest thing that had ever happened here.

Then the stroke came back.

What happened in between, the shells cannot say. Perhaps the fish rose. Perhaps it grew tired, or old, or simply *considered* it, drifted up and touched the glass and felt the frost on the other side and the violet sky beyond. Perhaps it wanted, for a moment, to be finished. And the mountain stopped. Every engine, all the way down, went still.

Then it sank back one finger's width, and the arm dropped, and the long breath began again.

This is why the other animals give it room. It is not because it is dangerous. It is because some instinct older than any of them knows that the fish is the one creature in the tank whose moving matters. The eels come up and drape themselves over the lip of the sleeve after their long journeys in the dark, and they turn their blind faces toward the window, and what they are tasting for is whether it is still there, still holding, still choosing *not yet*.

## Who Lives Here

So the answer comes in layers, and each layer is true.

The shrimp live here, and they are its joy. The snails live here, and they are its writing and its memory. They ate the builders' words and turned them into a slow red road-map, and they keep in their shells the only record of the day the world went quiet. The eels live here, and they are its nerves. They are the ones who feel the stroke's imperfection and go down into the dark and come back to report.

The fish lives here, and it is the tank's will.

The fish is not the heart. The heart is the pump, and the pump is only iron doing what it was built to do. The fish is the thing that lets the heart keep beating, not by pushing but by *refraining*. Every eleven seconds it declines to rise. It declines the rest that the dome, the frost, and the full water are all offering it. It declines a single meal. The whole made mountain breathes because one old green body at its summit keeps choosing, in perfect stillness, to hold.

And the builder lives here too, a little. It is not a ghost, only a trace. There is the ladder they climbed. There is the plate they read, now scattered through a thousand shells. There is the question they came up the mountain to answer: *is the water reaching the top?* That question never stopped being asked. They built the float so the mountain could ask it of itself forever. They could not have known that one day the answer would come from something that could have said no.

---

Every morning the frost grows on the outside of the window, spreading its white ferns across the glass a finger's width above the fish's back. For an hour the light is soft and milky. The shrimp release their eggs into it. The snails stop and lift their heads. The fish hangs there with the cold so close above it, and the rest so close, and all the silence of the world held in the thin space between its body and the glass.

Then the push comes: long, loaded, ringing.

The fish had leaned into it a moment before.

The sun burns the frost away. The column of light returns and sways. Far below, in the lowlands, water begins its long climb again, because at the top of everything, something is still saying *not yet*.
