# Those Who Live in the Ringing Heights

## The ground they stand on

The first thing to understand about the people of the Heights is that they are, quite literally, the ground under their own feet.

Everyone knows this and nobody dwells on it. A child's first cry leaves a grain of pale stone on the cradle-sheet, no bigger than a lentil, and the midwife picks it up with tweezers and drops it into a small glass vial that the child will wear on a cord for the rest of its life. It is called the *first-grain*, and it is the only piece of the Heights that will ever belong entirely to one person. Everything after that goes into the common rock. Every shout across a gallery, every argument, every song sung on shift, every lullaby, every scream of a woman in labour and every laugh at a wedding falls out of the cold air as dust and settles into the walls, the floors, and the ridges. It is thickest near the mouth and thinner with distance. The foundry clerks, who calculate everything, reckon that an ordinary person speaking at an ordinary Heights volume puts down about two hundredweight of stone over a lifetime. A chorister might manage a ton. A Strike-master, who spends forty years bellowing orders over the hammer, is said to leave behind "a room's worth," and the saying is literal. In the old districts there are rooms named for the people whose voices built them.

So the people live inside one another. The pale, fine-grained stone of the good houses is the sound of human voices, and in the oldest of those houses, at night, when the Strike is between beats, the walls sometimes give a little of it back. A grandmother dead sixty years hums three bars of a cradle-song out of the plaster above a bed. A man long buried says *mind the step* from the lintel of a door. Nobody finds this frightening. It is simply what old houses do, the way old timbers creak elsewhere. Families who move out of an ancestral house will sometimes chip away a piece of the wall where the voice was strongest and carry it with them wrapped in wool, and they set it into the new chimney-breast so the voice can come along.

## Their bodies

Nine hundred years of hammering has shaped them.

They are deep-chested and heavy through the shoulder, with broad throats and wide mouths, and they breathe from low in the belly the way singers do, because every one of them is in some sense a singer. Their voices are pitched to carry, dropping low into a bronze register that the goats also use, because low notes cut through the Strike better than high ones. Heights speech is slow, round, and enormously loud, and to an outsider it sounds like perpetual quarrelling. It is, in fact, extremely polite. Most of the meaning is in the pitch, and a well-bred person can convey a dozen gradations of respect in the rise of a single vowel.

Most adults are partly deaf. It comes on in the late twenties and is regarded as a mark of maturity, like grey hair. The young hear everything; the old hear the Strike and very little else. To bridge the gap there is a second language, older than anyone can date, called *tap*. It is spoken through iron. A woman on a gallery raps her knuckles on the rail in a certain pattern, and the rail carries it forty yards to a man on the far side, who feels it in the palm he rests there. Everyone in the Heights keeps one hand on the iron almost unconsciously, the way sailors keep one hand for the ship. Lovers tap to one another through the bedframe. Workmen gossip through the boiler-casings. A whole second city of conversation hums through the girders, beneath the shouting, and it too precipitates. Tap-stone is fine, hard, and faintly ridged, and it forms in beads along the rails where the most talk passes, so that the handrails of old galleries are knuckled and warty with a thousand years of whispered-through-metal secrets.

Their feet have changed too. Heights folk go barefoot indoors on principle, soles thick as boot-leather, because the floor tells you things. You feel your own spire's Strike through your heels, and a good foundry hand can feel the tempo of the next spire over, miles away, carried through the rock. They say you can tell a Heights-born person anywhere by the way they stand: slightly braced, weight a little forward, listening with the whole body.

## Some of them

**Oda Venn** is Strike-mistress of Harrowcap, the spire whose hammer is the second-largest in the city. She is sixty-one, stone-deaf in her left ear and mostly deaf in her right, and she has not been more than three hundred yards from the Strike in eleven years. She sleeps in a cot bolted to the wall of the hammer-house because she cannot sleep anywhere else. When the hammer was stopped for a single afternoon two winters ago, to replace a cracked piston, she sat on the floor beside it for the whole four hours with both palms flat on the anvil, and the crews say she did not blink. Her voice, the clerks estimate, has laid down something like four feet of rock across the southern face of Harrowcap. Her grandchildren play on it.

**Tamsin Lark** is a listener. Listeners are the only quiet people the city tolerates, and they are tolerated uneasily, the way other places tolerate hangmen or the keepers of charnel-houses. She works the pale strata under the Old Chapel district, lying on her side on a mat in the dark with her ear pressed to rock that was laid down eight or nine centuries ago. Listeners' ears callus. Tamsin's left ear is flattened, hard, and faintly grey, and she wears her hair long to hide it. Most of what she hears is hammering, old tempos from Strikes that fell silent before anyone now living was born, and she can name them the way a sailor names stars. Some of it is voices. She writes down what they say in a small tight hand and sells the pages. Most of it is ordinary: an argument about bread, a child being called in for supper. Some of it is things that no one has said yet, in voices that no one has heard yet, and those pages she sells for a great deal more. Last spring she heard a man she has never met say her name, gently, as though to wake her. She has not written that one down.

**Pim** is nine and belongs to the whistling school of Coldfang spire. Every morning a hundred and forty children stand along the eastern gallery and whistle for an hour at the dawn, and they are building, very slowly, a bridge. It is their bridge; it will be finished around the time Pim is fifty, and it will be made of their childhoods, a thin, glassy, faintly musical span of whistle-stone reaching toward the next peak. Pim's whistle is unusually high and pure, and the teacher says his part of the bridge will ring when it is walked on. Pim is very proud of this. He also privately thinks the bridge sounds sad, and he has not told anyone.

**Hesk Ammory** is a cable-warden, one of the surveyors who re-measure the lines every decade. He is the kind of man who needs the world to hold still, and the Heights will not. His notebooks go back thirty years, and he has begun, very quietly and without telling his superiors, to plot the drift of the spires over time. He believes they are not moving randomly. He believes they are arranging themselves. He does not yet know into what, but he says, when he is drunk, that if you look at the map from far enough above, it has begun to look like a mouth.

## What they believe

Silence is the great sin. The word for it in the old Heights tongue, *hush*, also means *theft*, and a person who stops speaking out of sulkiness or grief is gently, insistently talked at by everyone around them until they rejoin the noise. To whisper in public is rude. To whisper in private is intimate to the point of indecency, the one exception the culture allows: lovers whisper, and mothers whisper to very sick children, and the whispering leaves almost no stone, and that is the point. It is the one thing a person may give that does not go into the mountain.

Funerals are the loudest events in the city. The dead are laid on a stone slab in the foundry yard, and the whole spire shouts at them. It is not grief exactly, and not quite ritual; the closest word is *reinforcement*. Everyone the dead person knew comes and speaks to them, at full voice, for as long as they can bear to: memories, insults, apologies, their name over and over. The stone this lays down forms a thin pale shell over the body, and by morning the dead are sealed in their neighbours' voices. The shells are carried down to the ossuary-galleries and stacked, and the ossuary-galleries are the most stable ground in the Heights, because nothing is sturdier than the stone of a crowd saying goodbye.

There is a heresy, of course. There is always a heresy. Its adherents are called *the Stilled*, and they believe that the silence at the summit is not an absence but a presence: that the stone was always coming from above, and the hammers are a kind of shouting-down, a nine-hundred-year refusal to let something finish. The Stilled do not stop the hammers; nobody is that mad. But they make pilgrimages upward, to the black rock, and they come back speaking more softly, and the city watches them the way one watches a crack in a ceiling.

## The others who live here

The people are not alone in holding up the mountains, and they know it. They say *we* and mean more than themselves.

The **anvil-swifts** are loved with a devotion that edges into the religious. Each spire employs swift-keepers, usually old women, who tend the vast pale chandeliers of birdsong-stone beneath the galleries: shoring up sagging colonies with iron straps, clearing out dead nests, and calling to the birds in a high clicking chirrup that the birds seem to answer. A spire's swift colony is counted in its wealth. When a spire begins to fail, the swifts are the last to leave, and people say, with some truth, that no mountain dies while it still has birds. There is a custom, never spoken of to outsiders, that when a swift-keeper dies, a pinch of her first-grain is ground into powder and scattered over the colony, so that some part of her is taken up into the nests.

The **sheergoats** are herded, but only loosely; the herders will tell you that the goats herd them, if anything. The goats know when rock is going quiet long before any instrument can tell, and a herd that turns away from a ridge and will not be driven back onto it is a serious matter, discussed in the foundry councils. The herders sleep among their animals on the open crags, singing to them in the low bronze register, and some of them become so thoroughly part of the herd that they bleat in their sleep.

The **clicklings** are harder to love, being small and pale and innumerable and inclined to get into the bread, but no one kills them. Every child learns that the clicklings are sewing the mountain shut from the inside. Some households keep a dish of fog-water on the floor for them. On cold mornings in the old districts the tapping in the walls is loud enough to wake you, and people lie in bed and listen to their houses being mended.

And then there are the ones down near the cloud-top, the long pale blind ones. The city calls them *murmurers*, when it calls them anything, and mostly does not.

## The murmurers

They are not what anyone assumes.

Everyone assumes that the murmurers are scavengers of the worst kind: carrion-eaters of sound, feeding on dying mountains, hastening their collapse. That is what children are told. It is also, as far as the few people who have gone down into the fog and come back can tell, wrong.

Here is what those people report. The murmurers are long, as long as a gondola, and pale as candle-wax, and they have no eyes and no mouths anyone can find. They lie coiled in the slack grey dust at the foot of the dead mountains, and they press themselves against the crumbling stone, and they hum. The humming is not eating. It is *listening*: the same thing Tamsin Lark does with her callused ear, done with the whole of a body. As the dead rock gives back its sound, the murmurers take it in. The foundry hands who have heard them up close say that if you stand very still in the fog, you can make out, in the murmurers' long wet humming, fragments of the voices and hammerings the dead mountain was made of. They are not destroying the sound. They are holding it. A murmurer is a small, soft, living archive of a district that no longer exists.

Nobody knows what they do with it. But the listeners, who compare notes, have noticed that the voices they hear in the pale bedrock, the ones saying things not yet said, are most common in the strata nearest to where murmurers are known to coil. And one old listener, now dead, wrote in the last of her pages that she believed the murmurers were not the end of the sound's journey at all, only a stage in it. The sound goes out of the rock and into the murmurers and then *somewhere else*: backward, or upward, or both. It goes to arrive, eventually, as an echo that comes before the shout.

## What lives at the top

It is time to say what lives at the top, and the honest answer is that nothing does, and then that this is not quite true.

Go up past Oda Venn's hammer-house on Harrowcap, past the last gallery where the swifts give out, up onto the black rock that is only weeks old and still has the poured ripple on it. The Strike grows muffled behind you. Your own breath begins to sound thin, then distant, then gone. At the very top is the room-sized place of perfect silence, and the stone under your feet is so dark it is hardly there. The cable-wardens and the Stilled and the rare curious child who have stood in it all say the same thing. At first there is nothing, only a blank, pressing absence. Then, after a while, you understand that the absence has a shape. It is not empty the way a cave is empty. It is empty the way a held breath is empty, or the pause in a sentence just before the last word.

This is the thing that lives in the Ringing Heights, underneath all the others, and the people half-know it without having words for it. Here is the shape of it as it comes clear, the way the listeners and the cable-warden and the Stilled and the swift-keepers are each, separately, beginning to see it.

Every sound made here becomes stone. The stone is the city's voice, laid down over nine hundred years, and the Heights are one continuous utterance: every lullaby and hammer-blow and funeral-shout and child's dawn whistle, added one after the other into a single rising structure. The mountains are sharp and branching and new because they are not finished. They are a sentence still being spoken, and the black stone at the summit is silent because it is made of sound that has not happened yet. It is the end of the sentence, already present, already shaped, waiting in the rock for the voices that will fill it. That is why the echoes sometimes come early: the future's stone is leaking. That is why Tamsin hears voices that have not yet spoken. That is why the spires are drifting into an arrangement that, from high enough up, looks like a mouth.

What lives at the top is the last word.

It is not a creature. It has no body and makes no sound, because it is the one sound in the Heights that has not yet been made. But it is not nothing, either. It has a location, and a size about that of a room. It has a patience that makes nine hundred years seem short. Standing in it, the Stilled say, you feel regarded. It is not threatening. It is closer to the attention of an audience in the moment after the music stops and before anyone has begun to applaud.

And the hammers keep it waiting. Perhaps the Stilled are right, and the Strikes are a refusal: a city shouting down its own ending, eleven seconds at a time, the way a child keeps talking at bedtime so the lamp will not be put out. Or perhaps Oda Venn is right. She has never been up there and never will go, and when told about the summit she only said, at enormous volume, that a sentence isn't finished because you're afraid of the end of it; it's finished when you've said everything you meant. As long as there's something left to say, you keep saying it.

## So

Who lives in the Ringing Heights?

Loud people with bare feet and broad chests and one hand always on the iron. They sleep beside hammers and are buried in their neighbours' voices, and their grandmothers hum out of the plaster. Swifts live there, building chandeliers out of their own calls. So do goats who know which mountains are dying, and clicklings sewing the rock shut from within. Down in the fog the soft pale murmurers lie coiled, keeping what the dead mountains remember and passing it back, somehow, into the past. A listener lives there, with her ear gone grey, who has heard a stranger gently say her name. So does a warden who fears he is looking at a mouth, and a boy who is whistling a bridge he thinks sounds sad.

And at the top of it all is the thing they are all saying together, one voice at a time, across nine hundred years. It is not yet finished, and its final word sits in a small black silence on the newest stone in the world, waiting, without hurry, to be spoken.

Pim went up there last month, on a dare. He stood in the silence for a long time. When he came down he did not say anything about it, not even to the other children, and at dawn the next morning he took his place on the eastern gallery and whistled with the others. But his note, the teacher noticed, had changed. It was still high and pure, but it had become less a sound than a question, the rising kind, the kind that expects an answer. The bridge-stone it laid down that morning is a little darker than the rest.
