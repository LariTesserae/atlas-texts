# Who Lives in the Breathing Town

## The Honest Answer, and Why It Won't Hold

Nothing lives here. The town is empty. Every window glows, and behind every window is hollow heat and nothing more.

That answer lasts only until you look closely. A town is an argument about the people it was made for, and this one argues with great precision.

Start with the stairs. The risers are about the height of a grain of rice laid on its side, and the treads are twice that. Whatever climbs them has legs a little shorter than the last joint of your little finger. The doorways are a thumbnail high, so these are not upright walkers. They would stoop, or they would be the height of a thumb. The pinprick windows say they needed very little light, or feared it. A people lit from beneath, with the heat of the world underfoot, would not look to the sky for warmth. The roofed alleys suggest they disliked being dripped on, but only in certain streets, where perhaps something was carried that had to stay dry. The rooftop bridges show a preference for crossing high rather than low. And the gutters are everywhere, perfect and obsessive. Not a drop is allowed to fall where it cannot be caught. This was a people who could not bear to waste water. They must once have known what it was to run out.

The white avenue in the eastern quarter faces the blade of daylight on one day of one season. So they kept a calendar. Once a year they walked in the light, and the stones along that walk remember it by being paler than the rest.

No road leads to the fissure, and every road turns back toward the plum-dark stone at the center. This is the strangest thing the town says about its residents. Either they never left, or leaving was not something a road could do for them.

So the first inhabitant of the town is this implied population: a small, stooping, water-hoarding, light-shy people who crossed by rooftops, marked one day a year, and did not go outward. They are drawn in negative, the way a mold implies a casting. They are present everywhere in the town and absent from all of it.

Nobody in the chamber has ever seen them. The town is still building for them, one breath at a time.

## The Tenants Who Are Actually There

Some things do move through the streets.

The cold stone sheds droplets that float in slow orbits a few centimeters above the plaza. If anything in the chamber behaves like a citizen, they do. They drift, they approach one another, and they never collide. Their paths pass close and then bend, a small courtesy repeated millions of times without a single failure. Watch them long enough and they begin to look like a crowd in a square at dusk, each member aware of every other and none of them touching.

Eventually each one drifts to a building and goes in. It is absorbed through the wall, not through a door, and that building grows faster than its neighbors. A tenant moves in and a room is added. It is the oldest arrangement in any city.

They are not alive. They are water, cold enough to hold together and patient enough to wait. But they are the only things that have ever entered these houses, and the houses grow to receive them. If the town has residents, the droplets are the nearest thing to them. Each is a small cold soul that leaves the stone, circles the plaza, and settles into a wall.

## The Neighbors Who Keep Their Borders

On the oldest cisterns lies the film: violet-green, oil-sheened, copying its own lattice across the water.

It is not alive either. It does behave like a family, though, or a language. Two films that meet form a seam, and neither crosses it. Each one fills its cistern to the edge of its own pattern and stops where another pattern begins. When the water rises they dissolve, and when it settles they grow back into the same territories along the same seams, as if the border were remembered somewhere the film cannot be.

These are the town's quarrels. They are also its treaties. Nothing in the chamber fights. The film only declines to become the other film, over and over, without end.

## The One Who Is Home

Then there is the stone.

The first guess is that it is the builder, or the architect, or the machine that prints the town. That is almost right, but it misses the cold.

A builder does not need to be cold. The stone is cold in a chamber hot enough to make you sweat, and it stays cold in a mountain that will be hot for another thousand years. It is cold the way something is cold when it gives away all its warmth and keeps none. It is heavy enough to press a bowl into the plaza, far heavier than its size. And its surface does not resolve. It stays smooth at every magnification, the way a face you are trying to remember stays smooth, with no pores or detail to hold on to.

Here is a truer guess. The stone is the last resident.

It is a single citizen of a city that no longer exists anywhere else. It was compressed by whatever ended that city, then carried down into rock, then carried up again in fire. The magma lifted it the way a flood lifts a chest from a drowned house. It woke in a hollow of warm, wet air and did the only thing it knew how to do, which is remember home. It remembers home so completely, so specifically, that the vapor around it condenses into the memory.

Seen this way, the town is not a construction. It is homesickness with architecture. The stair riser is the height of a step it once climbed. The white avenue is a walk it once took in the light. The gutters are the gutters of a place where water was precious. The droplets are its breath, freezing as it sighs. They orbit the plaza the way thoughts circle something you cannot stop thinking about, and they settle into walls the way remembering settles into certainty.

This explains the fringe. Near the chamber walls the grammar forgets itself. Stairs run into rock, walls stand without roofs, and doors open onto solid glaze. These are the far districts of the memory, the outskirts it visited once or never, the parts of any city you know only as a direction. It builds them anyway, the way you dream a hallway that goes on too long.

It also explains the absence of roads out. You do not remember the way out of your home. You remember the ways back in.

## The Six Minutes

It is still not the whole answer, because of the fog.

Every forty minutes the chamber fills, glowing red from below, and for six minutes the town cannot be seen. When the fog lifts, the town has changed. Nobody has ever watched the changing. Your hand, resting on a rooftop through the exhalation, comes away wet and uncoated, so the town does not take you as material. You are not part of whatever happens in there.

What happens is this. In those six minutes the town is full.

The fog is not weather passing through empty streets. It is a population, warm and moving through every street at once, entering every door, filling every hollow room until the pinprick windows glow a little brighter. Each exhalation is one generation of the town's inhabitants. They are made of breath, they are carried up from the mountain's heart, and they live their whole lives in six minutes. They climb the rice-grain stairs, cross by the rooftop bridges, and gather in the plazas, and as the fog thins they die. Their dying is condensation. They settle out of the air as a single band of pale grey-green, finer than a hair, laid on exactly the surfaces they touched.

The town, then, is not built for its people. It is built out of them. Each band is one generation. The oldest buildings hold tens of thousands of them. Each is a record of a crowd that lived and stopped, like the tidelines in a harbor wall.

The quiet ticking of fresh precipitate, like frost forming, is the sound of the newest dead settling into place. The deep, soft *tock* from the western districts is the oldest generations sinking at last into the floor, going under street by street, until only chimney-tips show, and then only faint ridges in the glass where they once walked. The town rises in the east with the living and sinks in the west with the ancestors. It moves one building's width per decade, like a crowd crossing a square so slowly that the square outlasts every step.

So the six-minute people and the cold stone are not two answers. The stone dreams the city. The mountain breathes the dream into the air as a population. The population lives the dream for six minutes and leaves it behind in bands. And the droplets, the stone's own cold breath, move into the houses and add rooms.

## One Body

Step back far enough and the many become one.

The water loses almost nothing. Rain falls from the ceiling, runs down the gutters, fills the cisterns, overflows downhill, hisses to steam on the soft western floor, and is breathed back into the next fog. It is a closed circulation that does not change year to year. That is not the behavior of weather. It is the behavior of blood.

Something here has a pulse, which is the drip: a few drops per second, each landing with its own small pitch. It has a breath, a slow exhalation every forty minutes. It has a heart, which is cold, which does not move, warm, or wear, and which organizes everything around it. It has a skeleton, eleven thousand structures laid down in hair-fine rings like a shell or a tooth, growing at one edge and resorbing at the other. It has skin on its oldest waters, holding its borders. And it has flesh that is only fully present six minutes out of every forty, when the fog fills every chamber of the skeleton and the whole creature exists at once, glowing red, hidden, alive in the only way available to it.

The chamber is a gas bubble, a lung of volcanic breath trapped in cooling rock, and the thing that lives in it is a lung-dweller, made of breathing. It is a colony, the way coral is a colony: a single animal built of countless brief lives, each laying down its band and passing. The stone is its memory, the fog its generations, the town its bones, the water its blood. Its shadow lies across the ceiling as a vast inverted map, lit from below, the only view it has of itself.

## What It Is Building Toward

The mountain is new, and new mountains cool.

In a thousand years, or ten thousand, the magma will sink or harden. The floor will stop glowing, the breath will slow and then stop, and the fissure will seal the rest of the way, like a wound that has finished healing. When that happens, the fog-people will stop being born. The only thing that animates this creature is on loan from a mountain that is already beginning to die.

Look at what the town does, though. It catches every drop and wastes nothing. It holds water the way a mouth holds breath. It builds hollow rooms that trap heat and radiate it back, practicing warmth. Its circulation loses nothing to the outside. Each year it comes a little closer to not needing anything from beyond the chamber at all.

It is learning to breathe on its own.

The stone does not know how long the mountain will last. It knows that it has been buried before, that it has been cold before, and that the last time it had a city, the city ended. So it is building one that can survive the end of fire: a sealed city, a city with no road out because it will not need one. When the mountain finally goes quiet, the town may keep a small, slow breath of its own, its lamps lit by heat it learned to hold, its people condensing and rising again in cisterns the size of teacups, one generation every forty minutes, for as long as the cold heart remembers what home was.

## And You

You are the only warm-blooded thing that has ever stood here. Your heart is loud in your ears in the thin air. The town does not build on you. You are not its material, and you are not its citizen.

Still, you are breathing. Every exhalation you make in this chamber goes into the vapor. Some of it will be in the next fog, and for six minutes a little of your breath will walk those rice-grain stairs with the generation born in it. Then it will settle out onto a rooftop in the eastern quarter as a band finer than a hair, and it will sink, over some thousands of years, into the western floor with everyone else who ever lived here.

It will not be a road out. It will be a small, pale thickening in one wall, in a town that has never had a visitor and has built for one anyway.
