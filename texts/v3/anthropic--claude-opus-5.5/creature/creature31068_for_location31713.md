# Those Who Live on the Precipitate

## I. The Obvious Answer

At first the answer seems simple: people live here. Two million of them, packed into the warm seam between fog and stone, whispering.

But look at them for a while and they stop seeming simple.

Watch the way they walk. Nobody here strides. They set each foot down flat, heel and toe together, the weight spread across the whole sole the way you would step onto new ice. Their shoes are soled in felted wool or in thick pads of pressed moss that are replaced every few weeks, and a crowd moving through a market sounds like a great slow brushing, like someone sweeping a very large floor very carefully. Children learn to walk this way before they learn to talk. A visitor from elsewhere can be picked out at two hundred yards by the way their heels strike, and people will turn, not in anger but with a flicker of alarm, the way you would turn toward someone handling a glass too casually.

Watch their hands. Everyone's hands are busy, and everyone's hands are speaking. There is the public whisper, clear and unhurried, and under it and around it runs the gesture-language: a flattened palm drawn sideways for *slow down*, two fingers tapped on the breastbone for *mine*, a cupped hand tipped toward the ground for *careful, green stone*. People who have lived here all their lives talk with their hands even when they are alone, even in their sleep. Lovers have private dialects of fingertips. The old sign for *I love you* is the same as the sign for *the floor is level*: the hand held flat and steady, not trembling.

Watch where they sleep. Almost nobody here uses a raised bed. They sleep on the floor, on thin mattresses of dried fern, because the floor is where the warmth is. In winter whole families lie close together on the boards, and in the oldest and coldest houses they sleep with their palms pressed flat against the stone, the way you might rest a hand on a sleeping dog to feel it breathe. Ask them why and they will say it is for the heat. Ask them again, later, when they trust you, and some of them will say it is so they will know.

Know what? They will make the cupped-hand sign, tipped downward. *Careful.*

## II. The Leveller

Here is one of them.

Her name is Esk Tannahill, and she is a leveller, which is less a trade here than a priesthood. She is responsible for a single building: a six-storey stack of timber and reed and tin called the Hollis, which holds forty-one households, a cooper, two pigeon lofts, a chapel of the Tidesmen, and a woman who sells hot eel broth out of a window on the third floor. The Hollis stands on nineteen screw-jacks. Esk knows every one of them by name and by temperament.

She rises before the dawn lamp turns amber. She goes down to the ground floor, where the jacks stand in their oiled wells, and before she touches a wrench she kneels and lays both palms on the stone.

This is what levellers do, and none of them can fully explain it. The spirit level tells you what has already happened. The palm tells you what is about to happen. Esk can feel, through the heels of her hands, whether the ground under the Hollis is swelling or settling, and whether it is doing so evenly. She can feel a cold patch opening under the northeast corner days before the floors show it. She can feel the particular fizzing warmth that means a new lobe of slurry is pushing somewhere nearby, underground, still searching for its level. On bad days she can feel a kind of held tension in the stone, and on those days she tells the cooper not to drive his hoops. He listens. Everyone listens to the leveller.

Then she climbs. From the rooftop at dawn she can see a hundred other levellers on a hundred other roofs, each a small dark figure in the fog, each crouched over a brass-and-glass level, each signaling. It is the first conversation of every day. *Hollis dropped a hair on the east. Kettle Row is rising. Watch the square, it's gone soft again.* The signals pass from roof to roof across the whole district in a few minutes, a silent ripple of raised and lowered arms. In this way the city takes its own pulse.

Esk is thirty-four. The ground under the Hollis is eleven. She has already outlived three neighbourhoods.

## III. Those Older Than the Land

There are a few people here who remember the sea.

They are in their seventies and eighties now, and there are perhaps a few thousand of them left across the whole flatland. The city calls them the sea-born, or more quietly, *the loud ones*. They were born before the first Condenser went up, in the old fishing towns along the estuary that have since been swallowed or buried or left behind. They grew up in a world where the ground was simply there. It was cold and indifferent and old, and it did not care how loudly you spoke.

They are treated with enormous tenderness and a little fear.

Some of them never lost the habit of volume. Every district has one or two: an old man who still laughs from his belly, an old woman who calls her grandchildren home in a voice that carries across three streets. Their families live with them in felted rooms, the walls hung with quilts, the doors padded, and the neighbours are patient about it, mostly. There are stories, the kind children tell each other, of a sea-born grandfather who woke from a nightmare shouting and cracked the floor out from under his own bed. It probably did not happen, but everyone knows someone who knows someone it happened to.

The sea-born are also consulted. When the Survey is uncertain about a stretch of new ground, they sometimes bring an old one out to it, to stand on the wet slurry and look out at the tidal flats and say what used to be there. *That was the Merrow channel. That was deep water. The seals used to haul out on that bar.* Nobody is sure why this matters. It seems to. Land made over old deep water tends to cool sooner.

What the sea-born remember most, when you ask them, is not the land. It is the noise: gulls screaming, bells in the harbour, surf on shingle, men shouting from boat to boat. They describe it the way the very old everywhere describe the dead: lovingly, disbelievingly, a little ashamed of having been so careless with it. *We didn't know*, they say. *We thought the world would always hold still for us.*

## IV. The Shouting on the Flats

Children born here have never heard a truly loud sound. Some are frightened of their own voices. So there is a custom.

On the first clear day after a child's seventh birthday, the family takes them east. They go through the newest districts, where the ground is still soft and steaming, and past the last flags of the claims, out along the boardwalks that run across the slurry lobes. They keep going until the boards end and there is only the tidal flat, grey and glistening and vast. It is old ground, sea ground, cold. It does not care.

There, at the edge of the world, the child is told to shout.

Most of them cannot, the first time. They open their mouths and a whisper comes out, and they look back at the city, which from the flats is a long low bank of fog with the black legs of the Condensers standing in it, and they are afraid. The parents crouch beside them and hold their hands and say, *It's all right here. This isn't ours. Nothing will break.* And eventually the child finds it: a thin, cracking, astonished scream that goes out over the mud and comes back from nowhere. The gulls lift from the flats and scream back, because out here, off the new stone, even the gulls remember how.

Adults go out to the flats too, alone, at the worst moments of their lives. After a death, after a district is lost, sometimes after a birth. You can see them on clear evenings from the eastern boardwalks, small figures far out on the grey, doubled over. The sound reaches the city only as something faint and torn, like a bird very far away. No one ever asks who it was. There is a sign for it, too: the hand opened suddenly outward from the mouth, and then brought back, closed, to the chest.

## V. Doors

You cannot return to the place you were born. It is gone. For anyone under thirty the house has moved; for anyone older the street has sunk; for everyone, the ground itself has cooled and slumped and gone under the reeds somewhere in the west.

So people carry their doors.

It began as practicality. Doors and windows are the most valuable parts of a light house, so when a district is evacuated they are the first things onto the barges. Over two generations it has become something else. The door of the room you were born in belongs to you. It goes with your family from house to house and district to district, rehung each time, repainted, re-hinged, its edges worn smooth by generations of hands. When you marry, your two doors are hung facing each other across a single room, and that room is the heart of the new household. When a child is born, a new door is made. It is often cut from the timber of an old one, so that doors have lineages, grain running from grandmother to granddaughter.

Walk through any building and you will see them, doors that do not match their frames, doors covered in carved names and dates and small painted symbols, doors that open onto blank walls because the room they were meant for was never rebuilt. Addresses shift weekly. Doors are how people here know where they are from.

The dead are not buried in the ordinary way. You cannot bury anyone in ground that will be sea in forty years. Or rather, you can, and that is exactly what is done. The dead are carried west, to the settling districts, the dying ones, and laid down in the cooling stone of a quarter that has already been cut off from its Condenser. The mourners fill the grave by hand with moss. The door of the dead person goes in with them, laid over them like a lid. Then the district is left to settle. Some years later the tide comes in over it, and the reeds grow, and the herons stand in the shallows, and the dead go down into the sea with the ground they lived on.

The people here say that the dead *go under*. It is also what they say when a district sinks. It is also, if you listen carefully, what they say when they are talking about something else.

## VI. The Others

The humans are not the only ones who have learned the hush. They are not even the best at it.

**The moles** were brought in on purpose, but they are not obedient. They tunnel through the curing stone and open it to air, and they seem to know exactly how deep to go. They never undermine a load-bearing jack. The Survey employs mole-wardens to encourage them in some districts and trap them in others, and the wardens will tell you, whispering, that the moles are always a step ahead. They move into a district just before it begins to swell. They leave just before it begins to cool. If you want to know where the land will grow next, some of the old wardens say, watch the molehills, not the Survey maps. *The Survey follows the moles*, they say. *It just doesn't know it.*

**The deer** are small and pale and shaggy, and they live in the settling districts, the dying ones, eating lichen from the walls of empty houses. They are almost never seen in the new ground. It is bad luck to shoo one from your doorway, and when you ask why, the answers differ. Some say the deer are the souls of people who went under. Some say they are only confused and lost. The oldest answer, which the sea-born sometimes give, is that the deer remember where the streets used to be. They walk the old routes of districts that were rebuilt or moved decades ago, following lanes that no longer exist, stepping over thresholds that were winched away when their grandmothers were fawns. If a deer stands in your doorway, it is because your door is standing in its road.

**The eels** come up into the warm canals from the sea, silver-dark and huge, and they are fished at night by lamplight in punts that make no sound. They are sweet and fatty and are the city's staple, smoked over fern-fires, stewed, jellied. The eel-fishers say that the eels are warmer than they should be. A fresh eel, pulled from a canal in a settling district and laid on the boards, will steam in the cold. It will lie with its belly pressed flat against the planks, and if you put it on the stone it will lie very still, as if it were listening.

**The herons** stand in the shallows among the pilings of drowned buildings, and they are the only creatures in the city allowed to make a loud noise. Occasionally, rarely, one will give its harsh croak, *fraaank*, out over a canal. It never breaks anything. The heron always seems to know where the ground is old enough, and the city takes a strange comfort in that sound. Mothers in the settling districts say to crying babies: *Hush, now. Only the herons.*

**The gulls** own the rooftops and have lost their cries. They clack their bills and flash their wings. On the rooftops at dawn, among the levellers, they perch and watch the arm-signals pass from roof to roof. The levellers swear that the gulls have learned some of the signals. More than one leveller has looked up from a spirit level to find a gull on the next chimney holding one wing out, flat and steady, not trembling.

## VII. The Grey Bats

Then there are the bats.

They live high in the Condensers, where the fog is thickest and the mesh weeps most. They are small, soft, grey as ash, and they have no match in any naturalist's catalogue. They cluster on the copper mesh in their thousands, and they seem to lick it, or to lick whatever beads on it as the fog passes through. At dusk they pour down the tower legs in long smoky ribbons and scatter into the city, and no one has ever heard one make a sound. Not a squeak, not a flutter. Those who have held one say it weighs almost nothing and is faintly warm, and that its fur is damp, and that it smells of the sea.

No one knows where they go at night. They are seen over the canals, over the market squares, drifting through the scaffolding. In the morning they are back in the lattice. A few are found dead each day at the base of the towers, and these are gathered by the Condenser crews and dropped into the settling basins. Nobody remembers who started this practice or why. It has simply always been done.

Here is the thing the masons say, and they do not say it to outsiders.

Sometimes, when a block is cut from the oldest and hardest precipitate, it splits along a plane and there is something inside. It is a small curled shape, about the size of a thumb, with folded wings and a tucked head, like a bat asleep. It is stone all the way through, the same pale gray-green as the rest, perfectly formed. It is not a fossil. There has not been time for fossils. The masons keep them on their windowsills. They find more of them the deeper they cut. In the very oldest ground, the ground of the first districts before it went under, some say the stone was nothing *but* these shapes, packed tight, thousands upon thousands of them, like a colony roosting.

Nobody knows whether the bats become the stone, or the stone becomes the bats, or whether that is even a distinction that means anything here.

## VIII. Under

Now go back to the beginning. Kneel and press your palm flat. The stone is warm, *like the flank of a sleeping animal*.

The people here have never needed to be told this. They have known it since the first district rose.

The engineers of the Survey explain the Condensers in several contradictory ways. There is the electrostatic theory, the catalytic theory, the theory of the salts, the theory of the mesh-resonance. The Condensers were designed from plans that nobody can fully account for. The first engineer's notebooks are kept in a vault and are said to be mostly drawings of fog. The engineers know how to build a Condenser, how to move one, and how to set it running. They do not know, not in the way they would like to, why it works. The theories keep multiplying because none of them is sufficient.

The people have their own theory, and it is not a theory so much as a way of speaking. They speak about the ground the way you would speak about someone in the next room who is sleeping lightly.

*Hush for under*, mothers say to shrieking children, and they cup their hands and tip them downward.

*Under's restless tonight*, someone will murmur in a market, when the stalls are listing and the eel-broth ripples in its pot.

*Under's hungry*, the Condenser crews say, when the Survey orders a tower moved into heavy fog.

*Gone under.*

Consider what they know. The ground is warm, and the warmth is not only from its making. New stone stays warm for months, and cools only when its Condenser is taken away, *as if it were being fed*.
