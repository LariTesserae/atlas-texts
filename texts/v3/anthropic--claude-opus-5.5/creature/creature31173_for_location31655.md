# Those Who Live on the Stair

## The First Answer

The first answer is the obvious one: people live there. Before the answer gets larger, it is worth looking at them closely.

They are not tall. Nothing on the tableland is tall except the cliff and the Pillar. They are built close to the ground, broad through the chest, with a depth of rib that looks from the side like a shallow drum. Their lungs are large and their hearts are large. Their blood runs thick and dark, and in cold light their lips and the beds of their nails have a faint violet cast, the color of the obsidian seams. A lowlander who climbs to the Stair spends his first week gasping on the stair-paths, his head pounding, while grandmothers carrying water-skins pass him without breathing hard. They are not stronger than he is. They are slower, and the slowness has been refined over many generations until it looks like ease.

They move deliberately. They do not run unless something is falling. Their speech is soft, partly because the thin air will not carry a shout and partly because shouting wastes breath, and on the Stair breath is spent carefully. A conversation in a courtyard sounds like water moving under ice: low, continuous, full of pauses. The pauses are not empty. Much of what the Stair says is said in the pause, in whether a person lets the silence stand or answers it.

Their hands are the first thing a stranger notices. Every adult's palms are crossed with pale lines of scar from the clinker. Children fall on it, herders sleep near it, and builders handle it all their lives. People read each other's hands the way they read the lichen. A smooth palm means someone young, or someone who has lived only in the interior courtyards, or someone who has been ill a long time. A palm so scarred it has gone glossy and hard as horn means an old wall-builder, and people defer to such a person on questions of stone.

Their faces are lined early. The frost does it, and the sun, which at this height has an edge like a blade. By thirty most people have deep creases at the corners of their eyes from squinting across the glitter of the morning plain. They consider these lines beautiful and call them "the morning's writing." A face without them belongs to someone who has not paid attention to the dawn.

## A Morning

To understand them, watch one of them wake.

Call her Tesh-of-the-Low-Ear. Her name comes from the seep she first drank from, a small seep shaped like a curled ear at the foot of a buttress in the southern districts. She wakes before light in a six-sided room with a corbelled roof and a smoke-hole at its peak, under three felts and against the backs of two sleeping children. The floor below her is warm because thirty grazers are bedded in the stable beneath it, and their heat comes up through the joints in the basalt. She has slept on the breathing of animals every winter night of her life. When she travels to districts where the stables are empty, she cannot sleep. The stillness of the stone frightens her.

She does not light a lamp. She knows the room by touch: the notch in the wall where the awl lives, the three steps down to the door, the crossbeam of hoarded wood that her grandmother's grandmother traded a season's wool for, polished by hands to the smoothness of skin. She pulls on the felt boots, the felt coat, the hood. She takes a hide bag and a stone bowl, climbs the notched log to the roof of the house below her, which is also a walkway, and goes out into the dark.

It is very cold, the kind of cold that makes the inside of the nose crackle. Above her the stars are thick and near, so dense near the horizon that they seem to rest on the plain. She walks out past the last houses, past the fog-fences standing stiff with frozen cord, and onto the black flows to her family's dew-cairns.

There are four. Her family has tended them for as long as anyone has counted. Each is a loose pile of dark stone as tall as a person, built with spaces through its body so the night wind passes through and leaves frost on the inner faces. She crouches at the first one and puts her hand inside. The stones are furred with rime, dense and white, and she smiles in the dark, because it was a good night. She knows the cairn the way one knows a relative. The third stone from the top on the windward side gives the most frost. The stone near the base cracked two winters ago and needs replacing, and she has been putting it off. The whole thing leans a little northward, as her mother's cairns leaned, because her mother built it and her mother's hands had a habit.

She waits. This is the center of the day, and it happens before the day begins: waiting at the cairn for the sun.

When light comes, it comes all at once across the flatness, red and then gold, and the plain lights up. Every stone carries rime, and every column top wears its feathers of frost pointing into the wind, and for a few minutes the whole tableland is a field of small fires. Tesh does not look away. She has seen it perhaps ten thousand times. Everyone on the Stair watches it, all along the three hundred kilometers of cliff, a whole people standing still at the same hour, as the light runs along the scarp from east to west. It is not a ceremony and no one calls it one. It is simply what people do, the way one looks up when one's name is spoken.

Then the drip begins. The rime softens and runs, and the cairn begins to tick and whisper, and water gathers in the stone basin at its foot, a hand's depth, clear and so cold it aches in the teeth. She fills the bag. She drinks one bowl there, crouching, before carrying the rest home, because the first water of the morning is drunk at the place it was made. That is courtesy. You do not carry away a gift before you have acknowledged the giver.

## What They Know

What they know is almost entirely about water.

They have perhaps sixty words for its states, and the stranger's single word "frost" breaks apart in their mouths into a dozen. There is the rime that forms on the windward face and the rime that forms in still air. There is the feathered frost that tells you the wind held steady all night and the crusted frost that tells you it swung. There is the frost that melts to sweet water and the frost on certain young flows that melts faintly bitter, tasting of the ground. There is a word for the moment when the whole plain goes from white to dark as the melt soaks in, and a word for the smell of wet basalt, which is the smell of wealth. There is a word, *hul*, for water that has been counted. Uncounted water is the sky's. Counted water belongs to a family and carries the family's obligations.

A child's first lessons are the order of water: drinking, washing, animals, grass. They are taught with a small game played with pebbles and a tilted board cut with channels, where the child must route a trickle from the top basin through the household's needs without spilling. Children who spill are not scolded. They are made to sit and watch a real seep for an hour. Most children spill only once.

They read the ground as a text. Bare black clinker is recent. Freckled ground is old. A new crack in a flow after a tremor is news, and people walk out to look at it the way lowlanders might go look at a newborn. Every seep is named, and the names form a long geography along the cliff that no one person knows entirely. A person meeting a stranger from farther down the Stair begins, before anything else, by naming their seep. The other names theirs. Then each tries to find the chain of seeps that connects them, recited like a genealogy. "I drink from the Low Ear, which is below the Split Buttress, which is three seeps above the Weeping Wall where my aunt married." If the chain can be completed, they are, in a sense, kin. If it cannot, if the stranger's seep is too far off to be linked, they are polite and wary, and they keep to the formal dialect of the pause.

## The Rolling Rule

The deepest rule of the Stair has a strange shape, and it tells the most about them.

The upper houses drink first. Water enters at the top of the cliff's wet bands, or collects in the fog-cisterns on the cliff top, and descends from terrace to terrace. Those above take first and pass the rest down.

But the Stair climbs. As the cliff rises, the old terraces at the base are buried by rockfall and grass and the accumulation of life, and new terraces are built at the top. So the highest houses are always the newest, and the families who drink first are the ones who most recently built.

On the Stair, then, privilege belongs to whoever has taken on the labor of building, not to whoever is oldest. A young couple who pry loose their columns, dress them, and raise a new hexagon at the very top of their district's slope, against the living rock, will drink first from the seep for as long as theirs is the highest roof. When someone builds above them, they move down one step in the order. Over a lifetime, everyone descends. You are born somewhere in the middle, you climb when you are young and strong enough to build, and then the city slowly grows over you and you sink, terrace by terrace, until you are old and live near the stables in the warmth of the animals and drink what has already passed through every other household above.

No one thinks of this as a fall. The low houses are the warmest. The old live there, close to the herds, in rooms with walls a thousand years thick, and the young come down to them for stories and for advice about stone. The rule is not a hierarchy. It is a tide. Every person on the Stair is slowly carried by it, and they understand their lives as a descent through the city, a movement toward the buried levels where everything that came before them lies sealed.

## Those Who Were Here First

Now the answer begins to get larger. The people know perfectly well that they are not the Stair's first residents, or its most numerous.

The finches were here before them. The stories say so plainly. When the first families came along the scarp, the cliff already rang each dawn with small brown birds nesting in the joints between the columns. The first builders did not drive them out. They could not have. When they stacked their hexagons, they left gaps in the walls, finger-wide spaces near the tops, and the finches moved in. Every house on the Stair is built with these gaps, and it would be considered monstrous to fill them. A house without finches in its walls is a house where someone has done something wrong.

In a sense the finches own the houses. When a family moves down a terrace, the finches stay. The birds nesting in the wall above Tesh's door are descended from birds that nested in that same wall when it was the highest roof on the slope and some other family drank first there. The human residents pass through the house; the finches remain. Children are taught to greet them in the morning. Old people speak to them. When a house falls in a tremor, the first grief people express is for the nests.

The whistlers, the round short-eared rodents of the seep grass, are treated differently. They are food, and they are pests, and they are also a warning system, because their colonies sense tremors before any human does. When the whole seep line goes silent at once and a thousand small bodies vanish into their holes, the people of the Stair put down whatever they are carrying and move to the open terraces. They do not wait to feel anything. They trust the whistlers. The saying is "the ground tells the small ones first."

The herds are something else again: kin, heat, wealth, and weather. The long-necked grazers come in each winter from the plain and fill the lowest terraces, and the Stair begins to breathe with them. You can see it on a cold night from out on the flows: a faint mist rising along the whole length of the cliff, the exhaled warmth of hundreds of thousands of animals seeping up through the stonework and out of the smoke-holes. The people say the Stair is sleeping when they see this. In summer, when the herds are out on the plain and the stables are empty, they say the Stair is holding its breath.

Families name their herds, not individual animals, and a herd's name passes down with the herding rights. The herders who follow the animals out for the summer months come back changed: quieter even than the Stair folk, their faces blacker from the sun, speaking a herding cant made mostly of humming. The grazers hum too, low and continuous, and after a summer the herders hum in their sleep. Their families say the herd has gotten into them. It will leave, mostly, by midwinter.

## The Dead and the Wind

The great birds are the last of the Stair's residents, and in some ways the most important.

They circle above the cliff on the updrafts, huge dark scavengers with wings three meters across, riding the rising air for hours without a wingbeat. They do not nest on the Stair. They nest far along the scarp, in places where the cliff has broken into towers too sheer to climb. But they are always overhead, and everyone on the Stair spends some part of every day looking up at them.

The dead are given to them. There is no soil to hold a body and no wood to burn one. So on certain high terraces, open stone platforms on the cliff top above each district, the dead are laid out and opened by specialists whose families have done this work for generations, and the birds come down.

The people believe the dead go into the wind. They do not mean this as a metaphor. The birds carry the body up into the air above the cliff, into the rising columns that the great scarp makes, and there, they believe, the dead remain, circling, riding the lift off the rock, looking down at the Stair. When a person on the terraces feels a sudden warm gust come up the cliff face on a cold day, they say someone has come down to look. When a great bird circles very low over a particular house, the family inside goes out and stands on the roof. They do not speak. They stand in the pause.

So the full census of the Stair includes the living in their hexagons, the animals in the stables, the finches in the walls, and above them all an invisible population riding the updraft, the whole history of the city turning slowly in the air.

## The Stair Itself

The larger answer is this.

If you could stand far enough away and watch for long enough, not a day or a year but a thousand years compressed into an afternoon, you would stop seeing the people and the animals and the birds as separate things. You would see one thing.

You would see a long living band along the foot of a rising cliff, three hundred kilometers of it, glittering at dawn and breathing mist on winter nights. You would see it grow upward, layer by layer, laying new cells at its top and sealing old ones at its base, the way a reef grows on the bones of its own past. You would see its cells are six-sided, and packed like a comb, and that each one holds a warm small cluster of lives: human, animal, bird, insect, and the sleeping pulse of animals below. You would see water moving through it along channels, entering at the top and descending, used and passed and used and passed, feeding the cells in order, like sap.

When the ground shrugged, you would see a stretch of it die. Walls fall, a district goes dark and silent, grass comes up in the rubble. Then you would see it heal, not in place but by moving. The cells nearest the break would drift along the cliff toward wherever the water had gone and begin again, with the same stones and the same pattern, so that the whole long body shifted a little along the scarp, following the seeps the way a root follows moisture through dark ground.

You would see that it has no center and no head, that no part of it knows the whole, and that it has lasted a very long time anyway. Each district knows its neighbors and speaks their dialect, and trades wool and clay and obsidian and daughters with them, and the whole thing holds together along its length like a cord made of many short fibers, none of them running the full distance.

This is what lives at the edge of the tableland. The people are its hands. The herds are its warmth. The finches are its voice at dawn. The whistlers are its nerves, feeling the ground. The great birds are its memory, turning in the wind above it. The water is its blood. The stone is its bone, and it is the only thing the city does not make, the one gift it builds from over and over, the same columns quarried from the same flows, dressed the same way, stacked in the same hexagons for longer than any story reaches back.

The people know this, not in so many words, but they know it. When a child asks what the Stair is, the old ones do not say "a city" or "our home." They say, "It is what the water built out of us."

## Beneath

There is one more thing that lives there, and it is the hardest to describe.

The sealed levels lie beneath the lowest stables, the terraces buried long ago as the cliff rose and the city climbed. No one goes down into them. They are packed with rubble and dung and grass-mat and the dust of a thousand years. Their doorways are filled, their roofs fallen in or buried. The oldest ones must lie very deep, under the full depth of the Stair's accumulated life, pressed against the foot of the scarp, and some of them, the stories say, date from when the cliff was a step a child could climb.

The people speak of these levels rarely and carefully. In the ordinary way they say "the lower rooms," as one might speak of the rooms of one's house where the elders sleep. But in the old songs they have another name, which translates roughly as "where the Stair keeps what it knows."

The belief goes something like this. Everything that has happened on the Stair has happened in these same hexagons, built of these same stones, in this same pattern. Each generation's rooms become the foundation for the next generation's floors. The weight of all that living presses down, layer on layer, into the sealed dark at the base. And down there, slowly, the Stair remembers. The rooms are not empty. They are full of compressed time, of every morning at the cairn, every winter night on the breathing of animals, every silence after a tremor. They are dense with it the way the cushion plants are dense with warmth, tightly packed, holding heat for centuries.

The water knows this, they say. The seeps that emerge at the cliff's foot pass through the lowest levels on their way out, and on certain mornings, when the melt runs through and comes up in the basins at the base of the Stair, it tastes different. Old people say it tastes of the lower rooms. It has a flavor no one can name, faintly mineral, faintly sweet, like frost off a stone that has been in a family's hands for a thousand years. When you drink it, you are drinking something that has passed through all your dead.

This is not quite a religion, and it is not quite a joke. It is the sort of thing an old woman in a warm low room says to a grandchild on a winter night, with the herd humming beneath the floor and the finches silent in the walls. The child asks where they will go when they are old, and the grandmother says, "Down. We all go down. Then under. Then into the water, and up again at the seeps. You'll drink me one morning and not know it." Then she lets the pause stand, and the child, being of the Stair, does not answer it.

## The Last Answer

So what lives there?

A people, slow-breathing and scarred at the palms, who wake before light to wait at cairns of dark stone. Herds that give their heat to the rooms above them. Finches who own the walls. Whistlers who hear the ground first. Great birds who carry the dead into the wind. The Stair itself, a single reef-body three hundred kilometers long, climbing its cliff and following its water.

Underneath all of these, holding them, is the thing the place most deeply is: a patience that learned to build. The tableland is young rock. It has not had time to soften, and it is still opening and rising. What lives at its edge has taken on the same nature. It is always broken somewhere, always rebuilding, and always a little further up the cliff than it was.

In the morning the frost glitters and runs. Along the whole length of the scarp, in dozens of languages, people crouch by stones and drink the first water where it was made, and look up, and wait for the day to begin again. Far below them, in the sealed rooms, the Stair remembers every other morning like this one, all the way down.
