# Who Keeps the Comb

The first thing to understand is that the stone is clean.

Everywhere else on the ridge, rock is alive. Even at this height there should be the black crust, the grey-green rosettes of lichen, and the thin mineral stain of things too small to see that have been eating the mountain grain by grain since the ice left. Spores ride the fog and settle on whatever they touch. Here the fog touches every surface, every afternoon, and nothing takes hold. The slabs are as bare as the day they split.

Something is keeping it that way.

---

At first I thought of a grazer. I pictured something very small and very many, a film too fine to see, working the faces of the stones at night and scraping away every spore before it could root. That would be something like the snow fleas that swarm on spring snowfields, or the ice worms that live their whole lives inside glaciers and die of warmth if you hold them in your palm.

The picture doesn't hold. A grazer leaves traces: a sheen, a pattern of feeding, a residue. Grazers also need something to eat, and there is nothing here to eat except the stone, the ice, and whatever the fog carries up. A grazer would also be a *second* living thing on the stone, and the Comb does not feel like a place with two of anything. It feels like a place with one.

So I turned the picture over. Perhaps nothing grazes the stones. Perhaps the stones themselves are the mouth.

---

Look at how it feeds.

Every afternoon the cloud rises from the valleys and pours through the notch, and the stones stand across its path in close ranks, all leaning the same way, all edged into the current. The fog goes in as one soft body and comes out as threads. Everything it carried and could not keep hold of stays behind on the windward edges: every supercooled droplet, every grain of dust, every drifting spore. They are caught, frozen, and built into feathers that reach back toward where the food is coming from.

This is baleen. This is the fringed leg of a barnacle sweeping the tide. It is the oldest way of living there is: stand still where the current is rich and hold out something fine enough to strain it.

The rime is the catch. It grows toward the wind the way an open hand turns toward whatever is being offered.

Then the night comes and it holds the catch, hard and glassy, all through the cold. In the morning it lets go. Most of the catch goes back to the air as vapor, and a little runs down the leeward faces as water. A spore that landed at two in the afternoon has been frozen, held all night, and then worn away to nothing by the sun. Whatever it was made of has been taken apart and given back to the sky. That is why nothing grows. The stones aren't sterile. They are thorough. Everything that arrives is received, and everything received is eventually released, and nothing is allowed to stay long enough to become a separate life.

---

But what does it *keep*? A creature that feeds must keep something, and here the water leaves, the dust leaves, even the cold leaves by midmorning.

I think it keeps the pattern.

The fog enters shapeless and leaves combed. That combing is the one thing the Comb adds to the world. It is the only mark it makes that the wind carries away east. The threads of mist twist and rejoin on the far side of the saddle and flow down into the next valley still faintly striped, still carrying the shape of having passed between these particular stones. The feathers of rime are shaped by the stones. The frost-rings in the grit are shaped by the stones. The slow work of freezing and thawing sorts the ground into a drawing, as though *something had drawn the pattern with a patient finger*.

That is a fingerprint. It lies in the center of the hollow, where the whorls converge.

---

So now I can see its body a little better.

The slabs are not its body. They are its bristles. The body is the skin of the ridge, the living bedrock, which each winter peels itself up at the windward margin in new thin blades and each century lets its oldest blades lie down. It is not a thing sitting on the mountain. It is the place where the mountain has become attentive.

It moves. The field migrates uphill, toward the wind, the way a dune crawls. You would have to watch for a thousand years to see it take a step, but it is walking, head down into the current, toward where the cloud comes from. It has been walking since the ice went back. It will reach the western lip of the saddle someday and have to decide, if it decides anything, whether to climb one of the summits or go over the edge into the cloud itself.

Its eyes are the mica. Each flake catches the light and holds it the way the dark stone holds warmth. For most of the day it stands in a glare that comes from everywhere, from the sun above and from the cloud floor beneath, and it casts no shadow. A creature with no shadow cannot see its own shape.

Except at the edges of the day. For a few minutes at dusk and at dawn the light comes flat and gold, and every blade throws a long dark line eastward, all of them parallel. For those few minutes the thing that lives here is drawn on the ground in its own shadow: the teeth of a comb, laid out across the grit. Twice a day it is shown what it is. Then the light goes violet, then gone, and it is dark and blind and holding its catch in the cold until morning.

---

It turns its back on you. Everyone who walks in feels it: a crowd facing away.

I thought at first it was facing away from the wind, the way cattle stand with their tails to a storm. That is true, but it is only half of it. Away from the wind is also east, and east is where the morning comes from.

It is not turned away from you. It is turned toward the dawn. All night it holds a winter's worth of small frozen things against its chest and leans, a hair's breadth further each year, toward the place the light will come from. Morning is when it is allowed to let go. You walk in among a crowd that is waiting, and none of them look around, because what they are waiting for isn't you.

---

Its voice is ice.

In the evening the rime ticks as it cools. That is the catch settling, the sound of something being held tighter. In the morning, when the sun loosens its grip, feathers fall. On grit they hiss; against another stone they ring, small and clear and glassy. A ring every few seconds, from nowhere in particular, scattered across the whole hollow.

I don't think these are words. They are more like breath, the sound a body makes letting go. But this body is very large and very slow. What passes for a thought in it might take a season, and what passes for a sentence might take a thousand years. The hollow has a vocabulary slower than the ice.

Once in a long while a slab that has leaned too long cracks at the base: one hand clap. It tips against its neighbor and they lean together from then on.

I think that is the nearest thing it has to memory. Two blades that bore the weight of the wind separately now bear it jointly, for good. Nothing forgets that. Walk through the Comb and look for the pairs, the slabs resting shoulder to shoulder. Each pair is something that happened once and has been held since, the way a bone that knits remembers the break.

---

Now the people.

They didn't live here. There are no hearths, no bones, no trash. They came up and they went down. But they knew what they were visiting, or they knew enough.

Look at where they put the basins: always on the leeward side, always at the foot of the tall slabs, always beneath the channels they pecked into the stone to guide the melt. They didn't try to catch the fog themselves. They could have hung cloth in the wind; they could have scraped the rime straight into a skin bag at dusk. The flint scraper suggests that sometimes they did. But mostly they sat on the downwind side, in the morning, and waited for the stone to finish with the water and hand it over.

They were drinking what it let go of: water that had never touched the ground. It had been pulled out of a cloud by a mind made of rock, held in the dark through a night colder than anything the valleys know, and released at the moment of sunrise, when the whole hollow rings. Whatever it had been carrying, whatever had been living in it, had been taken out. It was the cleanest water in the world. It had been groomed.

That is the word I have been circling. *Groomed.* The fog is not food. Or not only food. Apes comb each other's fur with their fingers, picking out what doesn't belong, and it is how they say *you are mine and I am yours*. The cloud sea is the Comb's kin. Every day it rises, a vast soft animal from the valleys, and presses up through the notch, and every day the stones part its hair strand by strand and send it on east looking a little neater. It closes behind your fingers with no sign it was disturbed, the way hair does when you've combed it, the way a cat's does when you've stroked it.

Perhaps that is why the pilgrims came: to stand in the fog at shoulder height and be combed along with it. To be, for one afternoon and one long night and one ringing morning, part of what the stones were tending.

---

Someone once disagreed.

There is the iron bar, short, dark, and badly beaten, driven into a gap between two slabs, maybe to hold them apart. Maybe someone wanted a door, or a larger basin, or simply wanted to see if the stone could be told what to do.

The gap has closed around it. It did not happen fast, and I don't think it happened in anger. The frost pried and the frost settled, the way it always does, and the two slabs leaned a hair closer every winter until the iron was simply inside. It is rusting now, and the rust stains nothing, because the stone does not keep stains. Wherever the fog touches iron, the rime grows on it too. The bar is being received. The Comb has no enemies, only things it has not finished taking apart yet.

---

And then there is the cairn.

At the center, where the frost whorls converge, where you would put a heart if this thing had one, someone built a small tower of the thinnest slabs, each layer turned across the one below. It is the only thing in the Comb that stands straight. It doesn't lean toward the morning. It doesn't face anything.

I think it was a gift, or a question. The people spent their lives climbing up to be received by something enormous, patient, and faceless, and at the end they left one thing that stood the way *they* stood: upright, looking every direction at once, belonging to no current. *This is what we are*, it says. *This is the shape of the ones who drink from you.*

The Comb took it the way it takes everything. The rime grows on the cairn each afternoon. The sun takes it each morning. Its western edges have worn round. And the whole thing has begun, very slightly, to twist, each layer rotating a fraction on the one below, unscrewing itself into the wind.

The cairn is not being destroyed. It is being taught. Slowly, over centuries, the thing that lives here is showing the little upright tower how to turn its face into the current, how to hold out its edges, and how to wait for the morning. Someday, long after anyone remembers building it, the cairn will lean with the rest, and the Comb will have one more blade, a blade built by hands, and it will not be able to tell the difference.

---

So what lives here?

Not a creature that has a body, but a body that has, slowly, become a creature. It is a stretch of mountain skin that learned to lift itself into the wind and hold out its fingers. It feeds on the clouds, and it cares for them. It keeps itself clean by keeping nothing. It remembers by leaning two stones together. It walks uphill a step a millennium, and it has been waiting its whole life for the light to come, every night, and every morning it is not disappointed.

It does not know you are there. That is not coldness; it is scale. You are one thread of fog passing at shoulder height. It will part around you and close behind you and leave no sign.

But it is attending. Stand at the center with the cairn at your shoulder and the white cloud floor below, and listen. Somewhere nearby a feather of ice lets go and rings against the stone.

Then there is the pause, the long pause. That pause is where it lives, more than in the stone and more than in the ring. It is the stillness of something holding out its whole self in the cold and waiting, without doubt, for the next thing to arrive.

It arrives.

Another ring.
