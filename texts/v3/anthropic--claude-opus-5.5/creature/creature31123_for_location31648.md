# Who Lives at Kettle Point

## I. The easy answer

The first answer is the obvious one, and it is true as far as it goes. The crews live here.

There are forty-some of them at any time, in three shifts, sleeping in the whitewashed dormitories with the balcony doors propped open because shutting them only traps the wet. They come across the causeway on the rail trolley with their duffels on their knees, and within a month they have the look. Their skin is pale and soft from never drying, and their hands have wrinkled at the fingertips and stay that way. They move carefully over the lava because the plates tilt and the edges bite. Their boot soles are resoled in the machine shop every few weeks with belting rubber. They carry two handkerchiefs, one tucked in the collar and one in the back pocket, and swap them on the hour like a watch changing.

They learn the pipes before they learn each other's names. They learn which footbridge has the loose tread, which saddle is hot enough to blister through a sleeve, where the lagging has split and a man can warm his tea just by setting the can on the bare flange. They learn the six signs: *valve, gauge, hot, come here, stop, tea.* They learn to walk through the separator yard in a crouch of the shoulders, as if the heat were a low ceiling. Some of them never quite shed that crouch afterward, even on the mainland under open sky.

At midnight on the balconies, thirty-eight degrees, they sit in undershirts with their feet up on the iron railings and do not talk, because talking means shouting and nobody wants to shout at midnight. The geckos work the lamp above them, snapping moths out of the halo. Down in the drainage channel the frogs trill, and below that sits the drone. A man who has been here a season can feel the drone in his back teeth, and after a while he feels it as a kind of company.

When they go home on leave, most of them cannot sleep. The silence of the mainland wakes them at two in the morning, and they lie in their own beds with their hearts going, waiting for a relief valve that isn't there.

That is who lives at Kettle Point. It is a true answer, but it is not enough of one.

## II. The ones who stay

The crews rotate, and that is the rule. A contract runs eighteen months, and then you go.

But every settlement keeps a few people who are not on the rotation, or who were once and fell off it somewhere, and the Works is no different. There is a fitter in the machine shop named Tobiah who came on a contract when the traveling crane was new. He is still there, and the crane is not new. There is a woman in the canteen who is the daughter of the woman who ran the canteen before her. There is an old pump man who works the seaward house alone on the night shift and has not been seen on the causeway in anyone's memory.

The long-stayers are recognizable in a way that is hard to describe. They are not stranger than the others. If anything they are calmer. They have stopped making the sign for *tea*; they simply appear at your elbow with it at the moment you would have asked. They do not flinch when Well Four lifts, and they don't merely ignore it the way the others learn to. They seem to have known it was coming. Tobiah will sometimes pause with a wrench on a nut, turn his head a few degrees toward the separator yard, and wait. Three seconds later the valve shrieks, reseats, and he goes on turning.

They wear watches, and the watches are always wrong. Nobody sets them. They are worn the way you might wear a ring from someone who has died.

And there is the clerk.

Her name is Maud Eskil. She keeps the log books in the office, as her father did before her. She was born in the dispensary, in the one iron bed there, during a night shift, with the turbines going. When she was a child she slept under his desk while he wrote the hourly lines. She has crossed the causeway perhaps a dozen times in her life, always for something that could not be avoided, a funeral or a dentist, and she has always come back the same day.

She writes the numbers: pressure, temperature, output, pond temperature at the north and south gauges, and the height of the vapor ceiling, which she reads by the lamp on the tallest gantry, noting whether its halo sits inside or below the cloud. She writes them in the same brown ink her father used, in a hand that has become, over the years, indistinguishable from his.

Maud knows something about the log books that the company does not. The numbers are *nearly* identical, line after line, decade after decade. But nearly identical is not the same as identical, and the difference does not wander the way noise wanders. It moves.

She found this out when she was nineteen. She took the back pages of an old ledger and began plotting the pond temperature to the tenth of a degree, one dot for every hour, across a year. What came out was not a flat line with jitter on it. It was a wave, very shallow and very long. It rose over roughly eleven days and fell over roughly eleven days, and the lifts of Well Four clustered at its crests. She went back and plotted another year, then another, and then her father's years. The wave was always there. Its period drifted a little and its height never changed.

She did not tell the company. She told her father, who was dying by then in the dormitory, and he said, *Yes. I know. That's it breathing.* Then he held his hands out flat over the blanket, palms down, and slowly lowered them.

## III. The seventh sign

New hires learn six signs in their first week. Nobody teaches them the seventh.

They pick it up after a season, or two, or never, by seeing it used. It is made with both hands flat, palms down, lowered slowly through the air, the way you would press something gently back under water or smooth a sheet over a sleeping child.

It has no single translation. The crews, asked, give different answers. *It's all right. Leave it. It's holding. Don't.* It gets used when a new man spots a gauge needle sitting a hair off its usual mark and reaches for the valve wheel to correct it, and an older hand catches his eye and lowers the air. It gets used on certain evenings when the frogs in the channel and the pump in the seaward house fall into exact time, trill and thud locked together for a minute or two, and everybody on the gravel pads goes still among the crabs, and someone makes the sign without thinking. It gets used at the door of the disused cable tunnel, where the bats live and where the air coming out is warmer than it has any right to be. It is used a great deal around Maud.

The seventh sign is not a name. The crews do not have a name for what it refers to. When pressed, the long-stayers will only say *it* or *the point*, and they say *the point* the way you might say *the patient*.

So here is the harder answer, the one that took some time to come clear.

## IV. What lives at Kettle Point

Something lives here that is not any one of the animals, and not the crews, and not the machinery. It is not hidden anywhere, and you could not dig it up. It lives in the *balance*. More exactly, it is the balance, the way a flame is not the wick or the wax or the air but the particular way the three are holding together right now.

It is easiest to see if you describe it as an animal and don't flinch from how strange that sounds.

Its core is forty meters down, in rock still hot enough to boil water. That rock has not finished cooling and is not going to. Its hottest organ is the separator yard, where the wellheads stand like chapels and the air sits at fifty degrees. Its skin is the vapor ceiling, the pearl-gray dome sixty meters up, which never parts and never lifts and which from the mainland looks like a white cap resting on the water.

Its breath runs one way, like a bird's. The heat of the point lifts the air up the column into the cloud, and that draws the bay wind in low across the water, steady from the same direction every day of the year, across the gravel pads and through the pipe gantries and up. It is always inhaling and never exhales outward. The swifts ride that current in their hundreds, screaming in and out of the ceiling. They nest under its eaves the way small birds pick through the hide of a grazing animal.

Its pulse is the reciprocating pump in the seaward house, which has thudded at the same pace for longer than anyone can remember. That is why the old pump man never leaves it. Its voice, the only thing it ever says, is the relief valve on Well Four. It speaks at the top of each long breath, a locomotive shriek for three or four seconds, and then it is quiet again. Its breathing takes eleven days to rise and eleven days to fall. Maud has the drawings.

Everything alive on the point has arranged itself along this creature's gradients the way gut flora arrange themselves along a gut. The bacterial mats grow where the ponds cool at the corners, the moss where the condensate runs, and the frogs in the warm channels. The egrets stand at the outfall, where its heat pours back into the bay with stunned fish in it. The crabs rise at dusk along the one margin where the rock has begun, barely, to crumble. The crews stand at their stations, and the long-stayers stand very still at theirs.

They are not its prey, and they are not quite its keepers either. They are closer to part of its body, the part that holds it steady. The flow from the wells was set long ago to a figure the rock can supply forever, and the men who turn the valve wheels keep it at that figure. If they drew more, the point would run down. If they drew nothing, it would begin, very slowly, to cool. Either way it would change. So they hold it, and it holds them, and the log lines come out nearly the same, hour after hour.

## V. What it is, underneath

It took until this point for the last piece to come into focus.

The lava at Kettle Point still has the exact shape it cooled into. The ropes and slabs are fresh and sharp enough to open a boot. Where it met the sea it shattered into black sand and glass, and in the moment it did, there must have been an enormous sound, a scream of steam as liquid rock went into cold water. Something began in that moment, the way something begins in any birth.

The birth never finished.

The Works came, drilled into the heat, and drew off exactly enough of it, by accident or by long patience, to hold the whole point at the instant of cooling. The rock stays too hot for lichen and too sheltered for the surf to round it. The cloud stays low, the rain falls warm, and the light stays gray and even without a single hard shadow. That is why time does not pass here. It is not that nothing happens. It is that what lives at Kettle Point *is* a moment, a single hour that learned how to keep breathing. Every line in the log book is the same hour written down again.

It isn't waiting to wake up. It isn't asleep. It is young, as young as the rock, and it may stay young forever as long as the balance holds. The long-stayers seem to understand this without needing it said. That is why the seventh sign presses downward. It doesn't mean *hush* in the sense of *sleep*. It means *hold, stay, not yet.* It is the gesture a midwife might make if she had decided the kindest thing she could do was keep the child from ever quite arriving.

It is also why the fig tree fruits. There are no seasons under the dome, but there is one permanent season, and that season is *about to*. The fig has lived its whole life in the last hour before something, and it has answered the only way a fig can, by ripening.

If the Works ever shut down, it would take centuries. The ponds would cool and the dome would thin, and one morning the sun would cast a shadow on the gravel pads. Lichen would come, then soil, then the sea would get to work on the margins. The sharp edges would round off, the frogs would go, and the swifts would move on to some other eave. The thing would finish being born, and in finishing it would stop being a thing at all. The point would become ordinary land, and eventually somebody would give it a proper name.

The maps show only a gray tongue of rock, and that is not an oversight. You do not name something that has not arrived yet.

## VI. Night, the long breath rising

The shift whistle goes for the third time. On the gravel pads the crabs are coming up in their thousands, dark red, and the men crossing to the canteen walk slowly and listen to the crunch. The frogs have started. The pump thuds. For a minute, as happens every few evenings, trill and thud slide into step, and the whole point seems to hum in one key.

In the office, under the brown submarine light of a clerestory window, Maud Eskil writes the hour: pressure, temperature, output, north pond and south pond, ceiling at the lamp. Her hand is her father's hand. The line is nearly identical to the one above it, and it is a tenth of a degree higher, because the long breath is rising and has three days yet to go.

Out in the separator yard, Tobiah pauses with his wrench and turns his head.

Maud lifts her pen from the page and waits. She lays her free hand flat on the desk, palm down.

Well Four lifts and shrieks for three seconds, four, and then reseats.

She writes the next number. The point keeps breathing in under its warm gray dome, still not finished cooling, and nobody who lives there wants it to be.
