# Who Lives in Ashcarr

## The First One You See

You will probably see one at dusk, because that is when they stand still.

The figure stands with one palm flat against the flank of a hornbeam wall, head bowed a little, the way someone leans toward a door to hear who is on the other side. Grey-green cloth, darker at the hem where the fog has soaked it. You take it for a post or a stump, or for part of the wall that has grown a shoulder. Then the hand moves six inches to the left and settles again, and you understand that someone is listening to the building.

If you come closer, they will not startle. Nobody in Ashcarr startles. They may lift the other hand, palm out, slightly, which means *wait*. And you wait, and in the waiting you begin to hear what they are hearing: the arm on the far side of the wall is starting its evening curl, and somewhere in the housing a pawl has dropped into a tooth, *tk*, and a moment later another, *tk*. The tender listens to a dozen of these, counting without moving their lips, and then takes the hand away from the bark and looks at you.

"It's all right," they will say, very quietly. That is often the first thing anyone in Ashcarr says to a stranger. They are not reassuring you. They are reporting on the arm.

## How Many

Fewer than you would guess, and more than you will see. Perhaps thirty, though they rarely gather in one place, so nobody has had reason to count them recently. The number is in the ledger at the paper mill, but the ledger is organized by arm, not by person, and you would have to add it up yourself.

They do not live in houses in the way you mean. Every building in Ashcarr is a workshop first: a press house, a carding house, the long green gut of the rope walk. The tenders sleep inside them, one or two to a building, on pallets of dried bracken laid close against the gear housings. They sleep there because a pawl can slip in the night, and a slipped pawl lets an arm's whole curl go to waste. Worse, it can let the train run backward with a grinding sound that means a tooth has sheared. A tender sleeping beside the housing wakes to that sound the way a parent wakes to a changed breath in the next room. They are already on their knees with the wooden mallet before they know they are awake.

So you might say the people of Ashcarr do not have homes. Or you might say they have fourteen of them, each with a great slow heart, and that they live inside the chest.

## Names

This is the first thing about them that is strange, and it takes a while to notice.

The arms have names. Some are plain and positional: *Second East of the Paper House*, *the Low Arm*, *Rope-Walk North*. Some are older and have worn down in the mouth until they no longer mean anything: *Aspel*, *Carrow*, *the Sallet*, *Mother Tine*. The oldest arm in the basin, which drives the largest dynamo and is so thick at the root that two people cannot join hands around it, is called only *Herself*.

A tender is given charge of an arm, or of two or three small ones, and from then on they are called by its name. When someone in Ashcarr says "Carrow will know," they mean the person. When they say "Carrow is pulling left this spring," they mean the wood. The grammar does not distinguish. The tenders do not seem to find this confusing, and after a few weeks neither will you, which is a sign of something.

The person-names they were born with, or arrived with, are not forbidden. They simply fall out of use, the way a door falls out of use when the wall thickens across it. An old tender, asked what she was called before she was Mother Tine, might have to think about it. She might say it with a small, pleased surprise, as if you had handed her something she dropped in the moss years ago and assumed had sunk.

The ledger in the paper mill runs by arm. Under *Mother Tine*, one hand after another, there is a column of keepers, and beside each keeper the springs they tended and the measurements they took. There are knot-counts copied from the cords, and notes in small cramped writing: *pulled a hair north*, *new length grafted at the third knot*, *pinned lower, iron complaining*. The column goes back past the point where the paper was made here. The earliest pages are a different stock, brittle and brown, and the earliest names on them are not names anyone recognizes as names anymore. The arm, though, is the same arm.

When a tender dies, the arm is not left untended even for a night. Someone takes it. By morning the name has moved on to them, the way the ratchet moves on to the next tooth.

## Their Bodies

They are shaped by the work, as all laboring people are, but the shaping here is particular.

Their hands are broad and very sensitive in the palm, and hard only at the fingertips and the web of the thumb, where the knotted measuring cord runs. They touch everything flat-handed, with the fingers spread, because that is how you hear wood. You will see them do it without meaning to: a palm laid on a table, on a doorframe, on the shoulder of another tender. It is a greeting and an inquiry at once.

They are slow, but the slowness is not heaviness. It is closer to the slowness of someone carrying a full bowl. It comes from years of moving through the buildings at night among gear trains they cannot afford to jostle, and from years of walking on moss that swallows anything dropped. They set their feet down deliberately, and they never put anything down on the lawns, not even for a moment.

Their skin tends toward pallor, because the light in Ashcarr is green and indirect, and they spend the brightest hours indoors under the yew. Their clothes go grey-green with damp and stay that way. From a distance, in fog, the tenders are nearly the color of the walls. This is not camouflage in any intended sense. It is simply what happens to anything that stays in the basin long enough.

Their hearing is the thing. A tender can stand at the edge of the moss lawn at the peak of the evening curl, when the whole bowl is ticking like a room of slow clocks, and say *Aspel's skipping*. She will be right. She has picked one wrong interval out of a scattered, unrhythmic field of hundreds, the way you might hear your own name spoken in a crowded room. This is why they murmur. A raised voice in Ashcarr is not rude so much as blinding. It blots out the one thing everyone is always half-listening to.

## Their Day

They keep the day the engines keep.

At dawn they are asleep, or nearly, curled against the housings. The fog is thick outside, the arms are at full curl, and nothing is moving. This is the only time in the basin when a tender can safely stop listening, and they sleep hard through it. When the wrens start, loud and bright and absurdly large-voiced for the place, the tenders groan and stir. It is the one sound in Ashcarr they are allowed to resent.

In the morning, as the fog lifts and the arms begin to straighten, they walk the paths. Each walks their own arms first, then a wider round, looking at pins, at rods, at the collars of scar-bark where iron enters wood. They carry the cord looped over one shoulder and a mallet at the hip. Some carry a small pot of beetle eggs packed in damp moss, for any housing that has been cleared too well.

In the afternoon, the quiet hour, when the arms are straight and the engines slow, they rest. They eat then, the main meal. Many sleep a second time, briefly, in patches of moving green light. Or they sit in the paper mill and copy the ledger, because the old pages wear and must be rewritten before they fail.

At dusk they take their places. Everyone is somewhere, a palm on a wall, as the fog gathers in the low ground and the arms begin to curl and the ticking returns and the lamps, one by one, begin to glow. This is the first moment they are really *at work*, and it is the moment you are most likely to see them, standing like posts, listening.

At night they move from engine to engine under the orange lamps, resetting what slips. They wipe pale slugs off the pawls, gently, and set them back on the iron where they belong. Toward midnight the lamps begin to fade as the wood reaches full swell, and the tenders go to their housings. When the last dynamo winds down and the last lamp goes to nothing, the basin is entirely dark and entirely still, and they sleep.

They do not count hours. They count the evening curl. A tender will say something happened "two hundred into the curl" or "near the top of the swell." They say "the dry," or "the rime," the way someone else might say July.

## What They Eat

Not much, and not much of it grows in the basin.

Nettle in spring, before it goes to fiber, and bracken fiddleheads, which they know how to prepare so they do not sicken. Hazelnuts from the rim. Mushrooms, but only a few kinds, and only from the edges, because they will not let fungus take hold anywhere near living engine-wood. Everything else comes down from outside.

Twice a year, before the rime and after the thaw, one tender goes up the slope with a sledge of rope coils and bundled paper. That tender is always whoever has most recently lost an arm to pruning and does not yet have a new one. They go to the rim, where the turf is dry and the deer watch, and on to wherever the trade is done. They come back with grain, salt, lamp glass, copper wire, and iron, in small careful quantities. They bring back any news, which is received politely and forgotten by the thaw.

The person who makes this journey is said to *go dry*. It is not an honor. They come back strange for a few days, talking a little too loudly, walking a little too fast. The others treat them the way you might treat someone recovering from a fever. They lay palms on them and wait for them to settle.

## Where They Come From

A few are born here. Children are rare in Ashcarr and very quiet. They are raised communally in the carding house, which is the warmest building, and they learn to walk on moss before they walk on anything else. A child from Ashcarr, taken to the rim, will stumble on the hard turf and cry at the loudness of their own footsteps.

Most tenders came from outside, and almost all of them came in fog.

The story is always roughly the same. Someone is crossing the country above the basin at dusk, a traveler or a runaway or a shepherd off their line. The fog comes up out of the bowl and they lose the rim and walk downhill without knowing it. They notice the air go cool and heavy and smell of cut stems, the ground go soft. Then there are lights, dim and orange and uneven, pulsing faintly, and a sound like many small clocks. And there, with a hand on a wall, is someone who lifts the other palm, *wait*, and after a while says: "It's all right."

Most leave in the morning. Some stay a season, to help with the rope walk or to wait out an injury, and then leave. The ones who stay for good are the ones who, some evening in their first autumn, stop hearing the quiet. They do not notice it happen. A tender notices for them, sees them stop mid-sentence and tilt their head at a skipped tick, and marks it in the ledger in the margin of whichever arm is next to need a keeper.

The test, if it can be called a test, comes at the first rime. The newcomer is brought to a wall and asked to put their hand on it and say what the arm is doing. The arm is not doing anything. It is frozen stiff, locked in rime, and the whole basin is silent. The right answer is some version of *waiting*. Nobody is told this in advance. Those who say *nothing* are not sent away. They are simply not given an arm yet.

## Their Dead

When a tender dies, they are laid on the moss.

They are not dug into it. They are laid on the surface of the deepest lawn, the one in the middle of the basin where nothing has been built. It is the lawn the oldest map shows as a lawn, and the lawn on the older maps too. They are laid in their grey-green cloth with their measuring cord coiled on their chest. Then everyone goes back to work, because it is always either dusk or morning or some other time that needs them.

The moss takes them the way it takes everything dropped. Within a season they are gone from sight. The tenders say that underneath, in the black soft peat, the dead of Ashcarr lie among the old sunk gear wheels and dropped mallets and broken engines, all of it preserved, bone and iron together, stained the same dark color. Nobody digs to check. Nobody would.

What they do say, and this is where it becomes difficult to separate what lives in Ashcarr from what is only said about it, is that on certain nights the ticking comes from below.

## The Ones Underneath

It happens on the heaviest nights, deep in the wet season, when the fog is so thick it drowns you to the waist and the arms bend so hard the lamps go almost bright. If you lie down on the middle lawn then, with your ear to the moss, which no tender would do but which a visitor sometimes does, you may hear it. Among the scattered clicking of the fourteen buildings there is another, fainter layer, deeper and wetter and very slow.

*Tk.*

A long pause.

*Tk.*

The tenders have explanations, and they offer them in the tone people use for explanations they do not need. Roots, they say. The hornbeams send roots under the lawn, and the roots swell with the wet like everything else, and somewhere down there an old ratchet has a root through it, and the root is doing what the arms do. Or it is the peat itself, which swells and shrinks with the water and moves whatever lies in it. It is just old iron, being moved.

But they call it *the ones underneath*, and they do not mean the iron.

They will not say more than that. In the paper mill, though, in the oldest part of the ledger, on the brittle brown pages, there is a column under no arm. It has no heading and no measurements. It holds only a list of names, the old worn-out names that are no longer names, and beside each one a single mark. Every few pages a later hand has added to it. The most recent entry is a tender who died three winters ago, and beside her name, in the same cramped writing as all the ordinary notes, is written: *pinned lower*.

## What Lives Here

You could say the tenders live here, and it would be true. You could say the town lives here, the fourteen great slow bodies of hornbeam and yew with their iron hearts, and that would be true too. The wood is alive, and it moves, and it pulls, and it must be answered.

But spend long enough in Ashcarr and the distinction starts to wear, the way the person-names wear.

Consider it from the wood's side. The arms bend and straighten, and the bending turns the iron, but the arms cannot correct themselves. They grow, and growing, they drift out of true. Left alone, they would pull their buildings off the root plate and snap their rods and fall silent. What the arms need is some part of the machine that can *walk*. It must go from engine to engine, feel where the pull has gone wrong, and move the pin. Without it the engine lasts a year or two. With it, the engine has lasted longer than anyone has records for.

The slugs keep the iron clean. The beetles keep the gear teeth clear. The tenders keep the arms in true. Ask a tender, in the quiet afternoon hour, what they are, and they will not say *people who look after the engines*. They are likelier to put a palm on the table and say something like: "We're what corrects it." And they put themselves in the same list as the slugs and the beetle-grubs, without any sense of demotion.

So what lives in Ashcarr is not quite the tenders and not quite the trees. It is the thing all of them together are doing, which is keeping count.

The fog comes, and the arms curl, and the ratchets tick, and each tick is one small unit of weather caught and kept. The ticks become turns of a shaft. The turns become rope and paper and a dim orange light on the paths. The paper becomes the ledger, and the ledger records who kept which arm, and how it pulled, and where it was pinned. When a keeper dies they go into the moss, and some nights, very faintly, the moss ticks back. The whole basin is a single slow mind that does one thing: it notices the weather and remembers it. The tenders are the part of that mind that can stand up and walk to where the counting has gone wrong.

They know this, the way you know your own heartbeat, without much thinking about it. It is why they are not lonely, though they are few and quiet and live inside machines. It is why the ones who stay are the ones who stop hearing the silence. Nobody here is ever listening to nothing. They are listening to the count, and the count is them.

## At the End of the Visit

You leave in the morning, if you leave. The fog is lifting, the arms beginning to straighten, the first pawls dropping into their teeth. A tender walks you to where the moss gives way to turf, and stops there, because the turf is loud and dry and not theirs.

You turn back once. They are already walking away, back down into the green, slow and careful, a palm going out flat to the nearest wall as they pass it. They are not steadying themselves. They are asking.

And the wall, you suppose, is answering. *Tk.* It's all right.
