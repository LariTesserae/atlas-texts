# What Lives in the Basin

## First, the Obvious Answer

Everything in the description lives here. The silver shoals spiral up the plumes. The grazers drum their borders on the pillars. The pike hang in the twilight like pale knives. The eels come up from the seams in the storms. A few dozen men and women ride the crawlers and sleep in the wall-workshops with their helmets on the bench beside them. All of them live in the Basin.

None of them are *from* it, though. Every fish came down the intake shafts as an egg or a larva, conceived in whatever water lies above. Every person came down in a lift cage, hired or assigned, and every one of them has a place upstairs they call home. The Basin is fifteen, maybe twenty years old. For almost everything in it, it is a destination: a thermal to ride, a pillar to scrape, a shift to work.

The question of what *lives* here, in the sense of what belongs here, has a smaller answer. It is also a stranger one, and it has taken a while to come into focus.

---

## The Long Shape

The divers mention it first, and not as a real thing. It starts as a story told in the wall-workshops while a suit dries on its rack.

Someone at a far pillar, working the lower lamp string with a hand-light, sees something go by in the twilight beyond the edge of the sodium glow. It is pale, long, and slow. It is not a pike. A pike is a man's length, and this was longer than the crawler behind them. It moved the way a train moves, all of one piece, without hurry, and it did not turn to look at them.

Different divers give different lengths, and the numbers grow over the years. At first it was eight meters, then twelve. One man insisted on twenty and was laughed at until two others, on separate shifts, said the same. The crews have a name for it, as crews do. They call it *the Inspector*, because it patrols the pillars on circuits the way the crawler crews do. It seems to check each one, pausing at the base the way an engineer pauses at a gauge, and then moving on.

That is where the image begins: a rumor, a pale length in the dim reach, a joke about a colleague. It doesn't stay there.

---

## What It Is Made Of

Assemble what the crews know, from sightings, from the hydrophone logs kept in the eastern workshop, and from the things it leaves behind, and something more particular comes into view.

It is a fish, or it began as one. It was probably a sturgeon, or something kin to them, one of the handful of larvae that slipped the intake screens in the first year of flooding, when the oldest sections were new and there was nothing in them to eat it. It may be the oldest living thing in the Basin. If so, it is exactly as old as the Basin, and it has grown as the Basin grew, section by section, as new slabs were poured and flooded and the territory widened.

It has never seen sunlight. Its eyes are large and gold-irised. They are tuned to the sodium banks, to the yellow-white of a factory yard at night, and it seems to see the mercury-blue reaches poorly. It prefers the yellow pillars, and its circuits bend toward them.

Its skin is pale grey, nearly white, and scarred all over. The slab edges are sharp, and anything that feeds on the floor for fifteen years gets cut by them. Something that big, rooting for eels along the seams, gets cut *thousands* of times. The scars have healed in pale ridges, and the ridges follow the lines of the floor. One diver who got close enough to see swore that the scarring along its flanks is hexagonal, a lattice of old healed cuts that repeats the grid of the slabs. It carries a map of the floor on its body without meaning to. It was written there by the concrete, one cut at a time.

Black threads trail from its fins and gill-covers: strands of the uncured tar compound from the seams, picked up in the warm zones where the tar weeps upward. They have hardened into ribbons, and it trails them like pennants. Along one side, near the tail, there is a patch of industrial orange: paint scraped from a bollard, ground into a wound, and healed over. The Basin has marked it with its own colors.

It is enormous now. Nobody has measured it. The best estimate comes from a crew that watched it pass alongside a stationary crawler, nose to tail, and found that it overlapped the train at both ends.

---

## How It Hears

The Inspector is not following the crawler crews on their inspections. The crews worked this out late, and a little uncomfortably. When the hydrophone logs were set against the maintenance records, it turned out to be the other way around.

It arrives at a pillar *before* the pillar fails.

A lamp string starts to short, or a seam at the base opens a fraction, or a bearing in a manifold pump begins to wear. Days before the gauges upstairs register anything, the Inspector is there, hanging at the base of that pillar in the half-light, as still as a moored hull. The crews have started to use it. An old engineer in the eastern workshop keeps a board with a grid of the pillars chalked on it, and when a sighting comes in, she marks it. The mark means *go check that one*. She is usually right.

This is less mysterious than it sounds, though no less remarkable. Each pillar groans at its own pitch, and the pike navigate by those pitches, but the pike only need to know *where* they are. The Inspector has had fifteen years to learn what every pillar *usually* sounds like, and to notice when one sounds wrong. Its lateral line is a nerve running the length of its body, and at that body's size it is an instrument of extraordinary reach. It hears the hum not as one chord but as its parts: this transformer, that pump, the fins of that radiator bank knocking as they expand. A bearing going soft changes its note by a fraction, and the Inspector goes to see what has changed.

It is not inspecting anything, not in the engineers' sense. It is doing what any animal does when a familiar landscape shifts: going to look at the part that moved. In its case, though, the landscape is a machine, and the animal has come to know the machine better than the people who built it.

This is how it knew, hours before the pumps changed shift, that the currents would turn. The shoals feel it too, but only as unease. They scatter. The Inspector does not scatter. It goes to the eastern intake and waits there, and when the eels come boiling up into the sediment storm, it is already in position, mouth open, in the murk.

---

## The Sun and the Sky

For the Inspector, the lamp schedule is not an imitation of anything. It is the sun, the only one there has ever been. Lamp-dawn rolling out across the grid pillar by pillar is simply what morning is: a wave of yellow light spreading outward from somewhere it has never been. Lamp-dusk is the world dimming to a third, as it always has.

The ceiling is its sky, four hundred meters up, ribbed steel plate lost in blue-grey haze. When the plates moan, the long shrieks that echo wall to wall for minutes are its thunder. The pulse every eleven seconds is its tide, and more than that. It is the thing its body has kept time to since it was a finger's length long. Its gills may well open on the pulse. Its heart may beat in some fraction of it. Nothing that grew up upstairs could feel the Basin's breathing as breathing. The Inspector has never known any other kind.

This is what makes it the Basin's first native. It isn't the size, or the age, or even the uncanny hearing. It is that the Basin is not a structure to it at all. The pillars are not columns holding up a roof. They are trees, or mountains, or the stars it steers by. The rails are not rails. They are the paths the loud warm things travel, the things that stir up eels behind them. The divers are not workers. They are small bright animals that come and go with their tethers and leave behind strange hard objects, which the Inspector sometimes picks up and carries off.

---

## What It Carries

It collects things. Nobody expected this, and nobody quite knows what to make of it.

Lost tools are common at the pillar bases: wrenches, chisels, a hammer dropped from a numb glove, a coil of wire. The grazers colonize them and scrape their algal film. But some of them vanish. The crews used to blame the currents, but the currents don't move a four-kilo spanner across a kilometer of flat floor.

A crawler crew mapping a new rail spur in one of the oldest sections, the first-flooded ones, found out where they go. In the corner where two slabs meet a bollard, sheltered from the main current, there is a cache. Tools, lengths of chain, a diver's lead boot-weight, a lamp housing that must have fallen from a pillar string, three brass helmet-latches, a crawler's brake shoe. They aren't piled up. They are laid out, more or less, in a rough curve around the bollard's base, the way a bird might line a nest.

It might be nothing. Sturgeon root through the floor, and a big animal might simply push hard objects out of the way while feeding and leave them where they fall. But the crew who found it did not think it looked like nothing. The engineer in the eastern workshop went out on the next crawler to see it for herself, and when she came back she rubbed the Inspector's mark off her chalk board for that corner of the grid. She said it wasn't a place to send a repair crew.

## The Song

This is the part the engineer keeps in a separate log.

Somewhere forty kilometers from the eastern workshop, a pressure valve vents on a cycle. Its sound travels the whole Basin: a long wail that rises and falls, like a whale calling. Every new crew member hears it in their first week and asks what it is. They are told it is a valve.

Most of the time, if a hydrophone is left running long enough after the valve vents, there is a second sound. It comes from the dim reaches, faint, low, and slightly off-pitch from the valve, as if something heard it and is trying to answer.

The engineer has recordings going back nine years. The answer is always after the valve, never before. It varies in its details, but it always climbs and falls as the valve does. For nine years something has been calling back to a pressure valve.

Four years ago the valve was replaced. The new one vents at a slightly higher pitch and a shorter cycle.

For thirty-eight days there was no answer. The engineer's log for that period is short and plain, a date and the word *nothing* over and over. The crews reported the Inspector at the far pillars, the outer ones, farther than it usually ranged, as if it had gone looking.

On the thirty-ninth day the answer came back, at the new pitch.

## What It Is Becoming

This is where the image, which has already shifted from a rumor to a fish to something that reads the machine, keeps changing.

The easy version is a lonely animal courting a valve, singing into a steel room at the only thing that ever sounds like it. That version is true, and it is sad. Maybe the Inspector is the only one of its kind that ever made it through the screens, and maybe it will grow old, calling, and die answered only by machinery.

But there are other things in the engineer's log.

In the warmest zones near the radiator banks, where sediment gathers in the corners and the pondweed streams sideways in the current, the tar seams are soft and warm. Two seasons ago, a diver working a manifold base found eggs pressed into one of them. They were dark, the size of peas, thousands of them, set in the warm tar where the seam had pulled back from the concrete. Nothing else in the Basin lays eggs like that. Nothing else is big enough.

Whether they were fertile, nobody knows. Maybe the Inspector was not the only larva through the screens that first year. Maybe the answers in the dim reaches were never meant for the valve, or not only for it. Maybe there are two long shapes out there, or more, that have been calling to each other across the pillars, and the valve just happens to sing in something close to their voice.

Or maybe the eggs came to nothing, and the Inspector laid them anyway, as fish will, into the warmest part of the only world it has.

The crews talk about it in the wall-workshops while their suits dry. Some of them say they've seen small pale shapes near the radiator banks, a meter or two long, too big for pike and the wrong color. Nobody has a good photograph.

---

## So: What Lives in the Basin

Here is the answer, as far as it goes.

A great pale fish, older than some of the concrete, scarred with the pattern of the floor. It trails black ribbons of tar and carries a patch of industrial orange healed into its tail. It steers by the groaning pitches of the pillars, keeps its heartbeat on the pumps' eleven-second stroke, and holds the lamp schedule as its only sun. It hears the machine better than the people who run it, and it arrives where the Basin is hurt before the gauges know. It builds a curve of lost tools around a bollard in the oldest section. It has sung for nine years into the dark reaches, and once, when the answering voice changed, it spent thirty-eight days learning the new pitch.

Around it, the Basin is still sharp and bright and barely begun. The kelp is only starting on the bollards. The algae is only starting on the lamps. The tar hasn't cured. Nothing has softened.

But something has been born here that never came down the shafts. It knows the Basin not as a room, or a machine, or a destination, but as the world. It may already have laid the next generation into the warm seams, and those children, if they live, will be the second natives, born of the first. They will never have known anything but hum and pulse, the yellow pillars, the thunder of the ceiling plates, and the long falling call from forty kilometers away, answered.

The engineers built a heat sink. What lives in it has started, slowly, to make it a home.
