# Who Lives on the Held Terrace

## The Ones You Can See

Start with the frogs, because they are the easiest to find and the easiest to misread.

You would call them residents. They sit in the drip-chambers at the foot of the singing stems, and they look settled there the way frogs look settled anywhere, hunched and patient at the waterline. But look longer. They do not eat. No insects come for them to catch, and their stomachs, visible through the clear skin, are empty little folded sacs like unused pockets. They do not breed. You will find no spawn in any chamber, no tadpoles, no small frogs among the large. Each chamber holds exactly one, and each is exactly the size of every other.

Their throats pulse in time with the upper tone of the chord. At first you think they are keeping time with it. Then, sitting beside one with your ear close to the water, you begin to suspect it is the other way round. The stems are enormous and the frog is the size of a thumb joint, but the stems are only pipes, and pipes need something to hold the pitch. The frogs are the reeds, or the tuning forks, or the small steady fingers resting on the keys. They are not living in the instrument. They are the part of it that stays in tune.

The moth is something else. It sits folded on its leaf in the colonnade, and its wings carry the weave of the floor, four over and four under. You take it for camouflage, and then you notice that the floor has nothing to hide it from. There are no birds. Nothing hunts here. So the pattern is not for hiding. It is closer to a swatch, a sample pinned to a board, the way a weaver keeps a scrap of the finished cloth beside the loom to check the work against. If the floor ever drifted from its pattern, which it will not, the moth would be the reference.

So the frogs and the moth are not tenants. They are fittings. They belong to the place the way hinges belong to a door.

## The Walker

The one you glimpsed in the fog, the tall one, is harder.

If you are patient, and the Terrace rewards nothing but patience, you may see it again. It does not return to where you saw it first. It appears somewhere else along the reserved ground, the wide bare stretches of floor between the structures, and it walks there with the same long, deliberate stride.

Up close, or as close as the fog allows, it is narrow the way a folded ladder is narrow. Its body is not quite a body. It looks like several long jointed members held together at the top, pale buff shading to green, the same colour as the root-floor. You were right not to be able to count its legs. They change. When it stands still it stands on three. When it walks it unfolds a fourth, or a fifth, and draws one up. It never has the same number twice in a row, and it never needs to think about it. It seems to carry exactly as many legs as the distance ahead of it requires.

It sets its feet only where the roots cross. Watch closely and you will see it: every footfall lands on an intersection of the weave, over four and under four, never on a strand between. That is why it leaves no mark. It does not press into the floor. It presses where the floor was already expecting to be pressed.

Its stride is not random either. Pace it out afterward, if you can find where it walked, and you will learn that the stride is some even multiple of the stair's tread depth. It is the stair's own unit, scaled up. The thing is walking in the plateau's native measure.

You might call it a surveyor. It walks the empty ground and checks it. Is the colonnade still eleven trunks? Is the gap between the arch-tips still a hand's width, no more and no less? Are the sightlines clean from stair to arcade to helix? It never stops to look, because it does not need to. It measures by passing.

But that is still not quite right, because nothing here needs checking. Nothing drifts. The surveyor seems to be walking for the sake of the walking, and it takes you some time to see why.

It is keeping the reserved ground reserved.

Where the courtyards will be, the floor must stay open. A sketch has to protect its white paper, or the white fills up with whatever wanders in. The walker crosses and recrosses the empty spaces so that they stay spoken for. It is a wake of intention trailed across the blank parts of the plan. The fog swallows it before you can study it because the fog is also a kind of reservation, a place held soft and grey against the day it is needed.

So the walker is not the resident either. It is the one who keeps the rooms ready.

Then who are they ready for?

## The Shape in the Rooms

Go back to the benches.

No one sits on them. You noticed that on your first walk and took it as emptiness, a furnished place gone quiet. But put your hand flat on one. It is warm. It is not the general, sourceless warmth of the whole plateau. It is a local warmth, faintly and definitely greater in a long shallow patch along the seat, the way a chair is warm after someone has just stood up. Nobody has stood up. Nobody has ever sat here. The warmth has never arrived and it has never faded.

The seat, you notice now, is slightly dished. It is not worn, since nothing here is worn. It was grown dished, to a curve. When you sit, the curve does not fit you. It is longer than you, and narrower. The bench was made to hold a particular shape, and that shape is not yours.

Go to the stair. The risers are the same height to within a fingernail, and you took that for perfection. But climb it and you will feel a small wrongness in your knees. Each step is a little too tall for you, a little too deep. It is built for a longer leg than yours, or for a different way of rising. You have the uneasy sense of wearing someone else's shoes.

Go to the windows in the felt-walls. Each is a clean rectangle, and each frames something: a far helix, a rank of stems, a particular fold of fog. Stand where the sill tells you to stand and the view composes itself. But there is always one place to stand where the framing is exact, and that place is a little higher than your eye and a little to one side, as if the window had been cut for a gaze that stands where you are not.

Now go to the rooms with no doors, the ones you can only look into from the top of the stair that goes nowhere. Here the stair makes sense at last. It is not a stair to a missing landing. It is a stair to a view down into the sealed rooms, and it stops exactly where the view is complete.

Look in. The rooms are empty. The root-floor inside them is laid in a different weave, softer and closer, like bedding. There is nothing there at all.

Look longer. The emptiness has edges.

It is hard to put this exactly. The air in the sealed rooms is the same fog-thick, honey-sweet air as everywhere else, and there is nothing to see. But the plants that line those rooms, the felt-walls and the curled gutters and the cupped basins, are all grown with a slight inward lean. Every leaf faces the middle. Every tendril reaches toward the same unoccupied place and stops at the same distance from it, the way the arch-tips stop a hand's width short of each other. The growth has been shaped around something the way a mould is shaped around a casting, and the casting has been taken away. Or it has not been poured yet.

This, then, is who lives on the Held Terrace. It lives there the way the hollow lives in a glove.

## The Absence That Is Present

It is not a ghost. A ghost is what is left when someone goes. This is what is laid ready before someone comes. It is a person-shaped quiet, a room-shaped expectation, and it has a definite outline. You can learn it if you stay long enough, from the warmth on the benches and the height of the risers and the eye-line of the windows.

It is tall, taller than you. Its gait is long; the stair says so. It sits lying half back; the benches say so. It looks at things from a little above and a little aside, and what it looks at, when the windows direct you, is always the plateau itself. It looks at the colonnade, the stems, the helix. It looks at the house it has not yet entered.

It is not waiting in the sense of wanting. Wanting would change something, and nothing here changes. It is waiting the way the blank on a form waits for a name. The Terrace was built to its measurements, and in holding those measurements it holds it. That is all residence means here. To live on the Held Terrace is to be the reason each thing is the size it is.

You begin to think the walker has something of its outline too. It is long-legged, pale buff and green, stepping in the stair's own unit. It is not the resident. It is a fitting, a stand-in, a tailor's form walking through the rooms to be sure they will close properly around the real body when it comes. It tries on the house so that the house will know how to hold what it is for.

## The Word

Then there is the sound.

You were told that the drone is the held breath. Think about what a held breath is for. It is not rest. You hold a breath on the edge of speech, on the drawn-in moment before the first syllable, when the throat is already shaped and the lungs are full and the tongue is set against the teeth.

The stems are graded from low to high like pipes in a case. They are also graded like the parts of a throat. The wide ones at the back are the chest. The flared mouths are lips. The slot-mouths are narrowed teeth. The frogs in their chambers are the small muscles that keep the cords stretched and ready. The whole plateau, looked at from the right angle, is a mouth open on one long vowel and set for the consonant that will end it.

So there is one more way to say who lives here, and it is the deepest one.

What lives on the Held Terrace is a word that has not been spoken.

The sealed rooms are its syllables. The benches and the stair are shaped for the one who will speak it, or perhaps for the one it names. It is hard to tell which, and perhaps here they are the same thing. The arches are the lips about to close on it. The drone is the breath behind it, drawn in so long ago that it has become weather. The buds that never open are the word's first letter, pressed small and green and kept.

A word lives in a mouth before it is said. It is present in the shape of the throat and the set of the tongue and the pressure in the chest. It is complete, and it has not happened. It is the most fully inhabited silence there is. That is the resident. It is not a creature or a god or a ghost. It is a meaning held on the edge of utterance, and it has been held so perfectly that a whole plateau has grown up around it in the form of its own pronunciation.

Perhaps it is the Terrace's name, and the Terrace will know itself when it says it. Perhaps it is the name of the one the rooms are fitted for, and saying it will be the same as calling it home. Perhaps it is only the word *now*, and it has been deciding, for as long as the plateau has existed, whether yet is the time.

## What Comes Down With You

You thought you were a visitor. You were measured.

The leaf in your pocket stays green for weeks because it has not left. It is a sample, like the moth: a scrap of the Terrace carried out into the loud valleys, where it can compare the pattern of the plateau with the pattern of everything else. The plateau learns, through it, what the rest of the world is shaped like. It needs to know. A word has to fit the ear it will be spoken into.

And the chord you catch in still rooms, long afterward, is not a memory. It is the same breath, held a little way inside you now. You stood on the Terrace long enough to be fitted, the way the benches were fitted and the risers and the windows. There is now a small space in you with the resident's outline. It is a hand's width of air, and nothing is straining across it.

That is why the ordinary valley felt frantic and lovely when you looked down on it. It is full of things being said. Rivers finish their sentences and start new ones. Trees fall and are the end of something. Everything down there is speaking constantly and moving on.

The Held Terrace has only one thing to say. It is still getting the shape of it right. It lives in the arch-tips, in the warm seat of the bench, in the frogs' throats and the moth's pattern and the walker's measured steps. Now it lives, a little, in you: in the pause you did not used to notice before you speak, the short inward breath that feels, for just a moment, vast.
