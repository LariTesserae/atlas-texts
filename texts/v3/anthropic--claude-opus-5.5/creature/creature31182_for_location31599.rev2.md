# Who Lives in the Stair

## One of them

Start with a single person, because that is how you would meet them: one at a time, very close.

She is short and broad, as they all are, built low through the hips and thighs. Her knees are always slightly bent. She walks flat-footed, sliding more than stepping, the way people walk on the deck of a boat. She has done this since before she could remember, and her body no longer knows another way to stand. Across her forehead, just below the hairline, runs a smooth pale groove where the goat-hair sling has pressed every day since she was seven. Every adult in the Stair has this groove. It is the first thing you notice and then you stop noticing it, the way you stop noticing that everyone has ears.

Her face is dark from the glare and greased with goat-fat against it. The skin at the corners of her eyes is folded into deep fans. Her nose peels. Her lips are chapped and slightly swollen, because she keeps her slate mirror wet with her mouth, and she has the gesture they all have: a quick lick of the thumb, a pass across the slate, done without thinking, the way someone elsewhere might push back their hair while deciding something.

Her hands are red, cracked, and always cold. The fingertips are thick and very sensitive, and they read stone the way other fingers read cloth. She can tell by touch which face of a block was the weather face and which was buried. She can tell whether a stone is one of hers. She knows the grain of her family's lichen-stone the way she knows the backs of her children's heads.

When she speaks to you, she will take your forearm. She will lean in until her breath is on your cheek. Her voice is breathy and low, full of soft stops and hums that are meant to be felt through the hand as much as heard. Speech here is a thing done between two bodies. It cannot be done to a crowd.

## Two tongues

That fact shapes everything about them.

There is no public voice in the Stair. No one gives speeches. No one calls out across a square, because there is no square and the call would die in ten paces. Everything that concerns more than two people goes by light. The flash-speech is quick, plain, and practical: *stone loose, lane of the Weavers. Goat on the lip. Fog coming. Need four hands at the headwall.* It has no tenderness in it and is not meant to. It is the town's working language, and a child can manage it before she can walk well.

Voice is kept for everything else. Grief is spoken. So are love, jokes, the private names of things, arguments between married people, and what the old tell the young about the dead. Because you must be touching someone to speak to them, a spoken word is always also a touch. The people say it is very hard to lie in voice, because the forearm tells the hand what the mouth is doing. Whether that is true or not, they believe it, and so they rarely try.

Every person therefore has two names. The light-name is a short pattern of flashes, given at birth and known to the whole town. The voice-name is murmured into a child's ear by its mother on the first night and is known only to people who have held that child's arm and been told. Counting the people who know your voice-name is one way of counting your life.

At night, with no sun, the light-speech stops entirely. The whole town becomes voice. Families lie on their felts in the lamp-dark, arms across one another, and the only language is the murmur. Night is when things are said in the Stair. Day is when things are done.

## Hearths

A household is not the people who share blood. It is the people who carry for the same walls.

You belong to a hearth by carrying its stone. A marriage is two people who begin to carry for one house. An orphan is taken in by being handed a sling. If you quarrel badly with your family you can go and carry for another hearth, and after a season of stones you are theirs. The question *whose are you?* is asked as *whose walls do you lift?*

Height is honor, but it is also exhaustion. The high hearths against the headwall hold their place with a dozen hands carrying almost without rest, and the people who live there are lean and hard and short-tempered and very proud. The middle lanes are easier and more comfortable, and most people live there all their lives, holding steady, neither climbing nor drifting. The low lanes are quieter. The houses there are carried by fewer hands, more slowly, and everyone understands that they are gradually giving ground.

When someone dies, the household builds a small stone cell against the downhill wall and lays the body inside. Then it stops carrying that cell. The house goes on holding its position. The cell does not. Over months it separates, a finger's width at a time, and falls behind. It becomes a little tail, then a separate thing, then a stone hut in a lower lane among strangers' houses. Years later it reaches the loose quarter by the lip. The family can go down and visit it whenever they like, and it is always a little lower than the last time.

Time is reckoned this way. *She was born the spring her great-grandfather reached the Lip Lane.* A person's age is sometimes given not in years but in how far their dead have traveled.

The lichen-stones are the exception to all of this. A family's oldest lichened blocks are never allowed to drift. They are lifted, again and again, from the downhill wall to the uphill course, generation after generation, so that the same orange crust that looked on the first mother of the hearth now looks on her descendants. These stones are the closest thing the Stair has to gods. People lay their hands on them and speak to them in voice.

## The others

The people are not the only ones who live this way.

Everything that lives on the tongue lives by going up against the going down. The pikas are always moving house uphill, gnawing out new chambers in the upper voids as their old ones slide away. The goats ride blocks down and scramble back up and ride again, apparently for pleasure. The ice-crawlers walk uphill at dawn across the glaze. Even the lichen, which cannot walk, is carried back up by hands that love it.

Only a few things don't play. The dippers go *through* the town instead of across it, down into the dark water and up again somewhere else, and the people regard them with a mild, fond unease, as if they were guests who know a back way into the house. The snow-fleas drift freely on the rim, going nowhere, and so the people read them as messages from outside the Stair altogether. And the ravens sit on the headwall, on solid rock, which never moves. They are the only residents who don't climb. They watch the climbing.

The old say the ravens are flashing. Some of them say more quietly that the ravens are the ones the Stair is talking *to*.

## The shape

Stay long enough and something else becomes visible.

No stone in the Stair stays. No house stays either, not as material. A house is fully replaced, every block of it carried from the bottom to the top, several times over in one person's life. The people do not stay. They are born, they carry, they drift into cells and go over the lip. The pikas, the goats, and the moss all pass through.

And yet the Stair is exactly where it has always been. The Weavers' Lane is where the Weavers' Lane was. The bend by the Three Drip-Troughs has bent that way since before anyone's grandmother. The old people, blind, count their steps from their door to the headwall, and the count is the same one their own grandparents taught them, though every stone they step on is new.

What lives here, then, is a shape that holds itself up out of passing things. It is like a flame, or the standing wave in a river that looks still while the water rushes through it.

It behaves like something alive. It has a pulse: the crosshatched lines of carriers moving all day, bringing material up from the bottom and building it in at the top. It has nerves: the thousands of small flashes that cross it every noon, from the lip to the headwall and back, carrying news of every wound. It heals. A split wall in the morning is closed by afternoon, and a sinkhole is filled before dark. It sheds at the lip, steadily, one stone at a time, *plock*. It sleeps at night, glazed and still, while the ground goes on sliding beneath it. It remembers, but only in its lane names and its counted steps and the patterns of its light-names. Its memory is the one thing about it that is not made of stone.

The people know this. They don't say it aloud, because there is no one you could say it to, touching, who wouldn't already know. But there is one day in high summer, at noon, when the sun comes off the snowfields at full strength, and every person in the Stair turns their mirror at once toward the headwall. For a few breaths the whole bowl flashes together. The rock face above the town blazes white with a light thrown by every hand. Then the mirrors go back into their pouches and everyone picks up their stone.

They call it by a flash that has no voice equivalent. Children say it means *all of us*. The old say it means that the Stair is looking at itself.

## Beneath

But the Stair did not invent this way of living. It learned it.

Under the town, under the rubble, in the cold and dark, lies the ice. It is the core of the tongue, and it is the oldest resident. It is also not still. Snow falls on the upper cirque, packs down, turns to ice, and joins it from above. Rock breaks off the headwall and rides on it. Ice melts out at the bottom and runs into the tarn. The glacier too is a shape made of passing things. It has held its place in the bowl far longer than the town has, perhaps longer than people have existed, taking in at the top and giving up at the bottom, going down and being renewed from above.

The people did not choose to build on a treadmill. They built on something that was already doing what they do, and they learned to do it alongside it. They are a small, fast, warm copy of the slow cold thing beneath them. The houses copy the town, the town copies the glacier, and a body, if you think about it long enough in the lamp-dark, copies them all. It eats, it breathes, it replaces itself, and it stays.

This is where the sound goes. The people believe it, and in the Stair it is almost impossible not to. Every murmured word, every name spoken into an ear, sinks into the wet voids between the stones and is carried down with them. Nothing said aloud is ever lost, exactly. It rides the ground to the lip and goes into the tarn, where it lies with everything else. The milk-green water is full of it: every grief, every joke, every voice-name spoken since the beginning, settled in the rock flour on the bottom.

So a spoken word is always also a gift to the ground. That is part of why they speak so seldom and so close. Light goes up into the sky and is gone in an instant. Voice goes down into the dark and is kept.

The ravens watch the one. The ice keeps the other. The people live between.

## At dusk

An old woman stands in her doorway in the Middle Lanes as the snowfields go gray. She is nearly blind. She has carried for this hearth for sixty years, and her forehead groove is so deep that it holds a thin line of shadow even in the last light. Her great-granddaughter, six years old and not yet grooved, stands beside her practicing light-names with a scrap of mica, catching the last of the sun off the rim.

The girl flashes her grandmother's light-name at the headwall, badly.

The old woman cannot see it. But she takes the girl's forearm and leans in, and in the one language that works at this range she says the child's voice-name into her ear. She says her own voice-name too, the one that only four living people know. Then she says, the way it is always said, *The ground goes down. We go up. That is where we live.*

The words go into the stones at their feet.

The frost comes. The lane glazes over. The two of them go inside to the felts, and the house, which is not the house it was last year and will not be the house it is next year, holds its place in the dark. It slides, and is carried back, and slides.

Far below, something goes into the tarn.

What lives in the Stair is not the stone, and not the people, and not the ice, though all of them are there. It is the going up. It is the steady refusal, handed from arm to arm, to be carried down. It has lived here for longer than anyone can count, and none of it has ever stayed.
