# Who Lives at Hollin Fell

## The Tenants

The Sink has obvious residents. The swifts hold the walls and the beavers hold the floor. Stoats work the talus. A peregrine keeps the north rim, and dippers keep the passage mouths. They could all be counted, even if nobody has done it, and all of them are recent arrivals. They came because the ground opened, and they came down into the light the collapse let in.

They are tenants. The Sink is new, and its newness is the most important fact about it, so whoever truly lives there should be looked for in whatever is not new.

## The Small and the Continuous

Start small, where the lives run together.

On the gray snow in the shaded corners there are springtails, thousands of them, each smaller than a sesame seed. They are blue-black and jump when breathed on, and they scatter across the pitted surface like pepper. They graze the algae that bloom in the meltwater film on the snow itself. When the snow goes in early summer they go down into the moss and are never seen again until the next winter, when they rise out of the green as the drifts fall. They are the reason the old snow looks dirty.

In the moss cushions there are tardigrades, rotifers, and mites in their hundreds of thousands per handful. The moss is wet enough that it is really a pond, stood upright and given a shape.

On the tufa, under every seep, there is a film you can only feel, a faint slickness on the cream-colored stone. This is the builder. The tufa isn't laid down by water alone. Cyanobacteria and diatoms live in a skin across the wet rock. They pull carbon from the water to feed themselves, and in doing so tip the lime out of solution, so it crystallizes around their own sheaths. They entomb themselves, and a new skin grows on the surface of the old. They have been doing this since before there were swifts or beavers or anything with bones. The cities of birds and sticks that are turning to stone are being turned to stone by this third city, which is older, invisible, and always the uppermost layer. The swifts build on the fossil of their own ancestors, but the film was there first and will be there last.

In the roofed passages, where fog carries the light only so far, the ceilings are hung with threads. Fungus gnat larvae spin them: fine sticky lines let down from the stone in their dozens, beaded with droplets, each larva lying in a slime tube at the top. A few kinds glow. The glow is faint, a blue so dim that you only see it after your eyes have been in the dark for twenty minutes, and only if you stop looking straight at it. It is not a sky of stars, just a few points, scattered and uncertain, fishing for midges that drift in on the fog.

All of this is life, and all of it is the Sink. But it still does not answer the question, because the question has a shape that none of these fit. Something about the place is missing, a gap in the account, and the gap is this: *what was here before the roof fell?*

## Before the Collapse

The description treats the buried river as a geological fact, a thread the beads are strung on. But the river is old. It has been running under the fell for tens of thousands of years, cold and constant, lime-heavy, through passages that never saw light. The warm, damp air rising in hollows on the upland isn't the Sink's breath. It is the river's breath, and it was rising from those cracks long before the first block let go.

Things lived in that river. They had to, because a river is never empty. The pale, nearly translucent crayfish that come out at dusk to the beaver pools didn't arrive from outside. Nothing outside is that color. They came up from the dark. They are the Sink's evidence that there was a world down there before this one, a world that had already been going for a very long time when the roofs started to come in and flood it with daylight, swifts, and beavers.

And the crayfish were not at the top of it.

## The One in the River

It is long, as long as a person's arm. It is pale the way the crayfish are pale, pinkish-white where the blood runs close under the skin, and in places so thin-skinned that the dark shapes of its organs show through its flanks. It has a salamander's body, or near enough: a long tail flattened for swimming, four limbs that seem too small, with three slender toes on the front and two on the back. At the throat there are gills, feathery and blood-red, the only vivid color on it. They wave slowly in the current like weed.

It has no eyes that work. There are two dark points under the skin of its head where eyes would be, like the memory of eyes. It doesn't need them. Along its head and flanks it carries rows of sense-pits that feel the smallest shift in water pressure: the kick of a crayfish, the settle of a pebble, the change in the current where a drop hits a pool. Its skin tastes the water. It can feel electrical fields, the faint charge of a living muscle moving in the dark.

It hardly eats. Down there food is scarce, so it has learned to be scarcely alive. It can go years without a meal, drawing down on its own body, slowing its heart until it barely beats. It moves at a pace that looks to warm-blooded eyes like stillness. It can live a hundred years or more. Some of the ones in the passages under Hollin Fell were grown before the first recorded collapse. They were in the river when it was entirely a river, a single sealed dark, with no chambers, no fog, no birds.

## What the Collapse Means from Below

From the rim, the Sink is a story of arrival: new ground, instantly colonized, bursting with life. From the river it is a story of loss.

Each collapse is a wound in the dark. When a roof fails, light falls into a stretch of river that has never known it, and the water there becomes a different world. Algae bloom. Trout come upstream. Dippers walk the gravel and take whatever small pale things they find. The beavers arrive and dam it, warming it slightly, slowing it, silting it. Within a few seasons that stretch belongs to the surface, and the pale ones have given it up and pulled back into the passages that are still roofed.

This is why they are found only in the dark parts, the lowest, oldest, and wettest. They were not found there; they were left there. The roofed passages between the open chambers are the last pieces of their country, separated now into islands with broad bright lakes of daylight between them.

And they feel the collapses coming. The deep ticking of stone that a visitor sometimes hears in the passages, almost too low to catch, runs through the water too. It reaches their sense-pits as a steady fine vibration that shifts in character when a block begins to fail. They know the stone the way a sailor knows a hull. When a passage roof starts to go, weeks or months before it falls, they have already left.

## Fog Nights

This is the part hardly anyone has seen, and it makes them more than relicts.

The islands of dark are separated by open chambers, and a creature that lives in the dark should be trapped. Each small population should be sealed into its own stretch of passage, slowly dwindling. They aren't trapped. They travel, and they travel through the fog.

On the coldest still nights, when the heavy air has slid off the fell and filled the Sink, and the fog lies a meter deep along the floor in slow rivers, the chambers become something else. The fog is dark at night. It is saturated, cold, and completely still. For a creature that breathes largely through its skin and would die in a day of dry air, the way the filmy ferns would, this fog is almost water. It is a shallow, soft, lightless sea laid over the stepped pools and moss.

So they come out. They leave the passage mouths and walk, slowly, on their thin limbs, over the moss that is so wet that it drips, along the edges of the beaver pools, beneath the swift walls where forty thousand birds sleep in torpor and never stir. They follow the fog's own gradients, the ones too slight to see, because the fog flows from passage to passage the same way the river does and has the same sense of direction. They drop into pools and swim, and climb out and walk again. They are crossing their lost country, going from dark to dark.

They have to be back before the frost. At dawn the fog is thickest, but the sky is already brightening above the rims. By the time the swifts drop from the walls in their long breath of wings, the pale ones must be under stone again. Sometimes one isn't. A beaver finds it at first light, stiff on a dam crest, coated in frost the way the moss tips are, white on white. Or the peregrine does.

This is the one place the crayfish can't follow them. It is also why there are pale crayfish in the beaver pools at all. Those crayfish are the ones that stayed, adapted to the light badly and bred anyway, and so are slowly turning into something else. The pale ones never stay.

## The Direction of Things

Here is what the tenants don't know and the pale ones do.

The collapse isn't random. The buried river runs in a direction, and the dolines have been opening along it in a direction too, over thousands of years: upstream, or downstream, depending on where the rock is weakest, but steadily one way. The chain of beads is lengthening. The oldest chambers, the ones where the swift walls are thickest and the lowest tiers already stone, are at one end. The rims that still show bright fracture faces, the color of bone, where lichen hasn't reached, are at the other.

The pale ones live just ahead of the collapse. They always have. Their country isn't only the dark; it is the edge of the dark, the stretch of river the Sink hasn't reached yet. Each generation, very slowly, with a century per generation, lays its eggs a little further along, in the deep gravel where the springs rise, beyond the last roof. The Sink is following them across the fell, the way a wave follows something along a beach. It has been following them since before the swifts or beavers came. It will still be following them after the last roofs fall.

When the gorge is complete, in a thousand years, and the swift walls and beaver dams are part of the cliffs, the person running a hand along the stone will feel the cups and branches under the lime. What they won't feel is the thing the Sink was always a record of. There will be no stone salamander in the tufa. Pale things in the dark don't fossilize; they leave nothing. But somewhere past the end of the gorge, under a fell of heather and wind-flattened grass that looks like nothing, there will be a hollow where the air is warmer and damp, and a thin mist rising from cracks in the turf.

That is them breathing. That is who lives at Hollin Fell. It is the one the whole bright crowded city of birds and beavers and moss is chasing, slowly, without knowing it, across the upland. It never makes a sound, it never sees the light, and it is always one roof ahead.
