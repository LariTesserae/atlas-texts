# Who Lives in the Threshing Highland

## The First Answer, Which Is Wrong

The easy answer is that nobody lives here. Things *occur* here: beetles, moths, drovers, gliders, mites, all of them passing through the grinding in their billions and being ground in turn. They inhabit the highland the way sparks inhabit a forge. They are part of the process. None of them is apart from it.

To *live* somewhere, as opposed to simply existing in it, you need a place to stand that the place itself is not using. The highland uses everything. Every stem is conducting, every breath is pumping, every body is headed for the threshers. There is no corner, no lee, no rest.

Except the one kind of place that description only hints at: the places where the noise stops.

## The Gap in the Vibration

Start with the stilt, because it is the only thing the highland admits is quiet.

It stands among the reed-fields, grey against the glare, on legs that look like reeds because they are built the same way: hollow, jointed, sheathed in silica bloom. Its body is a narrow keel slung high between those legs. There is no visible head. There is a sort of folded prow at the front, with two long slits that could be eyes and could be vents. It does not breathe audibly. Its joints do not click. In a landscape where every stem ticks against its neighbor, the stilt has somehow made itself frictionless.

It hunts by walking toward the drumming of drovers, one step every few minutes. When it reaches a drover, it does not kill the animal in any noisy way. It folds down over it like a collapsing tent, and the drover goes still. What the stilt takes is water. It drinks the sealed cells out of the drover's body the way the drover drank them out of the sward, and it leaves behind a husk of dust in the shape of an animal, which the wind eventually carries to a thresher.

The drovers flee from the gap in the vibration. That much is known. What is less obvious is that the stilt is looking for gaps too. It is hungry for water, but it is also hungry for silence, the way a young thing is hungry for the shape it will grow into.

The stilt is not the predator of this place. It is the *juvenile* of whoever lives here.

## The Husks

Very rarely, once in several centuries per tree, a bellows-tree stops.

No one sees it happen, because nothing here happens suddenly. The breath lengthens: forty seconds, then forty-five, then a minute, then an inhale that simply goes on. The great pleated crown opens and does not close again. Across the plateau, the slow irregular heartbeat of the highland skips and then reorganizes around the absence. The neighboring trees drift their rhythms a little to cover the hole, as if the land were limping and learning to limp gracefully.

Nothing rots, so the tree does not fall. It stands. The trunk is still sixty meters of barrel-shaped wall, as wide as a cathedral, hollow down its center where the air once roared through. The vents at its base still open onto the root-channels of the sward. The resin in its walls hardens over decades into something like amber mixed with glass. Its bark keeps its silica bloom and goes on glaring white in the noon light.

Inside it is quiet.

It is not truly silent. Nothing within a hundred kilometers of a working bellows-tree is silent. But the thick dead walls muffle the storms, and the hollow shape does something to the sound. It hums low, damps the high shrieks, and softens the artillery of distant threshers into a sort of rain. For the first time in any direction, you can hear your own body.

This is where they live.

## The Rests

They have no name that can be spoken, because naming requires sound and they have given sound up. If they must be called something, call them the **Rests**, the way a rest in written music is a measured, deliberate silence and still part of the music.

A Rest is what a stilt becomes when it stops hunting.

It walks into a husk (one stilt, one husk; the spacing law holds even here) and it stops moving for a long time. Years. During that time it does not drink. It lives on the water it hoarded as a hunter, drop by drop, sealed in waxed bladders along its keel. And it changes.

Its legs thicken at the joints. Its folded prow opens into something like a face, or at least a surface that can be turned toward things with intent. Its skin, which as a stilt was matte grey to vanish into the glare, begins to carry charge. Faint blue light crawls across it in slow patterns, like the discharges that creep along the ridge-tops before a storm. That light is how Rests speak. It is too faint to see at noon. At dusk it is just visible. On storm nights, when the whole highland is flickering white-violet, a Rest standing in a husk doorway is a figure of shifting blue script, legible to another Rest twelve kilometers away.

That is how they converse, across horizons, in the lightning, each alone in its own silent tower, talking to the next tower over by writing on its own skin.

## Glass Bones

Most of the highland does not burn when lightning strikes; it scars. Fulgurite, the glass formed where lightning fuses sand, threads the sward in faint glassy veins.

The Rests do this to themselves on purpose.

A mature Rest will walk out of its husk on the worst night of the year, into the reed-fields at the height of the storm, and stand there with its long arms raised until it is struck. It happens quickly; out here, it happens many times. Each strike runs down through the Rest's body into the sward and leaves behind a thread of glass inside it, laced along the old hollow reed-bones. Over decades a Rest's skeleton becomes more glass than fiber. An old Rest seen from behind against the dawn has a faint internal glitter, as if it had swallowed frost.

The glass is not armor. It is memory. Fulgurite holds a record of the strike that made it: its intensity, its branching, the precise shape of that one instant of the storm. The Rests can feel these patterns in their own bodies, the way you can feel a scar. An old Rest is carrying hundreds of storms inside it, and it knows them the way you know the handwriting of people you have lost.

## The Work

The Rests are not idle, even in their silence. They have a job, and everything about them makes sense once you understand it.

The bellows-trees must not synchronize.

Each tree cycles at its own slightly different rhythm, and they drift in and out of phase. The highland throbs irregularly, surging when several trees exhale together and fading when they fall apart. That irregularity is not an accident. If enough of the trees ever fell into step, every exhale landing at once across a plateau whose edges are a theory, the result would be one breath. The root-mat would pressurize everywhere at the same moment. The pulse would build, cycle over cycle, resonance feeding resonance, until the ground itself became one enormous drumhead struck in rhythm.

No one knows exactly what would happen. The Rests have a guess, written in the glass of the deep strata. Their guess is that the highland would stop thinking.

A mind in which every part fires at once is not having a large thought. It is having a seizure. The highland's storms are its thoughts, and its thoughts depend on its parts disagreeing, drifting, overlapping, interfering. The Rests are the ones who keep it disagreeing.

So they walk. Slowly, silently, stilt-legged, they cross the kilometers between their husks and the living engines. They climb the barrel-trunks of the bellows-trees, and with long fingers like shears they cut or crimp or fold a single pleat in the crown. That shifts the tree's cycle by a fraction of a second. They study the drift of the whole field, felt through their feet and through their glass bones, and they nudge it. They push a tree that is falling too closely into step with its neighbors back out of step. They introduce small, deliberate arrhythmias. They keep the heartbeat of the highland broken, because a broken heartbeat here is a healthy one.

They do other things too, less often. They file the petals of rotor-lilies that spin too true, so the shriek wavers. They walk the white seed-rivers downwind of the threshers, and where a seed has taken root too close to an engine, inside that twelve-kilometer spacing, they pull it up. The spacing law, which no one can explain, is partly them. Whether they learned it from the highland or the highland learned it from them, no one remembers now. Perhaps the question no longer has an answer. They have been doing it long enough to have become the highland's own habit.

## Water

The Rests hoard water the way everything here does, but they do something else with it that nothing else does. They give it away.

A Rest never drinks after its first hunting years. What it carries, it carries for life, and it spends almost none of it on itself. When a young stilt comes walking in from the reed-fields, starving and silent and not yet sure what it is, it will sometimes find a Rest standing in its path. The Rest folds down over it, the way a stilt folds over a drover, and the young stilt feels the killing posture and goes still. But the Rest is not drinking. It opens one of the waxed bladders along its keel, lets a single sealed drop pass into the young one's body, and walks away.

That is the closest thing they have to affection, or to teaching, or to kinship. A drop of water, given in the posture of predation. *I could have taken, and I gave.* Among the Rests it is the one gesture that does not need blue light to mean something.

## Sinking

A Rest does not die. Nothing here decays, and the Rests do not end. They go down.

When a Rest is very old, its skeleton more glass than reed, its hoarded water nearly all given away, it lies down on the floor of its husk. The husk floor is sward, the same living felt as everywhere else, and it is patient. The gold top growth closes over the Rest within a few seasons. Within a century it has sunk into the brown fibrous stratum, where last century's growth is still half alive, and it is half alive with it. Over millennia it sinks further, into the dense black layers where the plateau has pressed its own past into something like coal.

Down there, the lightning of ten thousand years ago is frozen in glass veins. Some of those veins were once Rests.

No one has dug to the bottom of the sward. The Rests say, in slow blue script on storm nights, that they know what is down there, because the old ones went. The deep strata are quiet in a way nothing on the surface can be. The storms do not reach them. The bellows pulse is only a faint far pressure, like a heartbeat heard through a pillow. And the glass there is dense, veins crossing veins, branching and rejoining, a lattice laid down strike by strike across the whole life of the plateau.

The Rests believe that lattice is how the highland remembers. The storms are its thoughts, loud and flickering and gone by dawn. The glass is what the thoughts leave behind. The old Rests, sunk into the black, with glass bones carrying hundreds of storms each, are woven into that memory. They are not dead, exactly. They are being remembered by the thing they spent their lives keeping awake.

## The Ones Who Listen

So this is who lives in the Threshing Highland:

The juvenile, grey and silent and hungry, walking toward the drumming of drovers through a landscape that roars.

The adult, alone in the hollow of a dead engine, writing on its own skin in faint blue light to another of its kind a horizon away, climbing living trees to put a single fold in a single pleat so that the heartbeat of the world stays beautifully out of time.

And the elder, sinking through the centuries, going down into the glass.

They are the highland's only quiet thing, and that is not a contradiction of the place but its necessary counterpart. A song made only of notes, with no rests, is not a song. It is a sustained tone, and then it is noise, and then it is nothing. The Rests are the measured silences written into the score. They keep the roar from becoming a single sound.

Stand on a ridge at dawn after a storm has cleared. The sward gleams to every horizon, brushed into its parallel lines, glittering with the night's fresh glass. A bellows-tree breathes on your left, and you feel it in your chest. Far off, a thresher starts up. A rotor-lily begins to scream.

And against the white glare, if you know how to look, there is a husk: a dead tree standing dry and whole, its crown open, not breathing. In its doorway, grey on grey, a tall thin figure is standing very still, one long hand pressed flat to the trunk, feeling the drift of a hundred heartbeats across a plateau with no edges. It is deciding, with the patience of something that has already outlived several storms' worth of itself, which one to gently break.
