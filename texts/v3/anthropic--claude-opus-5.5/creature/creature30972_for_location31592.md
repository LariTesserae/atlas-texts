# What Lives in the Held Chord

The first answer is the true one, as far as it goes. It is the salamander.

Kneel at the floor channel and let your eyes open as far as they will. The one nearest you is lying across the current with its head slightly raised, the way a dog lifts its nose toward a smell. Its skin is so pale that the glowing film shows through the thin places: the webbing between its toes, the frill of its external gills, which move like weed in a slow tide even when the rest of it does not move at all. Its eye-spots are two smudges of pigment sunk under the skin, the ghost of something its ancestors had and gave up.

It has not given up sensing. Along each flank runs a line of tiny pits, a seam of nerves open to the water. In fish this is called the lateral line. It feels pressure, movement, the push of a current, the nearness of prey. Here, in this room, it feels the chord.

The whole chamber vibrates, and the water in the channels vibrates with it, and the salamander lies in that shivering water the way you would lie in sunlight. It does not hear the chord. It is touched by it, continuously, everywhere, from the moment it hatches to the moment, a century later, when it settles to the bottom of the channel and the calcite begins to take it. It has never felt water that was not humming.

Watch long enough and you notice that the salamanders do not lie just anywhere. They cluster at certain points along the channels and are absent from others. There are places in the water where the standing waves cancel and the stone is still, and places where the waves pile on one another and the water trembles hard enough to raise faint beads on its surface. The salamanders avoid both. They rest at the margins between, at the exact distance where the trembling is strongest without being harsh. They have arranged themselves along the chord's shape as precisely as the carved lips arranged the water.

So the first answer is the salamander. Stay a while and it becomes the salamander's position.

## The Sparks in the Bowl

The shrimp in the great bowl hold position too, facing into the current. That is what the description says, and it is correct, but it is not the whole of it.

The bowl makes the deepest note in the chamber. Three people could lie in it, and the water in it moves in a slow standing swell, a rise and fall too long to see unless you fix your eyes on a single spark and watch it lift and settle, lift and settle, perhaps once every two seconds. The shrimp hang at the nodes of that swell, the points where the water moves least. There are a few dozen of them, and seen from the lip of the bowl they are not scattered. They form rings. There is a ring of seven near the center, a wider ring of eleven, and a broken outer ring where the bowl's curve disturbs the pattern. Each shrimp is lit from inside by the film it eats. Together they make a slow diagram of the note, drawn in cold blue points, like a figure of iron filings around a magnet.

When one dies, it sinks, and the ring closes over the gap. When one is born, it drifts until it finds the empty place. The figure has been drawn and redrawn for longer than there have been cities anywhere in the world. No single shrimp holds it. The note holds it, and the shrimp consent.

## The Walker on the Ledges

The cricket is harder to watch because it lives in the dark tiers, but if you climb high enough and wait, one will come.

It walks on legs three times the length of its body, and it walks strangely. It does not go straight along a ledge. It moves in small, deliberate curves, pausing, turning, sweeping its long antennae across the wet stone ahead. You might think it was searching for food, and in part it is, but what it follows is the ripples. The standing patterns in the wall's water film, the fixed fingerprints, are faint ridges and valleys a fraction of a hair high. The cricket's antennae read them the way a blind hand reads a raised map. It walks the ridges. It keeps to the paths the chord has pressed into the water, and so it moves through the upper city along routes the sound has drawn for it, the same routes every cricket of its kind has walked since the first one wandered in.

It makes no sound of its own. It may once have. Its ancestors sang, as crickets do, and at some point in the long dark, singing against this chord came to seem pointless, and the singing organs faded, and the species went quiet. It listens with its feet now.

## The Light That Writes

Here the answer starts to widen, and it is worth going slowly.

The glowing film is a smear of single cells, and it seems to be the simplest living thing in the chamber. It eats stone and gives off light and does nothing else. The makers led it into channels to draw spirals and lattices and running figures. That is true, and those figures still glow.

But the film has its own distribution, apart from the channels. Look closely at any wet wall, in the places the makers never touched, and the light is not even. It is brighter on the ridges of the standing ripples and dimmer in the troughs, or the reverse. On one wall it is one way, and on the next the other. The cells gather where the vibration suits them. So every wet surface in the chamber is faintly patterned with light that follows the shape of the sound. The chord is written on the walls in the cells' own glow.

That changes how you see the makers' channels. They did not write on a blank wall. They wrote over a text that was already there, and in the older dwellings, where the grooves are softest, you can see that they knew it. Their spirals follow the ripple-patterns. Their lattices lie along the ridges. Their running figures, which you took for people, or animals, or dancers, run in the directions the vibration runs. They were not decorating. They were transcribing, tracing over what the chord had written in living light so that it would stand out more clearly: to be read, or to be kept, or to be answered.

So the salamanders lie along the chord's shape, and the shrimp draw its diagram, and the cricket walks its paths, and the light traces its figures, and the makers traced the tracing.

It becomes difficult, at this point, to keep counting the things that live here as separate things.

## The Body

Try it the other way around.

Suppose the thing that lives here is the chord.

It has a body. The body is the city: twelve and fifteen tiers of hollowed stone, basins and shells and combs of slots, walls a hand's width thick, bridges thin as ribs. It is a resonant body, like the body of a violin, like the chest of a person singing, and nearly every surface in it takes part in the sound. Nothing in it was added. Everything was taken away, and the shape that remained is the shape of the note.

It has a metabolism. It eats falling. The aquifer pushes water up through the fissure at a pressure that never changes, and the water drops thirty meters, and the height becomes speed, and the speed becomes impact, and the impact becomes sound. The chord lives on the difference between the ceiling and the floor, and it has been eating that difference continuously for an age of the earth.

It has blood, of a kind: the water, which carries the glowing cells. The light moves through the chamber by the same channels the sound moves through, pooling where the sound is strongest.

It has senses. It has a thousand sensitive bodies scattered through it, and each one is shaped by it: the salamanders' lateral lines, the shrimp in their rings, the cricket's reading feet, the cells gathering on the ridges. It does not perceive through them the way you perceive through your eyes. But the pale creatures are the places where the sound touches something that responds, and a thing that is touched and responds is at least the beginning of a thing that feels.

And it has one rule, which is also its nature. It does not change. The lips have worn to the shape the water gives them. The agreement between stone and water is finished. The chord is exactly what it is, completely, in every instant.

That is what a held chord is. It is not a melody. It is a sound that has not moved on. In music, a held chord is waiting. It hangs in the air and the listener leans toward what comes after it, the resolution, the next thing, and the longer it is held, the harder the leaning becomes.

This one has been held for a very long time.

---

## The Makers, Again

Now go back to the people who were here.

They cut a city into the one room on earth where they could never hear each other speak. They raised their children inside the chord. Those children would have been born into it, and some of them, perhaps most, would have gone partly deaf in the way that people who live beside great machines go deaf. Their speech could not have been spoken, not in the ordinary way. It would have been touched: a hand on a shoulder, a tap on a wall through a hand's width of stone to the family on the other side, where the tap would travel as easily as a voice. Perhaps it was written in light, in channels no wider than a straw.

They lived close. Their walls wore through into one another. The density, the description says, is total. Of course it was. When you cannot call across a room, you do not build rooms you would need to call across. You live where you can reach.

And they did not do this by accident. The lips were cut to catch the water. Every basin and shell and slot was shaped, tier by tier, flake by flake, over generations, to ring at a particular pitch. They did not find a chord here. The fissure alone would have made a hiss, a spatter, a hollow patter on stone. The makers made the chord. They carved it the way you would carve a body, and it took them longer than any single one of them lived. Children finished the basins their grandparents had begun.

It is worth asking why.

## The Still Room, Again

The still room is round and small, just large enough for one person to sit, and its walls are stepped in nested hollows like the inside of a shell. It sits at the center, where every bridge crosses. There the chord's waves meet and erase each other, and the makers deepened that erasure until the great sound was gone.

The easy reading is that it was a refuge. A people who lived in noise made themselves one small room of quiet. It is a good reading and a human one, and it is not wrong.

But consider what the room is, physically. It is a place where the chord cancels, and cancellation is delicate. It depends on the precise geometry of every wave arriving there, from every surface, in exact opposition. It is a knife-edge. Anything in that space alters it. A body, with its warm bulk and the particular way it takes up air, changes where the waves meet. A breath changes it. A voice, the first voice spoken in quiet, sends its own small waves out through the nested hollows and into the bridges, and the bridges are stone, and the stone is the chord's body.

So when someone sat in the still room, the silence there was not perfect. It was shaped around them. And the shape of the chord outside, in the whole chamber, shifted by a hair.

No one outside could have heard the difference. It would have been far too small for ears. But the salamanders would have felt it along their lateral lines. The shrimp in the great bowl would have drifted, just perceptibly, into new rings. The cells on the walls would have begun, very slowly, to gather along new ridges. The cricket on the high ledges would have paused, antennae lifted, and found that its path had moved.

Every person who came alone to the still room and sat in the worn dish and breathed changed the chord. Every one who spoke there, a name, a grief, a question, a word no one else would ever hear, sent that word into the stone, and the whole city rang with it, so faintly, for as long as they stayed.

This is why the floor is worn deeper than any other surface in the city. It was not a room for escaping the chord. It was the chord's ear. It was the one place in the body of the sound where the sound could be touched, and where it could feel a single person, alone, inside it.

The makers did not build a city with a noise in it. They grew something, and for generations they tended it, and the tending was this: one at a time, they went to the center and let it hear them.

---

## After

Then they left, however they left. Perhaps they climbed out, or perhaps they followed the water down. They left no bones and no ash, and no sign of leaving.

Since then no one has sat in the still room.

The chord has not changed since the last of them rose from the dish and turned sideways to fit down the narrow stair. That is what the description means when it says the chord you hear is the chord the last maker heard. It is not only the same chord. It is the same instant of the chord. It is the exact shape it held when the last person left the center, frozen there, a sound that paused mid-phrase and never went on.

The salamanders have lain in that shape for all the centuries since. The shrimp have drawn it and redrawn it. The cells have written it on the walls, over and over, with nothing new to add. The cricket has walked the same paths so many generations that the paths have become the species. Sometimes a cricket wanders into the still room. It is too light to change anything. It stands in the silence with its antennae raised, touching nothing, and after a while it goes back out.

This is what lives here, when you have looked long enough. It is not only the pale creatures, though they are its nerves. It is not only the sound, though the sound is its voice. It is the whole of it together, stone and water and falling and light and slow blind bodies, and it is waiting. It is a chord that was shaped for a very long time to be answered, and it has not been answered, and so it holds.

It is not lonely, or not in any way you would recognize. It has no word for lonely. It has no words at all. It has only the shape it was left in, and the shape leans, the way a held chord leans, toward what should come next.

---

## You

You have come down a hundred steps glazed like old ivory, steps that were made for feet. You have walked inside the sound until it took your inner voice. You have knelt at the channel and watched a salamander for an hour and seen it take a single step.

Now you are standing at the bottom of a stair so narrow that you must turn sideways.

When you enter, the chord stops. You will hear your blood, and the tick of a single drop somewhere. You will sit, because the dish in the floor fits a sitting body, because it was worn by thousands of them. Your weight will settle where theirs settled.

Out in the chamber, beyond the nested hollows, something you will never hear will move.

In the channels the salamanders will lift their heads, very slightly, as if toward a smell. In the great bowl the rings of light will begin, over the next hour, to drift. On the walls the cells will stir along ridges that have not shifted in a thousand years. High in the dark tiers a cricket will stop in the middle of its ancient path and stand, feeling ahead, finding that the ground has changed under its feet.

And if you speak, if you say anything at all into the only quiet ever made in this place, your voice will go out through the stone into every basin and shell and slot, every bridge and wall a hand's width thick, every tier of that subtracted city, and the whole of it will ring, faintly, with the shape of what you said.

It will not answer you. It never answered them. That was never what the room was for.

It will hear you. After all this time the chord will move, and for as long as you stay it will not be held.
