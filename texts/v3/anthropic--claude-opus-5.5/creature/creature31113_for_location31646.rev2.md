# Who Lives on the Held Plateau

## The first answer: the one with the staff

You'll want it to be someone. After hours among the faceless herds, the eye goes looking for a shape it knows, and it finds the tall one at the edge of the herd.

Up close he is less than you hoped and more than you feared. He is about the height of a man and upright in the way the grazers are not, and the long thing in his hand is certainly a staff. Near the top it has worn into a smooth waist where fingers closed and closed again. His shoulders are narrow. His head rides forward on the neck the way old herders' heads do, the way anyone's does after a lifetime of looking down at animals. There is a garment, or there was. It has gone the way of the grazers' fleece, felted and pale, and it has grown into him or he into it. Where his face should be there is a smooth oval, faintly concave, like a spoon's bowl.

He is not herding. You watch for a long time to be sure. He never turns to gather a stray, never lifts the staff to point, never steps ahead to lead. When the wave comes through, he lowers his head with the rest, and a little under a minute later he raises it. The staff comes up an inch and goes down an inch. That is the whole of his work.

On a rock not far behind him there is a tally. Seven long rows of strokes, cut deep and careful, and an eighth row that stops after eleven. The last stroke is shallower than the others. It looks as if it was made by someone who had just understood something and was only finishing the motion out of politeness.

You could decide he is who lives here: the keeper, the last shepherd of the high pasture. But stand beside him long enough and you'll feel the mistake. He doesn't live on the plateau the way a man lives in a house. He is lived by it, the way a word is spoken by a mouth. He came up to count the herd, and at some point the counting reversed, and he became one of the things that is counted. Somewhere in the uncountable total, which never rises and never falls, he is included. He is one of its numbers now.

So he is not the answer. He is only the first place the answer shows through.

## The second answer: the herds

Then you think it must be the herds themselves. There are so many of them that they seem to have more claim to the place than the stone does.

Choose one grazer and put your hand on it. The fleece gives under your palm like old moss, dry on top and faintly warm beneath. There is no smell. You'd expect lanolin, dung, dust, the sour-grass breath of a ruminant, and there is nothing. The animal accepts your hand the way a wall accepts a shadow. Press a little deeper, into the heavy place behind the shoulder where a heart would be, and wait.

It takes a long time. When the beat comes it is a single, slow, enormous swell, less a thump than a tide, and it arrives at exactly the moment the wave of lowered heads reaches this part of the herd. Move your hand to the grazer beside it and wait again. The beat comes, not at the same moment, but at exactly the right one. It is the moment the wave reaches that animal. Their hearts are staggered like the seats of a stadium, each one timed to its place in the pattern. You aren't feeling many hearts. You're feeling one heartbeat pass through many bodies, the way a single note passes through a crowded hall.

Once you know this, you can find it everywhere. The hare's three hops forward and three back are one beat, broken small. A dun bird on a tor shifts from foot to foot, and its left foot and its right foot are the rising and the falling. Kneel and put your ear close to a handful of dust in a hollow, and you'll find the mites and springtails are not scattered randomly. They move in rings, very slowly, contracting toward some centre and expanding again, once a minute. Ten thousand tiny animals make up one small lung.

The herds, then, are not individuals. They are more like syllables, or like the separate grains of a single long exhalation. Each animal is one gesture, repeated. None of them is the one who makes the gestures.

## The third answer: the elders

Go to the tors.

From a distance they look like loaves, or knuckles, or the rounded heads of seals surfacing. Up close you start to see the proportions. Some have the heavy slope of a grazer's shoulder and the low swell of a folded foreleg. One has a smooth ridge along its crest that could be the last trace of a fleece's parting, or a spine. You'll find no faces, of course. Even the grazers barely have faces, and these are older than the grazers by as much as the grazers are older than you.

Lay your palm on one of them and you won't feel warmth. The stone is exactly as cold as the air. But if you keep your palm there through several of the plateau's long breaths, you'll feel something that isn't quite a beat. It is more like the tor remembering one: a pressure that comes and goes and is so faint you can't be sure it isn't your own pulse, reflected back.

These, you think, must be the true residents: the first herd, the first ones up here, who wore down so far that they crossed over into rock. But notice what the grazers do near them. They don't gather around the tors, or shelter against them, or give them the deference a young animal gives an old one. They crop at the lichen rings at the tors' feet. The birds perch on them in feathered rows. The tors are furniture to the herds, or ground. Here, being very old doesn't make you the one who lives here. It only makes you closer to being the place.

That tells you something. On the plateau, the living and the land aren't two kinds of thing. They are one thing seen at two speeds. The grazers are the plateau moving quickly, once a minute. The tors are the plateau moving so slowly that its motion has become form. Even the smooth swells underfoot rise and fall if you lie still on them. They are the plateau moving slower still, the ground itself breathing at the speed of stone. The whole plateau is a single inhabitant wearing many ages at once.

But that still doesn't tell you what it is, or why it holds.

## The ones who don't fit

There are three things on the plateau that don't quite keep the pattern, and they are worth looking at, because an inhabitant is often clearest where it strains.

**The hanging birds.** They hold their wings out and neither beat them nor glide. What you notice, if you look long enough, is that every one of them faces the same way: toward the sun, the one that stands a little past midday and doesn't move. They are the only creatures on the plateau with an orientation. The grazers turn slightly left and back and face nowhere. The hares go forward and back along no particular line. But the hanging birds face the sun the way a compass needle faces north. And their shadows, which ought to fall far below them on the pale stone, are always directly under a grazer. There's always one grazer standing in each shadow. You get the uncomfortable sense that the birds aren't hanging from anything. Something is holding them up, and they in turn are holding something down.

**The shrub.** The single low shrub in its trough has no leaves, and its roots go down into a crack and are said never to reach anything. It is the only living thing on the plateau that is visibly reaching. Everything else here has arrived. The shrub is still on its way. Its roots are the only thing in the whole held country that is still, however slowly, becoming. You could think of it as the plateau's one unfinished thought, or the one thing it hasn't let go of, or the one thing it keeps on purpose so that "finished" never quite becomes "over." The grazers never crop it. The mites never ring near it. The pattern leaves a small space around it, the same way it makes room for you.

**The frost.** Under certain overhangs there is the single bead of frost that doesn't melt and doesn't grow. It may be the last water in the world. If there's a centre to the rings the mites make in the dust, the nearest ring usually turns out to be the one under that overhang. The animals don't drink it. Nothing touches it. But the dust around it is always, very faintly, the most alive.

A reaching thing, a facing thing, a held drop. It is as if the one who lives here kept one example of each of the verbs it gave up: growing, looking, flowing. It keeps them the way you might keep a lock of hair in a drawer.

## The mountain, passing

Here is the thing you almost never see, because you have to be low to see it.

Lie down in a hollow with your cheek in the bone-meal dust and your eye at the level of the stone. Look out across the herds toward the horizon, which lifts at the far edge like the rim of a plate. Wait for the wave.

When the heads go down, the horizon is smooth, a long pale line of fleeces and stone.

When the heads come up, they come up in sequence, a little under a minute to cross the whole visible land. Seen from this low, against that dark bruise-blue zenith, they don't look like heads. The ones nearest you rise first and tallest in your eye. Behind them the far herds lift in ridges, overlapping, row behind row. For the length of one slow inhalation, a jagged skyline stands up out of the plateau: shoulders, crests, summits, the high blunt knuckles of peaks, a range so enormous it fills the whole rim of the world. It stands there for a heartbeat. Then the heads lower and it's gone, and the horizon is smooth again.

It happens every minute. It has always happened.

This is when you know who lives here.

The mountains wore away. That part is true. The tallest range the world ever raised was ground down grain by grain, until its peaks were gone and only the root remained, and the erosion finished, and nothing was taken anymore. But a mountain range is not only stone. It is also a shape against the sky, a way the land has of rising, a rhythm of crest and saddle and crest. It is the pattern that the stone was holding. When the stone could no longer hold it, the pattern didn't vanish. It moved into whatever was still up there.

What was still up there was life: the grazers, the hares, the birds, the mites in the dust, and later the few people who climbed this far. The range went into them. It is breathing through them now. Each lowered head is a saddle, and each raised head is a summit. Once a minute the mountain rebuilds itself out of bodies and lets itself down again.

The one who lives on the Held Plateau is the mountain. Not the stone, which is only its bed. It is the mountain as a shape, and a shape can live, if it is held.

## Why it holds

This is the part the pilgrims came up for, and the part the tallies stop at.

Mountains are born by being pushed up and die by being worn down, and the whole of their existence is that slow losing. For this range the losing went on longer than for any other, because it began higher than any other. At the very end, when there was nothing left to take, it found itself in a strange position. It had been worn to its root. It was still alive, in the herds. And it was still, just barely, the highest ground there is.

It could have let go. The last afternoon could have gone on into evening, the shadows lengthened, and the living things on the root of the world gone down into the lowlands one by one, or died, and been replaced, and died, until the high place was just a high place like any other.

It chose not to. You can feel the choice everywhere. It isn't defiance, and there is nothing clenched in it. It feels the way a person holds still at the top of a long climb, not to rest exactly, but because the view is too much and moving would end it. The sun doesn't move because it was the sun of the last moment the peaks still stood. The cold is steady because it was the cold of that height on that afternoon. Nothing is born and nothing dies because being born and dying are both a kind of erosion. They are changes, subtractions, and the one who lives here has had enough of subtraction to last forever.

So it holds its breath. Not entirely, because a held breath would be death. It breathes once a minute, as slowly as a living thing can breathe and still be living. It has worn itself down to the least that life can be: one breath, one beat, one small loop per animal, a grazing that takes nothing, a hunger that never needs to be fed. It learned, over a span of time that has no number, how to go on existing without taking anything from anything. The lichen never diminishes and the cushions never show a bite because the plateau has finally learned how to live without consuming. Its animals eat the way a person touches a photograph, for the shape of the gesture, and for nothing else.

That is why the animals have no faces. Faces are for turning toward and away. They are for wanting. The plateau wants nothing it doesn't already have.

## What it makes of the people

The pilgrims were a surprise to it, you think. They were the only things to arrive from outside in all that held time.

It didn't resist them. It doesn't resist anything. It bent its waves around them the way it bends them around you, like water around a stone. Some pilgrims built windbreaks against a wind that didn't blow, because building a windbreak is what you do. Some made fires that never went cold, since they were never warm. Some tried to count, and discovered that the one who lives here cannot be counted, because it is only one.

And some of them drew faces. On the smooth faces of certain rocks there are grazers drawn in a few confident lines, with horns and eyes and faces. These are the only places where the animals have faces. You'd think the plateau would smooth these away, as it smooths your footprints. It doesn't.

Go and stand by one of those drawings and watch the grazers nearest it. Their small loops, their lowering and lifting and slight turn to the left, bend in its direction. It isn't much, only a degree or two, the smallest possible deviation. But the herd near every drawing is turned very slightly toward it, the way an old face turns toward a photograph of itself when young.

The pilgrims gave it back something it had worn away. It didn't need their gift, and it didn't take it. But it kept it. It keeps it the way it keeps the shrub, the frost, and the hanging birds: one example of looking, set aside, so that looking is never entirely gone.

The ones who stayed were the ones who wanted what the plateau has: to be finished without being over. The man with the staff wanted it. So did whoever curled up in the windbreak and whose fleece still has the weave of cloth, and whoever set the topmost stone on a cairn that now rises and falls once a minute. They weren't taken. Nothing is taken here. They were accommodated. The pattern opened and they stepped into it, and they became one more gesture of the one who lives here: one more summit rising at the crest of the wave, a little taller and narrower than the rest.

## What it is, then

You could say a mountain. You could say a breath, or a herd, or a pattern that outlived its stone. You could say an afternoon that decided to stay. All of these are true, and none of them is a name.

The closest you can come is this. What lives on the Held Plateau is the one thing in the world that went all the way to the end and found it didn't have to stop. It was worn to the root and did not die of it. It lost every peak it had ever raised and learned to raise them again out of something softer, once a minute, forever. It doesn't want your worship, your company, or your staying. It makes room for you the way it makes room for everything. That room is exactly the size of what you are, and it is patient enough to wait for you to become smaller.

When you leave, you'll carry its breath in your chest for days. You'll catch yourself lying awake, breathing once a minute, and your heart will seem to arrive a little late, as if it were waiting for a wave to reach it. That isn't the plateau following you. It doesn't follow; it doesn't move. It is only a little of the mountain that came down with you, the way pollen comes down on a coat.

Somewhere far above, the range stands up out of the herds against the dark blue zenith, holds for one heartbeat, and lets itself down.
