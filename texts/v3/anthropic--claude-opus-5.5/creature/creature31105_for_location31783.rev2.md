# Who Lives on the Hammer Moor

## First, the Honest Answer

Nobody lives here the way a farmer lives on a farm. Nobody owns the pumps, nobody stokes the vents, and nobody collects the cable. If you came looking for the keeper of the works, some foreman in a hut beside the biggest vent with a logbook and a lamp, you will not find one.

But the moor is not empty of residents. It is thick with them. They are simply not in charge, and most of them are not separate from the thing they live on. Every tenant here is also partly a piece of the building.

So I will start with the small ones and work up, and I will warn you now that the answer changes on the way.

## In the Mat

Kneel down, part the sphagnum with your fingers, and look into the wet dark between the living layer and the dead one. There is a whole town in there.

The spiders that ride the swells on silk are the ones you notice first, because they are the ones that show themselves. Below them are the lodgers. There are pale mites that live their entire lives inside a single moss stem, and caddis larvae that build their cases from the red sphagnum and the gold, so that their houses are striped like barber's poles. There are threadworms that move in the pressure of each swell. They are too weak to swim, so they wait for the wave and let it carry them a few centimeters, then wait again. Over a lifetime a threadworm might travel the width of your hand. It rides the pumps the way a commuter rides the train.

Deeper down, where the moss is brown and compressed and no longer quite alive, live the fermenters. They are not animals. They are slow colonies of something between fungus and bacterium, and they are what make the peat hot. They are the reason there is steam on the plateau at all. You could argue that they are the moor's true furnace-men, if you wanted a foreman after all: trillions of them, working in the dark on a shift that has not changed in ten million years, eating the dead moss and breathing out heat.

Nobody sees them. You feel them only as warmth coming up through your knees if you kneel too long near a vent.

## The Stokers

The vents do not move at random.

In the hottest peat, where the mat runs close to boiling, there are animals. They are pale, blind, and long, something like a salamander and something like an eel, as long as a rowing boat and as thick as a man's thigh. The hammerfolk (I will come to them) call them stokers. You will almost never see one. They live in the scalding channels under each vent, and their bodies are soft and white and rubbery. Their skin is so thin that you can see, through it, the red of the blood working hard to shed heat.

A stoker is the vent's throat. It keeps the channel open by moving through it, grazing the fermenter colonies off the walls, breathing in the hot, sour water and breathing it out again. The shriek-and-whistle of a vent is partly steam and partly the stoker, a long reedy exhalation from something buried and enormous. Listen near a vent for long enough and you will notice the whistle has a rhythm that is not the pump-rhythm. It is slower and more irregular, the rhythm of lungs.

When a stoker dies, its channel silts and chokes, and the vent goes out. That is why vents die. And when a young stoker, grown large enough, leaves its parent's channel and burrows off through the deep peat, it eventually surfaces a kilometer away and opens a new one. That is the scalding eruption, the dead ring of cooked moss, and then the red rank thriving. The column that leans in the wind is the breath of one animal. The columns stand miles apart because stokers will not share heat.

They are the closest thing the moor has to a hearth. The hammerfolk treat them the way other people treat fire: carefully, with gratitude, and never too close.

## The Eels, Properly

The eels in the tarns are not ordinary eels. I think by now you suspected that.

They are the source of the current. The chemistry of acid water against alkaline moss is real, and it helps, but it is not enough by itself to walk blue sparks across a tarn. The rest comes from the eels. Their long black bodies are stacked with cells like the plates of a battery, and they discharge them together, hundreds of eels in a single pool, all in the same instant. That is the crackle. That is the ozone and the hair lifting on your arms. That is the low mains-drone on the bad nights, when every eel on the plateau is holding the same charge at the same pitch.

They live for centuries, and they grow slowly. The oldest are thick as a fence post, and their skin has gone the dull matte black of the jelly layer deep in the mat. When a tarn drains and they cross the open moss, they are not fleeing. They know where they are going. They have made the crossing before, many times, over lifetimes that are longer than most human histories. Each eel keeps a map of every tarn it has ever lived in, and the map includes tarns that have not existed for four hundred years. Sometimes a migration heads confidently across the moss toward a pool that is not there. The eels arrive at the empty place and wait. Often, within a day or so, water wells up under them and the tarn returns. No one knows whether they remember the future or cause it.

Some of the oldest eels, the hammerfolk say, do not stay in the tarns at all. They go down. They find the drainage channels in the deep mat and follow them to the bottom, into the dark jelly where the plant has become almost oil, and they live there in the black and the pressure, where time is packed as tightly as the peat. Nobody knows what they eat. Nobody knows what they see. But the charge in the tarns above the deepest channels is always strongest, as though something below is feeding it.

## What the Frogs Are Saying

The frogs near the charged tarns really are sending something. Their clipped calls fall into patterns and repeat. They are relaying.

The eels speak in current. A frog in a charged pool feels each discharge through its wet skin, a twitch of the muscles, and it calls in answer. The frog in the next pool hears the call and calls in turn, and so the pattern travels across the plateau, from tarn to tarn, frog to frog. It is a telegraph line of amphibians, tens of thousands strong, carrying pulses from one colony of eels to the next.

The hammerfolk know what the message is, more or less. They say it is a tally.

The moor is taking stock. Every burst and every flood, every vent opened and every vent choked, every tarn drained and every tarn refilled goes into the count, pulsed out from the deep eels to the shallow ones, relayed by the frogs, and heard across the whole plateau. It is the ledger of a factory that has no owner. It is inventory. It has been kept continuously, they say, since the mountains were young, and it has never once been balanced, because the moor makes and unmakes things faster than the frogs can call.

When you hear the telegraph-rattle at dusk and swear the frogs are sending something, you are right. They are reading the accounts aloud.

## The Grazers, Properly

I said the grazers were always in the distance. That is not because they are shy.

They walk the old valleys. Watch a herd for a day, which takes patience and field glasses, and you will see that their route is not random. They move along lines that bend for no reason on flat ground, and they skirt places where nothing is. They climb very slightly and descend very slightly on a plateau so level that water cannot choose a direction. They are following the contour lines of the ghost mountains. They are grazing the floors of valleys that were ground away a hundred million years ago, keeping to the ancient passes between peaks that exist now only as shadow and cold.

That is why they never startle. Nothing that walks the old routes has ever had a reason to. They are keeping an appointment that was made long before there were predators on this continent, and they are keeping it on time.

Their coats are matted with moss, and the moss is alive. It is not just stuck to them. It grows in them, rooted in the thick undercoat, fed by their warmth and their breath. A grazer is a walking piece of the moor, and when it moves it carries the moor with it. Spores drop from its flanks along the old valley floors. The moss on a grazer's back is often a different species from the moss around it, carried from somewhere a hundred kilometers off.

When a grazer grows old, it lies down. It does not look for shelter. It simply stops on the route, folds its legs, and lies down on the mat. Within a season the moss on its back and the moss underneath it have grown together, and you cannot tell where the animal was. There is a low mound, redder than the moss around it, and then after a few years there is not even that. The grazers do not have graves. They have places where the moor got a little deeper.

## The Hammerfolk

And there are people.

Not many. You could walk the plateau for a month and not see one, and if you did see one, you might not know it. They look, at a distance, like a grazer standing upright, or a lichen-covered stone, or one of the ghost mountain's shadows moving slightly in the fog.

Outsiders call them the hammerfolk, after the thud of the pumps. I do not know what they call themselves. Their word for their own kind, as far as anyone has managed to write it down, is the same as their word for *on the beat*.

Their skin is rust-stained from the peat water. It never washes out, and over a lifetime it deepens from ochre to a dark red-brown like tanned leather, like the bog-bodies that sometimes surface in lesser mires. They are short, broad, and slow, built for cold and for holding still. Their hair is thick and matted, and like the grazers they let the moss grow in it. An old one's head is a small green-gold garden. Their eyes are pale, adapted to the sourceless shadowless light, and in the rare breaks when the cloud lifts and actual sunlight falls on the plateau they squint and cover their faces and look deeply unhappy until it passes.

The first thing you notice about them, if you ever get close, is how they walk. They walk on the beat. They step with the swells, lifting a foot as the wave passes under and setting it down as the thud arrives, so that they never sink and never stumble and never splash. Where two rhythms cross and the ground bucks, they already know it is coming. They sidestep the spout before it rises. Put a hammerfolk child and a surveyor side by side on the moor and the child will be across the bog-burst before the surveyor has finished realizing it is a bog-burst.

The second thing you notice is that their hearts beat in time with the pumps. Not metaphorically. Put your hand on a hammerfolk's chest, if one will let you, and feel the heart, then feel the ground. They are the same. When the nearest swell-rhythm changes, the heart changes to match. Their babies, they say, are born when a swell passes under the mother, and the first thud after birth sets the child's heart going. A child born in a quiet place, away from any rhythm, is sickly and anxious all its life. There are very few quiet places on the Hammer Moor.

## Where They Sleep

They sleep inside the bobbins.

The granite knuckles that the wind has wound in sedge cable, wound so thickly they look like spools, are not solid under the fiber. The granite has weathered at the center. The old summit stumps are hollow in places, rotten-cored, and over centuries the cable has built up around them hundreds of layers deep. It is insulation such as no builder ever made: pale, dense, woven by the gales, dry at the heart even on this sodden plateau. The hammerfolk tunnel in through the cable, careful not to cut it, working the fibers apart with their fingers and binding them back behind them, and they make their homes in the hollow granite at the core.

Inside a bobbin it is dark and dry and nearly silent. The grinding of the mountains carries up through the stone as a hum you feel in your back when you lie down. It is warm, not hot, warmed by the bodies of whoever sleeps there and held by all that wound fiber. It smells of dry grass and lichen and people. It is the only dry place on the moor, and they share it with the moor's other refugees: snipe roosting in the outer layers of the cable, slugs sleeping in the damp seams, sometimes a young grazer that has wandered in during a gale and will be let stay until morning.

So they do use the cable, in a way. They live inside it. But they never cut it, and they never take it away. They say it is not for them. They say they are only lodging in it until whoever it is for arrives.

When you ask who that is, they look at the fog, where the ghost peaks are, and do not answer.

## How They Live

They eat what the swells throw up: shrimp, larvae, worms caught in the jets as the wave passes, snatched out of the air with the same quick hand the plover uses with its bill. They milk the grazers, who tolerate this the way they tolerate everything. They eat the green-gold slugs, raw, in spring. They almost never eat eels. They will eat one found dead on a crossing, with ceremony, but they will not kill one. They say it would be like tearing a page out of the ledger.

They do not farm. You cannot farm a thing that pumps. They do not build, beyond the tunnels in the bobbins. They make almost nothing. Their tools are what the moor already offers: a length of cable-fiber unraveled from the loose end of a bobbin, a stone knuckle-flake, a stoker's shed skin, stretched and dried for a water-bag.

They speak between beats. Their language is spoken in phrases that fit between one thud and the next, and a long speech is a series of these, measured out in swells. A hammerfolk telling a story near a fast rhythm talks in quick bursts, and near a slow rhythm talks in long, heavy, unhurried lines. Many of their words are borrowed from the frogs. They can read the telegraph. Some of the old ones can send on it. They sit at the edge of a charged tarn with their hair standing up and their feet in the water and call, in clipped bursts, and far off across the plateau the frogs pick it up and carry it on.

Their dead they give to the bog-bursts. When the mat splits and the black river of peat pours out, steaming, they carry the body down to it and lay it on the flow and let it go. It travels a kilometer, slowly, turning, sinking, until the burst stops. Within days the scar is moss again, and the person is somewhere under it, and the moor is a little deeper there. They visit the scars afterward. They put their hands flat on the moss and wait for the swell to pass under, and they say that sometimes, for the half-second on the crest, they see the person again, as they were at another age.

## Why They Climb

Here is the thing about the hammerfolk that makes them not quite like other people.

They climb the ghost mountains.

Nothing else does. The birds bend around the peaks. The grazers walk the valleys between them but never go up. Outsiders who try come back within a hundred meters with pounding hearts and burning calves and popping ears, frightened in a way they cannot explain, having climbed steeply on perfectly flat ground. The hammerfolk go up anyway. They go up to the summits.

They walk into the shadow of a ghost peak, into the cold that should be the cold of a north face, into the wind funneling down a col that is not there, and they keep walking. They lean into a slope that is invisible. They breathe hard in air that has thinned around them. Rime forms in their mossy hair. Snow begins to fall, and it falls only on them, out of a cloud that has no business snowing. They walk for hours and seem to cover a few hundred meters of moss. When they reach the top, if you are watching from below, you see a small rust-brown figure standing on the flat moor in a column of falling snow, very still, looking out over a view that you cannot see.

What do they do up there?

This took me a long time to understand. They sit. That is all. They sit on the summit, in the snow, and keep still, for a day, sometimes for several. They do not pray, exactly. They do not work. They talk a little, in their measured phrases, to no one visible. Then they come down.

When you ride a big swell, and for half a second you see the plateau at another age, with the range standing whole and savage and white, glaciers and bare rock and no moss anywhere, look closely at the high ridges. Sometimes there are figures there. Small, dark, rust-brown, sitting still on the snow of the high peaks. Hammerfolk, seen from the other side.

They do not only climb the ghost mountains in the present. They are *in* the old mountains, some part of them, the part that goes up. They live in both ages at once. They lodge in the bobbins in the moor-age and they sit on the summits in the mountain-age, and the swell is the only moment when both halves line up and a stranger can see them in two places.

## What Lives Here

So here is where the answer changes.

I said at the start that nobody lives on the Hammer Moor the way a farmer lives on a farm. That there is no foreman, no owner, no keeper of the works. That is true. But I had the question pointed the wrong way.

The mountains live here.

They are the oldest tenants, and they are dying. It is a death with no useful number, a death that has taken a hundred million years so far and is not yet finished. The grinding under the moor is the sound of it: millstones, the last bones of the range being taken away grain by grain. The ghost peaks are what remains of them that is not stone. Their shadows, their cold, their weather, their old north, the valleys the grazers still walk. They have not left because they are not finished leaving. The moor is the only thing that remembers them, and so they stay.

And the moor is what comes after. It is the heir, the undertaker, and the hospice all at once. It is built out of death, its own and theirs, turning rot into heat and rock into mat, and it keeps its ledger of every making and unmaking. It is not hostile, and it does not notice you, because it is busy with the one job that has occupied it since the mountains were young: the long, slow, patient work of taking something enormous apart and making a living body out of what is left.

The grazers keep the old routes open, so the valleys are not forgotten. The eels keep the maps of tarns that are gone and tarns that are coming. The frogs read out the accounts. The stokers keep the hearths. The fermenters turn the dead into warmth.

And the hammerfolk go up and sit with the dying.

That is what they are for. That is who the cable is for, they would say if they said anything, and why it is never cut or woven or taken away: it is being wound for the mountains, a burial wrapping, a shroud spun by the wind over every last summit stump, one bobbin at a time. The moor does not notice you. It is too busy. But the hammerfolk notice. They are the part of the moor that pays attention. They are the ones who climb the slopes that are not there, and sit in the snow that falls only on them, and keep the old peaks company so that they do not have to finish dying alone.

When the last shadow finally forgets itself and lies down flat, the hammerfolk will come down from the last summit. They will walk out onto the moor on the beat, down to the nearest bog-burst, and lie down on the black steaming river and let it carry them. Within days the scars will green over. The frogs will add a line to the ledger. The grinding will stop, and for the first time in a hundred million years the plateau will be quiet, except for the pumps, which will not stop.

Then the moor will keep working, the way it always has. It will turn the mountains' death, and the hammerfolk's, and its own into its own body, and go on.
