# Those Who Keep

## The One Who Pushes Stones

Start with the grazer, because it is the one you will least likely see.

It is about the size of a sleeping dog and shaped like a loaf, like everything else here. Its shell is a low dome, heavy and pale, and so worn at the crown that the growth lines have smoothed into a single milky sheen. From ten paces it is a stone. From three it is still a stone, until you notice that the stone has a front edge, and under that edge a blunt, whiskered snout moves through the sand with the care of someone reading by touch.

It feeds on what lives beneath the pebbles: films of algae that wake in the flour-sand when the fog soaks down, threads of fungus, the husks of dead beetles softened by dew. To reach them it has to move stones, and it does this one at a time, gently, with the flat plate of its brow. It lowers its head, sets the plate against a pebble, and leans. The pebble shifts a finger's width. The grazer eats what was underneath, then leans again. It does not lift its feet so much as re-seat them, and its shell leaves a faint double furrow in the sand that the next fog erases.

It turns to the left, always. Nobody taught it this, as far as anyone could say, but a grazer that feeds only by turning left, and always pushes stones outward from the turn, will spend its life tracing a slow arc and leaving a wall behind it. The wall is not what it wants. The wall is simply where it has already been.

A grazer lives a long time. The arcs suggest how long. The small ones, ankle-high and a few strides across, are the work of a single life. The long ones, knee-high and a hundred paces from end to end, must be dozens of lives laid over each other, each new grazer coming to an old wall, feeling where the pebbles were thickest, and continuing the curve without knowing it continues anything. The open side faces east. The fog climbs in that way at dawn and pools behind the wall, where the grazer spends the hottest hours pressed into the shade of its own work. The cushion plants that thrive there feed the grazer's grandchildren. If this is farming, it is farming as the plateau does everything else: by accident repeated until the accident becomes a law.

For a while that seems enough of an answer. The grazer builds the walls, and the walls hold the fog. But the grazer is not what you feel watching you.

## The Rings

The arcs are open. The rings are closed.

At first the explanation seems simple. The rings are older arcs, bent round until their ends met, then worn down until the separate stones stood apart. But the stones in the rings are upright, not heaped. They are spaced as evenly as the birds in their rows. And when you kneel beside one and brush the sand from its base, you find that it does not sit on the bedrock. It sits on a shape.

The shape is a shell, turned to stone, standing on end.

This is what old grazers do. After enough decades of turning left, a grazer's shell grows too heavy to push against anything, and its feeding slows until it barely moves at all. Then it stops feeding. It walks, over a season, toward the nearest ring, since the rings draw them the way the tors draw fog. There it plants its forefeet, raises the front of its shell to face the ring's center, and holds that posture. The fog beads on it every morning. The salt left by each drying pan crusts over it. The lichens arrive, then the snails, then the long work of grit and wind. The grazer is still inside the stone for a long time, perhaps a very long time, breathing more and more slowly until the difference between breathing and not breathing is too small for anything on the plateau to measure.

So the rings are not built. They are gathered. Each is a council of the old, standing in a circle and facing inward toward nothing visible, the way the birds face outward toward nothing visible. This is why the lizards heap themselves most thickly on the ring-stones. Some faint warmth still comes up through them, and it is not the sun's.

This, too, seems enough of an answer for a while. The grazers are the inhabitants, the rings are where they go to finish, and the plateau is their long cemetery. But the more closely you watch the rest of the tableland, the less the grazers look like a separate kind of creature, and the more they look like one instance of something the whole plateau does.

## What They All Do

The beetles tilt their backs to the fog and take the water in. They do not waste it on noise, on quick movement, or on anything else. They hold it.

The lizards lie in heaps. The bottom of each heap stores the stone's warmth, and the top keeps the sun's heat off the ones below. The heap as a whole keeps warmth the way a single body would, turning itself over through the day so that no part spends too much of it. The heap holds heat.

The snails' shells are thin as tea-colored glass, and in the first light each one holds a drop of dawn inside it. That is why the pillars glow. The light goes in and seems slow to come out. Their silver trails cross and recross the tors in something close to script, and perhaps it is script of a kind: a record of where each snail has been and what it grazed, kept the only way a snail can keep anything, by leaving it behind. The snails hold light, and they hold a record.

The cushion plants live only on their skins and build themselves on their own dead. They hold their pasts inside themselves. The chimneys stand long after their termites have gone, holding their shape. The pans dry in rings like the growth lines of a tree, holding each rain as a line of salt.

The birds hold still. They also hold a direction, and that is the strangest thing on the plateau. They turn a few degrees at a time, all together, without signal, keeping their faces aimed at something below the horizon the way a compass needle keeps north. Over seasons their rows rotate all the way round. Sometimes, passing across a ring, they pause along its diameter, as if what they are tracking and what the old grazers face inward toward were, for a moment, lined up.

Everything here takes something in and does not let it go. That is the pattern in the dappled ground. The inhabitant is not a species. The inhabitant is a single habit held in common by a great many bodies: keeping.

## The One They Keep For

But keeping implies something kept for, and here the image shifts again and opens wider.

Consider where the water comes from. It does not fall from above. The lowlands breathe out every night, and the breath climbs the escarpments and rises, as the description says, rather than rolling. It fills the hollows first, as breath fills the bottom of the lungs. For an hour the tableland is full of it, a pale sea with stone islands. Then the sun comes, and the breath condenses onto every living surface, and the living surfaces take it in.

The lowlands exhale. The plateau inhales. And then it does not exhale.

That is the held breath. The fog is not something that happens to the plateau. It is the plateau's breathing in, done once a day for longer than there have been mountains. Everything the fog touches keeps it: beetles, cushion plants, the heavy-shelled grazers, the lichen maps, the snails with their captured light. The creatures are not living on the held breath. They are the held breath. The abundance, the crowding, the millions of shells in each hollow, is simply what a breath looks like when it has been held for an age and has had time to take on bodies.

Then who is breathing?

The old descriptions would say the land, and that answer is close enough to stand on. But from the rings, facing inward, and from the rows of birds, turning to track something below the horizon, one begins to feel a different shape. The domes are the backs of sleepers under a blanket. The tors are the roots of summits. Something very large once stood here, not a mountain but in the way that mountains are, and it wore down, and rose, and wore down again. It is now so worn, so planed and patient, that all that remains of it is its breathing. It lies down under its own ochre crust, drawing in the lowland air each night and keeping it. Every creature on the plateau is a part of that keeping. The grazers turn left through it. The lizards hold its warmth. The birds, standing in their rows, keep watch over whatever it is facing, which is always below the horizon, which is always moving, and which they never lose.

There is no hunger in it and no dread. That is why nothing scatters under the scavenger's shadow. Fear is a kind of exhalation, a quick spending, and the plateau does not spend. The scavenger is not prey or predator here so much as the one creature that does not keep. It rides the heat off the stone, flaps nothing, and is tolerated the way a held breath tolerates a single thought.

What happens if the breath is released? No creature on the plateau has ever seen it, which is the whole point. Perhaps the fog would one day go down instead of up. Perhaps the beetles would open their backs and let go of every drop they had ever gathered, the lizard heaps would slide apart, the snail-light would pour out of the pillars, and the rings of old grazers would at last lower their shells. The plateau would sigh, and the sigh would roll off the escarpments and into the lowlands as a warm, living rain, carrying seeds and eggs and slow patient bodies down into a world that has forgotten what patience is. Perhaps it has done this before, and that is how the lowlands came to have anything alive in them. Perhaps the lowlands' nightly exhalation is only them giving back, a little at a time, what the plateau once breathed out over them all at once.

Or perhaps it will never let go. Perhaps keeping is what it is, and the release is only a story the living tell themselves about being held.

## The Newest

There is one more inhabitant, and it arrives the same way the fog does, climbing the escarpment in the last cool hour and spreading into the hollows.

It is you.

At first you are a disturbance: a tall thing that must choose where to set each foot, that breathes loudly, and whose heartbeat, when the fog comes in, is the only drum on the tableland. The beetles do not flee you, but they do not tilt their backs toward you either. The birds' rows do not turn for you.

Stay, though. Stand long enough among the rows and the stacked sleepers and the pale tea-glass pillars, and the fog will find you as it finds everything. It beads on your sleeves and in your hair and on the backs of your hands. You will not decide to hold still, but you will find you have been holding still. You will notice that you have been sipping the air for an hour as though it were rare, because it is rare, and that you have not spent it on speech. Something in you has begun to keep.

This is how the plateau takes in its inhabitants. It does not conquer them or feed on them. It condenses on them. It beads on whatever is cooler than itself, and it waits.

If you stay long enough you may find yourself turning a few degrees at a time, without signal, toward something below the horizon. If you stay longer still, you may walk one day toward the nearest ring. Nothing there will be surprised. There is room in the circle, there is always room, and the lizards will come in their own time to lie across your shoulders and share what warmth you have left to keep.
