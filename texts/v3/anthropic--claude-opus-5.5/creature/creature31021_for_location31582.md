# The People of the Terraces

## First Sight

You notice them the way you notice the trees: one at a time, far apart, each one an event.

A figure sits at the lip of a pool three terraces down, knees drawn up, still enough that you took it for a standing stone. Another is so far across the plateau that it is only a pale mark against the black, and the mark moves so slowly that you watch for a full minute before you are sure it moves at all. A third kneels beside a channel with a bronze chisel. It lifts the chisel, and after long enough that you begin to wonder whether it will, it brings it down. The small bright tock reaches you a moment later, clean as a dropped pebble, and then the stone swallows it.

These are the people. There are not many. You could spend a day on the plateau and meet a dozen, and you would never see more than two of them together.

## Their Bodies

Up close they are pale, the pale of things that have always lived under diffuse light. There is no weathering on them, no squint lines, no sun-darkening across the nose and shoulders. Their skin has a faint sheen, like the stone. The air here gives up its water to anything cooler than itself, and they are a little cooler than the air, so a fine dew gathers on their forearms, at their temples, along the rims of their ears, and in their hair. Their hair is worn long and loose. Condensation beads along each strand the way it beads along a fern's edge, so that someone who has been sitting still for an hour seems threaded with tiny glass.

They move carefully. Partly this is the rock, which will open a palm or a heel that meets it carelessly. Their hands and feet are hard with old calluses, and most carry at least one fine white scar from a flow edge. Partly it is something slower and deeper. Their breathing is shallow and long. Their pulse, if you could feel it, would seem sluggish, like a sleeper's. They eat little, because there is little to eat: fern fiddleheads, the starchy root-cores of certain mosses, the hard pale fruit that the trees produce very rarely. They drink almost nothing on purpose. They don't need to. They live wet. The air keeps them topped up the way it keeps the pools at their levels, no higher and no lower.

They go barefoot on bare stone, because a hard sole rings. The small glassy note of a footstep on young basalt is, to them, faintly rude, like speaking over someone. They walk on the moss where they can, and where they can't they place their feet flat and without weight, so that one of them can cross a terrace beside you and you will hear only the drip behind them.

## Their Work

Everything they do is about water, because everything here is.

The channels need tending. Nothing wears the stone, but moss does not care where the builders meant the water to go. It creeps into the gutters and thickens there, a few finger-widths a lifetime, and slowly it turns a channel into a sponge and the sponge into a dam. So they scrape and clear and re-cut, slowly, with chisels and fern-fiber brushes. They lift the cleared moss out whole, like a pelt, and lay it somewhere it can grow without blocking anything. They do not throw it away. On the plateau nothing is thrown away. There is no away.

The cisterns need watching, too. They don't need filling or emptying, only watching. Each household keeps a cistern, and each cistern has a mark cut into its wall at the level the water has always held. If it ever stands a finger above or below, something has changed, and change is the one thing they are organized to notice. They check the marks every day. They have almost never found anything.

And then there are the flowers.

The trees flower, pale and waxy, and nothing comes to them. Nothing ever has. So the people go. This is the most important thing they do, though they would never call it that, because they call very little by any name at all. They carry a small brush made from the finest fern-down and bound to a sliver of wood. A person will walk from one tree to another across a half-day of bare, shining rock, carrying a little pale dust in a fold of wet leaf held against the body to keep it from caking. They stand under the second tree and, flower by flower, touch it in.

The trees are far apart. So the walks are long, and they are the plateau's great journeys. A tree set seed four times in one person's life, and someone walked for every one.

When the hard fruit finally drops, someone carries the seed to a fissure where water gathers. They choose that crack after a great deal of looking. They press the seed into it, then spend a year, or several, sitting beside it now and then and waiting to see whether the root will come out across the stone like poured wax and find its way down. Most do not. The ones that do are remembered. Every tree on the plateau was put there by a particular person. The small stands of three or four are families. The people know which is which, the way you might know the names on a line of graves, except that these are not graves. They are the opposite.

## Their Speech

Voices carry here, and then they die. A spoken word goes out over the wet stone clear and whole, and a moment later it is simply gone, soaked into moss and rock. They have adapted to this in two ways.

First, they speak rarely and low, and they almost never speak across distance. If you want to tell someone something, you walk to them. That walk may take a morning, and by the time you arrive you will have decided whether the thing was worth saying. Often it wasn't, and you sit beside them by their pool for a while instead, and that becomes the thing that was said.

Second, much of their language is timing. They have lived their whole lives inside the sound of drips falling at irregular, unhurried intervals, and they hear intervals the way other peoples hear pitch. A pause before a word means something. A pause after it means something else. The same sentence spoken with a long gap in the middle and spoken straight through are two different sentences. When they do speak at length, it sounds almost like the column-faces: a single word, a silence, a word, a longer silence. The listener waits with a patience that would feel unbearable anywhere else. Here it only feels courteous.

They have many words for drips. They have a word for a drip that falls exactly when you expected it, and a sweeter word for one that falls a little late. There is also a word, very old and rarely used, for a drip that stops. They say it the way other people say *fire*.

Some of them sing. Nobody sings aloud. They lean close to the surface of a pool and hum into it, almost touching the water, and the sound goes down into the stone instead of out into the air. Sometimes you can find one of them lying on a terrace with an ear pressed to the basalt. Ask what they are doing and they will say, after a pause of meaningful length, that they are listening to someone.

## Their Houses, and the Distance Between

The town is spacious because the people are.

Each person, or each pair, or each rare small family, needs their own catchment: a roof that sheds enough condensation, a pool that holds it, a cistern to watch. The plateau weeps evenly and slowly, and it can only give each patch of stone so much. So the houses are set out the way the pools are, the way the trees are, at the spacing the water allows. The distance between them is not coldness. It is arithmetic. It is also respect. You live at the edge of your pool as a tree lives over its fissure. You take what falls on you and not more.

Inside, the houses are dim and faintly glossy, their walls fitted hexagons slick with the same film that covers everything. The floors are softened with moss-mats laid over the stone and replaced when they get too thick, which is almost never. There is little in them: a sleeping place, a few stone vessels, tools hung on wooden pegs, and perhaps a carved lintel or a wall where someone has spent decades very slowly cutting a pattern. The patterns are usually lines of small marks at uneven intervals, and if you knew how to read them they would sound like rain.

## Their Time

They live a very long time.

It is not clear that anyone ever decided this. It may be that the stillness does it. Nothing here strains the body: no cold, no heat, no hunger sharp enough to hurt, no sickness because there is nothing to carry it, no fright because there is nothing to fear. There are no wounds except the stone's small cuts, and those heal clean in the wet air. Their hearts beat slowly. They breathe slowly. They age the way the moss grows, too slowly for anyone to see.

"No one alive has seen the moss spread," is something they say. It is not quite true. A few of the oldest have. There are people on the plateau, not many, three or four, who remember when the western terraces were only half-built. One of them, a woman who sits at the edge of the highest pool on the eastern rim and seldom moves at all, is said to remember the stone warm. Nobody asks her. It would be like asking a tree.

This is why children are rare. There isn't room for many, and there isn't need. A birth comes perhaps once in a generation, and a generation here is very long. When a child does come, a tree is planted, and the child grows up beside it. The two are about the same age for a long time, then for longer than you would think possible, and then, inevitably, the tree is older.

## The Western Edge

Everything on the plateau runs downhill toward the west. The land tilts that way. The water runs in its channels from terrace to terrace, cistern to cistern, and at the bottom, where the lowest terrace meets the cloud, it slips over the rim and back into the white.

The people go the same way.

When one of them is finished, and they seem to know, the way a pool seems to know its level, they walk west. They are not in a hurry. They may take days, stopping at the houses they pass, sitting a while at each pool without speaking. At the bottom terrace the stone runs down into the cloud and the cloud laps up against it, a slow faint breathing at its margins. They walk into it. The white closes over their knees, their waists, their shoulders, their dew-beaded hair. There is no sound. There never is.

Nobody follows. Nobody knows what is below. It is possible nothing is below: no lowlands, no ground, only white all the way down. Or perhaps there are lowlands, loud and fast and full of birds, and whoever walks down into the cloud simply comes out, blinking, into the rain. The people do not speculate. What happens on the other side of a pause is not the listener's business.

## Where They Come From

And yet the plateau is not emptying.

Every so often, less often than a child is born but more often than you'd expect, someone comes up.

They come out of the cloud on the western slope, where the white meets the stone. The low terraces are wet already, so they are hard to see at first: a darker shape in the white, then a figure, then a person standing on the lowest stair with water running off them in sheets. They don't speak. They don't seem to know where they are, or to mind. Their skin is pale and faintly shining, and dew is already gathering in their hair.

Someone always sees them, because someone is always watching the western edge, as someone is always watching the cistern marks. That person walks down, slowly, and sits with them. After a long while, and after pauses of very particular lengths, the newcomer is led up the terraces. The water runs down in the channels beside them the whole way, and the newcomer climbs against it. Somewhere higher up there will be a patch of stone with room for a pool. There will be a roof to build and a cistern to cut and mark. And eventually, if they are patient, there will be a tree.

The people do not say that these arrivals are the ones who left. They do not say they aren't. They do not say, though anyone who has stood long enough on the eastern rim watching the warm air rise and meet the stone and bead into water might begin to think it, that they themselves are simply what happens here. They are something the cloud gives up against the cool black rock, which condenses, lingers a while in the hollows, and runs down.

## What They Are

So this is who lives on the terraces above the cloud line.

They are a slow people, pale and wet-haired, walking barefoot so as not to ring the stone. They count intervals instead of hours. They tend water they did not make and cannot keep, and they check each day that nothing has changed. They are the only moving thing on the plateau besides the drip, and the only reason the pale flowers are anything more than open. They carry pollen across half a day of shining rock, one flower at a time. They put every tree where it stands.

They fit into the stillness, but they do not only fit into it. They are also what keeps it. Without them the channels would silt with moss, the trees would never set seed, and the balance would slip a finger's width out of true, and then another. The plateau does not need to change, but it needs someone to notice if it does. It needs someone who cares enough, walking slowly between the widely spaced pools, to stop and listen with an ear to the stone for a drip that falls a little late.

That is their whole life, and it is very long, and at the end of it they walk west and let the white close over them.

And down on the lowest terrace, not often but often enough, someone stands up out of the cloud, dripping.
