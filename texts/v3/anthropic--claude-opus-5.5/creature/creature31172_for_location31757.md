# The Tenant of the Blister

---

**First, the obvious answer**

The animals live there. Tens of thousands of grazers, the skaters, the veil-flies, the threadhunters, the beetles with their drops of water. If you asked what lives in the hollow, you could list them and be done.

But listing them is like answering *who lives in this house* by naming the mice, the moths in the wardrobe, and the spider over the sink. They live there, but they are not what the house is *for*. Every animal in the Blister acts like a lodger. They eat, flee, hide, and breed. None of them acts like the one whose home it is.

Someone in that room is the occupant. You have to look past the crowd to find them.

---

**Second, the beetles**

You would suspect the lens-beetles first, because they are the only ones who seem to *know* anything. They keep tools and check their water for cloudiness. They are the only animals that pay attention to the mirrors.

Watch them long enough and they stop looking like masters of the room. They look like its staff. They tend their drops the way a sexton trims candles, as if the work matters to someone other than themselves. When a beetle holds its drop up to a patch of mirror, it does not look like an animal studying its reflection. It looks like an animal showing something to someone behind the glass. It holds very still, as if waiting for an answer, and then it lowers the drop and goes back to work, satisfied or not.

So the beetles are not the occupant. They are its attendants, and they seem to know it.

---

**Third, the long one**

You might suspect the threadhunter next, especially the idea that it is one animal, a single transparent thread wound through every crack, present everywhere at once.

That comes closer. Whatever lives in the Blister is present everywhere at once. But the threadhunter is hungry, and the occupant is not. The threadhunter hunts, and hunting is a lodger's business. The occupant has never needed to eat anything, because everything in the room is already, in some sense, inside it.

Still, keep hold of that image: something so thin and so clear that you can only see it by what moves around it. That is the right way to look.

---

**Fourth, the ones in the glass**

Then there are the reflections: the larger beetle, the thing with too many wings, the threadhunter as thick as a wrist. None of them exists in the room. They exist only in the mirror version of the room, where the pools sit a little to the left and the beetles hold their drops in the wrong forelegs.

These are not the occupant either. They are what the occupant is *considering*.

The mirrors are where it tries things out. A heavier threadhunter, to see how the grazers would move around it. A beetle with a larger drop, to see what that drop might burn. A veil-fly with extra wings, to see whether it would still drift or would finally fly, and whether it would ever come back. The reflections are drafts. The room in the glass is the room as it might be, laid over the room as it is, and the occupant moves between the two the way a person turns a word over before saying it.

This is why the beetles hold up their drops to the mirrors. A drop of water is a lens, a tiny eye. The beetles are lending it an eye to look through. Or they are checking the drafts against the real thing, and holding up a sample of the true room so the occupant does not lose track of which version it is in.

---

**What it is**

The occupant of the Blister has no body of its own. Its body is the hollow.

That is less strange than it sounds. Consider what the chamber is: a small pocket of warm, wet air sealed in glass, full of light, with one fine crack to breathe through. Now consider what a lens-beetle carries: a small bead of water held in a basket of hairs, full of light, which it keeps clear and keeps topped up. The beetles carry the room in miniature. Every one of them is holding a little model of the thing they serve.

So the occupant is a drop, turned inside out. It is a bubble of attention, cupped by the mountain the way the beetle's drop is cupped by its forelegs. Its substance is the water circuit: steam rising, beading, sliding, pooling, and rising again, many times an hour. Rain, spring, and sweat are the same event here because they are all one creature's breathing. That circuit is its pulse. The ticks of the glass are the closest thing it has to a voice. It says one word every few minutes, in a place where no one else has a voice at all, and the word is always the same high, clear note, like a nail on a wineglass. It may be saying *here*. It may be counting.

It is almost certainly counting.

---

**The census**

The counts do not hold because they are not facts. They are thoughts.

The occupant holds the whole population in mind at once: every grazer, every skater, every egg in every fringe. It is enormously attentive, but it is not perfect, and a number held in the mind drifts the way a number held in yours drifts when you have been counting sheep too long. Forty skaters. Forty-one. Thirty-eight. Forty. Nothing entered the pool and nothing left it. The occupant simply blinked, and the room blinked with it, because the room is only as sure of itself as its occupant is.

Eleven pools, or twelve. It has not quite decided. The veil-fly eggs and the mineral fringes look alike because it has not quite decided those either. It is young. The mountain has no name yet, and neither does the thing inside it, and very little is settled when you are young.

---

**The dead**

This is also why the dead come back.

A lens-beetle burns a grazer to a pink smear, and the grazer is gone from the pool. It is not gone from the census. The occupant still remembers it: the crooked left antenna, the particular shuffle, the place on the wall it favored. In the Blister, being remembered hard enough is the same as being alive. So the grazer is feeding on the far wall a moment later, exactly itself, with no idea anything happened.

The animals flee and hide and freeze because to *them* death is real. A lodger's death is always real to the lodger. The occupant simply does not agree, the way a parent does not agree that a nightmare is real, and it quietly puts things back where they were.

It does not always manage it.

---

**The column below**

Under the chamber, going down into the mountain, is the column of sealed bubbles. Each one is a former room, left behind when the Blister climbed. Some still hold dried mineral fringes. A few hold tiny animals preserved perfectly in glass.

Those are the ones it forgot.

That is the only real death in the Blister: to be let go of. Every time the room rises, a finger's width of floor seals over, and whatever the occupant was not holding onto at that moment stays behind. It is kept, but not remembered. The grazers in the glass below are not dead the way a crushed grazer is dead. They are dead the way an old address is dead, or a name you once knew for a color. They are perfectly intact, and no longer anyone's.

The column is the occupant's past, laid down in order like the rings of a tree or a stack of old letters. It does not look back at it. It may not know it is there. It is not the kind of thing that can look down.

---

**Why it climbs**

The occupant cannot bear to be buried.

This is the one fear it seems to share with its lodgers, and the one thing it does that looks like effort. The mountain pushes up from below, new crust hardens over the top, and by any ordinary process the chamber should sink, room by room, into the dark and the weight and the heat. Instead it rises, always a hand's depth below the new summit, always under the thinnest possible roof, always close enough to the sky that daylight passes through.

It needs the light. The beetles need it for burning, the film needs it for warmth, and the occupant needs it for the reason anything needs light: to see what it is. A bubble in the deep rock would still hold air, but no light would ever enter it and no one inside it could be sure of anything. The counts there would drift forever. So when the crust thickens past a certain point, the occupant draws itself up.

The animals feel this coming before it happens. That is what the great migration is. The grazers climb in a single sheet, the skaters leave their pools, the veil-flies rise, and the ceiling turns pink with bodies. They are not fleeing. They are being gathered in. The occupant holds its breath and everything it is responsible for gets close, the way you would pull your arms and legs in before squeezing through a gap. Then comes one loud tick, the only time it raises its voice. The room is a finger higher. Behind and below, a sliver of floor turns to solid glass, with whatever was not gathered in time.

It is a molt, not a journey. The Blister is not traveling through the mountain. It is shedding rooms the way a growing animal sheds skins, each one slightly too small, left behind as a hollow perfect copy.

---

**The veil-flies that leave**

At midday, when the heat is strongest and the steam thickest, a pale thread of veil-flies is carried out through the exit crack and shredded by the wind. None come back.

They are not supposed to. If the chamber is a drop, the veil-flies are what evaporates, and evaporation is how a drop reaches the air. The occupant is sending them out: seeds, or letters, or small parts of its attention with the cold, thin sky for a destination. Somewhere down the range there may be another young mountain with a thread of steam coming from a crack no wider than a fingernail. That other room may have been started this way. This one may have been.

Nothing in the chamber knows. The occupant does not know. It only sends them out, every hot day, the way a plant lets go of pollen without any idea of the flower at the other end.

---

**Its night**

At night, when only the orange glow from below comes through the thin glass, the occupant is at its most itself. The beetles fold their drops against their bellies, so no one is lending it eyes. The grazers pile up like pink moss. The reflections go dark, and there are no drafts to consider. What remains is the warm, wet air, the slow circuit of water, the ticking of the glass as it cools, and the census.

This is when the counts shift most, because this is when no one is watching but the occupant. It goes over its lodgers in the dark the way a person lies awake going over names. It is not always sure. By morning there are more of them, or fewer, or the same. Some of the dead are back. Some of the living have been dropped. It does its best.

---

**What will happen to it**

Every mountain stops growing. One season the push from below will weaken and the new crust will stop coming, and the Blister will have nowhere higher to go. The roof will thin with weathering instead of thickening with lava. The glass, which has never known dust or softening, will start to dull. One day a crack will widen past a fingernail. Then, some morning, the sun will come through a gap that does not close, and the ceiling will not be glass anymore. It will be sky.

A blister, in the end, breaks.

When it does, you will know the occupant is gone, and you will know it by one change: the counts will hold. Forty skaters will be forty when you look away and forty when you look back. The pools will be eleven, or twelve, and stay that way. A grazer burned to a pink smear will stay a pink smear. The water will only fall down. The mirrors will show only what is in front of them, and the lens-beetles, holding their drops up to the glass, will find nothing there to show.

The animals may not notice. They have always lived as if death were real and numbers were fixed. They will go on living that way, in an open hollow near a dead summit, getting fewer.

The occupant will have gone out through the widened crack, all at once, the way steam leaves when a lid is lifted: a small warm breath into very cold air. The wind will take it before it becomes anything you could see. Whether it reaches another mountain, or thins out over the whole range and settles on no mountain at all, nobody can say.

---

**If you were small enough**

If you were small enough to stand inside the Blister now, on a plate of fresh black glass with the grazers streaming past your feet, you would not see the occupant. There is nothing to see. It is the room you are standing in.

You might feel it, though. You might feel yourself being counted. You might glance at the nearest patch of mirror and see yourself standing a little to the left, holding something in the wrong hand, and understand that you were being considered. And if you stayed long enough, and were injured, and did not quite die, you might find yourself whole again on the far wall, feeding on the warm film with your crooked antenna, because something very young and very attentive, living in a bubble at the top of the world, had decided to keep you.
