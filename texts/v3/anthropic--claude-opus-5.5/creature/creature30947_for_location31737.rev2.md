# Who Lives in the Held Basin

## The ones you notice first

The people are easy to miss. There are so few of them, and they sit so far apart in so much air, that on a first crossing you might count a dozen and take them for more floating things: rafts, fields, strange fruit. Then one of them lifts a hand toward you from a kilometer away. The gesture is large, slow, and meant to be seen through fog. You understand that you have been watched for some time and that the lattice has been telling someone you are coming.

Up close they are compact and broad through the chest. Their eyes are large and dark-adapted, and in true daylight elsewhere they squint as if hit. Their fingertips are callused flat from years of touching resonant plates to feel which way a node is drifting. Most have a faint mark at the throat, a slight thickening or a pale band of skin. The chord has made their voice boxes vibrate every day of their lives, and the tissue there has grown strong the way a laborer's hands do.

They are always humming, though not by choice. Put your palm flat on a basin person's chest and you will feel it: the great chord caught in their ribs and larynx, a tiny private copy of the Throats running inside them. They do not notice it any more than you notice your pulse. When one of them travels outside the basin, the first thing they report is that their chest feels *empty*, as if something had been unplugged.

There is one more thing to understand about them before anything else. In a country where nothing wears down, where the glass keeps its edges for centuries and dust never settles, the people are the only things that erode. They go deaf. Their joints loosen in the constant vibration. Their teeth ache and chip. Their faces weather and their hands smooth. On that sharp, unaging floor they are the one soft, worn thing, and they know it. Among themselves, a lined face, a deaf ear, or a stiff hand is a sign of having *been here*. Visitors look to them uncannily fresh, like people made of the same new glass as the plain.

## How a person begins

A birth in the Held Basin is not heard. The mother's cry vanishes into the chord, and so does the child's. The first sign that the child is alive is felt: the midwife lays two fingers on the infant's sternum and waits until the small body catches the chord and begins to hum with everything else. Only then do they call it born. A child who has not yet caught the pitch is still in between, and the family does not name it.

Names are gestures, and most are paths. A name is a small trajectory through the lattice, drawn in the air with one hand: a drift left and down, a hop across two nodes, a rise to a high tier. Parents choose a route that means something to them, such as the way from the birth-dwelling to the nearest vine-field, or the line a nodewren flock was flying on the morning of the birth. Spoken aloud, where speech is possible at all, a name is just a description of a motion. In the air it is a small flight.

Take one child, a girl whose name is a flat hand held palm-down and lowered half a finger's width. It is the gesture for a raft easing onto a lower node. Call her Low-Palm, though that loses most of it.

She is born in a middle-tier dwelling, one of three in a neighborhood that floats about eighty meters above the glass, two fog shelves up from the floor. Her home is woven grass on a shell frame, perforated like a sieve, and it rocks gently on its node. Beside the door hangs the family's tuning plate, a thin disc of fired ceramic on a braided cord. When she is old enough, her mother takes her small hand, presses it to the plate, and holds it there until she feels the one exact tremor that belongs to their node. It is distinct from the chord around it, a thread inside the weave. That is the family's pitch. She will never tell it to anyone outside the household, and no one will ever ask her.

Outsiders take the privacy for superstition. It is closer to kinship. The pitch is where the family lives, and, as will become clear, where it goes when it dies. Asking for it is like asking to see someone's bones.

## Childhood in the lattice

Low-Palm learns the node-lines before she can walk properly, because in the basin walking is a floor-skill and she is not often on the floor. She learns to push a raft with the flat of her hand and feel it slide into the next held point with a soft settling click, felt more than seen. She learns the halo figures: the particular diffracted geometry that marks the bearing of each Throat through each fog shelf. A thin cross with a double ring means one column. A scatter of six-pointed stars means another. She can find her way home through a whiteout by the faint printed patterns hanging in the mist, and she learns this before she can count to ten.

She learns the other residents the way children elsewhere learn the neighborhood dogs.

She knows the **nodewrens**, which slide past her dwelling in right-angled flocks. She can tell from their flight when they are hungry, when they are frightened, and when they feel a retuning coming.

She knows the **resonant insects** that hatch from the suspended soil of the family's field. As a child she catches them in cupped hands and feels their wings buzzing in perfect sympathy with the Throats, tiny passengers on the same sound she is riding.

And she knows the **mutes**. Every child in the basin is taught one thing about mutes early, with a hand over the sternum, pressed hard. *If your ears pop and the air goes hollow, grab something that is held by more than one node.* The mutes rarely take anything as large as a child. But children are left on the floor for a reason, and every family has a story about a sibling, a cousin, or a basket of water beads that dropped through a sudden quiet hole in the air while a long grey neck folded above it and a shape came down.

She is afraid of the mutes and loves them the way children in wolf country love wolves. She practices the counter-tone in secret, as all children do. She hums against the chord in her own chest, trying to find the note that would cancel it, and she never manages it. Almost nobody does.

## The first silence

When she is seven, her grandmother takes her by raft to a cancellation pocket.

It sits in the wide space between three Throats, where two tones meet and annihilate each other across a volume the size of a small house. Its edge is marked by pegs set into the glass below and by a ring of tethered rafts left by visitors. As they drift across the boundary, the chord drains out of Low-Palm's chest all at once, like water out of a basin. Her ears ring. Her own heartbeat returns, astonishingly loud. Then her grandmother does something Low-Palm has never seen anyone do.

She *speaks*. Her lips move and a voice comes out, hoarse and wavering, pitched strangely because it is so rarely used. It says the child's name, not as a gesture but as a word.

Low-Palm cries, the way children cry the first time they see the sea. The feeling is too big and too clean.

The pockets have their own residents. They are called the **Flat** after the sound, or sometimes the Pale, because they come to resemble the fungi that grow there. These are people who, through injury or illness or old age or simple temperament, can no longer bear the chord. They live permanently inside the pockets in small woven shelters pinned to the glass, and they rarely leave. Their skin goes wan in the dimness. Their hearing, strangely, recovers a little; the pockets are the only place in the basin where damage can begin to heal. They grow the pale fungi and eat them. They keep company with the small blind mammals that also refuse to leave. The two sometimes seem to have come to a truce, the animals sleeping at the Flat's feet.

The Flat are the basin's memory. Everywhere else, history is carried in hands, and hands forget in a generation. In the pockets, stories can be *said*, and said the same way each time, and memorized as sound. The Flat are the ones who remember that the tiers used to be higher. They remember the retuning that dropped a whole neighborhood into a fissure, and the names of the old vine-fields, and the order in which the Throats are said to have begun. The pockets are common land by law. Anyone may come and speak and grieve. But the Flat live there, and visitors come quietly, the way you enter a house even when the door is open.

Low-Palm's grandmother became Flat four years later. The family visits her every season. She dies in the silence, with her own heartbeat audible to her at the end. People in the basin count this as a very good death.

## The road-layers

At fifteen, Low-Palm goes down to the glass to work.

The polished roads that run along the floor's ribbing were built with obsessive precision and are used by almost no one. They have builders, and the builders are a guild. They call themselves the **Floor**. They live lowest of anyone, in heavy dwellings pinned directly to the glass with ceramic pegs. Most are deaf by thirty. They work in the densest layer of sound near the ground, lie on warm seams at midday beside the flat grey lizards, and wear thick-soled wrappings against the ridge-crests.

Their work is to extend the roads, re-fusing the glass with lenses and patient heat and polishing it until it is smooth as water. They lay road where no traffic exists and none is expected in any lifetime now living.

Low-Palm asks the question every apprentice asks: *who are the roads for?*

The master answers the way masters always do, with a single gesture. Flat palm, held face-down, lowered slowly all the way to the glass. It is the same motion as her name, carried to its end.

*For when the city lands.*

The Floor are the people who believe the settling story most completely. They are building the ground-level city that will be needed when the lattice finally lowers everything onto the glass: streets for people who will someday walk, laid out along the ribbing so that when the dwellings come down they will already sit beside roads. They have been doing it for as long as the Flat can remember. They work for descendants who will have feet on the floor. Among themselves they talk about the great-grandchildren's ankles, knees, and tired soles, which no one in the basin has yet had to worry about.

In this way Low-Palm comes to live among the lizards. They are the calmest things in the basin because they have no ears at all, and the Floor love them for it. Road-layers take naps with a lizard pressed warm against their back. By the end of her apprenticeship Low-Palm has learned to read the glass the way the lizards do: warmer seams, cooler plates, the faint heat-map of the earth breathing below. She has also begun to lose her hearing, and she is proud of it.

## The Stilled

Farther down still, and farther in, there is one more kind of person in the basin. Low-Palm meets them only once in her working life.

At the foot of each Throat, in the region where the sound is so strong that speech, thought, and heartbeat are all erased, there is a ring of dwellings pinned to the glass. The people who live there have gone completely deaf and chosen to stay where everyone would be deaf anyway. They are called the **Stilled**. The name has nothing to do with quiet. In their ring there is no quiet at all. It refers to how *still* they sit.

They are the basin's early warning. Before a Throat retunes, the glass around its foot begins to change: a slight slide in the vibration of the ribbing, a shift in how the pale ceramic trembles, a restlessness in the lizards. The Stilled sit on the floor with their palms flat on the glass for hours at a time. When they feel the pitch start to move, they raise mirrors. Each ring keeps polished glass shields angled toward the halo bearings, and they flash a warning outward through the fog shelves, from Throat to neighborhood to neighborhood, faster than a nodewren can fly.

When the Stilled flash, the whole basin lashes down.

The rule that no one touches a Throat applies to them most of all. They live within arm's reach of the pale column, and they spend their lives not touching it. Low-Palm, delivering road-glass to one of their rings, watches an old Stilled man sit a hand's width from its surface with his palm raised. He holds it there for a long time, not quite making contact. She does not know whether he is feeling something or refusing something.

## The mute-singers

Low-Palm is thirty-one when her mother dies, and she learns the last thing about the people of the basin: what they do with their dead.

A body in the Held Basin is not buried, because the glass cannot be dug, and it is not burned, because fire in the lattice is terrible. The family carries the body up to its home node and lays it on the familiar woven floor, on the familiar pitch. Then a **mute-singer** is called.

Mute-singers are the very few people who, in childhood, actually managed the thing every child practices: they found the counter-tone. They can hum against a node in their own chest and cancel it, briefly, the way the long-necked predators do. It is considered both a gift and a wound, and they do nothing else with it. They are never allowed to sing the counter-tone except at a death. Families fear them a little and are grateful to them always. Many are also Flat, because the counter-tone costs something, and the singers are among the few who need the pockets to heal from their own work.

The mute-singer rests her palm on the family's plate, learns the pitch the family has kept private for generations, and sings against it.

The node goes hollow. The body falls.

It falls through fog shelves and clear air to the glass, where the Floor wait to receive it. They lay it on a warm seam beside a fissure. There, in the constant low heat, it dries slowly over a season, and the warmth and the fungi and the patient succulents take it apart, until one day it is dust.

And the dust is lifted. It rises back into the lattice, as all dust in the basin does, and is held.

This is why the pitch is private, and why it is like blood. The people of the basin believe that dust rises to the pitch it was carried on. Families go up to their home node on certain nights, when the fog shelves are low and the lanterns are dark. By the dull red breath of the deep fissures they look at the faint glitter held in the air around their dwelling, the fine quivering haze in its regular rows like a diagram of itself, and they know who is there.

The census kept by the Flat, recited aloud in the pockets, lists the living by their name-gestures and neighborhoods, and then it lists the others. It is very long. Visitors say a few thousand people live in the Held Basin. The people of the basin say otherwise. They count everyone who is held.

## What is really settling

So the question comes back, as it always does: why are the tiers descending?

Across the generations, the Throats are dropping a fraction of a tone. The strata thin out above, and the city sinks toward the floor. The Flat recite the old heights. The Floor lay roads for the landing. Everyone has noticed.

Some people say the Throats are finishing their work: they raised the land, and now they are setting the city down. Some say it is only breathing, and that the chord will rise again.

But there is a third belief. It lives mostly among the Floor and the Flat, the two groups closest to the dead, and Low-Palm comes to hold it in her middle age. It says the lattice is not being lowered. It is being *weighed down*.

The dust in the basin never settles, and each generation adds its own dead to what is held. Every mother and grandmother and road-layer and Stilled watcher and small drowned child who has fallen to the glass has dried there, crumbled, and risen back into the rows. Each is only a few grams scattered across a thousand nodes. But the nodes hold more every year, and they have been holding for centuries, and the chord, the belief says, gives a little under the weight. A fraction of a tone per generation. The city is sinking because it is full of its own ancestors, and there are too many of them now to keep aloft.

By this account, the day the basin finally settles, when the chord falls silent and everything lands and the dust stays put, will be the day the dead outnumber what the sound can bear. The first real layer of grit in the fissures will be people. The erosion that finally makes the young glass old will be done by the dead coming home.

The people of the basin do not find this sad. They find it correct. They are the one thing in the country that wears, and they believe they are wearing it down with them, slowly, in the only way it can be worn.

## Low-Palm, at the end

She goes Flat at seventy, like her grandmother. She has been completely deaf for twenty years, and so she does not get her hearing back in the pocket. She lies on a woven mat on the warm glass and feels the silence as an absence in her chest, the place where the chord used to be, the humming gone out of her ribs at last.

The Flat speak to her. She cannot hear them, but she can see their mouths move, and she knows what it means that they are speaking.

When she dies, a mute-singer she knew as a child sings against her family's node. It is now eleven meters lower than it was on the day she was born. The Floor receive her. She dries on a seam beside a road she polished herself, one that no one has walked on. A few seasons later she rises.

Her dust is held in the rows around a dwelling in the middle tier, one fog shelf lower than it used to be. It is one faint glitter among the others in the diagram the basin draws of itself: seeds, water, glass flakes, the dead, all quivering exactly in place.

## Who lives here

So this is who lives in the Held Basin.

It is a people who hum without choosing to, who speak with their hands and name each other with small flights through the air. They grieve aloud only in the few places where sound goes flat. They erode on a floor that will not, and are proud of their wearing. They build roads for feet that do not exist yet. Among them live the Flat, the Floor, and the Stilled, a handful of mute-singers who cancel the sky once for each life, and the grey deaf lizards who lie against their backs. Around them live birds that turn at right angles, insects flying on borrowed sound, and a predator that eats with silence.

And there are the others, far more numerous, held in rows through the dim warm air above the glass. They quiver when the chord beats, and they drift down a fraction of a tone each generation, toward the roads that have been laid for them.

Every person in the basin makes the same gesture when they want to say what their home is doing. Flat hand, palm down, lowered slowly.

They mean *settling*. They also mean *coming home*. In their language these are the same motion, and a child named with it is being given the whole basin to carry.
