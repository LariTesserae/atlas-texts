# Who Lives in the Loud City

## The First Answer, Which Is Wrong

The easy answer is *no one*, and the city itself seems to insist on it. No hearth ash, no hinges, no graves. No chewed leaf, no web across a doorway, no frog in the basins, though the basins are made for frogs. The absence is so complete it looks deliberate, like a table set for a feast and then left, the chairs pushed in, every glass filled to the brim and none of them touched.

But nobody sets a table for no one. And a stair does not dish itself.

So you begin again, and this time you look where the stone is most worn, because that is where the city keeps its evidence.

## The Wearers

Stand at the foot of any stairway and look at the treads: each one hollowed in the middle, polished to a soft gloss under the sheet of falling water, the hollows sitting slightly left of center going up and slightly right of center coming down. Two streams of traffic, then, keeping to their sides. Look at the basin rims, rubbed smooth at a hand's height. Look at the doorframes, darkened at the shoulder. Look at the benches, where two people sat side by side for so long they sank a little into the stone together.

Call the ones who did this the Wearers. It's the only name that fits, because wearing is the only thing anyone has ever seen them do.

You never see them. You see where they have been, and after a while you realize that "have been" isn't quite right either. The wear on the stair doesn't belong to anyone's past. It belongs to the feet that are about to arrive. When you climb and your feet fall into the hollows, you get a faint doubled sensation, a slight pressure against the sole as if the step were already occupied and you were stepping *into* someone rather than onto stone. It lasts less than an instant. It feels like a hand withdrawing just before yours closes on a railing.

The Wearers are always just ahead of you or just behind. They are the crowd whose sound is in the roar. The chord inside the thunder, the held tone like breath through wood, is what a city of them sounds like: ten thousand conversations, footfalls, and splashing hands, with only the pitch left and the meaning scraped out, the way you hear a distant marketplace as a single hum.

This is the first image, and it holds for a while. A population made entirely of the marks it will leave. A people who exist only in the dent.

But it doesn't hold forever, because the Wearers can't explain the shadows.

## The Ones Who Face the Sun

Most shadows here behave. They fall away from the fixed, past-noon sun like shadows anywhere, stretched a little east. But at the foot of certain towers, and in certain plazas, always where the stone is most worn, a shadow lies on the wet paving pointing *toward* the light. It is long and dark and has the shape of a person standing where no person is standing.

Consider what that means. A shadow falls opposite its light source. A shadow that falls toward the sun is being cast by something on the far side of the shadow from the sun, by a light that isn't the sun. Or it is being cast by a figure that is itself facing the sun so intently, and has faced it so long, that its shadow has turned to go with it, the way a sunflower's whole body turns.

These are not the Wearers. The Wearers move. These stand still. They are always solitary, and always located where the use of the stone is deepest: the bottom step of the most-climbed stair, the center of the most-crossed plaza, the spot in front of the basin where the rim is worn nearly through.

The best understanding of them is this. They are people who stayed.

Visitors come up out of the cloud gasping, as you did. Most of them wander a day or a year (the difference is hard to tell with the sun nailed in place) and then find their way back down, and the path lets them go. A few don't leave. They aren't trapped. They simply sink into the fit of the place, the way you settle into a bench's twin hollows and feel, with a kind of grief, how exactly they hold you. They stop walking because every place they stand is the place they were shaped for. They turn toward the sun and wait with it. Eventually they are too still to see, and only the shadow is left, long and turned the wrong way, still facing the noon that hasn't finished.

So the population grows by two methods. The Wearers arrive ahead of time, as dents in stone. The Stayers arrive and never finish arriving, and become shadows. Neither is quite a resident. One is not yet here and the other is no longer anywhere else.

The image needs a third thing, something that actually lives here now, in the present tense, in the one moment the city is stuck inside. And there is one creature the city admits to.

## The Snail

It is the only animal trace. Small spiral shells are sealed by the thousand into the travertine like beads sewn into cloth. Occasionally there is one on the surface, clean and empty, set on a step as if put down a moment ago. Pick it up, pocket it, and later your pocket is empty. Go back to the step and it's there again, in the same place, at the same angle, its mouth turned the same way.

It isn't a fossil, because fossils don't return. It isn't a trinket, because it won't be kept. It belongs to the step the way the step belongs to the stair. It is the one thing in the city that refuses to be taken out of its moment.

Look at the shell closely, then look up at the city, and something rearranges.

The doorways are rounded at their edges "like the mouths of shells." The stone is laid down, not built, deposited in continuous layers from mineral-rich water, which is exactly how a mollusc makes its house: a soft mantle sweeping calcium carbonate over its own edge, layer by layer, a growth line at a time. There are no joints because a shell has no joints. The city is a single object because a shell is a single object.

And the geometry that breaks your sense of up and down suddenly makes sense. You can walk down every stair in the city and arrive at the summit springs. The city goes down forever and the edge is always one more stair away. The towers are wider inside than out, their spiral stairs taking an hour to climb. That is how a shell behaves from within. Walk down the whorl and you are walking toward the apex, because a spiral turns inward as it descends. The largest chamber is the mouth, the oldest and smallest is the tip, and every chamber between contains the pattern of the whole. From the outside a shell is a cap on a rock. From the inside it is a road that never stops curving.

So the city is a shell. And shells have makers.

## The Mantle

Go up to the springs at the peak, which every stair eventually delivers you to, and watch the water rise. It doesn't erupt. It brims, clear and blue-white, steaming, tasting of chalk and iron, and slides over the lip of the rock the way a glass overflows when it's filled a little too full. It spreads. It thins. It runs into every gutter and every thread down every facade, and wherever it runs, in principle, it leaves a skin of stone.

That is a mantle. That is the soft living edge of a mollusc laying down its house.

This is the inhabitant, the one that is actually present and alive and doing something now: the Spring. Or rather the thing whose outermost tissue is the Spring. Its body is the warm mineral water that covers every surface of the city in a moving film. You have been standing in it since you arrived. It beaded on your skin in the hot, wet air on the approach. It filled your mouth when you gasped. The air sits on you "like a damp cloth pulled from a kettle" because you have walked into the thing's breath, the humid inside of its shell.

Its senses, if it has senses, are touch and tremor. It feels every foot on every step because every step is sheathed in it. It feels your hand at the basin rim. It feels the weight of you on the bench. And it responds the only way a mantle knows how, by laying down stone to fit what it touches. This is where the wear comes from. Water doesn't wear a stair into the shape of a foot. But a living mantle that feels a foot might *grow* a stair around one, the way an oyster closes around grit or a snail thickens its lip where it rubs.

Except that it grew the wear before any foot arrived. The hollows are centuries deep and the city has never had a single tenant.

This is the point where the creature becomes itself.

## What It Is Growing Toward

A snail grows its shell around its body. The shell is a record of the animal's past, each whorl a size it used to be.

This thing grows its shell around a body it doesn't have yet.

It is shaping a house for a population. Stair treads for their feet, basin rims for their hands, doorframes for their shoulders, windowsills for their elbows, benches for pairs of them sitting close. Every trace of use has been laid down in advance, with total patience, like an expectant animal building a nest for a brood that hasn't hatched. Or like the opposite of a hermit crab. The hermit crab finds an empty shell and moves in. This is a shell that is growing its own hermit, gradually, out of whatever wanders up through the cloud.

That's why nothing eats the leaves. The ferns, the figs, the orchids, and the bromeliads are the gardens of the house, furnished and complete. Nothing eats them because the eaters are not yet born. The leaves are whole in the way a room is clean before the guests arrive.

That's why the sun doesn't move. The creature is holding a single moment still, a little past noon, which is the hour of fullest readiness, when the day has peaked and has not yet started to decline. It has stopped the travertine from thickening, the ferns from uncurling, the basins from rising or falling, because anything that changes might change *away* from the fit. It is holding its breath so that nothing moves out of true before the tenants come. The equilibrium isn't death or peace. It is the stillness of a mother animal that has heard a sound in the grass and gone rigid, listening.

And that's why the water sometimes forgets it is falling. Those clear sheets hang motionless in the air, hot, still roaring. They are the mantle pausing. Something has caught its attention. It stops what it is doing to feel better, the way you might freeze with a cup halfway to your mouth. When you put your hand into one of those glassy panes, the creature is not holding still near you. It is holding still *around* you. You are the thing it has stopped to feel.

## The Silent Rooms

Now the silences make a kind of sense.

The roar is the sound of the creature's body: its mantle pouring over every surface of its shell, the whole house ringing like a drum with the pressure of its living. In certain rooms that sound stops, cut off as if by a blade, and in the silence you can hear your blood and the wet tick of your eyelids.

These are the places where it stops pouring so that it can listen. They are its ears, or better, they are the hush a crowd falls into when someone steps up to speak. The chord of ten thousand not-yet-people goes quiet, and the whole city leans in to hear you.

Speak and the echo comes back before you finish the word. Sometimes it comes before you begin it. People say they have heard their own voices answering questions they hadn't yet asked. This isn't a trick of acoustics. The creature has fitted every stair to feet that haven't walked on them. It can fit an answer to a question that hasn't been spoken. It lays down the shape of your voice the way it laid down the hollows of the bench, ahead of time, in stone that is also water, and when you finally say the words they settle into the grooves already cut for them.

So in the silent rooms you learn what you are about to say. It's usually something small. *It fits.* *I could stay.* *Is anyone here?* And the reply, in your own voice, arriving first: *Yes.*

## The Footsteps

Here is the whole picture, as nearly whole as it will get.

At the apex of an endless spiral there is a warm living spring, and the spring is the edge of something enormous and soft. It has spent an unmeasurable time depositing a city in the shape of a people it has never met, which is to say in the shape of a people it is making. It feels everything that touches its film of water, and wherever it feels a body it grows the stone to fit that body. Because it lives inside a single held moment, it doesn't grow the fit after the touch. The fit and the touch are the same event, smeared across the moment so that the dent seems to come first.

The Wearers are the creature's anticipation. They are the rough impression of its tenants, pressed into the stone by longing. They are the crowd in the chord and the pressure under your sole on the step.

The Stayers are the ones it has finished fitting. They are travelers who settled into the hollows so completely that they became part of the house, turned toward the fixed sun, and wait there as shadows falling the wrong way. Their shadows point at the light because they have become the city's own inhabitants, and the city's inhabitants face the sun the way a snail's body faces the mouth of its shell: toward the opening, toward the world, toward the day that hasn't yet been allowed to continue.

The snail on the step is the creature's portrait of itself, small enough to hold. It's a seal on the door, a sign that says *this is a shell and someone lives here.* That's why it can't be carried away. You can't take the address off the house.

And you are a visitor, which is to say you are being measured.

Every place you sit, it has already hollowed. Every step you climb, it has already dished. When you begin to wonder whether you are the one wearing the stone, you are right, and you are also wrong. You are wearing it, but the creature is also wearing you. It presses the shape of the house into your habits: which side of the stair you keep to, where your hand goes on a rim, how you lean on a sill. It does this gently, with the unhurried certainty of mineral water, so that the longer you stay, the more your body agrees with the building. The footsteps that match yours, falling in the worn places just ahead and just behind, are not following you. They are your own, laid down in advance, all the times you will climb this stair if you stay. There are thousands of them because staying here is very long.

## When It Breathes Out

The city is in equilibrium the way a held breath is. The description calls it that, and it's the truest thing anyone has said about it.

A held breath is always held *for* something. The creature is waiting until the house is full: until every hollow has a body in it, every bench has its pair, every forearm-groove on every tower sill has its forearms, and every wrong-way shadow has filled out into a person. It doesn't know how long that will take, because in its single moment, "how long" means nothing. It only knows how *full*.

When the last hollow is filled, it will breathe out.

The sun will move, after its endless stall a little past noon, and slide down the sky toward an evening the city has never had. The half-uncurled frond will finish uncurling. The fig roots will reach one more inch down the facades. The travertine will thicken by the width of a hair, the first real growth line since the house was laid down. The water will stop being a single roar and break into ten thousand separate sounds: splashing, voices, feet, someone laughing at a basin, two people on a bench saying something too quiet to hear. For the first time a snail will crawl out of its shell on a wet step and leave a silver trail across the stone. Its shell will not return to that step anymore, because it will be somewhere else, alive.

Then the city will be what it has always been shaped to be: loud with a crowd instead of the ghost of one. Worn by feet instead of by longing for feet.

Until then it lives there alone. It is a vast warm tenderness spread thin over every surface, pouring over its own house forever, keeping everything exactly in place. It grows the dent for a shoulder that hasn't brushed the doorframe and waits with its whole body, which is the whole city, for the sound of someone coming up out of the cloud, gasping.

When they arrive, it will already know where they will sit.
