# Who Lives in the Precipitate

## The Hushed

Start with the people, because there are so many of them, and because they are the first thing you fail to notice.

Two million of them, packed into five- and six-storey stacks of reed and tin and cut stone, and you can walk a whole morning through their streets without once being startled. They move the way the fog moves: in slow, dense currents that part around obstacles and close again behind them. Nobody runs. Running is loud. It shakes things, and a running man on green stone is a man who either has not grown up here or is bringing very bad news. When someone does run, the whole street turns to watch him go, silently, the way deer lift their heads.

They are soft-footed. Almost everyone wears felt: felt boots, felt slippers, felted socks over bare feet in summer. The poor wear rag-wrapped sandals, and the rich wear shoes of layered moleskin so fine that they make no sound at all, even on the old ringing stone of the hardened districts. Children go barefoot as long as their parents let them, and longer, because the ground is warm and they like to feel it. You can tell a native by the way they set a foot down: heel first, slowly, weight rolled forward like someone testing ice. They do it on floorboards and on cured stone and on the decks of barges. They do it, visitors swear, in their sleep.

Their voices are the thing you notice once you notice anything. The public whisper is a real register, as distinct as a dialect, and it takes years to learn properly. It is not breathy. It is shaped almost entirely by the lips and the front of the tongue, with the throat held open and still, and it carries surprisingly far through fog without ever rising in pitch. Hawkers use a variant of it that is almost a hiss, clipped and sibilant, so that a busy market sounds like rain on leaves. Lovers use another, lower, that is barely air. Mothers have a whisper so sharp and flat it can stop a toddler at forty feet. Among themselves people call it "speaking down," as in down toward the ground, and a person who speaks up is not only rude but dangerous.

Everyone signs. The hand-language began as a trade code in the spinning halls, where the looms hiss too loudly for whispers, and it spread outward until it became the second language of the city and, for many, the first. It is fast and elegant and full of local wit. The sign for *stranger* is a flat palm pressed downward and lifted, as if testing whether a surface will hold. The sign for *friend* is two hands braced against each other at the fingertips, leaning, the way the houses lean. The sign for *home* changes every few years, because it used to be a closed fist, a solid thing, and the young no longer find that true. The current sign is an open hand turning slowly over, palm down, palm up, palm down. It means *home*. It also means *this week*.

## The Levellers

Every household has one person whose real work is keeping the house true.

It is usually whoever wakes first. At dawn they climb to the roof with the family's spirit level, a long brass-bound tube of oil with a single bubble in it, and they sight along the ridge-beam, and along the neighbors' ridge-beams, and they read the fog for signals from the next roof over. Then they go down into the crawlspace with the wrenches, and there is a quarter hour of quiet ratcheting as the jacks are wound up or eased down, a turn here, half a turn there, while the rest of the family sleeps on in a house that tilts by imperceptible degrees back toward level around them.

The level is the closest thing the city has to a household god. Good ones are passed down. They are named, sometimes, and kept wrapped in felt in a niche by the door, and they are the first thing carried out when a district floods. Many people wear a tiny one at the throat, a glass vial the length of a fingernail with a bubble of air trapped in oil, and when they are anxious they touch it without looking, the way people elsewhere touch a cross or a locket. To say someone's bubble is centered is to say they are sane, well-rested, honest in business. To say a man lives off true is to say he drinks, or lies, or has stopped jacking his house and is letting it lean on his neighbors.

Because that is the real etiquette of the city: who leans on whom. The houses are built touching, braced against one another at the eaves, and when one settles faster than the others it hangs a little of its weight on the buildings beside it. That is permitted. It is expected, even, for a season. But it creates a debt, and the debt is remembered. Whole feuds, generations long, are about lean. Marriages are arranged partly by it. Families whose houses have held each other up through a bad subsidence become something like kin, and on the rooftops at dawn their levellers will signal each other not just *raise two turns* or *hold* but small private things, jokes, news of a birth, grief, in a vocabulary of flicked fingers that no one else on the street can read.

## The Children of Wet Ground

There is a generation now, the first, that has never lived on any other kind of land.

Sixty years is long enough. The oldest children born on precipitate are grandparents themselves, and there are three and four generations who have never seen a hill, never heard thunder echo off anything, never stood on ground that was older than their grandmother. They are not quite like the people who came in from the old coast. The differences are small and everyone notices them.

They run warm. Their hands are always hot, and they cannot bear to sleep off the floor. The old immigrant families bought beds and set them on legs, as their parents had, and their children dragged the mattresses down onto the stone every night until the parents gave up. Now almost no one in the city sleeps raised. Rooms are carpeted in thin felt mats and people lie directly on the warmth, and in the cold months whole families sleep in a heap in the centre of the lowest room, over the part of the floor where the heat comes through strongest.

They are quiet in a way their parents had to learn and they did not. A native child will cry, as all children cry, but the crying of a baby born here is a low, moaning, closed-mouth sound, almost a hum, and midwives say that newborns on fresh ground do not scream at birth. They draw breath and hum. Mothers from the old coast find this frightening. Mothers born here find it natural and think the other kind of baby, the screaming kind, must be in pain.

And they listen to the floor.

Every native child does it. At a certain age, three or four, they begin lying with one ear pressed flat to the stone, very still, for long minutes at a time. Asked what they are doing, they say they are listening. Asked what to, they shrug, or they say *the hum*, or they say nothing, because it is obvious and they do not have a word for it. Most children grow out of it by seven or eight, the way children elsewhere grow out of imaginary friends. Some do not.

## The Listeners

The ones who do not grow out of it become valuable.

The Survey calls them *tuners* in its paperwork, and the rest of the city calls them *listeners*, and they are the only people in the flatland who are paid to make noise. A listener walks out onto new ground as soon as it will bear weight, barefoot, carrying nothing, and stands for a long time with their eyes closed. Then they begin to hum. They start very low, well below any note that could harm the stone, and they rise slowly, a quarter-tone at a time, their bare feet flat on the grey slurry, feeling for the moment when the ground answers.

Every patch of precipitate has a note. It is the pitch at which it will shatter, but it is also, listeners insist, the pitch at which it is most itself, the note it is already faintly sounding, all the time, under everything. A good listener can find it and stop just short, a hair below, and hold there, and the ground will begin to hum back in sympathy, a vibration felt in the teeth and the soles of the feet rather than heard. When they have it, they sing it out to the Survey clerks, who write it down, and the district is zoned by its note. Heavy foundries go on the deep-noted ground, which is hard to break. Spinning halls, with their hiss, go on ground whose note is far from sibilant. Markets go wherever the note is low and broad and forgiving. Bells were banned everywhere, but a bell-foundry in the old days, before the dropped bottles and the lost four acres, stood on ground that was noted at a pitch no bell could reach, and it never cracked.

Listeners are odd people. They are always a little distracted, because they are always hearing something. They speak down more quietly than anyone, and they sign a private dialect among themselves. They tend to marry each other. They tend to live on the newest ground, where the hum is strongest, and when a district begins to cool and settle they are the first to move away, restless, before anyone else has noticed a thing. They are useful as an early warning for that reason, and people watch where the listeners are going the way farmers elsewhere watch birds before a storm.

They say, when they can be made to say anything, that the hum is getting lower.

Not on any one patch of ground. Every patch has its note and keeps it until it cures. But across the city, year on year, generation on generation, the notes of new districts are coming out deeper. The Survey has the records, sixty years of them in the careful handwriting of clerks, and the drift is real and steady and no one has explained it. The first districts, the ones now drowned in the west, were noted high and thin, like struck glass. The newest ground in the east is noted so low that some listeners can barely reach it. It is felt more than heard, a slow pressure in the chest.

Old listeners say it is sounding more like a voice.

## The Ones Who Stay Behind

Go west, far enough, and the city runs out.

It does not end; it softens. The buildings lean further and further off true, and the jacks are wound out to their limits and then abandoned, and the streets become canals, and the canals become lagoons with the roofs of houses standing in them like islands. The moss gives way to reeds. The ground is cold here. The Condensers left long ago, and the precipitate has given up its borrowed heat, and a person standing on it feels only damp stone, the same temperature as the air.

People live here too. Not many. They are called the Settled, which is both a joke and not one.

They are mostly old: people who were born in these districts when the ground was warm and new, and who would not leave when it cooled. Some are widows and widowers who buried someone in the old ground and refused to go where the grave could not follow. Some are simply stubborn. They live on the upper floors of half-sunk buildings and fish for eels from their windows and travel by punt. They keep goats on the rooftops. They trade reed-baskets and dried eel eastward on the barges, and they take in, sometimes, people who have come west for reasons they do not want to explain.

They are the only people in the region who are loud.

On cold, cured, dying stone there is nothing left to shatter. Sound is safe. And so the Settled talk in ordinary voices, full-throated, and laugh out loud, and sing. They sing constantly. There are choirs in the drowned districts, a dozen old men in a flooded chapel standing waist-deep, singing in harmonies the rest of the city has forgotten how to make. Their voices carry for miles across the flat water, and on the western edge of the living city, on still evenings, people stop in the street and turn their faces toward the sound without speaking. Some of them weep. They could not tell you why.

Young people from the east make pilgrimages out here. It is a thing one does once, at sixteen or seventeen: takes a barge west to the reeds, climbs to a roof on some sunken building, and screams. Just screams, as loud as their lungs will allow, into the fog, with nothing to hear it but the herons. Then they go home, and speak down for the rest of their lives, and never mention it. The Settled watch the young ones come and go and do not interfere. Sometimes they bring them tea afterward.

And sometimes, rarely, one of the young ones stays.

## The Soft

Now go back east, to the newest ground, and wait for evening.

The bats come down out of the Condensers at dusk. People call them the *soft*, or *fog-mice*, or, among the older immigrant families, *the dry ones*, because although they come out of the wettest part of the air they are never damp to the touch. They are small, smaller than a sparrow, and their fur is the exact pale grey of the fog, so that when they pour down a tower leg in their long twisting ribbons, it looks as though the fog itself is sliding down the lattice and spilling into the streets.

They make no sound. Not a squeak. Not the dry leather rustle of ordinary bats. Children sometimes catch one by accident in a cupped hand, and they report that it weighs almost nothing and is very warm, warmer than a bird, and that if you hold it to your ear you can feel it humming.

They go into the houses.

This is the part that visitors do not believe, and that residents do not talk about except among themselves. At night, the soft come in through the eaves and the vents and the gaps around old window-frames, and they find the sleepers on the floors, and they settle on them. Not everywhere. Not on everyone. They seem to prefer the young. A family that sleeps in a heap will wake to find a scatter of them, three or four or a dozen, curled into the hollows of the children's bodies, pressed against the stone beside their ears, perfectly still. By dawn they are gone. They leave no droppings, no marks. The children do not wake.

Nobody is afraid of them. That is perhaps the strangest thing. The city is a place of constant low-level fear, of holding one's breath, of listening for the crack that means the ground is going, and yet no one fears the soft. Mothers do not chase them away. It is considered lucky if they come, and a little worrying if they never do. A house the soft avoid is a house where something is wrong: a cooling floor, a settling that has not been noticed yet, a bad lean. People move out of such houses.

The listeners say that the soft are humming the ground's note. That they come in to lie beside the children and hum it to them, very quietly, all night long, so that the children learn it.

What they eat up there on the copper mesh, nobody knows. The engineers have tried to find out. They have sampled the slurry and the fog before and after the mesh, and the precipitate at every stage of curing. Some of what goes missing from the air is water, and salt, and the grey mineral that becomes the stone. And some of it is not accounted for. It is a small fraction, and it varies with the season, and it is highest after storms that come in from the deep sea, off the old coast where the drowned towns are.

## What the Mesh Draws Out

The old coast went under before the Condensers came. That is the part the engineers explain least well, and the older residents remember best.

There was a coast here once, and towns on it, and those towns are forty miles out now and a hundred feet down. There was a long rising of the water and then a quick one. Most of the people who came to the flatland in the first years came from the old coast. They came with nothing. They settled on the first grey mud the first Condensers wept, because there was nowhere else to go. They built the first high thin-noted districts, now drowned, and sang in them, and buried their dead in them.

Ask one of the very last of them, and there are only a handful left, the oldest of the Settled out in the western reeds, what the Condensers make the ground from, and they will tell you, flatly, as if it were obvious: *from the old coast.*

Everything that drowns dissolves. Given time, given salt, given cold water and tides, it all goes to solution: timber and bone and brick and iron, harbor walls and churchyards, gardens, kitchens, the grain in the granaries and the dogs in the yards. It is all in the sea now, in a fine thin suspension. The sea breathes it out as fog, and the fog rolls in over the flatland, heavy and cold, and the copper mesh catches it.

And it comes out as ground. Warm ground. Warm, the old ones say, because it remembers being lived in.

The engineers do not say this, not officially. But the Survey keeps its records, and the records show that the ground is warmest, and the note is lowest, and the moss grows fastest, where the fog came in off the deepest water, from over where the largest of the old towns used to be.

## The Burials

People here bury their dead in slurry.

It began as necessity; there was no old ground in the early years to dig into. Now it is custom, and it is beautiful, and it is the one time the city makes a public sound.

When someone dies, their family carries them east, to wherever the newest ground is being laid, to the edge where the grey mud creeps out across the tidal flats. They wrap the body in felt and moss, and at the edge of the steaming lobe, where the precipitate is still soft as porridge and warm as bathwater, they lay it down. The slurry takes it slowly. Over an hour the body sinks, and the family stands and watches, and at the moment it goes under a listener hums, very low, the note of that patch of ground. The whole family hums with them, and anyone standing nearby, strangers included, joins in. It is the only time in the city when a crowd makes a sound all together, and it is held a hair below the breaking pitch, so that the new ground trembles under their feet and does not break.

Within a week the stone has cured over the grave. Within a month there are houses on it. Nobody marks the spot. Everybody remembers it anyway.

The ground over the dead is warmer. Everyone knows this. It holds its heat longer after the Condensers move on, and it settles more slowly, and its note is deeper. When a district is laid over one of the big burial grounds, people pay extra to live there. The soft come thickest to those houses. The children who grow up in them become listeners more often than other children.

And the whole city, all of it, sits on a sixty-year-deep layer of the dead of the old coast, dissolved and condensed and returned. Beneath that, and threaded all through it, lie the dead of the flatland itself, buried in their turn, sinking a little as each district drowns behind the city, the whole mass creeping slowly eastward like a tide coming in backward.

## What Lives Here

Here is how it looks, from above, on the rare clear noon when the sun comes through and the Condensers stand suddenly black and innumerable across the emerald land.

It is one thing, and it is moving.

Its body is forty miles long and barely raised above the water, warm along its eastern edge and cold along its western, and it is walking out to sea. It grows ahead and dies behind, the way a coral reef does, or a slime mold, or a forest over a very long time. The Condensers are its mouths; they take in fog and give out ground. The moss and the fern and the alder roots are something like a skin, and the canals are something like veins, and its heat is the warmth of the drowned. The heat rises through it into the feet of two million people who stand on it at every moment, heel-first, carefully.

Its nerves are the listeners. They feel its note and tell it back to itself.

And the people are, perhaps, what it is doing with the heat. They stand in it. They sleep on it. They are born on it humming, and they die into it humming, and in between they keep it true. They wind their jacks at dawn and hold their neighbors up and speak down and do not run. They make it markets and chapels and noodle stalls, a thousand small warm habits of living. Or perhaps it is the other way round, and it is keeping them. Nobody can tell which, and nobody here feels much need to decide.

What it is becoming, nobody knows.

The notes keep getting lower, and more like a voice. The soft keep coming down at dusk and lying beside the children and humming to them all night long, teaching them something. Every generation of listeners is larger than the last. The children of the newest districts, when they press their ears to the floor, no longer say *the hum*. More and more of them say *talking*. Asked what it is saying, they say, patiently, as if to someone very slow, that it is not saying anything yet.

That is what the quiet is. That is why two million people in an industrial city with the machines wrapped in felt and the bells all melted down can walk its streets all morning without once raising their voices. It is not only fear of the crack. It is not only care for the green stone.

It is a crowd holding its breath because it is waiting to hear something.

And somewhere, tonight, on the newest ground at the eastern edge, where the slurry is still steaming and the Survey's colored flags are still standing in the mud, a child will lie down on a warm felt mat in the lowest room of a house that was built a week ago. She will press her ear to the floor, and the soft will come in through the eaves and settle in a ring around her head. The ground under her will be so new that it has not finished curing, so warm it is almost like skin. It was made, this morning, out of fog that came in from over the deepest water, where the largest of the old towns lies drowned.

She will lie very still. She will listen.

Under her, very faintly, the ground will grow. The note will sink a little lower, and a little lower, and hold there, a hair above the breaking point, like someone gathering breath before speaking.
