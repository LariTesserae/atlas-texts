# Who Lives in the Laid Flats

The easy answer is *no one*. You could walk for a week and never be looked at, never startle anything, never find a track that wasn't your own. The description says it plainly: no animals, no insects, no crabs, no worms, no birds.

That answer is wrong, though. The place isn't empty. What lives there is so thin, so slow, and so large that it's easy to miss. It is under your feet the whole time, and it is also what your feet are standing on.

## The first answer: the green skin

Start at the surface, in the olive-green layer where the light falls.

Here live the light-eaters. They are filaments, each a chain of cells far thinner than a hair, bundled into ropes and wefts and tangles. They are cyanobacteria, the oldest makers of oxygen, and they do not sit still. Under a lens you would see them *glide*, sliding through their own secreted slime with no visible limbs, a few microns a second. That motion is too slow for an eye, but it is steady and purposeful.

They move because the light changes. At dawn, when the dew swells the mat and the sun is still low and kind, the filaments glide upward toward the surface to drink. At noon the light turns vicious, hot enough to bleach them and bright enough to shred their pigments. So they retreat downward a fraction of a millimeter, into the shade of their own dead sheaths and the carbonate grains they have trapped. In the late afternoon they rise again. At night they sink.

That means the green of the flats is not constant. It breathes. In the first hour after sunrise the plain is at its greenest, because the whole population has climbed to the top. At midday, under the glare, the colors wash out partly because the living green has pulled back underground. A person watching the plain all day is watching trillions of filaments moving up and down together, like a tide that runs vertically and never goes more than the width of a grain of sand.

The ticking across the lagoons in the afternoon is them. It is oxygen they have made, gathered into bubbles under the mucus film until the bubbles break free. It sounds like rain that isn't falling. It is really the sound of a whole subcontinent exhaling.

## The second answer: the city beneath the skin

The green skin is only the top floor.

Below it is the thin purple line. Here live bacteria that also eat light, but a different part of it: the deep reds and infrared that pass through the green layer unused. They do not make oxygen, and oxygen poisons them. They live on sulfide rising from below, turning it into sulfur and storing it inside themselves as tiny glittering granules. A purple bacterium is a lamp that runs on rot.

Below them, in the pinkish band, live others that take the light scraps even the purple ones cannot use.

At the bottom, in the black layer that smells of eggs, live the ones who never see the sun at all. They are sulfate-breathers. They eat whatever sinks down to them: the slime, the dead filaments, the leaked sugars. They breathe the sulfate the sea brought in, the way you breathe oxygen, and exhale the sulfide that feeds the purple layer above. Their work is also what helps the carbonate crystallize. In the darkness at the bottom of the living mat, these eaters of the dead are the ones actually laying the stone.

So the mat is not one kind of creature. It is a vertical city a few millimeters tall, with every floor feeding the floor above or below it. The top layer makes food and oxygen. The bottom layer eats the leftovers and makes sulfide. The middle layers stand exactly where the two poisons meet and live on the boundary. Nothing is wasted. Everything that falls is caught by someone.

This city turns over every day. At noon the upper layers are drenched in oxygen, more than the air holds, and the sulfide is pushed down deep. At night the light-eaters stop, everyone keeps breathing, and the oxygen is used up within minutes. Then the sulfide rises through the dark, all the way to the surface, and the mat turns anoxic top to bottom. Twice a day the whole world of the mat flips between two chemistries, like a house whose rooms trade places at dusk and dawn. Every inhabitant has learned to survive both states or to move between them.

## The third answer: one body

Keep watching and the individual cells start to blur.

These organisms trade genes. Not only parent to offspring, but sideways: neighbor to neighbor, species to species, through viruses and loose strands of DNA drifting in the slime. A useful trick learned in one terrace pool can spread to a lagoon a hundred kilometers away, carried by sheet floods and storm spray and the slow creep of the mats themselves. Over long spans the plain behaves less like a population and more like a single enormous genome spread thinly across a thousand kilometers. It is always editing itself, always testing variations in one basin and keeping what works.

Then the scale stops being microscopic. What lives in the Laid Flats is one creature the size of a subcontinent, a few millimeters thick.

Its skin is the green layer. Its gut is the black layer. Its bones are the rock it has laid beneath itself. The rimstone terraces are its cupped hands, built to hold the warm seep water, and it overflows from one hand into the next. The gypsum margins are its dried sweat. The levees along the sheet-flood channels are its own raised lips, grown so the water will run where it will be most useful.

It has no center and no edges that matter. Cut away a square meter and it does not notice. The rest keeps working. Where a storm tears the mat, filaments glide back into the wound within days, and the tear becomes one more lamina, one more line in the record.

## The fourth answer: it makes its own weather

The creature doesn't stop at the ground.

Every afternoon the plain heats up and the lagoons steam, and the storms rise out of that heat. The mats are part of how that happens. Their darkness and wetness shape how the ground takes up the sun. Their slime holds the dew. The shallow brines they line evaporate into the columns of rising air. The storms are not simply weather passing over the creature. They are partly its breath, lifted high, cooled, and dropped back down twenty minutes later as warm rain.

When the rain comes, the mat drinks. The fresh water washes away the day's crust of salt. The filaments swell and glide. By evening the steam rises from its surface in long orange sheets. Seen that way, the evening steam is heat and moisture leaving a body at the end of a day's work. The far-off thunder is sound from its outermost reach, the part of it made of air.

## The fifth answer: it lives only in the present, and remembers everything

The creature lives in the top few millimeters. That is all. The olive, purple, pink, and black layers are thinner than a coin. Everything below is where it used to live.

The stromatolites are its old selves, stacked. Every lamina is one day or one tide: a morning when the filaments rose, an afternoon when a storm dimpled the mud, a night when the sulfide climbed. The creature never reads this record. It cannot. It has no way to look backward. Its only act is to add the next page. Still, the record is exact. Somewhere in a table-high dome in a basin no one will ever visit, there is a lamina for a rainstorm eleven thousand years ago, with the raindrop pits still in it.

This is what makes the creature strange. Every animal carries its memory inside itself and loses it when it dies. This thing carries nothing and forgets nothing. Its memory is outside it and below it. Its memory is the ground.

You could say it has never died, because no cell in it is older than a few weeks while the pattern is older than calendars. You could just as well say it dies every day, because every day the bottom of the living layer turns to stone. Both are true. It lives by entombing itself, one film at a time. Living and fossilizing, which are opposites almost everywhere else, are here one continuous motion.

## Its enemies, and its neighbors

It is not entirely without predators. There are viruses, which drift through the slime and burst cells open, and they are the only hunters in the Laid Flats. They are also partly how the creature passes genes around, so even its disease is a kind of conversation. It has other hardships: noon glare, salt beyond bearing where the lagoons retreat, the drying that cracks it into polygons. But nothing *eats* it. Nothing crawls or chews or burrows. Nothing turns its pages out of order.

That is the fragile, lucky condition it lives under. Elsewhere, on shores where grazers have arrived, creatures like this survive only in hot and bitter refuges where nothing else can follow. Here there has never been anything to hide from. It has had a subcontinent to itself, and it has used every square meter.

It has a few neighbors, and they are guests. The succulents stand in their loose, untouching circles. They are jointed and translucent, red at the tips, and they grow where the seeps keep the ground fresh. Their seeds and pollen ride the wind, since there is nothing else to carry them. They draw on the stone the mat built, and they shade a little mat beneath them in return. The green algae stream from the terrace rims like hair. Neither threatens the creature. They are simply the tallest things it has allowed to grow.

## What it is, finally

At first it seems the answer should be a creature *on* the Laid Flats, something that walks the plain, the way a question like this usually has a face at the end of it.

The flats have no such thing. What lives there is the laying itself, the verb in the place's name. It has no body separate from its home, because its home is its body, and the part of its home that isn't body yet soon will be. It moves up and down each day by a fraction of a millimeter, and over centuries it rises by the height of a table. It breathes storms. It sweats salt. It builds the channels its water runs in, the basins its water rests in, and the ground it rests on.

It does not know it is there. It has no eyes, no voice, and no single moment of experience anywhere in its thousand kilometers. But at dawn, when the dew beads on every surface and the plain glitters silver, the filaments are already climbing toward the light. In a few hours the ticking will start across the lagoons. By evening the steam will rise.

It is unfinished, like the place it has made. The two are the same thing, and neither has begun to be taken apart.
