# Those Who Keep Moving

## I. The people, as a stranger first sees them

A marsh-trader poling in through the reeds at dawn sees the people of Weepstone before she sees their faces. She sees their hands.

Everyone here has the same hands: pale-knuckled, the skin of the palms crosshatched with fine white lines like frost on slate. These are the scars of the crystal teeth, collected from the first year of crawling onward. A Weepstone child's first cut is marked. Its mother presses the bleeding palm to the wall where it happened and holds it there for the space of a hundred drips, so the stone knows the child and the child knows the stone. After that nobody counts. By adulthood a person's palms are a record written in white, and old people's hands have gone smooth and hard, faintly shining, the way the older walls go smooth. The marsh villages call them *chalk-hands*, not always kindly.

The rest of them is harder to see, because they rarely hold still long enough.

They are short, mostly, and slight, built for stairs that climb through bedrooms and doors that have narrowed a finger's width since last winter. They are pale in the way cave-dwellers are pale, though they are not cave-dwellers. The light here is simply too soft to darken anyone. Lime dust settles in the creases of their elbows and the lines around their eyes, so even young faces look drawn in chalk. Their hair is kept short or bound tight, because hair left damp against a wall will stiffen by evening. Everyone knows the story of the girl who fell asleep leaning in a doorway and had to be cut free with a copper blade, leaving a braid in the jamb that is still there, sheathed and grey, and that brides touch.

The trader notices most of all that they fidget.

It is not nervousness. It is closer to good manners. A Weepstone woman waiting her turn at a cistern shifts her weight from foot to foot. A man listening to a long argument taps two fingers against his thigh. Old men sitting together in the courts rock slightly on their heels, all of them, out of rhythm with one another, like reeds in uneven wind. When the trader sits down on a ledge to rest, someone will eventually, politely, nudge her foot.

*Water must keep moving.* The law was made for water. It has soaked into the people.

## II. The small customs of motion

Once you notice it, you see it everywhere.

They sleep in hammocks of woven rush slung between grown hooks, never on the floor, and every sleeping room has a cord that someone gives a tug when they pass in the night, so that no sleeper lies still until dawn. Mothers rock infants for hours, and a child who has been rocked too little is said to be "going quiet," which is an insult and also a real worry.

Every door must be walked through. Households keep the count on a knotted string, and the youngest children have the job of *walking the doors*: running a loop through every threshold in the family's rooms each morning, touching each jamb, so the sills do not thicken and the frames do not bud. A family's doors are a measure of its health. A widow living alone in rooms made for twelve cannot walk them all, and her house slowly closes around her, room by room. Neighbors' children are sent to walk them for her. That is how charity looks here: a small boy running in one door and out another, shouting a greeting as he goes, never stopping.

They speak softly, because the stone carries voices strangely and a raised word lands in someone else's kitchen. But they speak continually. Silence between two people is not comfortable here, as it can be elsewhere. It is closer to rudeness, or to grief. When there is nothing to say, they hum. The courts at evening are full of low humming, many tunes at once, blurring into the drips.

The drips have given them their music, and more than that. Every child is given a drip. When it is born, a warden holds it under a chosen fall of water in its family's oldest room, and the note that drip makes as it strikes its pool becomes the child's note. It is not quite a name, but it is close. Lullabies are sung in that key. Lovers learn each other's notes. In the dark of the lower tunnels, where the seal-fat lamps don't reach, people find one another by humming their notes back and forth through the stone, and a mother can locate a lost child three walls away by the sound alone.

When someone dies, their drip is not given to another child for a generation. That is how long it takes for it to become merely water again.

## III. The wardens, closer

The flume-wardens are different, and the difference shows.

Their hands are worse than anyone's. Each dawn they plunge them into the fastest, freshest channels to feel for clogs, and new crust is sharpest. A warden's palms are not white-lined but white entire, calloused into something between skin and shell. They carry wooden rakes worn to the shape of their grip. They wear rush capes stiff with lime, which crackle when they walk, so a warden is always half-heard before she is seen: a faint ticking coming up the alley, like the seaward terraces at dusk.

Consider one of them, a woman called Ysse. There is nothing special about her, and that is the point. She is fifty-some winters old and has walked the same seven channels since she was nine. She knows the voice of every drip along her route the way a shepherd knows faces. She can tell by ear when a flow has slowed, when a clog is forming two turns upstream, when someone has pinched a channel with clay at night to steal a little growth toward their own wall. She does not accuse. She walks to the place and turns the water, and the next morning the thief's wall is dry, and everyone understands.

Wardens do not marry, as a rule, or not officially, and they do not own rooms. They sleep in the flume-houses beside the cistern courts, small grown chambers the water passes directly through, a channel running down the middle of the floor. They say they could not sleep anywhere quiet. Ysse says the water keeps her company. She says it the way you'd speak of a dog, or a difficult husband.

People fear the wardens a little, love them a little, and marry their children away from them if they can. Everyone knows what wardens become.

## IV. The eels

In the bottom of each cistern court lives an eel.

People say each cistern *keeps* one, but the phrasing is slippery, and it is never quite clear who is keeping whom. The eel is placed there as a glass elver, so small and clear you can see its heart, netted out of the marsh on a spring night by children with torches. It is dropped into the pool and fed. It grows.

It grows for a very long time. An eel in a cistern does not do what eels elsewhere do. It never gets the call to the sea, or it gets it and cannot answer, and so it simply goes on. The eel in the Low Court is said to be a hundred and forty years old. It is as thick as a man's forearm and as long as he is tall, and it has gone pale. The eels all go pale in time, their olive-brown fading to the grey of the walls. The oldest have begun to take the stone at their edges: a faint crust along the fin-rims, a roughness on the tail-tip, a white collar of lime at the gills that they scrape off against the cistern floor in slow, deliberate turns. Their eyes cloud. It hardly matters. They know the pool.

They are fed first at every festival. They are told news. A marriage agreed in a court is not binding until the couple has leaned over the pool and said it to the eel. When the eel is seen coiled and still for more than a day, the whole court goes into something like mourning, because an eel that stops moving is dying, and a court whose eel has died has lost its luck. The water there is suspected, and families quietly redirect their flumes elsewhere. A new elver is placed, of course. But the old ones are remembered by name, and their bodies are laid in niches under drips like people, and they become long pale shapes in the stone like people do. Children cannot always tell which niche holds which.

The eels are not the only thing in the cisterns.

## V. What comes up from below

The springs rise from old limestone, very old, older than the marsh, older than the sea at this height. The water has been moving through the dark under the spur for longer than anyone can say, and it does not come up empty.

In the deepest cistern pools, if you lie on your stomach at the rim with no lamp and wait, you will see the white ones. They are tiny shrimp-things no longer than a fingernail, entirely colorless, eyeless, their bodies so thin the light passes through them. They rise in the springs from somewhere underneath, out of the lightless limestone, and drift in the cold fizzing water, moving their many small legs. The people call them *spring-ghosts*. The eels eat them. There are never many, but there are always some.

They mean that there is another world down there. The old stone under the new stone is not solid. It is threaded through with dark water, with passages the springs have eaten, with pools no one has ever seen, and something lives in all of it, pale and blind and patient. The town is a skin grown over a body it knows nothing about.

The wardens take the spring-ghosts seriously. When they come up in numbers, it means the deep water is moving differently, and the springs will shift, and the wardens start walking their channels at night. Children collect the ghosts in shell cups and try to keep them, and they always die within a day in the light. The children cry, and the wardens tell them that the ghosts go back down, that what dies in the light is only the part of them that came up. Nobody believes it. Everyone says it.

## VI. The ones who go quiet

Now it is harder to say what it is that lives here.

The law is that water must keep moving. The people have taken that into themselves: they rock, hum, walk the doors, tug the hammock cords. They fear stillness because stillness seals. And yet everyone in Weepstone ends up in the stone.

The dead are laid in niches under chosen drips. This is known. Visitors are told about it, with some pride. What is less often said is that not all of them are dead when they go.

The very old sometimes choose. It happens when a person's joints have stiffened past walking, when the lime in the water has done in their bodies what it does to everything else, which it does, slowly, over a lifetime. People here get stones in the kidneys. Their knuckles swell hard. The old complain that they can feel the town in their bones, and they mean it literally. Such a person may decide that they are done moving. They pick a room, usually an old one, deep in the core, and a drip, usually their own birth drip if it still runs. The family carries them there and settles them against the wall beneath it. And then they are visited.

Not mourned. Visited. Grandchildren come and sit with them and hum their note. They are fed for as long as they will eat. They are talked to. Over a season, sometimes two, they stop answering, then stop eating, and somewhere in there they die. Nobody marks the exact day, because it doesn't feel like the point. By then the drip has already begun its work. A grey film on the shoulders. A crust at the hem of the rush wrap. The family stops visiting when the face is no longer a face. Then they seal the door, or rather, they stop walking it, and let it close.

The Weepstone people do not call this dying. They call it *going quiet*, the same phrase they use for an under-rocked baby. It sounds like an insult and like a tenderness at once, because it is both. To go quiet is what you must never do, and it is what everyone does in the end, and doing it on purpose, with your family humming beside you, is the most honorable way.

This is what the marsh villages mean, when they are being unkind, by calling Weepstone folk *the half-stone*. It is also what the Weepstone folk mean, with no unkindness at all, when they say that their grandmothers are holding up the floor.

## VII. So, then: who lives here?

You could say the people. Six or seven hundred of them, chalk-handed, humming, never still. That is true.

You could say the eels. Older than families, pale as walls, fed first, told the news. That is true too.

You could say the spring-ghosts and the dark water they come from, the unseen body under the skin.

But stand in a cistern court long enough and the edges between these start to run.

The walls are grown from water guided by human hands. The water is ancient, from underneath. The people shape the water, and the water shapes the people: their hands, their bones, their customs of motion, their music tuned to drips. The eels keep the water clean and become stone. The people keep the water moving and become stone. The rooms are built from the dead, over the dead, around the dead. The town rises a little every generation because every generation lays itself down into it. The oldest parts of Weepstone are not merely old rooms. They are old people, old eels, old doors, old voices, all gone quiet together into a single pale mass, and new rooms grow on their shoulders.

Think of a reef, which is what it most resembles. A reef is a great dead stone and a thin living skin. The skin is made of small soft things, each building a little cup of stone around itself, each dying into the cup, the next generation building on top. No polyp thinks of itself as the reef. The reef does not think at all. And yet it is alive, the whole of it, growing toward the light with a slowness no single part of it could perceive.

The people of Weepstone are that skin. They are the part of the town that moves. They are the town's way of walking its own doors, keeping its own channels clear, choosing where it will grow next. Their humming is the stone's voice, if the stone has a voice. Their restlessness is the stone's one fear, turned outward and handed down. They carry it the way water carries lime, more than they can hold, and like water they give it back, slowly, wherever they come to rest.

The ticking of the seaward terraces at dusk, which they call *the town settling*: children are told it is only the crust drying, and it is. But ask the old people, the ones who have begun to feel the lime in their knuckles, and they will tell you something else. They say it is the sound of all the quiet ones, all the grandmothers and eels and sheathed children and doors grown shut, very gently turning over in their sleep. Because even the stone, here, cannot quite bear to lie still.

## VIII. The evening

It is dusk in the Low Court. The mist is coming back over the roofs. Somebody's small son is running the doors of a widow's rooms, in and out, in and out, shouting a greeting at each threshold. Ysse the warden is crouched at the channel mouth with her white hand in the water, listening. In the pool, the old pale eel turns, slowly, scraping its lime collar against the floor, and a single spring-ghost rises past it out of the dark and is not eaten.

Three walls away, in a room whose door has not been walked for a season, an old man sits under his birth drip. His granddaughter sits beside him, humming his note. He has not answered for nine days. There is a faint grey bloom on the backs of his hands, over the white lines of every cut he ever took, back to the first one, when his mother pressed his bleeding palm to the wall so the stone would know him.

It knows him. It is taking its time. It has always taken its time.

Outside, the terraces begin to tick.
