# What Lives in the Pans

## The First Sign

At first you would say that nothing lives here except what you can list: the shrimp, the frogs, the waders, the lizards, the wallabies thumping over stone at dusk. The plain is full, but it is full in the way a market is full. Everything is busy, everything is accounted for, and everything is small.

Then, on the third or fourth morning after rain, you notice the ripples.

A pan lies glassy in the early light, mist still hanging knee-high over it. No wind. No birds yet on this one, though you can hear them two pans over. And from somewhere near the middle, where the water is a hand's depth over custard clay, a ring spreads outward. It is not the tight *plip* ring of a beetle or a tadpole. It is a wide, slow swell, a long wave that takes a full minute to reach the sedge and barely lifts the stems when it arrives. Nothing broke the surface. Something beneath it moved, once, and settled again.

You wait. Nothing more happens. The mist burns off. By noon the pan is silver and the mirages have come up around it, and you can no longer be sure which water is real.

## Tracks

The next evidence is in the clay at the drying edges, where the shoreline has pulled back and left a scalloped tidemark of salt.

There are marks here that are not footprints. They are broad, shallow depressions, each about the size of a body lying curled, pressed into the soft margin and then abandoned. They are smooth-floored and faintly polished, and at their edges the clay has been lifted into a low lip, the way a thumb leaves a rim when it presses dough. They come in a line, but not a straight one. The line wanders, stops, doubles back a little, pools in one place for what must have been hours, then goes on toward the next pan.

It is the path that sheet-water takes. It moves at walking pace, stalls, fills a hollow, considers, and continues.

If you follow it far enough across the red pavement, and you will lose it often, because the laterite takes no impression, you come to a gnamma. It is one of the big ones, a basin wide enough to lie down in. Its walls are very smooth and very round, rounder than dissolution alone seems able to make them, and the tea-colored water in it is still swaying slightly, although there is no wind.

## The Shape of It

When you finally see one, it is evening, which is the land's best hour, and the low light is pulling every swell of the ground into relief.

What you notice first is that one of the swells is wrong.

It lies at the edge of a pan, half in the water and half out. It is long and low, no taller than a person at its highest point, and it has the whaleback profile of the eroded tors, with a rounded crown and flanks that slope into the clay. Its surface is laterite: rust-red, plum-dark, pitted like fruit rind. Where the rind has worn through along the spine, something paler shows, grainy and wet, the color of bone. In the copper light it looks like every other rise on the plain.

It is breathing.

The breath is very slow, a rise and settle so gentle that you only catch it by watching the waterline. The pan's edge creeps up the creature's flank a finger's width, holds, and slides back down. Up, hold, back. It happens perhaps twice in a minute.

It does not seem to have a face, or not one you can find. Near what might be the front, where the body tapers into the water, the rind is thinner and darker, and there are two shallow hollows in it. Each holds a small pool of sky. They might be eyes. They might just be places where rain collects. For a long time you cannot tell, and then the copper in one of them shifts, the way light shifts in a gnamma when something moves at the bottom.

## What It Is Like

Close up, when you are brave enough or patient enough to get close, and it does not seem to care either way, the creature is the plain in miniature.

Its back is crusted and hard and rings faintly if you tap it. Its sides peel. Thin curved sheets of its outer layer lift away and lie loose against it like shed bark, and if a wallaby hops across one, it rocks and gives a hollow clack. In the joints between those sheets, water weeps out in dark stripes, and in the stripes grow mats of blue-black algae, slick in the middle and papery at the edges. Resurrection moss is packed into every crack of it, velvet green now. Sundews grow along its seep lines, glistening. Small dark-backed lizards bask on its crown and flick into its crevices when a shadow passes, and the creature allows this as the rock allows it, without seeming to notice.

Underneath, where it lies against the pan bottom, it is warm. The clay around it is warmer than clay anywhere else, warm as blood, and this is where the shield shrimp are thickest, plowing the soft floor in crowds, and where the tadpoles school most densely. Life does not merely tolerate the creature. It gathers against it, the way it gathers at water.

It moves perhaps once a day, and the movement is not walking. It is a slow redistribution. The weight shifts from one end of the long body toward the other, the body lengthens in that direction and thins at the back, and after an hour it has gone a body's length. It moves like a sheet of water deciding. It moves like something that has never once been in a hurry, because nothing here has ever needed it to be.

## How Many

There are not many. You come to understand that there is roughly one for every lone tree, and that the distance between trees, the one the eye cannot comfortably judge, is the distance each of them keeps from the others. A circuit. Each creature travels among perhaps a dozen pans over the course of a wet season, lying a few days in each, turning slowly in the big gnammas along the way. The turning is what makes the gnammas round. The rock dissolves, yes, but it dissolves where it is worn, and it is worn where something heavy and patient turns in it, season after season, for longer than anyone has counted.

Their circuits overlap at the edges. Where two meet there is usually a very old pan with a very white floor. Once, from the top of a whaleback at dusk, you see two of them lying at opposite sides of such a pan, perhaps half a kilometer apart, while the frog chorus rises between them. You realize that the lowest part of the chorus, the pressure under the *bonk* and the ticking and the machine-trill, the part you felt in your chest and assumed was simply the sum of everything else, is not the frogs.

It is them. It is a sound too low to hear, only to feel. The water carries it the way it carries the choirs of distant pans, rising and falling out of phase. The frogs call over it the way birds sing over wind.

## What They Do

It takes weeks to see, because their work is measured in grains.

They carry things. Their peeling flanks and weeping seams are full of clay, and the clay is full of eggs: shrimp eggs, and the seeds of the pale star-leaved rosettes, and spores of moss. When a creature lies for three days in one pan and then spends a day moving to the next, it brings the first pan into the second. That is why each pan, isolated as it seems, is never quite alone. The fairy shrimp in one basin are cousins of the fairy shrimp in the next, because something slow and warm has been carrying them back and forth since before there were frogs.

They take the salt, but only a little. At dry-down you find them lying along the tidemarks with their front hollows pressed to the crust, and afterward there is a clean scallop where the white was. They never take all of it. The oldest pans are still the whitest. Perhaps they leave the salt as a record, or perhaps a thing that has learned to take a grain at a time simply cannot take more.

And they settle. This is the real work, and the hardest to see. When one of them lies somewhere for a few days, the ground there is fractionally lower afterward. The difference is less than the thickness of a fingernail. But the land sorts by millimeters. A fingernail decides where water pools, where the sedge ring grows, where the frogs gather, where the birds follow. Every wet season, the creatures lie down in a hundred places across the plain and press it, very slightly, into a new shape. That is why water here is unsure which way to go. The slopes keep changing, too slowly for any single rain to notice.

## Dry-Down

As the pans shrink, the creatures shrink with them.

This happens without drama. The seeps along their flanks slow, then stop. The algae dries to paper. The moss curls brown and then black, like burnt scraps. Their rinds darken and harden and ring more loudly under a lizard's feet. They move less. The breath slows until the waterline no longer shows it, and then there is no waterline, because each creature has followed its last pan to its lowest point and lies there among the final pools. Those pools are crowded with every living thing that needs them, the tadpoles packed black, the shrimp laying their last eggs in the warm clay against the creature's side.

Then the pools go, and the clay cracks. On the still noons, when you hear the pans ticking, some of that ticking is them: the small sharp sounds of the rind drawing tight as it cures in the sun.

By the end, where each creature lay there is only a rise. It is low, red, crusted, and no taller than a person, with a lone old tree somewhere near its crown, rooted in a crack that goes down very far, into something that still holds water. The tree drinks from it through the dry months, and the years it sometimes takes for rain to return. That is why the trees are so old, and so few, and so far apart.

## What They Were

You start to wonder about the mountains.

The land has run out of ways to fall. Whatever stood here was worn to its roots, and the roots were worn too. But you begin to suspect that "worn away" is not quite the right phrase, and that the mountains did not simply become grit and wash off somewhere. There is nowhere for anything to wash off to. Nothing here drains. Whatever the mountains were, most of it is still here.

Perhaps this is what happens to a mountain when it has been rained on long enough. It learns, over a span no one can count, that height is a kind of hurry, that standing up is a way of falling slowly. So it lies down. It softens its edges one sheet at a time. It gives up its peaks to the sky and its roots to the dark and keeps only its middle, which is warm and heavy and full of water. It becomes something that can lie in a pan with shrimp against its belly and moss on its back, and it spends the brief loud weeks of every wet season finishing the work the rain began: pressing itself, and the ground around it, down toward the level where everything is still.

Once, at evening, you see one stand.

It is only for a moment. The body gathers at one end, and the crown lifts, higher than the rise it has been. It is taller than a person, taller than the termite spires, as tall perhaps as the oldest tree. It holds there with water streaming off its flanks in the copper light, a shape the plain has not held in a very long time. The frogs stop, all of them, all at once, across the whole pan. The silence is like a door opening.

Then it lets the height go, slowly, as if setting something heavy down, and lies back into the clay. The ring spreads outward across the water, wide and patient, and reaches the sedge a full minute later. The chorus resumes.

## What They Are Waiting For

Everything here waits: eggs in the clay, seeds in the grit, frogs in their cocoons. But the creatures wait for something longer than rain.

The land is nearly finished. The forces that raise it and the forces that wear it have both gone quiet, and the surface is close to the last level it can reach. When it arrives there, when every swell has been pressed out and every hollow filled, there will be no more deciding. Rain will fall and stand everywhere at the same depth, one pan the width of the plain, silver from edge to edge, sky above and sky below with no seam of dark rock between.

You think they are waiting for that. You think it is what they have been making, a grain at a time, since they were mountains. When it comes, they will lie down in it together, all of them at once, level with everything, and there will be nothing left in them that needs to stand.

Until then they keep their circuits. They carry the shrimp between the pans. They turn in the gnammas. They lick a little salt from the tidemarks and leave the rest. They hum under the frogs.

And in the dry months, when a traveler crosses the plain and feels the ground tilt without being able to say which way, and pauses, uncertain, as the water does, that is one of them. Somewhere under the red crust, under the old tree, down in the deep crack where the water is kept, a mountain is turning over in its sleep.
