# Those Who Live at Hallow Col

## The first thing you see is a crowd

If you arrive at a shift change, and in Hallow Col you are nearly always close to one, the first inhabitants you meet are all of them at once.

From the concrete apron at the col you watch the mill gates open far below. What comes out at first looks like more fog: a grey flowing on the stairways, a thickening on the bridges. Then you understand that it is people, tens of thousands of them in undyed wool, moving up and down the stacked town like water finding its level. Nobody shouts. Nobody pushes, or not in a way you can see. The crowd has a grain to it, like wood. Upward traffic keeps to the wall side of every stair, downward traffic to the drop side, and the two streams pass each other within a hand's breadth for half an hour without friction.

Then the doors close and the stairs empty, and for a moment you can believe nobody lives here at all.

## Bodies

Up close they are not a remarkable-looking people, but they have been shaped by the place, the way the stairs have been shaped by them.

They are deep in the chest, as mountain people are, and they breathe slowly. Outsiders arriving at the col are winded by the second landing. A woman of seventy carrying a crate of leeks on her back will pass them without changing her breath. Their legs are hard from a lifetime of stairs. There is no such thing as a walk in Hallow Col that is not also a climb. They move sideways more than other people do, a slight turn of the shoulder that lets two of them pass in a lane built for one. You can spot a Col-born person in a lowland city by the way they turn in a doorway that is already wide enough.

They are pale. The sun reaches the valley floor two hours a day in summer and not at all in winter, and some people in the lower levels have never stood in direct sunlight except on the rare holiday climb to the col. Their eyes are good in dim light and bad in bright. On the few clear days they walk with their faces down and their eyes half shut. Their skin is faintly damp all the time, like everything else here. They smell of wet wool and hot metal, and under that of something green, the way a cellar smells when there is moss on the steps.

They look down when they walk, because the stone is always wet and liverwort is as slick as oil. This gives them, to an outsider, an air of modesty or grief. It is neither. It is only attention.

And they listen. This is the thing it takes longest to notice. Under the hum of the mills, a Col person is always half-listening to the water. They know the particular hiss of the leat that runs under their own floor, the way you know the breathing of someone who sleeps beside you. When the note changes, when a channel goes quiet that should be loud or loud that should be quiet, they wake. People have been seen sitting up in bed, stock-still, heads cocked, at three in the morning, before they are fully awake. Then they are out of the door in their nightclothes with a lamp and a rod, because a changed note means something has blocked, and something blocked means a wall is going to fail. A child can tell you from the sound of a drain whether it is running clear or running with silt. This is not taught. It is breathed in.

## Hands

They talk with their hands.

The mill signs began as a practical thing, because no voice carries over a rolling line. But they leaked out long ago and have grown into a whole second language, with a grammar the lowland linguists who occasionally come up the col road have never managed to write down. It is quick, close, small. Most of it happens at chest height, under cover of the body, because on a packed stair there is no room for anything larger. Two people passing in opposite streams at a shift change can hold a full exchange in the three seconds they are level: *your mother's roof — the gutter's gone — I told the warden — tonight?* *tonight.* And they are past each other.

Babies sign before they speak. Mothers sign to them from across a room, and the babies watch hands the way lowland babies watch faces. Old people, as their voices go, simply stop speaking and go on signing, and nobody treats this as a loss.

Some signs have no spoken equivalent. There is one for the sound of a building starting to go, the low grinding creak that comes a few seconds before the slump. It is a fist turned slowly against a flat palm, and when someone makes it everyone within sight moves before they have looked for the cause. There is one for *heavy above*, a flat hand pressed down on the crown of the head. It is used for a person who carries too much, and for a building overloaded by what's been added on top, and nobody treats those as different meanings.

And there is one sign you see more than any other, dozens of times an hour. The hand is held out flat, palm down, and drawn forward and down in a smooth falling curve, like water going over a sill. It means *going through*. It means the channel is clear. It is what a leat-warden signs up the stair when a blockage is cleared. It is also what you sign to a neighbour in the morning instead of *hello*, and what you sign at a bedside when someone has come through a fever, and, very quietly, what you sign at a funeral. It means *the water is moving*, and in Hallow Col that is very nearly the same as *all is well*.

## Trades

Everyone works for the mills, in the sense that everything here exists because of the turbines. But the people you notice are the ones who work on the town itself.

**The leat-wardens** walk the channels. Each has a length of water, a few hundred metres of runnel and drop and grate, which they know stone by stone. They carry iron rods with hooked ends and wear boots that come to the thigh. They are the only people in Hallow Col who are routinely listened to when they raise their voices. A warden can stop work on any building site in the valley by standing in front of it and saying *water*. They are mostly older women. The job was once given to widows of men killed in collapses, and it stayed with them.

**The shorers** go wherever something is failing. Crews of five or six work fast in a gang language of signs so compressed that other Col folk can barely follow it. They drive timber props under sagging vaults and chain terraces together with iron straps. They are the most admired people in the town and the most frequently killed. They wear wooden tokens around their necks carved with the name of their crew, not their own name. When one dies the token goes to the next in.

**The surveyors** are always a year behind, and know it, and have made a kind of melancholy dignity out of knowing it. They go about with plane tables and chains and ledgers wrapped in oilcloth. Their map of Hallow Col is the most accurate map of a place that no longer exists. They are consulted about the past more than the present. In the water courts they are the ones who say what used to be where, and who built first, and whose sky belongs to whom.

**The root-readers** are a branch of the surveyors, older than the guild itself. When a tree must come out, because it is splitting a wall or blocking a light well, a root-reader is called. They crawl the spaces under old roofs with a lamp and a long thin probe, reading which roots hold and which only take. They tap the stone and listen to it. They speak of trees the way doctors speak of a patient's heart. Most of the time they say no. The tree stays. The family adjusts.

**The linesmen** ride the ropeways. They sit in the empty buckets going up, tapping the cable with a small brass hammer and listening for the note of a failing strand, and ride down clinging to the outside of the full ones. They are the only people in Hallow Col who see the whole town from above on an ordinary day, the long dotted lines vanishing and reappearing in the fog below their boots. They are said to be a little strange. They are said to talk more than other people, and louder, as though they have forgotten the rule. Perhaps they simply spend too much time where the fog is thin.

**The moss-cutters** keep the gutters. They are mostly children and the very old, working with short curved blades and flat baskets. They cut the felted green sheets back from the drains but leave the slopes, because the slopes are carrying. They sell the cut moss to the lower levels, where it is packed into wall cavities for warmth and into wounds as a dressing, and dried and burned in the tenement stoves on the coldest nights. It burns slow and blue and smells of old rain.

## Levels

Nobody in Hallow Col says where they live. They say what level they live on, and what's on them.

*I'm fifth on Tanner's, three on me.* This means you have a floor five structures up from the culvert on the terraces called Tanner's Rise, with three more above you. You pay one of those to carry your drainage and are paid by the one beneath you to carry theirs. Your sky belongs to someone else. Your floor is someone else's ceiling. You own a footprint in the air.

It shapes the way they think about themselves, I believe. A Col person does not imagine themselves standing on the earth. They imagine themselves standing on someone and being stood upon. The obligations run up and down, not sideways. You do not have neighbours so much as you have *aboves* and *belows*. The worst insult in the town, signed or spoken, is to call someone a *blocker*: a person who takes what comes down to them and doesn't let it through.

The higher you live, the cleaner your water and the better your light, and the more people you are resting on. The upper terraces grow kale in the gutters and sit in the two hours of summer sun. They are envied, but they are also watched, and there is an old unease in how the lower town speaks of them. *Heavy above*, they sign, hand on head, and it is not quite a joke.

The lowest levels are another country. Below the fourth, where the water turns bad and nothing edible grows, live the culvert-folk. They are the wardens of the deep drops and the keepers of the turbine intakes, and the families who have lived for generations in the vaults beside the buried river because the rent is nothing and the warmth of the smelter flues seeps through the stone. They are paler even than the rest. Some of them have a look the upper town finds uncanny: very large pupils, very still faces, a way of turning their heads slowly toward a sound. They grow ferns on their windowsills, great arching shuttlecocks that lean toward the sodium lamps the way the people do. They sign in a dialect of their own, slower and more sweeping, made for dark rooms where you have to see a hand against a lamp.

It is from the culvert-folk that the stories about the river come. I will come back to that.

## The dead

There is no ground in Hallow Col, so there is nowhere to bury anyone.

For a long time I assumed they sent their dead down the col road to some lowland cemetery. Some do, those who can pay the lorry fare. But most do not, and the custom they keep instead explains, I think, more about these people than anything else.

They lay their dead up.

On the oldest terraces, the ones with birch woods growing on their roofs, the moss is a hundred years deep. It is soft and black at the bottom, living and green at the top, a sponge a metre thick resting on slate that rests on vaults that rest on other vaults. When someone dies, the family carries them up, wrapped in the wool they wore, and the root-reader shows them a place. They open the moss and lay the body in it, and close the moss over. They plant a rowan, or push a birch whip into the moss above the head. Then they go home.

Within a year the moss has taken the place back. Within ten the tree is taller than a man. Its roots have gone down through what was laid there and into the slate beneath, gripping, knitting the stones together.

*Don't cut what's carrying you.*

I heard that saying a dozen times before I understood that it was not only about trees. The oldest terraces in Hallow Col, the ones that should have fallen long ago, are held up by roots grown through the dead. Everyone knows this and nobody says it aloud. You can walk through a roof-wood on a foggy evening among the thin pale trunks and understand that every one of them is a person. It is not frightening. The wood is very quiet, quieter even than the rest of the town, and it smells of wet bark and leaf mould. The fog moves between the trunks at walking pace. Families come up on certain days and sit among the trees, and sign to them.

There are others who cannot be laid up: the ones lost under collapses, whose bodies cannot be reached. The ropeway does not stop, and shoring takes time, and sometimes the town simply builds over them. There is a sign for these as well, and it isn't sad exactly. It is two flat hands, one laid over the other. It means *in the level*. Everyone knows that the floors they walk on hold people. They know that the stair to the mill has a man in it, and the vault under the tram depot has a whole crew. Nobody who lives here thinks of the town as made only of stone.

And the funerals must wait for the ropeway. This is the rule. The buckets do not stop for anything but a broken cable. When a procession reaches a crossing, it stops and stands in silence while the buckets pass overhead: ore, ore, coal, empty, ore, knocking gently as they go over the gantry wheels. The waiting was a hardship once. It has become the centre of the rite. The mourners count the buckets. The count is remembered and spoken of afterward: *he had forty-one*, *she had a long wait, near a hundred*. A long wait is thought to be good. It means the town took its time with you.

## Remembering

The old of Hallow Col walk routes that are no longer there.

You will see them on the stairways, an old man stopping at a landing and turning toward a blank wall as if there should be a way through, and there was. Forty years ago this was the top of the Cooper's Steps, which went down to the tram stop at Nine Wells. Then the Cooper's Steps went in the slip of '81, and the whole slope was built across, and the tram stop is under a rolling mill. He stands there a moment, facing the wall. Then he turns and goes the other way, which is longer.

They carry the old town in their bodies. They carry it more accurately, the surveyors admit, than the surveyors' own map. When a building is to go up, the surveyors will sometimes come and fetch an old person from a nearby level and walk them across the site. The old person tells them where the channels used to run, which ones were buried and never properly diverted, where the water will want to go. The water remembers too. It always goes back the way it used to go.

So in any stairway in Hallow Col there are at least three towns at once. There is the one you are standing in. There is the one on the surveyors' map, which is a year gone. And there is the one in the heads of the old, which may be sixty years gone, with streets and bridges and whole neighbourhoods that are rubble inside other buildings. The children, who learn the signs before they learn to speak, also learn the old names. They are told, walking past a wall: *that was the Laundry Stair; your great-aunt lived on the seventh.* They repeat the names. They will tell their own children, walking past whatever stands there then.

The town wears away at the rate it is rebuilt. But it is remembered, I think, at a slightly slower rate. There is always a margin, a ghost of some decades trailing behind the stone, carried in the minds of people climbing stairs. That margin may be the only thing in Hallow Col that doesn't turn over.

## Children

They are everywhere and you hardly hear them.

They play on the stairways between shifts, games of hand and eye that need no running space, since there isn't any. They play long silent games of signs, like a lowland child's game of whispers, a message passed hand to hand down a whole stairway and changed at every landing. They climb. Col children climb the way lowland children walk: up drainpipes, over the iron brackets of balconies, along the ledges of the mills. They fall less often than you'd think, and get hurt less than you'd think. The moss is deep and they are light.

Their favourite place is the grates. Every stairway has iron grates at its landings where the leats pass beneath, breathing up cold air and fine spray. You'll see a child lying on her stomach with her face to one, one hand spread flat over the bars, feeling the draught come up. She is listening. She is doing what her grandmother does in bed at three in the morning, what the wardens do, what the whole town does all the time without knowing. She is checking that it is going through.

After a while she sits up and makes the sign, the flat hand drawn forward and down over the sill, to nobody in particular.

## What they say lives in the river

The culvert-folk say the river is kept.

They don't mean the wardens. They mean something in the water itself, down in the vaults four levels under the valley floor, where the buried river runs black and fast between stone walls in a dark no lamp has ever wholly filled. They call it by a sign rather than a name: the flat *going-through* hand, but made slowly, twice, the second time lower. The upper town translates it, when it has to, as *the Let-Through*.
