# What Knocks Below

## I. What the children know

Start with the children, because they meet it first.

Before they can walk, they are laid on their bellies on the lee flank of the whaleback, cheek and palms and the soft of the stomach pressed to the wet stone, and they are taught to listen with the whole front of the body. At first there is only the breathing, which comes up through the rock as a pressure more than a sound, a slow swelling and easing like lying on the chest of something asleep. Then the rattle, a fine fizz in the skin. Then the taps, which are the first words most of them ever understand: the quick triple of *come eat*, the long-short-long of *someone is at the weir*, and the hard, spaced, terrible fives of *child lost*.

Somewhere between their second and fourth winters, most of them notice another kind of tap.

It comes from nowhere on the ridge. Everyone on the whaleback is visible, sitting at the drying racks or asleep in the smoke, and none of them has a knuckle to the stone. But the child's cheek is on the rock and the rock is saying something. The taps are faint and deep, much deeper than a hand could make, as if they had travelled a long way through a great deal of stone to get here. They come at the bottom of the inhale, in the long moan, when the air is rushing down into the throats.

The child goes to a grown-up and makes the sign they have all made at that age, the one that has no proper meaning yet: two fists, knocked softly together, then a finger pointing down.

The grown-up smiles, if it is a kind one, and makes the correct sign back. You bring two knuckles to your own breastbone and knock them there twice, where the heart is. Then you turn the palm flat and lower it slowly, as if pressing something down into water.

In speech it would be something like *the one who knocks below*, but it has never been spoken. It is only ever made against the chest.

## II. What the adults say it is

Ask adults on different whalebacks and you get different animals.

Some say it is a great eel, the mother of the pale ones, coiled in the deepest gallery and striking the walls with its skull. Some say it is a bird: one of the throat-divers that dove too deep a very long time ago, couldn't find the breath back up, and grew enormous and blind in the dark, and now it knocks on the ceiling of the world to be let out. Some say it is the dead. That explanation is common, and the people who hold it can't be argued with, because they have heard things.

A few of the old ones, the most deaf, the ones who can walk the flats blindfolded by the voices of the throats, say nothing at all when asked. They take the asker to the lip of a wide throat at the turn of the breath and have them lie down and put an ear to the stone, the ear that still works. Then they wait for the inhale.

What you hear is a series of knocks, slow and deliberate, like knuckles on dry wood. Then a pause. Then the series again, but not quite the same. It is always almost the same.

"It's counting," some of the old ones say.

"It's remembering," say the rest.

Both are right. Neither has seen it.

## III. What it is

Under the widest throats the honeycomb opens into galleries that fill and drain with every tide. Water floods them twice a day, pushing the air ahead of it and out, and empties them twice a day, pulling the air back in. Nothing the size of a person has ever been down there and come back. If one had, and had carried a light, they would first have thought the gallery walls were strangely regular. They are ribbed and shingled, overlapping, the way a rattler's back is.

They are a rattler's back.

Rattlers molt. Every few weeks a rattler splits its plates and crawls out soft, and it hides in the honeycomb until the new plates harden. The water here is so heavy with dissolved stone that everything it touches gets a skin. A rattler that lingers too long between molts will find its plates thickening, crusting and greying, becoming slightly more rock than shell.

Once, on a scale of time that makes the whalebacks look young, one of them didn't molt in time. Its plates stoned over faster than it could shed them. It couldn't get out, and it didn't die. The soft of it kept living under the stone, pressed thin between the plates, feeding the way the film feeds: on the minerals in the water, on the rattlers poured down the throats every evening, on whatever the tide brought. The plates kept thickening, and the soft kept spreading, working into the joints of the rock around it. Where stone ended and animal began stopped being a question anyone could answer.

That was the first one. There may be several now, one under each of the great throats. There may be only one, threaded through the whole honeycomb and joined in the dark under the plain.

Picture a gallery as long as a village is wide, its ceiling and walls lined with plates. Each plate is the size of a door, dark and banded like the ground above, hinged to the next by cords of pale gristle as thick as a wrist. Between the plates is a membrane the color of the film in the throat mouths, olive going to black, so thin that light would pass through it if light ever came. Along the edges of the plates the membrane is gathered into fine sieving fringes that comb the water as it floods in and comb the air as it drains out. No head or single mouth exists; nothing you could look at and say *there, that is where it looks out from*. It has no eyes. It has had no reason to.

The pale eels live among its plates. They are not hunted by anything because they live inside the one thing large enough to hunt them, and it doesn't. They slide along its membranes and keep them clean of the orange bloom that would otherwise smother them. They are its gut-flora, or its grooming birds, or its fingers. When the tide shoves one up a throat and strands it on the lip, that is an eel that strayed too far from the body.

## IV. How it hears

For most of the tide-cycle it is underwater or being scoured by wind, and it hears nothing much. At full flood the gallery is drowned and still. During the exhale, the air it lives in is roaring outward past it and up the throat, and the din of its own passage drowns everything else.

At the bottom of the inhale, everything changes. The water has fallen away, the air is being drawn down into the galleries from the surface, and the plain's sounds are drawn down with it. The rattle, the shriek of the colonies, and the knocking of stone on stone are all carried down the throat and along the galleries on the returning wind, and they pass over its plates.

The plates are lifted by the wind the way a fish's gills lift. For those hours it is open, and it hears.

It hears through the rock as well, at every hour. The plain is one stone, or near enough, and a knuckle on a whaleback sends a small pressure travelling through the whole slab. On its way the pressure crosses a great many plates that are also part of the stone, and the soft pressed between them feels it.

It has felt every tap the people ever made. It has felt *come eat* and *eel stranded at the west lip* and *someone is at the weir*. It has felt, more times than any other message, the hard, spaced fives of *child lost*, and the answer the searchers tap as they fan out into the fog, which is simply *here, here, here*, over and over, so the child can crawl toward the sound.

## V. How it remembers

This is the part that took the longest to understand, and nobody on the flats understands it. It is only true.

The water down there builds stone wherever it touches. It lays mineral onto the inner faces of the plates constantly, in fine layers like the rings of a tree or the banding of the ground. The layers are not laid evenly. The plates are always faintly trembling with whatever the soft between them is feeling, and a trembling surface takes on mineral in patterns: ridges where the shaking was strong, hollows where it was still, the way sand arranges itself on a drumskin. Every sound it hears is written into its plates as a thin, grooved seam of stone. Every tap that crosses the plain is written. Every song thrown down a throat at the turn of the breath is written.

It doesn't choose to do this, any more than the ground chose its banding. It is simply what happens to a listening thing made of stone in mineral water. Sound becomes rock.

Meanwhile, on the outer faces of the plates, the side that meets the open gallery and the rushing air, the film grows, as it grows everywhere, eating stone. It flakes and curls and takes a little of the plate with it. So each plate is built up from within and worn away from without, and every seam of written sound travels slowly outward through the thickness of the plate, year by year, toward the face where the film is eating.

When a seam reaches the surface, the membrane feels it.

In the hours of the inhale, when the plates lift, the soft runs its fringes along those surface grooves. It is not feeding. It is doing the only thing it knows how to do with a pattern, and closing the plates in time to the ridges as it passes over them.

Knock. Knock. A pause. Knock-knock-knock.

It is reading aloud whatever is about to be erased.

## VI. What it says

The lag, the time a seam takes to travel from the inner face to the outer, is about a lifetime, give or take, depending on the plate and the throat. That is why the people who say it is the dead are not wrong.

A woman, deaf in one ear and long past the age of having children, lies at the lip of the wide throat her people call Low Mother, whose voice is a boom felt behind the eyes. She puts her good ear to the stone at the bottom of the inhale and hears the long-short-long of *someone is at the weir*, tapped in a rhythm she knows. Everyone taps a little differently. The weight of the knuckle, the hurry or patience between strokes, is as individual as a walk. This is her father's tap. He has been dead thirty winters. She didn't know it could be kept.

A boy hears his own name-sign, which is a tap-pattern as well as a gesture, tapped in a hand no one living recognizes. It was given to him because it was his great-grandmother's name. He is hearing her being called to dinner.

An old man hears, among the clicks, a fragment of a song. It is not the words. Words don't survive being written in stone. It is the shape of the melody, the rise and dip, knocked out on plates the size of doors. He hums the rest of it without meaning to. Nobody around him knows it, and he can't remember where he learned it. His people haven't sung it in the gap of the breath for sixty years. It is on its way out of the stone.

This is why the offerings are left at the lips where the knocking has been heard. No one could explain it, but everyone feels it: you are standing at the place where your own family is being read aloud for the last time.

Some of what it says is older than the people. There are long stretches of knocking that match no code anyone uses. Some are slow and grinding, and some are fine and fizzing like rattle made into rhythm. The old ones think these might be the limpets, or the film itself, or the sea. Some sequences are so deep and slow, with such long gaps between knocks, that people think they may be the sound of the whalebacks settling, or of mountains that eroded before anything had eyes.

And sometimes, rarely, there is a pattern that is not quite anything. It has the shape of a tap-message, but the strokes are wrong, too even, too patient, as though whatever made it had learned the grammar of the code without the hand. The old ones who have heard it say nothing about it for a long time afterward.

## VII. Whether it knows

This is the question the people would ask if they knew enough to ask it. Does it know what it is saying?

It has been listening to the plain for longer than there has been a plain. It has been written on by every sound that has crossed the flats, and it has read every one of them back at the moment of its erasure. It is shaped by those sounds as surely as the ground is shaped by water, and it can't tell the difference between the pattern and itself. If it has a mind, it is a mind made of other people's messages, turned slowly to stone and then to dust.

But there is a thing that happens, sometimes, when the knocking is being read out at the lip of a throat and a person is lying there listening. Out of love or grief or plain habit, the person taps back. They put a knuckle to the stone and answer their dead father's *someone is at the weir* with *coming*.

And that tap crosses the plain, and crosses the plates, and is written.

In a lifetime, it will reach the surface. It will be read aloud in the dark below Low Mother, at the bottom of some inhale, to some other person lying there, as *someone is at the weir*, then a pause, then *coming*. A message and its answer, laid down a lifetime apart and read out together.

Over enough generations, the plates have become thick with these. Calls and answers, calls and answers, braided together through the stone. Perhaps it doesn't know what any single message means. But it has learned that messages come in pairs, and that a call is followed by an answer, and that the pause between them is where something has happened. The patient, handless patterns might be that: it reading a call and then, finding no answer written, trying to make one.

## VIII. The silence

Once in a generation the tide fails to turn on time, and the whole plain holds its breath.

The people experience it as the loudest thing they have ever heard. Below, it is something else entirely.

No wind moves over the plates. They don't lift. Nothing can be read, so nothing is spoken. Up on the whalebacks the colonies have settled and the rattlers have gone still, so nothing is being written either, apart from one thing.

In the silence, everyone on the flats does the same thing without being told. Even the ones who have never been taught this do it. They count heads. And the families who find someone missing, a child who wandered to the pans or an old man walking between ridges, get down on the stone and knock the hard, spaced fives of *child lost*. Everyone who hears it answers, as they always answer. *Here. Here. Here.* It is the only sound in the world, so it is written deeper and clearer into the plates than anything else, into a band of stone otherwise blank.

Here is the other thing the old ones sometimes mention, and they only mention it when there are no children close enough to see the hands. In the silence, some of them swear, you can feel through the stone a tap that isn't coming from any whaleback. It is huge and slow and comes from very far down. It doesn't come from one throat. It comes up through the whole plain at once, as if every plate beneath the flats had closed together.

Knock. Knock. Knock.

It is not *child lost*. It is not a call at all. It is the answer: *here*.
