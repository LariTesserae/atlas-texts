# Those Who Keep the Line

## The Anheli

They are small people, most of them, broad through the hand and narrow through the shoulder. Generations of climbing stone that cuts have shaped them in a particular way. Look at an Anheli hand and you will find the heel of the palm thick as horn, yellowish and ridged, the skin there so tough it can be pressed flat against a basalt blade without opening. The fingertips are the opposite. Because no Anheli child is ever permitted to touch rock with them, the fingertips stay soft, pale, and unscarred through a whole life, as tender as the inside of a wrist. They are extraordinarily sensitive, and the Anheli use them for everything that is not stone: for testing the dampness of a felt, for sorting seed by feel in the dark of a granary, for reading the tension in a net, for touching one another.

Their skin has never known sun hard enough to burn it or wind dry enough to crack it. It is pale and faintly translucent, and it is always a little damp. Everyone in Anhel is always a little damp. Their hair grows heavy and dark and is worn in thick braids greased with wool-fat, so that the constant beading of moisture runs off instead of soaking in. On an Anheli head in the morning you can see droplets strung along each braid like glass beads, and people shake their heads gently when they come indoors, the way the goats do.

Their eyes are large and set somewhat wide, suited to the grey, even light. Visitors from over the ridges, on the rare occasions anyone has come, have said that the Anheli seem to look at things for a long time. This is true. In a place where nothing changes, looking is how you notice the one thing that has.

They dress in felted wool, grey and undyed, because felt holds warmth even when wet. Over it they wear a sleeveless leather shift when climbing, and always the palm-guards, which are passed down and re-stitched rather than replaced. A dry garment is a small luxury. In every house there is a stone peg beside the hearth where one shirt hangs drying, and whoever has worked hardest that day gets to wear it to sleep.

## Speech and the Tapping

The Anheli speak low. Their language is soft-edged, full of breathy consonants and long, murmured vowels, with very few hard stops. It carries well across a room and very poorly across a slope. The cloud drinks sound, and the Anheli learned long ago that anything shouted toward the edge simply vanishes. It is not echoed, not answered, only taken.

So for distance they tap.

Stone carries what air will not. A chisel struck on a bedrock outcrop sends a small clear tick along the basalt that can be felt, and faintly heard, several houses away. Over generations this became a language of its own: a system of taps, pauses, and double-strikes that the Anheli call *the rim's speech*. Every house has a tapping-stone, a smooth-topped boss of living rock near the door, worn slightly concave in the center by generations of the same small bronze hammer. News moves along the Rim Road this way, house to house, in both directions, so that a message tapped on the eastern spur at dawn may arrive, much relayed, on the western side by dusk.

The tapping is quiet and constant. When a visitor hears the distant chisel-sound of Anhel and thinks someone is carving, as often as not someone is talking.

Children learn the rim's speech before they are fluent in the spoken tongue. A small Anheli child who wants her mother, and cannot see her, will crouch at the tapping-stone and knock the house-sign and her own name, and wait, head tilted, palm flat on the rock, for the answering tick.

## Names

A person in Anhel is named first by their house and only second by themselves.

The distance-stones along the Rim Road are numbered, counted outward from each of the four great cairns. A house is known by its cairn and its count: *Third-from-the-Eastern*, *Nineteenth-toward-the-North*. A person's full name is their house's name followed by their own, and their own name is usually small, almost a nickname. Among strangers the house-name is all that matters. It tells you where on the line someone belongs, how far their water runs, which warden they answer to, which neighbors share their nets.

When an Anheli marries, one partner walks to the other's house and takes its name. The Anheli do not think of this as losing a name. They think of it as moving along the line, the way water moves along a trough.

## The Fixed Number

There is something about Anhel that no Anheli says plainly, though every one of them knows it by the time they are grown.

The line is closed. It runs around the whole valley and meets itself. The distance between houses is fixed and marked in stone, and it is never broken. So the number of houses is fixed, and it has been fixed since before the oldest marker was set. Anhel cannot grow. There is nowhere for it to grow into. Above the damp band the rock is dry and the cisterns would stand empty. Below it is the cloud.

And yet the houses are not overcrowded, and they are not abandoned. Every one of them is occupied, and none holds more people than its cistern can water. The numbers stay balanced, as the goats' numbers do, and the swifts', and the frogs' in the basins, as if they were counted.

The Anheli have one custom that answers this. They do not call it a law, and it is not written on any warden's stone.

When an Anheli grows very old, there comes a morning when they go down to the lowest terrace with everyone else to look at the cloud and check it against the carved mark, and they do not come back up. They take off their palm-guards and leave them folded on the terrace wall. They walk down past the flooded cress-beds, slowly, onto the last few paces of wet rock. Then they walk into the cloud, the way the waders do: chest-deep, then neck-deep, then gone.

No one stops them, and no one follows. Their household stands on the terrace until the surface has closed and smoothed, which takes a very short time. Then someone taps the house-sign and the name on the tapping-stone, a particular slow pattern, and it passes along the rim in both directions. By evening every house in Anhel has heard it.

Some time afterward, a season's length if the Anheli had seasons, a child is born somewhere on the line. Not always in the same house. Not always nearby. But the Anheli, who keep count of everything, have kept count of this too, and the count has never failed.

They call the old person's walk *going to the floor*. They say it gently, without dread. It is only the thing a person does when their part of the line is kept.

## The First Dwellers

Beside every door stands a tree-fern: ringed, slow, as thick as a thigh, its crown of fronds spread over the doorway like a hand held over a candle. The Anheli say each one was planted by the first person who lived in that house, and that none has ever died.

What they do not tell visitors, and rarely say even among themselves, is where the first dweller is.

The first dweller did not go to the floor. The first dweller is under the fern.

When the line was first laid, the story goes, in the days before the count began, each house was built by one person alone. When that person grew old, they did not walk into the cloud, because there was not yet a custom for it. They lay down beside their door, and their household planted a fern over them. The roots went down into them and through them and they became, in the Anheli way of thinking, the house's oldest member. Not a ghost. Not a god. An elder who simply no longer moves.

So every morning, when someone steps out of an Anheli house, the first thing they do is lay their soft fingertips against the ringed trunk of the fern. It is the only living thing they touch that way before breakfast. They are greeting the first person who kept their part of the line, who is still there, still keeping it, growing so slowly that no one has ever seen them change.

## Law and the Wardens

Each spur of the valley has its warden, chosen by the households of that spur and serving until they go to the floor. A warden's work is almost entirely the keeping of distances. They walk their stretch of the Rim Road once each day, touching each distance-stone as they pass, checking that no net has been strung out of its place, that no channel has been diverted, that no terrace has crept outward and begun to steal another house's air. They settle disputes over water, which are the only disputes the Anheli have in any number.

The worst crime in Anhel is crowding. To build closer than the stones allow, to string a net into a neighbor's share of the air, to cut a channel that takes more than its share: these things rob water, and in Anhel water is life, and it is also order. The punishment is old and quiet. The offender is sent to walk the Rim Road, the whole loop, three days around, touching every distance-stone in the city with the heel of their hand and speaking the name of every house aloud. It is not cruel. It is meant to teach them, in their body, exactly how much line there is and how precisely it is shared.

Most who walk it come back very quiet, and they are the most careful keepers of the distances afterward.

## Carving

Nothing in Anhel weathers, and the Anheli have taken this deeply to heart. A mark cut into stone here will last, as far as anyone can tell, forever. Its corners will stay crisp, its edges sharp. So the Anheli carve very little, and very carefully.

What is carved is what should not change: the numbers on the distance-stones, the house-signs on the lintels, the level-mark on each lowest terrace showing exactly where the cloud should lie. At each great cairn there is also a tally-stone, a basalt column covered in tiny grouped strokes: the count of days, kept since the count began. One stroke is added every evening by the cairn-keeper. The columns have long since filled, and new ones have been stood beside them, and now each cairn is ringed by a small forest of tally-columns, grey and crisp and unreadable in their sheer number. No one reads them for meaning. The count is not kept in order to know anything. It is kept in order to have been kept.

There is, somewhere on the northern arc, a lintel carved wrong. A house-sign was cut with one stroke too many, generations ago, by a young carver whose hand slipped. It can never be corrected, never worn smooth, never softened. It is exactly as sharp today as it was the moment it was made. Every Anheli child is taken, once, to see it. They are not taken to be frightened. They are taken to understand.

## The Animals, Considered as Neighbors

The Anheli do not quite think of the animals as separate from the city. The goats are household; they come home at dusk uncalled and lie against the warm outer wall by the hearth. The frogs in the cisterns are considered a sign of good water; a cistern without a frog is emptied and scrubbed. The moss-hares belong to no one and are left alone. The snails are food. The swifts are admired and never harmed, and the Anheli love to watch them skim the edge of the cloud and leave their brief furrows.

The ravens are different. They sit on the house-peaks in pairs and watch the terraces, and the Anheli regard them with a wary respect, as something like wardens of their own. There is a saying: *the raven counts too.* No one knows exactly what they count. But when a pair of ravens moves from one house-peak to another, the household they have left will go down and check its level-mark twice.

It has been noticed, and quietly remarked on for as long as there has been remarking, that none of the animals of the edge make any sound. The frogs pulse their gold throats and do not sing. The ravens never call. The goats walk softly. The swifts are silent even in the nest.

The Anheli do not think this is an accident of nature. They think the animals are keeping quiet for the same reason they are.

## The Waders

The waders are not the Anheli's animals, and they are not quite wild. They come down out of the green belt now and then, high-shouldered and thin-necked, their heavy coats dragging with water, and they walk past the lowest terraces without hurry. People stand aside for them. No one touches a wader, and no one would think of eating one.

They walk into the cloud.

Days later, one walks back out, somewhere on the rim, not necessarily where it went in. Its legs are matted with floor-silt: fine and pale, somewhere between ash and ground bone, and it never quite dries. The Anheli are careful with floor-silt. They do not throw it out, and they do not use it. When a wader comes out near a house, the household will gently comb the silt from its legs with a bone comb and gather it in a small clay jar, and the jar is sealed with wax and set in a niche in the house wall, beside all the others. Old houses have many such jars. No one opens them.

Children sometimes ask whether the waders are the old people who went to the floor, coming back. The adults do not say yes. They do not say no. They say, *the waders go down and come up*, which is true, and then they find something that needs doing.

If you watch closely, you may notice that when a wader comes up out of the cloud, the Anheli nearby lower their heads a little, and that the old ones do it most deeply.

## The One at the Center

And there is one more who lives in Anhel. The Anheli do not name it, and the city is built, in every stone and custom, around it.

Look again at the cloud. It lies in the bottom of the bowl, level to the width of a finger, its surface textured like fleece. *Like fleece*: the Anheli use that word, the same word they use for the flank of a sleeping goat. Its surface does not drift. But if you lie flat on the lowest terrace, as children sometimes do when no adult is watching, and put your eye very close to the edge, and wait a long time, you may see the surface lift, by less than the thickness of a hair, and settle. Then, much later, lift and settle again.

The damp band, the forty paces of saturated air in which everything in Anhel lives, is warm by the faintest degree compared with the air above it. The Anheli know this with their soft fingertips. It is the only warm thing in the valley that is not a hearth.

They do not say what it is. They say only that the cloud is listening, and they tell their children so, and the children mostly believe it, and the adults, when they are honest with themselves, believe it too. The whole of the Anheli way of life has the shape of courtesy toward something very large that must not be disturbed. There is the low speech, the tapping instead of calling, and the rule against crowding the line. The animals keep their silence. The tally is kept but never read. The dead are given back to the floor, and new children come out of that giving. The waders go down and come up again.

Everything that lives on the rim lives in its breath.

## The Keeping

The Anheli believe that they keep the line. Each morning someone from every house walks down to the lowest terrace and checks the white surface against the carved mark and nods. Their stories are all about keeping, and their fear is all about the cloud moving: if it rose they would drown, if it sank they would wither. Their vigilance feels to them like the thing that holds the world in place.

But there is another way to understand Anhel, and the oldest Anheli, the ones close to going to the floor, sometimes arrive at it on their own.

The city is a circle. It runs unbroken all the way around the one who lives at the center, one house deep, perfectly level, every house at exactly the same distance from its neighbors, every level-mark cut at exactly the same height. It is a line drawn around something asleep. It is the most careful thing the Anheli have ever made.

And the cloud does not move.

Perhaps, the oldest ones think, it is not only the city that watches the cloud. Perhaps, each morning, in the grey-gold light that comes at the same pale angle over the eastern ridges, something at the bottom of the valley also stirs by the width of a hair and looks up. It sees the ring of low houses exactly where they were, the nets strung where they have always been strung, the distances kept, the level unbroken all the way around. And it is reassured, and it settles, and it holds itself still for one more day.

Two keepers, each holding still for the sake of the other.

The Anheli have one word for "the day," and it is the same word for every day. The oldest of them say, when they are near the floor and are no longer afraid to say such things, that it is not their word at all. It is the word the cloud says to the city, every morning, when it finds the line where it left it.

Then they fold their palm-guards and set them on the terrace wall, and go down to learn the rest.
