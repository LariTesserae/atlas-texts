# Those Who Keep the Combed Mountain

## The First Answer

The first answer is that nothing lives here, and you will believe it for most of the morning.

It is an easy answer to believe. The place has been swept of life more thoroughly than any house. There is no lichen, no moss, no bird, no fly, and no footprint in the frost dust. You can stand in the middle of the glare with the combs dripping around you, and the only living thing on the mountain is the warm, breathing, slightly foolish shape of yourself.

But the notch appears in the basin wall. The slab moves. Stone is struck twice before dawn. The first answer does not survive the first night.

The second answer is that something lives here which hides. That is wrong too, though less wrong. Nothing on the Combed Mountain hides. It simply cannot exist where you are.

## What Comes Up With the Cloud

Watch the fog in the early morning, as it breathes up over the lowest terraces and climbs.

It does not climb evenly. It climbs the way a crowd climbs stairs, pressing, parting, finding the channels and following them upward against the grain of the water. When it reaches the combs it passes through them, and the combs keep it. The book-learned answer is that the droplets condense on cold stone. That is true, and it is also what a net would say about fish.

Each morning the mountain catches a portion of its people out of the sky.

They come up diffuse. In the cloud they are not anything yet, only a tendency in the vapour, a slight preference for staying together. The fins comb them out of the air and gather them into drops, the drops into runnels, and the runnels into the pecked grooves. By the time the fog has burned off and the sky has gone that hard dark blue, they have been poured into the lattice. For the rest of the day they are water.

## By Day, in the Channels

This is when you are among them and do not know it.

The trembling skin of light in every channel is them. So are the patient basins that each fill exactly to the notch before passing anything on, and the refusal of anything to rush. They are not asleep, exactly. They are something closer to *waiting in line*. A basin is a room where one of them lies still and flat and full of sky until its turn comes. Then it slips over the quartzite lip with that faint *tsst* and lies still in the next.

In this state they have no hands and no intentions. They taste of almost nothing, faintly of stone. If you drink from a basin, you are drinking a little of someone, and they do not mind. Some of them will leave you again as breath, and rise, and be caught again tomorrow.

They are not cold because the mountain is cold. The mountain is cold because they need it to be.

## The Cold Hours

After sunset the basins begin to seal, one by one, across the whole slope. The sound is small and dry: *tick*.

Each tick is one of them waking.

Ice is not a stillness. Ice is water that has decided on a shape, and at this height, in the dark, they are finally cold enough to decide. Across ten thousand basins they draw themselves together into structure. It is milk-glass under the stars, and it is also ten thousand small bodies stiffening into readiness. The mountain counting to itself is the mountain taking attendance.

Most of them stay in their basins through the night. They hold the stone together from inside, pressing a hair's breadth outward, testing every joint. This is how the walls came to fit so tightly that water cannot leak through them, only weep. The walls have been leaned on from the inside, every night, for longer than anyone has been counting. The builders feel for the loose places and the builders press, and by morning the loose places are no longer loose.

But some of them climb.

## The Hands

Go back to the ledge at the ridge, to the row of dark cobbles that belong to no rock on the slope.

Each has a worn flat on one side and, on the other, a polished hollow shaped exactly to the ball of a thumb.

They are not tools, or not only tools. They are *molds*.

In the cold hours a thread of water climbs from the highest channels up to the ledge and pools in each thumb-hollow. There it freezes. Ice remembers the shape of what holds it, and the hollow holds the memory of a thumb, so what freezes there is a thumb. From the thumb the frost spreads: rime feathering outward over the dark stone, crystal building on crystal, into the ball of a palm, the heel of a hand, four fingers of hoarfrost closing around the cobble.

Twenty hands wake in a row on the ledge, made of clear ice with the dark stone held in their grip, the way a bone sits inside a fist.

They have no arms, or none that would satisfy you. Behind each hand is a long, glistening, branching thread of ice running back down to the water it came from. In the starlight it would look like frost creeping, only frost does not creep with purpose, and this does. The hands lift. They find the line of holes along the living rock. They strike.

This is the sound before dawn: stone on stone, twice.

Each strike deepens a hole by the thickness of a frost-crystal. It is slower than anything you have a word for. It is also enough, because there are twenty hands and the nights never end, not really; they only pause for the day. The deepened holes fill with meltwater, and some of the builders who are not hands pour themselves into them and lie down in the rock and freeze, and push. A frost wedge is not a technique here. It is a person, sleeping with their back against the mountain, growing.

The slab that moved overnight was carried by many threads of ice at once, lifted clear, and laid flat a pace away in the time it takes a dark hour to pass. Nothing heard it. Ice makes no noise when it is careful.

## Why You Never See Them

You are warm.

That is the whole of it. Your breath is warm, and your skin, and the air that clings to your coat. Wherever you stand, you carry a small season with you, a pocket of air a degree or two above freezing. In that pocket they cannot hold their shape. If you walk the terraces at night with a lamp, the ice melts back from your feet in a faint halo. The hands on the ledge, if you ever reached them in time, would be only twenty wet cobbles and a sheen of meltwater running back into the holes.

They do not flee you. Around you they simply *stop being*, the way a word stops being when no one says it, and they begin again when you have gone. The work is never seen continuing because to see it you would have to be colder than a living thing can be.

Lie still enough, long enough, far from any lamp, and you might feel it at the edge of sleep. Around your body the ticking would draw closer, one basin, then the next, as the ice tested how near to you it could come. They are curious. They would not harm you. They would simply wait, with a patience you would not survive, for you to become the same temperature as everything else.

Some travellers have.

## Who Taught the Hand

Consider the thumb-hollows again.

Something wore them, and it was not ice. Ice polishes nothing; it only fills. Someone once sat on that ledge with a cobble from the valley in their fist and struck stone against stone, for years, until the ball of their thumb had worn its own shape into the rock they held. Someone warm. Perhaps the first of the frost holes along the ridge were theirs. Perhaps the first terrace was theirs, a little basin to catch fog to drink, because nothing else up here gives water.

Then that someone was gone, as warm things go at this height. The cobbles stayed in their row, and the hollows stayed in the cobbles, and one night the meltwater pooled in them and froze, and was a thumb.

That is where the builders learned hands. They did not learn them from bodies; they had never had those. They learned them from the space a body leaves in stone. Every basin on the mountain is the same size because it is sized to one person's reach. Every channel is a hand's width because a hand once measured it. The whole lattice is a dead climber's gesture, repeated by ice until it covered a mountain.

They are not that person. They carry on the work the way a riverbed carries on the shape of a river that has moved. They do not remember who taught them. They remember only the shape.

## What They Are Keeping

It is not for themselves. The combs catch more of them each morning, and yes, they seem to want that. But a people made of water does not need ten thousand cisterns and walls that do not leak. It needs no paving sloped a finger's breadth toward the channels. It certainly does not need every chip and pebble set as a shim, every spore of lichen scraped from every fin, every insect that blundered up out of the cloud taken apart in the cold hours and carried away.

This is weeding. The whole mountain has been weeded down to bare clean stone and bare clean water, for the sake of one thing that is allowed to stay.

Go to the deepest cistern and put your face down into the dark shaft, close to the wet stone, where the light almost fails. There is the velvet film, too thin to name, dark as the inside of an eyelid.

It is the only green on the mountain. It is not really green. It is the colour green would be if it had never met the sun.

They built all of this for it: the cold that never lets anything else take hold, the water that tastes of almost nothing, the shafts so deep that the glare cannot reach down and burn. The film grows at a rate that would make the travertine seem hurried. In a century it thickens by the width of a breath on glass. They tend it without touching it. In the cold hours they lie frozen in the upper shaft, above it, a lid of ice to keep the night steady. In the day they trickle down the walls to it, one bead at a time.

You will want to know what it is. They do not know either. They know only that it is the first thing on the mountain that is *alive* in the way you are alive and they are not. They know it began by itself, without any of their making, in the dark at the bottom of the well. And they know that the thing to do with something that began by itself is to make the whole world around it quiet and clean and cold, and wait.

Once or twice an afternoon a single hollow note rises from far down a shaft. That is one of them letting go of the wall and falling the last distance, to land beside it.

## The Lowest Terraces

The builders do not all go back to the cloud.

Some of them stay too long in the flow. The water that runs through the lattice carries dissolved rock out of the fresh-split slate, and every basin the builders pass through leaves a little of the mountain in them. Most shed it at the rims, as that white crust thin as eggshell. But the old ones have carried it for longer. They drift down, terrace by terrace, over longer and longer times. They grow slower and heavier, and they begin to lay themselves down.

The travertine lips are them. The white mineral filling the joints of the dry walls, gluing slate to slate from the outside, is them. When a builder grows too heavy with stone to rise again as fog, it spreads itself over the rim of a basin and stays there, a fingernail's breadth of white, holding. The lower you go, the more of them there are, until the rims swell, the grid softens, and the basins begin to look less like cells in a comb and more like the folds of something grown.

Up here, the hands cut sharp edges. Down there, the old ones round them off. The two kinds of building meet in the middle of the slope, and the meeting is not a quarrel. It is a life. You are young at the ridge, all edge and frost and striking. You are old at the cloud line, all softness and sediment, lying down in the shape of what you once made.

## What Lies Beneath the Cloud

The terraces go down into the cloud and do not come out.

Every morning the cloud breathes upward, and every day the water runs back down into it. You might call that weather. From where you stand at the saddle, with the white floor spread flat below you and the lattice descending into it, it looks more like circulation.

No one has walked down through the cloud on this side and returned to say what the terraces become. But consider what the old ones are making as they lay themselves down: rounded, swelling, grown rather than cut, something that the grid gives way to. Consider that the combs are thin fins spaced a finger apart, which is to say a gill, which is to say a lung. Consider that the whole slope is a structure for taking water out of breath.

Consider, then, that it is not the mountain that breathes.

Perhaps something lies beneath the cloud that has been built down there for a very long time, softly, by the old ones, in white stone layered thinner than a hair. Its breath rises as the morning fog. Its breath is caught in the combs, turned into builders, and sent back down to it as water, carrying more of the mountain than it took. Perhaps the hands at the ridge are not its servants but its growing tip. They are the cold fingers of something reaching slowly upward out of the cloud, through the rock, toward that sky which is so deep a blue it seems almost dark, as though darkness were not far above it at all.

And perhaps the velvet film in the wells is where it will begin to think.

## What Lives Here

So the answer is not one thing.

At the top there are hands of frost that wake in the thumb-marks of someone long dead and strike stone twice before dawn. Through the middle there are ten thousand still bodies of water waiting their turns, and at night ten thousand bodies of ice pressing the joints shut and counting themselves in the dark. In the deepest wells there is something too thin to name, which is the only thing here that anyone is allowed to love. At the bottom there are the old ones lying down in white stone. Below them, beyond sight, there may be the one whose breath all of this is.

None of them will be seen by you. You are the warm thing, the one weather they cannot build in.

Walk down off the saddle before night, if you mean to leave. Behind you the basins will seal with their small dry ticking, one by one, across the whole slope. If you stop at the last rise and listen, you may hear it count one more than it did the night before.

Up on the ledge, one of the hollows has always seemed a little less polished than the rest, as though the stone were still learning the shape.
