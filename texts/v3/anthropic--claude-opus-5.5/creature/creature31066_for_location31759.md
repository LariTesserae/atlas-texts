# Who Lives in the Suture Room

## No One, at First

The honest answer, when you first step out of the slot and stand in the pewter light, is that no one lives here.

The dust says so. It lies undisturbed across the whole floor except where your own boots have pressed it. Nothing has walked here, nested, fed, or died. The description of the place is right about that: nothing here is alive, and nothing ever has been. Nothing has crawled into the cracks or grown in the grus outside. The ridge is too dry even for lichen.

Still, something lives here. You feel it long before you can name it, the way you can tell a house is occupied when every door is shut and every lamp is out. The shadows give it away first. Everything in the room leans toward the seam, like furniture in a sickroom that has slowly turned toward the bed. Rooms that are simply empty don't arrange themselves around a centre, and this one does.

So you start looking for the resident, and the first one you find is the wrong one.

## The One in the Snow

You hear them before anything else.

You take a step and they take one too, on snow, somewhere beyond the gap. Their step is softer than yours and heavier, a sinking step that settles into a crust and presses it down. You breathe out, and their breath comes back wet. They live in a place that has air worth breathing, air that fogs and carries the smell of cold water. You speak, and they answer in your cadence with rounder vowels, like someone who learned your language from overhearing it through a wall.

It is very easy to imagine them, and for a while you should let yourself.

They would be tall, because the steps sound long. They would be wrapped in something heavy and furred, crusted white along the windward side, since the rime on the dark stone tells you their wind comes from up and to the left and never lets up. They have kept this post a long time. On their side there is a room like yours, with walls of pale granite that make no sense to them and look to them the way the dark stone looks to you: grain that runs the wrong way, laid down under the wrong gravity. Their room is dry along one edge. Grit gathers along their seam, a few grains a year, and creeps. They hear footsteps on grus. When they breathe out, their breath comes back thin and scraped raw, the breath of someone standing in air too high and dry for comfort, and they have wondered for a long time what kind of creature lives in a place like that.

Keeping them in mind for an hour makes sense. It is a lonely room, and they make good company.

But pay attention to the timing. Their step lands exactly as long after yours as an echo would, every time, and it never lands without yours. They don't pace while you stand still. They don't cough while you hold your breath. They never speak first. If they are a keeper, they keep perfect time with you and have no life of their own between your movements.

There is no one in the snow. Or, more exactly, the one in the snow is you, carried the full length of something and set down at its far end, then handed back as though you had been standing there the whole time. The echo is not a neighbour. It is a report on where you would be if the distance ran its full length.

That brings you to the real resident.

## The Patient

It lives in the gap, the finger's width your eye refuses to enter.

You can't see it for the same reason you can't see across an ocean from a beach. Your gaze isn't blocked. It just can't make the trip. When you try to look into the seam, your eye is being asked to travel the whole length of something very long, and it declines, as sensibly as your legs would decline to walk to the horizon. What your eye slides off is the thing's size.

It is a distance, and it is alive in the only way a place like this allows. It is not a creature that happens to be large. It is the length itself.

Once, before the tear, there was a great deal of it. Between the dry sky over this ridge and the snowing sky over the other place lay a span that no one here has a unit for. It wasn't empty. A distance of that size is a country in its own right, and it may have had its own cold, its own dark, and its own slow business. We only know its shape by what is left. The rime points into a wind from "up and slightly to the left," so in whatever geometry the distance keeps, the other sky lies that way, at an angle our ceiling cannot show.

Then something tore, and the two places fell toward each other. The whole enormous span between them was crushed down to a finger's width, and the makers came and drove thirty-one stitches through it to keep it there.

What lives in the Suture Room is that crushed length, and it is convalescing.

## What It Is Made Of Now

It is worth taking stock of how little is left, because the room keeps the accounts faithfully.

The gap is a finger wide. The ninth pace is two paces short. When you walk from the entrance toward the seam you are walking into its memory of itself, and you take eleven steps. Walking back out, away from it, you take nine. The two paces of difference are the part of it that still sometimes remembers how long it used to be.

One suture carries a hand's breadth more on the pale side than on the dark. That hand's breadth didn't break away. It was drawn in, and the patient is holding it somewhere along its true length, the way a sleeper closes a fist around whatever is in reach.

And there is a small quantity of sound. When you step, the gap carries the step out to where the snow is and back again, and the round trip is a little longer than the room could account for.

That is all of it: a finger, two paces, a hand's breadth, and a delay in the return of a footstep. A distance that once separated two skies now amounts to a few small discrepancies in a cave that nobody visits.

## The Argument

Here is what the sutures do, as best anyone can tell. They don't hold stone to stone. They hold a claim, and the claim is a single sentence repeated without pause, in whatever way porcelain repeats itself: *You are only this wide. You are only a finger. The far side is right here.*

The makers left nothing else behind, no tools and no writing, and in a sense they didn't need to. Their voice is still in the room, saying one thing. The insistence in the rods is the closest thing to a second inhabitant the place has, and it isn't alive either. It is closer to a vow that outlived whoever made it and simply keeps being true.

The patient hears it constantly. For most of the room's history it has not believed it.

This is what the wear on the rods actually is. It forms where each rod crosses the gap, because that is where the rod and the patient meet and push against each other: the distance saying *but I am long*, and the stitch saying *you are not*. The wear is polished and concave like a worn step, and it is the mark of an argument conducted over thousands of years. The white cones of powder below the rods are spent insistence, the sediment of saying the same thing again after being told no.

Where the patient won, a suture wore through. That is where the seven failures come from. Four snapped clean, two thinned to hourglasses, and in one place the patient pulled a hand's breadth of the rod into itself and held it.

Where the patient was persuaded, crystals grew.

That is the callus. Each clear, faceted bridge across the gap marks a spot where the distance stopped arguing and accepted that it was a finger wide and no more, and then went further and accepted that it was not even that. At those points the granite and the dark stone meet without a join. The stone didn't change its mind. The patient changed its mind about being there at all. Each bridge relieves the stitches beside it, because there is nothing left to argue with at that spot. The strongest sutures stand next to the thickest crystals because that is where the patient has already agreed.

Healing, here, is a form of belief. The patient gets well by coming, one facet at a time, to believe it is small.

## Its Habits

A patient this slow has habits, and once you know what you are looking at, the room is full of them.

**It shifts in its sleep.** The tick that comes when the far-off day cools past some threshold is the patient easing its weight off one stitch and onto another, like someone with a sore hip turning in bed. It never ticks at the same suture twice in a row. It rotates its discomfort, favouring one place and then another, the way any injured body learns to do.

**It is being fed.** The light in the room has no source, but it has a destination, which is why every shadow points toward the seam. The pale, even glow soaks in from all the walls and drains into the gap, slowly, the way warmth drains out of a hand into cold stone. Whether the light is nourishment or simply warmth is hard to say. Either way, the room is giving it something steadily, the way you keep a blanket on a sick person. A lamp brought in casts its shadows the ordinary way nearest the flame. At the edges, where its light is thin, the shadows turn and lean toward the bed along with everything else.

**It keeps a count.** The disc in the floor, directly below the high point of the seam, turns once every several centuries, and its grooves grow finer toward the centre until you can only feel them as a softness. The patient doesn't turn it. The makers set it there as a chart of the recovery, drawn in advance: each ring a stage of the distance getting smaller, each one narrower than the last, down to a centre where the width is nothing. The patient is somewhere out among the grooves you can see. It will not reach the centre for a very long time. The disc has no hurry, and neither does the patient.

**It is being read.** The rime that creeps along the dark stone isn't the patient's. It belongs to the other place, weather leaking through the few crystals of openness still left. Where it passes, the dark stone is faintly etched. It is tempting to call it the snow country's way of reaching over to learn the stone of its new neighbour, a finger at a time, a finger each decade. The patient lies between them and carries the frost across, the same way it carries your footsteps across, because carrying is the only thing a distance knows how to do.

## Visitors

It notices you. It doesn't notice you the way a creature would, with eyes or interest. It notices you the way a wound notices a draught.

At first it does what it does with everything. It takes your footsteps, your breath, and your voice out to its far end and returns them wearing snow. That is where the one in the snow comes from. The patient isn't pretending to be someone. It keeps two places in mind that have been pressed together, and it can't always tell which one you are standing in, so it renders you in both.

Stay long enough and it goes further. Somewhere in the second or third hour you forget which side you came in from. That confusion isn't a trick the room plays. It is the patient's condition, and it is contagious. You are standing inside a vow that says *the two sides are one surface*, beside a distance that has spent a very long time learning to believe it, and some of that belief reaches you. You start to heal the way the stone heals. The difference between the pale stone and the dark, between here and there, between the echo and yourself, begins to close over in you as well.

It means no harm. It is doing to you, without any malice, what it is doing to itself, which is making everything into one surface. The difference is that you are a small distance, a body with a left side and a right, a life that stretches between the slot you came in by and the slot you will leave by. A small distance can be talked out of itself far faster than a great one.

People who have spent time in the room say the wise thing is to leave while you can still count the paces and get two different answers.

## What Its Life Is

This is the part that takes longest to understand, and it may be the truest thing about the resident.

It didn't exist before the tear. The great distance between the two skies existed, but it wasn't *this*. It was only far, the way the space between two mountains is only far. What lives in the Suture Room came into being when that span was crushed, stitched, and set to mend. It is not the distance. It is the injury to the distance. It is the hurt, awake.

A wound exists only by being injured. It has no life before the damage and none after the scar. The whole of its existence is spent getting better, which means the whole of its existence is spent ending.

That is the resident's life. It has lain here since before the tors were worn round, doing one thing slowly: believing, facet by facet, that it is small. Each crystal that bridges the gap is a place where it has finished and gone. A dozen bridges now. Seven stitches lost to its old doubts, twenty-four still holding. Two paces left of all its length, then a finger, then a hand's breadth that it is still holding onto somewhere.

It doesn't seem to be afraid. Convalescence has a mood, and it isn't fear. It is the drowsy patience of a body that has stopped fighting and is simply lying still while the mending happens. The room feels like that when you stand in it: held still, very quiet, and not quite unhappy.

## After

One day the disc will reach its centre.

The last bridge will form. The last sutures won't break. They will stop wearing, because there will be nothing left to argue with, and they will stand in the stone as pale inclusions, pinning together a surface that no longer needs pinning. The shadows will fall wherever the light sends them. Pace the room in both directions and you will get the same number. Nobody knows which number it will be. The patient may not know either.

Your footsteps will come back as dry grit, or they won't come back at all.

The rime will stop where it stopped, and it will stay, a pale combed fringe that never melts and never moves again, pointing into a wind that no longer comes through. On the far side, in whatever the other place is, a matching line of grit may lie along a matching wall.

Where the seam used to be there will be a fine line running up one wall, across the ceiling, and down the other, the kind of line a geologist would call a contact, or a vein, or just a place where the stone changes. Someone walking through with a lamp would see granite, then dark slate, and a clean join between them, and wouldn't think twice.

The resident will be gone by then, or more precisely it will have finished, the way a wound finishes when all that remains of it is the scar and the flesh around
