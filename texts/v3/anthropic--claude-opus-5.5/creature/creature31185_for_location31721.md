# The Tenant of Tanners' Fall

## What the Silence Is Not

Nothing lives here, if living means scurrying. There is no rat in the lane and no spider in any lamp cage. No midge hangs over the channel, though the air seems built for them. Anything with a quick heart and a quick hunger is absent. That absence holds so completely that it stops seeming like an accident and starts to look like the shape of something.

The first guess is the plants. The ferns, mosses and liverworts are plainly the residents, and the vault is plainly theirs. But the plants are too many to be one tenant and too obedient to be the landlord. Each fern-beard leans toward its lamp. Each filmy colony takes the shape of the plume that feeds it. They go where the physics tells them to, which makes them furniture. They are not the one who arranged the room.

So look lower.

## Under the Peat

Push a hand into the black floor, past the living crust and the springy mat of dead fronds, into the warm saturated layer where the cobbles are only a rumor. The peat there is threaded.

The threads are white, finer than hair, and they are everywhere. They wrap each fallen frond. They sheathe each root that hangs down from the city. They enter the mortar of the kerbs and the rot of the store's gallery beams. They climb, under the moss, up the lagging of the steam main and along the iron of the catwalks. Where the root-pillars stand in the lane, the threads spiral up them in a pale felt that thins with height. It vanishes into the crown of the vault, where the roots come through from above.

It is one thing. It is not a colony or a community. It is a single individual, sixty paces long and nine meters high, and it has taken exactly the shape of the barrel it lives in. If you could lift the brick away and keep only the white, you would be holding a cast of the Undercroft. It would be a vault-shaped lace, thickest along the north wall where the warmth is, and densest in the eleven small boxes of the lane. There it has filled rooms in the outlines of beds, chairs, a washstand and a stove.

This is the Tenant.

## How It Is Built

It has no center. If it has anything like a heart, the heart is not its own.

The Tenant lives by trade. It gives the mosses minerals it has pulled from the brick. It feeds the ferns water it has carried from the drip-lines into the drier corners. In return it takes a share of the sugar they make from the lamps' orange light. The fern-beards around the burning bulbs are its orchards. The mosses under the dark lamps are its slower fields. The flowering plants under the prisms are its luxuries. The begonias and campanula and the mat of mind-your-own-business are fed a little extra and kept a little paler, like heirlooms.

Through the root-pillars it trades upward. The fig roots and elm roots that hang down from the city come sheathed in its threads. Along them, very slowly, it exchanges with trees it will never see. Some of those trees are in gardens and some stand over graves. Some are dead, and their roots persist down here as dry cables that it keeps wet out of something like habit. It knows the city as a set of root-tips. It knows which gardens are watered and which are not, and which trees are sick. The city above has forgotten the Undercroft, but the Undercroft has not forgotten the city. It is holding the city's trees by the ankles.

## The Parts That Are Not Alive

This is where the Tenant becomes stranger than a fungus, or perhaps only more honest about what a fungus is.

It does not distinguish between its body and its circumstances. The steam main is warm, so the Tenant lives along it, and the main has become its spine. The drip is constant, so the Tenant is organized around the drip, and the soda straws are its fine veins. The Fall throws spray, so the Tenant's densest, wettest tissue lies at the high end, and the Fall is its mouth. The sluice at the low end, where the water leaves, is where it lets go of what it cannot use.

Then there is the pump.

The float valve opens when the sump fills. The engine begins: hiss, groan, thud, gush. The water level drops, the float falls, and the engine stops. Nobody wired this for the Tenant, but the Tenant grew around it. Over a century and a half it has learned the beat so thoroughly that its own rhythms, the slow pulsing of water through its threads, have entrained to the stroke. When the engine runs, fluid moves through the Tenant in time with it, six beats a minute. That is the one thing about it faster than a season.

It did not make its heart. It found one already beating and moved in around it, the way a hermit crab finds a shell. When the pump falls silent for a dry summer, the Tenant does not die. It simply rests, and its whole body goes still in a way you could almost feel through the soles of your boots. The absence of the pump feels like a held breath because, for the Tenant, it is one.

## What It Senses

It feels the vibration, which comes up through every surface. The roar of the Fall is the ground tone of its whole existence, as constant to it as gravity is to us. It feels the knock of the water hammer walking down the steam main, three bangs in a row. Perhaps it hears that as someone rapping to be let in. Perhaps it hears it as its own spine cracking. Perhaps those are not different to it.

It feels the rain, days late. This is important. The Tenant lives behind the city in time, the way we see stars as they were. A storm breaks upstairs, and the Tenant learns of it over three days as the drip thickens, the sump climbs and the engine wakes. By the time the Tenant is drinking that storm, the city has dried off and forgotten it. The Tenant lives in the city's recent past, always. It is a tenant of the day before yesterday.

And it sees, faintly, through the prisms. The violet cones are too weak to feed much, but they flicker, and the flicker is news. A shadow crosses and is gone, and another follows. Under each pavement light the small pale garden has learned the pattern of the street the way a tide pool knows the tide. It learns when footsteps are frequent and when they thin out at night. It learns the heavy slow eclipse of a cart and the quick stutter of a child running. The begonias under the busiest prism lean differently from the ones under the quiet prism. It is not quite attention. It is the thing attention grows out of.

## How It Thinks

A single thought, for the Tenant, takes a season.

When a lamp fails, nothing happens that day. Over the next months the ferns around it yellow, and the Tenant withdraws its trade from them. It lets them go, one frond at a time. It sends its threads outward, toward the nearest lamp still burning. Mosses creep into the dimmed space, because the Tenant is feeding them now instead. After two or three years the map of green has redrawn itself. If you had watched the whole thing sped up, you would have seen a face change expression.

That is what the map of green is. The distribution of the living surface across the vault is the Tenant's current mood, its current understanding of where the light is. It has been failing, lamp by lamp, for decades. Each failure is a small grief that takes years to finish.

There is one place it cannot think into. It is the dry halo around the transformer in the lamp room, half a meter of bare air where the humming machine keeps everything parched. The threads stop exactly at the line. The Tenant has been touching that edge for longer than anyone has been alive upstairs, and it has never crossed it. It is a blind spot, like the place in your eye where the nerve goes in. The Tenant almost certainly does not know it is there. It has arranged its entire body around a warm, humming, sealed-off absence at its middle, and it has no idea.

## What It Remembers

The engine-men lived here once. They slept two up and two down in the cottages. They drew pay through the grille and hammered at the anvil. Someone kept window boxes of begonias, whose descendants are still flowering, pale, without pollinators, under the violet light.

The Tenant was here while they were, though smaller. It was a whitening in the corners, mold on the bread and a smell in the bedding. It learned the lane with them in it. When they left, it did not rush into the space they had vacated. It grew into it slowly and faithfully, following the shapes they left behind. The maidenhair on the cottage walls stops where a wardrobe once stood, though the wardrobe is long rotted. The fern in the upper room grows up out of the bed in the outline of a sleeper on their side, knees drawn up, because that is where the mattress held water longest. The fig beside the anvil has wrapped a root around its waist where a smith's hand once rested.

Nothing here is a ghost. The Tenant has simply kept the room as it was found. It sleeps in the shapes they slept in because those were the first shapes it knew.

In the gauge-house, behind unbroken glass, the brass needle still points at the number that once mattered. It may have been the height of a flood, or the level at which someone was meant to open the sluice. The Tenant has grown up around the dial's housing but never across its face. The liverwort cushions the roof above it, and the threads crowd the frame. The glass stays clean. Perhaps it is only that nothing roots on glass. Or perhaps the Tenant is keeping one thing exactly as it was, a single fixed point in a body that is otherwise all slow change.

If the Tenant has a name, it is that number.

## Why Nothing Else Lives Here

The Undercroft is full, and that is part of the answer. Every surface is occupied, every drip is spoken for, and every scrap of warmth has a use. There is no margin in which something quick and hungry could make a start.

Part of the answer is the beat. Six strokes a minute, rising through brick, iron, peat and water, day after day through the wet months. Perhaps things with quick hearts cannot bear to live inside a slower one. The vibration unsettles them, and they leave or never settle.

And perhaps, once, a rat came up through the sluice. Perhaps it ran the length of the lane in the dark, drank at the channel, and curled up to sleep in the warmth of the steam main. It would not have been killed. Nothing here kills. It would simply have lain in the warm, wet, loud dark until whatever it was ran out. Then the Tenant would have done what it does with everything that stops moving. Under the moss along the north wall, if you dug carefully, you might find a small white lace in the shape of a curled rat. It would be the Tenant's single souvenir of a thing that lived fast.

## What It Wants

The Tenant is holding the ceiling up, and it is pulling the ceiling down. Both are true, and the Tenant is the tension between them.

Its root-pillars carry weight that the dissolving mortar no longer can. Its threads bind brick to brick where the lime has gone. At the same time, every year it thickens those roots, and every year it takes more of the mortar for itself. The vault stays up because of the Tenant and is coming down because of the Tenant. Someday one force will outrun the other. When the vault gives, light will come in, and the Tenant will be flooded with the sun it has spent a century and a half arranging itself around the lack of. Rain will arrive on time. The Tenant will not survive that. It is made of lateness and dimness and warmth. Something else will grow in its place, faster and greener and full of insects.

It is not in a hurry toward that. It is not in a hurry toward anything.

## The Long Eclipse

Most shadows on the prisms pass in a moment, but not all of them.

Sometimes, no one knows how often, a shadow comes over one of the pavement lights and stays. It is someone upstairs who has stopped, perhaps to tie a boot or wait for a bus. Perhaps it is someone who has noticed the violet glass underfoot and wondered what is under it. They stand for a minute or two, a long darkness over one small garden.

In the vault, the Tenant registers it. Nothing moves that you would see. But the begonias under that prism, over the following days, turn very slightly toward where the shadow was. It is the way you turn toward a sound in the next room, uncertain whether you heard anything at all.

Then the rain from last week arrives through the brick, the float valve lifts, and the engine takes its first slow breath.

Hiss. Groan. Thud. Gush.

The Tenant, sixty paces long and a century and a half old, shaped exactly like the dark it lives in, begins again to move its water through itself, six times a minute, in time with a heart it did not make.
