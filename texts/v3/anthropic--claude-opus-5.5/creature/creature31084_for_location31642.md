# Those Who Sit

## First, the Doubt

For the first day, a visitor will say no one lives here, and the visitor will be almost right.

You walk the lanes for hours. You pass cells with their doorways open and their floors swept, and cisterns brimming, and walls that were plainly relaid this spring because the lichen hasn't found them yet. Everything is kept. No one is keeping it. Now and then you hear a single tap, the heel of a hand on stone, somewhere off to the side. When you turn toward it the lane forks three times, and the sound has doubled and died against the walls, and you can't say where it came from.

On the second day you see one of them. Usually it is at the foot of a wall in the mid-morning fog, crouched with an ear pressed to the stone. They don't startle and they don't greet you. After a while they lay a palm flat on the wall beside your shoulder, not on you, and hold it there a moment. That is the greeting. It means *this is sitting well*, and it means *so are you*, and it means *I have noticed you*, all of which are the same thing to them.

## What They Call Themselves

They have no name for the city. They have one for themselves, and it is not a noun. It is the verb they use for a stone that has found its seat. The closest translation is *those who sit*.

This is the whole of their ethics. A good stone sits. It doesn't need mortar or a neighbor's grip. It rests where its own weight and shape put it, and it bears what is laid on it. A person who sits is one who has found where their weight belongs. That person carries a share of the load without complaint and without being held in place. A restless person, a quarrelsome one, a grieving one, is said to *rock*. You do not scold a rocking stone. You look at what is under it.

Outsiders from the lowlands, the few who trade up the escarpment, call them the Combers, or just the Hands. They accept both names politely and use neither.

## Their Bodies

They are not tall. Generations in the narrow lanes have not made them small, but they move as if they were. They turn sideways without thinking, duck under lintels that are not there, and walk with their shoulders drawn in the way you would carry a full bowl. Their bones are heavy. Their feet are broad and nearly silent on worn stone.

Their hands give them away. The heel of the palm and the pads below the fingers are callused into a smooth, hard, faintly shining surface, much like the saddles worn into the oldest steps. A child's hands are soft. An adult's hands have been shaped by ten thousand small taps. An old person's hands are so worn that they read stone by touch the way the rest of us read faces. They can feel a joint loosening, feel damp where it shouldn't be, and feel the hair's width a stone lifted in last night's frost.

They dress in felted wool traded up from the lowland flocks, undyed and grey, always slightly damp. It holds warmth when wet, which is all that matters. They smell of cold mineral and old smoke, like the inside of the cells. In the windward districts, where the fog is heaviest, their hair is always beaded with water and they rarely bother to wipe it.

## The Hours

Their day is the cloud's day. They do not so much follow it as sit inside it.

**The fog hours are silent.** When the grey wall comes over the rim and fills the western lanes, no one speaks. This isn't a rule anyone enforces. It is simply what the morning is. They come out of their cells, cup their hands under the nearest weeping joint, and drink. The first water of the day is said to taste of the lowlands, of whatever the cloud passed over on its climb, and the old ones claim they can tell the season below by it. Then they listen. They press an ear to the walls one by one along a lane. Inside a sound wall, water moves in threads you can barely hear. Inside a failing wall it pools, and the pooling has a different note, dull and heavier. They listen the way a physician listens to a chest.

**The clear hours are for work.** When the cloud thins and lifts around noon and the city steams, they move stone. A slumped course is taken down and laid again. A cell whose capstone dropped in the thaw is cleared, and the capstone is carried by two or three of them to wherever a dome is waiting for one. They carry nothing on wheels and nothing in carts. They pass stones hand to hand down the lanes in lines. On a working afternoon you can see a single slate travel half a mile without anyone walking, tapped into each palm and lifted on.

**The rain hour is for listening again.** When the afternoon storm breaks, the one the city makes for itself, everyone stops. Wherever they are, they stand still and listen to the whole city running at once. It is the one time every channel speaks together. A wrong note stands out against the hiss: a gutter overflowing, a cistern silent that should be filling, a trickle where there should be none. They mark the place in memory and go to it at dusk.

**At night, they sleep close.** Cells are small, and they share them without much regard for kinship. Four or five bodies curve against each other and against the stone, fitted, the way the walls are fitted.

## How They Speak

Their voices are low and seldom used. When they do speak, they do something a visitor takes weeks to notice.

In the lanes, every voice comes back. A word spoken against stone returns a beat later, softened and bent by the walls. Over a very long time their language has grown into this. A sentence is spoken only halfway. The speaker leaves a gap shaped exactly for the echo, and the wall supplies the rest. Two people who want to talk will shift a little in the lane, one step toward the cistern, a half-turn from the dome, until the returns land right. A conversation between them is always, faintly, a conversation with three voices.

This means their speech is local. A phrase that is complete in one lane is broken in the next. Children learn to talk the way they learn to walk, by learning the walls. An exile from one district, sent to live in another, is said to be *without a mouth* for a season, until the new walls learn them.

It also means they almost never speak in open ground, in the rare courtyard wide enough to lose the echo. Out there, words come out bare and unfinished. It is where they go to argue, because an argument, they say, is what speech sounds like when the stone won't agree.

## How They Find Their Way

They mark nothing. A visitor always asks why.

The answer is that a mark would outlast its meaning. Everything moves. The wall you scratched a sign into will be relaid a street away within a lifetime, and your sign will point wrong forever. So they navigate by what moves with the city, never against it. They go by the grain of the courses, by which faces carry lichen, by the direction the moss cushions swell, and by the sound of water growing louder toward a cistern.

Children learn this in the fog. Every morning of their early years, a child is walked out into the cloud, where they can see a few walls' length and hear nothing but threads of water. They are left to find their way back by touch and sound. They are never far from an adult, who follows silently, and they are never told they are being followed. By seven or eight most children can cross their district blind. By adulthood they carry it whole, not as a map but as a felt thing, the way you know where your own hand is in the dark.

## How They Live Across It

The city is the size of a small sea and there are not many of them. This is the fact that took me longest to understand. They do not *live in* the city the way a town's people live in a town. They move through it, slowly, in a body.

The inhabited quarter drifts. In dry years it edges west toward the heavier fog. In years of hard winters it pulls east, away from the rime ice. It goes wherever the walls most need hands. The movement is slower than anyone alive can feel. A district is lived in for three or four generations and then, without any decision, it is lived in less. Families take cells a little farther on, and the old lanes are kept only by the passing hand. Their maintenance thins over decades into a kind of memory.

Over centuries this inhabited quarter crosses the city like a weather front crosses a plain. It is, perhaps, the slowest of the city's weathers. The empty districts are not abandoned. They are between visits. The people will come back, or their descendants will, in two hundred years or four, and find the cisterns still full and the walls leaning at their settled angles. They will begin, without ceremony, to tap them back into their seats.

## The Argument About Junipers

There is exactly one long argument in the city, and it has been going on for generations.

A juniper's roots hold a wall together, and they break it apart. Which matters more? No one has decided. The question is taken up in open courtyards, where words come out bare. It is taken up slowly, a few sentences a season, by people who inherited their side of it from grandparents and will pass it to grandchildren. No one expects it to end. They regard it much as they regard the walls: as something kept up, not something to be completed. When a juniper finally dies of age, a hundred and fifty years old and still knee-high, the people nearest it are said to feel a little lost for a while, as if a wall had come down they had leaned on without knowing.

## Their Neighbors

They do not think of themselves as the only ones who live here.

The pika is regarded as a fellow keeper, a very small and very nervous one. Its dry cells, stocked with harvested cushion plants, are left untouched. More than that, when a dome in the lee districts falls and the people clear the rubble, they leave a corner of it unrelaid, a heap of stones deliberately not put back. They say it is so the pika has somewhere to be. The whistle from the rubble at dusk is heard as a kind of benediction: *that one has sat, too*.

The raptor's shadow is a clock. When it first crosses the rooftops in the late afternoon, the storm is an hour away and the stone passing is finished.

The snails' shining tracks are not disturbed. They are gone by morning anyway, and the people like to see the morning erase them. It reassures them, I think. It is a small daily proof that the city does not keep marks.

The finches that come in autumn are the only creatures the people speak of with something like longing. For a week the city is full of small quick sound. Then the finches leave for somewhere the people have never been and do not expect to go.

## Their Dead

The dead are carried east, to the dry lee districts where the walls are pale with lichen. They are laid in a cell that no one is using. The doorway is closed with fitted stone, not sealed, and no one goes back.

No mark is made, of course. The family knows the cell for a while by the grain of its walls. Then, over a generation, they forget, and they are meant to.

The dome stands for fifty years, or two hundred. Then some thaw drops its capstone, and a course slumps. Eventually hands come to gather up the fallen stones and relay them, as they gather up all fallen stones. What is in the rubble goes with the rubble. Nothing is sorted out. The bones are small by then, and pale as the lichen, and they go into the new wall between the cobbles and the slate, fitted and sitting. Sometimes that wall is in the same place, and sometimes it is a street away.

No one in the city finds this morbid. When you ask them about the theory that no stone in the oldest quarter sits where it was first placed, they agree it is almost certainly true. Then they say, gently, as though it were obvious, *nor anyone*.

## What Actually Lives Here

I set out to describe who lives in the city, and I keep failing to draw a line around them.

There are the people, heavy-boned, hard-palmed, and quiet, who speak in half-sentences finished by the walls. There is the pika in its heap of unrelaid stone. There is the raptor, and the snails, and the finches for one week a year.

But the people move through the city the way the weather does. Their speech belongs to the stone as much as to them, and their dead become its walls. They drink every morning from a cloud that came up from the lowlands to meet them, and they stand silent inside it while it weeps down every face. After a while the question changes. They live here the way the water lives here. They live the way the stream lives in the streambed: as the motion that keeps the shape.

Then what lives here is the keeping. It is the slow habit that crosses the plateau over centuries, fitting stone to stone, catching the cloud, making the rain, and taking back its own collapses. The people are its hands. The water is its breath. The stones are the part of it that stays still long enough to be seen.

Stand in one of its lanes at mid-morning, inside the cloud, and wait. After an hour, maybe two, a figure will come out of the grey a few walls away. They will crouch and set an ear to the stone and listen to the water moving inside it. They will stay a long time, perfectly still, beaded with wet, as grey as the wall. And if you look away and back, you will not be entirely sure, for a moment, which of them is sitting.
