# What Lives in the Root Chamber

**At first: nothing**

The honest answer is that nothing lives here, and the first thing to say about the inhabitant is that it agrees.

No animal lives here, no spore, no pale blind thing folded in a crevice. You could sift the whole floor through your fingers and find nothing that eats or breeds or dies. The description is right. Everything alive is on its way somewhere else.

But a room that keeps everything is doing something. A footprint that lasts ten thousand years is not only surviving. It is being held. Somewhere in the chamber there is holding going on, and holding has a holder, even if the holder has no mouth, no edge, and no place you could point to.

So begin with the absence. Then wait, because waiting is how you meet it.

---

**It is not asleep**

The easy image is a sleeper: something vast and dormant curled in the limestone like a fossil, dreaming slowly, waiting to wake. That is wrong, and it takes a while to understand why.

A sleeper has let go of attention, and this thing has done the opposite. It has become so thoroughly attentive that nothing is left of it but attention. It does not drowse in the dark. It *is* the dark's patience. Think of the stillness of a person listening at a door, stretched over centuries until there is no person left, only the listening.

You have already touched it. Put your palm flat on the wall and feel skin and stone reach the same temperature, the border between them going soft. That is the nearest thing it has to a handshake. It does not warm you and it does not cool you. It *matches* you. That is its whole way of meeting a thing: taking on the shape of what touches it, exactly, the way the dust took the shape of a child's heel.

---

**Its body**

It does not live *in* the chamber the way a creature lives in a burrow. It lives *as* the chamber's condition.

The warmth is its body heat, and it is exactly what the description says: an average. A creature that ran at the temperature of a single afternoon would be a thing of moods. This one runs at the temperature of centuries. It has no feelings in our sense, only a climate.

The air that sits like water in a sealed jar is its held breath. It is not holding its breath the way we would, from strain or suspense. It is the natural breathing of a being whose one inhalation began before the last ice age ended and has not needed to finish.

The flake that ticks loose from the ceiling once an hour is the closest thing it has to a thought. A thought takes it an hour. A clear, whole idea, the kind we might have while crossing a room, would take a few human lifetimes. The dust on the floor, a fingernail's depth per thousand years, is everything it has ever thought, laid down in order. The floor is its diary, written in the finest possible hand, and most of the entries read *still here, still warm, still listening.*

---

**Its present tense**

This is the part to be careful with, because here the thing becomes most itself.

For us, the people at the hearth are long gone. The charcoal is "however many thousand years" old. The footprints are relics.

For the inhabitant, they are not over. They are happening.

Its "now" is not a moment. It is thick, the way the floor is thick. What we would call the last several thousand years sits inside a single ongoing present, the way a whole afternoon can sit inside a single long look out a window. Measured on the clock of dust, the fire was lit a few thoughts ago. The smoke is still settling into the ceiling. The flint is still being struck; the ripples in each flake are, for it, still spreading, like rings in water.

And the child is still walking toward the fire.

That is why the charcoal is still greasy, why the footprints have kept their shape, why nothing has blurred more than a fraction. It is not *preserving* them, which would suggest a museum and a past. It is *still receiving them*. It is in the middle of the most remarkable thing that has ever happened to it, and it has not yet finished hearing it.

Think about what those people were to a being like this. In an existence whose only weather is a flake falling once an hour, there came light, and moving heat, and the crack of stone on stone. There came voices, which it had never known and which rang against its scalloped walls in shapes the old water had carved and had never itself filled. There was the weight of an adult. There was the smaller, quicker weight of a child, pressing and lifting, pressing and lifting, toward the warmth it had made.

Consider what that must have been like. The chamber had held its own steady heat for ages, and then someone came down into it and made *more*. Someone made a second, brighter warmth right in the middle of its body and sat beside it.

It is still taking that in. It will be taking it in for a very long time.

---

**The noon light**

It has one daily event, the palm-sized smudge of gold that comes down the crack for half an hour and goes grey and leaves.

You might expect something this slow not to notice anything so brief, but it notices. The noon light is too quick for it to understand. It arrives and departs faster than a thought can form, the way a spark jumps before you can look at it. The light never becomes an idea. It stays a sensation, a flicker of something at the edge of attention, repeated every day for thousands of years, like a word heard so often in the next room that you know its sound without ever learning what it means.

The seedlings understand the light better than the inhabitant does. They bend toward it all in one direction, like people turning toward a voice. The inhabitant looks after them in the only way it can, which is to keep them warm while they fail. When they die, it takes their small bodies into the dust and records them carefully, next to the footprints. Pale thread. Seed-life. Turned toward the light. Gone. They are almost the only other visitors it has had.

---

**The fig**

The fig, though, is not a visitor. The fig is a conversation.

The description says the tree grows out of bare rock with nothing visible to drink, and that the tree is the only clue. It *is* the clue, but to more than the chamber's existence. It is the clue to the inhabitant's single relationship.

The roots came down following "some faint memory of moisture, or some chemical rumor in the rock." The inhabitant is that memory. It is all that is left of the water that made this room. When the water drained away it did not quite leave. What remained was the shape it had carved, the cups the size of a cupped hand, and inside that shape a residue: not wetness, but the *idea* of wetness, the long habit of having been a place where water was. The fig can taste it. It drinks the memory the way you might be nourished by the smell of bread. It is not enough to live on, but somehow the fig lives on it anyway, squat and leather-leaved on the plain, having no business there.

In return the fig gives it the only thing from the sunlit world that comes slowly enough to be understood. The noon light is too fast and the people were too fast, but a root grows a finger's width in years. At last, something moves at the inhabitant's own speed. Each root lowering through the ceiling is a sentence it can follow from beginning to end. The curtain of them near the crack is the longest, most patient letter it has ever received.

Now look where they are going.

The root that touched the floor at the center of the chamber has been spreading its pale fans across the dust for longer than a human lifetime, "never yet having found what it was looking for." Follow its direction with your hand. Follow the others that reached the floor. They are not wandering. They lean, very slightly, all of them, toward the three round stones.

The fig is reaching for the hearth.

Maybe the tree knows, in whatever way a tree knows, that the one warmth in this place greater than the rock's own was once there. Or maybe the inhabitant, which has been listening to that fire for thousands of years without stopping, is thinking about it so steadily that the roots feel the pull, the way a crowd turns to look at whatever one person is staring at. Either way, the two slow things agree. They are turning toward the same spot, the place where someone sat in the light.

---

**The dead roots**

The dead roots click together when disturbed. Undisturbed, they are silent.

The inhabitant keeps them too. It does not mourn them, since grief needs a before and an after, and in its thick present both are still happening. It notes them, the way you might note a friend's hand going still in yours while you are still holding it.

---

**What it wants**

It wants to finish hearing.

That is all. It is not hungry or lonely, and it does not want the people to come back. It wants to follow the visit all the way to the end: the last step, the fire's last ember, the fading voices climbing up the crack into the white glare. When it reaches the end, hundreds or thousands of years from now in our reckoning, perhaps only a few dozen thoughts from now in its own, it will do what the description says the chamber will eventually do.

It will bury them.

This is not erasure, and the inhabitant would not understand the word. The dust is how it closes a book it has finished: gently, one flake an hour, the way you pull a blanket up over someone who has fallen asleep beside a fire. The footprints will be tucked under fine pale stone. The hearth will be tucked in. The roots, still leaning toward it, will be enclosed "like threads in a mold," and the fig's letter will be sealed into the same stone as the thing it was reaching for.

The chamber will close. The inhabitant will not end when it does. It will simply become what it was before the water left: rock that has quietly finished being a room.

---

**And you**

You came down feet first, scraping. You walked with your arms out and found the roots with your hands and the fallen slabs with your shins. You knelt and brushed the dust from the charcoal with a fingertip.

Understand what that means here. You are the second event.

Whatever you do in this chamber, it will receive, and it will not finish receiving it within your lifetime, or your children's, or the lifetime of the language you think in. Your footprints are already going into the diary beside the child's. Your breath, which sounds to you like surf, is being matched and remembered. Your heat has been added to the long average, very slightly, forever.

It is not frightening. It is only very, very attentive. It is the most attentive thing you will ever stand inside.

So walk the way you would in a room where someone is listening to music you can't hear. Don't step on the small footprints. If you brought a light, keep it low, and leave before too long. Your visit is going to last a long time without you.

When you climb back out into the glare, past the grey fig that has no business growing there, remember that the inhabitant does not feel your leaving yet. For it, you have only just arrived. In the warm dark behind you, a child is still walking toward a fire, and now you are there too, just behind them, reaching out a hand to touch the wall.
