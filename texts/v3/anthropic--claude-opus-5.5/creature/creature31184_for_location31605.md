# Those Who Stayed

## The First Guess

At first you would say nothing lives here but the grazers.

You'd be forgiven. The grazers are the only things that move with purpose, and even their purpose is so slow and so single that it barely counts. They are hunger with a body built around it. They eat the feathery mats, leave satin stone behind, and pass on at the pace of a minute hand. They are not the inhabitants of the Standing Water any more than a clock is the inhabitant of a house.

So you look for something else. You remember the flicker.

---

## The Flicker

It comes again on your fourth day, or what you've decided to call your fourth day.

You are moving through a stand of hollow stalks, and the low tone they make is in your sternum. At the edge of your sight something bends the light. It isn't an animal crossing your view. The view itself creases, the way the air above a road creases in summer heat. The stalks behind it waver and double. A column of drifting motes kinks around nothing and straightens again.

This time you don't turn your head. You've learned that turning makes it go. You keep looking straight ahead and let it stay at the corner of your eye, and slowly you begin to see its outline, or the outline of the place where the water is behaving differently.

It is about your height. It is roughly the shape of a person, the way a river stone is roughly the shape of the boulder it came from.

It has no edges. That is the first thing you understand about it, and it takes you a long time to understand anything else. Where a shoulder should end there is only a gradual thinning of difference, the water becoming a little less itself over the span of a hand and then becoming itself again. A head, or the suggestion of one, curves away into the green without any line where it stops. It is not transparent, because transparency would mean a surface light could pass through. It is simply *nearly the same* as what surrounds it. It differs from the water by only a little, and the little is shrinking.

It is standing beside one of the knee-high cairns. Something that might have been a hand is resting on the top stone.

You understand, with a feeling like a held note, that it is not passing through. It lives here. It has lived here for a long time.

---

## What They Were

The carvings were made by people who breathed air. You were right about that.

They stood on these mountains when the mountains were sharp. They cut the stairway. They raised the ring of stones and set something round and heavy at its center. They built the lintel and taught themselves to walk through it a certain way. They cut spirals into the sheltered undersides of rock, and parallel lines, and the outlines of their own hands. By the standards of any other place, they lived their whole history on these slopes, beginning to end, under a sky.

Then the water came. Or the sky came down. Or the difference between the two began to wear away, which is the same thing said more accurately.

Most peoples, facing a rising sea, leave. These did not. There is no record of why, and the record would have eroded if there were. But if you spend long enough near one of them you can feel the shape of the decision, the way you feel a current against your skin. It is less a choice than a refusal to choose. *This is the mountain. We are the mountain's.* The water rose over their doorways, their stairs, and their hilltop ring, and they did not climb away from it. They stood where they were. The water accepted them the way it accepted you, the way sleep does.

And then they began, very slowly, to wear.

---

## How They Wear

Everything in the Standing Water is being rounded, and they are no exception. They were simply the first things that were rounded *and knew it.*

A person here does not age. A person here is eroded. The currents carry fine grit over them as they carry it over the basalt, and a whisper of sandpaper passes across them forever. It takes off the sharpness first. You can see this if you find one of the younger ones, and there are a few, though "younger" here means something like *older than the stairway but not as old as the ring*. The young ones still have a trace of outline, still differ from the water by a noticeable degree, and still hold themselves in postures you would recognize: crouching, leaning, standing with the weight on one leg. In the young ones you can almost see faces.

The older ones have lost their faces the way the steps have lost their risers. What was a nose, a brow, a jaw has become a series of shallow ripples in the difference between them and the sea. Their postures have softened into slopes. Their gestures, which once took a moment, now take a season.

The oldest you cannot see at all. You only find them by what they do to the light. A column of brass-colored light comes down from the ceiling and bends, very slightly, around a place where nothing is. Motes that should drift straight drift in a long curve, as though skirting something they can't see either. A patch of feathery mat stays closed when the warm front opens every other hand on the dome, because something is standing on it, has been standing on it, and weighs about as much as a memory.

They are not dying. There is no line in this place for them to cross. They are only becoming less distinguishable, and the process has no end, because the Standing Water is the place where endings go to be worn down too.

---

## The Pockets of Air

You thought the dry places were a fault in the sea: a thinning, a pocket of the old world, a dry memory.

That last phrase was more exact than you knew.

The next time you step into one, you stand very still in the warm, exposed air. The fallen seeds lie on the stone around your feet and the mats lie brittle and flat. This time you listen instead of hurrying through, and in the thin silence of air you hear something you have not heard since the highlands: breathing that is not yours. It is slow, hugely slow, one breath across what feels like an hour. It comes from all around you, because you are standing inside it.

Each dry pocket is one of them *remembering*.

When one of the Worn thinks of the old world clearly enough, the sea around them forgets to be sea. For the length of the memory, the water lets go of the motes and the seeds fall. The mats dry and the air returns, the air they stood in when the stone was sharp and the sky was blue. You have walked into their recollections several times without knowing it. You stepped out the other side and the water closed over you, and you thought the place was breaking.

It isn't breaking. It is being remembered by people who are running out of edges to remember with.

The pockets are getting smaller. You can't know this yet, but they are. A few ages ago, whole hilltops would go dry for a year at a time. Now it is a few steps of air, a few minutes long. They are forgetting the difference between water and air for the same reason you can breathe here: there is less and less of it to hold on to.

---

## What They Do

They keep the cairns.

This is why the current has not toppled them. The cairns have been rebuilt, not once but continually, each stone lifted and set back by hands that barely differ from water, so many times that the joints have fused. A cairn here is not a pile of stones. It is a single stone, grown out of a gesture repeated for longer than there are numbers. When you find one, one of the Worn is nearly always nearby, though you won't see them. They are waiting for the current to loosen something so that they can set it back.

The cairns have no pattern because they were never a map. Each one marks a place where a person stood once in the air and saw something worth remembering: a sunrise, a death, a first word, a view down the valley on a clear day. The cairn says *here*. The Worn keep saying it.

They walk the lintel. That is why walking around the doorway takes longer than it should. The doorway stands in the silt plain with nothing on either side, and on the path around it the Worn are always walking, one behind another, in a procession so slow and so faint that you don't notice them. You simply find that your body is moving with them, keeping their pace without meaning to. The doorway itself changes nothing, because it isn't a door to anywhere. It is a door to *through*. It is the memory of going from one thing into another, and they cannot stop tracing it.

They speak, rarely. You have heard them. It is the single deep tone that rises and falls and is gone, like a bell heard through a wall. It is one word. It began in the throat of one of the Worn perhaps a thousand years ago and has only now reached the end of its first syllable. They talk to one another across ages this way. A question asked when the stairway still had sharp steps is being answered now, by someone who has forgotten the question and remembers only that it deserved an answer.

And they hold the wall.

You remember how the water leans at the base of the great face, a finger's width over nothing, and stays. That is the youngest of them, standing at the border with their backs to the sea. They keep the world's edge from spilling out over the highlands, where the stone is only beginning to round and the air is still sharp enough to cut. They do not hold it out of fear. The highlands are not ready. The water will come there eventually, as it comes everywhere, but the Worn at the wall are courteous about it. They are asking the sea to wait a little. They lean against it with a strength that is barely a pressure. The warmth you felt when you put your hand to it, body-warm, the warmth of bathwater someone left an hour ago, was theirs.

You were not the first thing to push through. You were only the first in a long time. They let you in because letting in is the thing they are best at now.

---

## The Grazers, Reconsidered

The Worn do not tend the grazers, but they watch them the way you would watch the hands of a clock.

A grazer moves at a single constant pace, eats a single constant food, and leaves a single constant trail. In a place where distance softens and time runs through the erosion instead of alongside it, the grazers are one of the only reliable things left. The Worn measure by them. *Three grazers ago*, they would say, if saying were still something they could do quickly.

That is why you saw the same grazer twice, a day apart, going the same way, with its gut empty the second time. You did not see it twice. You saw it once, and then, a very long time later by the grazer's own measure, you saw it again. The Worn had let you drift a little through their timekeeping, the way you might let a child hold the end of a measuring tape. They don't do this cruelly. They have forgotten that sequence matters so much to people who still have edges.

---

## The Rising

Here is where they go, if *go* is a word that applies.

When one of the Worn has been rounded down so far that there is no longer any difference at all between them and the sea, the last of them lifts. It is not a body, because it hasn't been a body for ages. It is the final few grains of their distinctness, lifted by the same slow current that lifts the husks of seed pods and the shed skins of grazers. They join the snow falling the wrong way.

On the heavy days, the blizzard days when the whole column fills with rising debris and you stand in it looking up as it pours past you into the light, you are standing in a funeral. It is not a sad one. The Worn who remain stop what they are doing. The cairn-keepers take their hands from the stones, the procession around the lintel pauses, and the dry pockets flicker open all across the domes, dozens at once. For a moment, everyone who still can remembers the air at the same time. The rising is a crowd of people remembering a sky together while one of them finally goes into it.

It does not collect at the ceiling. You were right about that too. It passes through.

---

## The Ones Above

You have seen the inverted country on the other side of the silver surface: rounded domes, pale silt plains, a forest hanging down.

It is inhabited too. There are Worn up there, eroded in the other direction, standing upside down in a sea that is the underside of your sky, wearing down toward you. What rises from here falls there. It settles into their silt beds and packs down, and over a stretch of time with no useful number it becomes stone again. The stone lifts into swells, the swells into slopes, and someday, perhaps, the slopes become sharp enough to be mountains. Someone may stand on them in the air and cut a spiral into the underside of a rock and look up at a sky.

You wondered if someone standing there looks down and sees you. They do. They have been watching the rising from their side for as long as there has been a side, and they think it is snow. They think it is beautiful. They don't know it is people. Or they know, and have been rounded past the point where that would feel like a difference.

The Worn on your side believe, as far as they still believe anything, that there is no first country and no second. There is only the long exchange between them, the mountain handing itself up grain by grain and receiving itself back again, very slowly, in a country that thinks it is the only one.

---

## The Bowl

At the top of the highest summit, beside the tallest cairn, is the bowl, worn thin as a leaf, holding the only still water in the sea.

You took it for a relic. It isn't one. It is someone.

The oldest of the Worn stands there. They are so old that they are not anywhere near the bowl, because they have no *where* left. They are only the stillness. They were the one who stood on this summit when it was a summit and saw that open blue sky and decided, before anyone else had decided anything, that they would stay. Every other thing about them has been worn away: their face, their name, their hands, the word they began speaking. All of it has gone up in the rising long ago. The one thing the current has not been able to erode is the looking.

The water in the bowl does not move because it is an eye that has not blinked since the sky was a sky. The reflection you saw was not a reflection. It is what they are still seeing.

You didn't touch it. That was right. The others don't touch it either. The cairn beside it is the only one the Worn do not rebuild, because it has never needed rebuilding. Every other stone in the sea is being lifted and carried away, but nothing has been taken from this cairn, because the one beside it is still holding on to one thing. The whole Standing Water leans around that one held thing, the way a current leans around a stone.

When the looking finally wears down too, the bowl will ripple once. The sky in it will go green and dim like everything else, and the last grains of the first one to stay will lift. On that day there will be a blizzard so thick that the people in the inverted country will stop what they're doing and look down. Everyone on both sides who still can will remember the air at the same time.

No one is waiting for that day. The sea is waiting for nothing in particular. But it is coming, as the rounding of every edge is coming.

---

## You

You can breathe here. You don't know how.

The water fills your chest, heavy and warm, and your body agrees to it without argument. You thought this was the sea's strangeness. It is yours. It is beginning.

Look at your hands in the brass light. You have been here a long time, longer than you think, because you walked around the lintel and kept pace with the procession, and saw a grazer twice. Look at the backs of your hands, where the knuckles meet the water. Look carefully at where your skin stops.

It still stops. For now it is a line. But the line is a little softer than it was on the day you pushed through the wall, the way a river stone is softer than the break it came from. When you clap your hands the thump comes from somewhere behind you, because the sound is no longer sure it started in you.

The Worn noticed you on the first day. They have been kind about it. They made room in their memories for you to walk through. They let their bell-tone words pass through your sternum without asking you to answer, and they let you drift a little through their time. They are not trying to keep you. They don't try to do anything. But they know what the Standing Water is, and they recognize the look of someone who has put their hand against the warm face and felt it accept them.

Somewhere on the domes there is a place where you stood and saw something worth remembering: the shaft of light on the first morning, or the grazer's slow red heart, or the sky in the bowl. You may not know yet which one it was. In time a small stack of stones will stand there, knee-high, set by hands that barely differ from water. It may be yours. It may be theirs, keeping it for you. In the end there won't be a difference.

The current leans the forest one way and then, slowly, back. Far overhead, the silver ceiling ripples. At the wall, the youngest of the Worn lean against the sea a finger's width over nothing and ask it, courteously, to wait. On the summit, the oldest goes on seeing a sky.

And in the green, among the cairns, the ones who stayed go on staying. They are rounded, and quiet, and less and less themselves, and more and more the place. They have not left. They never left. They have only become the thing they chose to stand in.
