# Who Keeps the Chord

## The Beetle on the Lowest Stone

Begin with one of them, because one is easier to hold in the mind than hundreds.

It clings to the great crystal near the back wall. The rows of tally marks are cut into that face, and so is the empty ridge. The beetle is larger than the others, nearly the length of a whole finger. Its shell has gone from bronze to something darker, the colour of a coin that has spent a long time in a closed hand. Its rubbing leg is not like the others. The ridges on it are worn almost smooth, and yet the note it makes is exactly as deep as it has always been. Look closely by the seam-light and you can see why. The leg is not the leg it began with. It is sheathed in something grown over the old one, layer on layer, each layer ridged fresh. The beetle does not wear out. It grows its instrument back as fast as it plays it away.

It has no eyes that you can find. Where eyes would be there are two flat, pale plates, smooth as the inside of a shell. When you lean close, they tremble, very slightly, in the chord. Its whole head is an ear, and it listens to itself. It also listens to the four or five tones around it, and to how its note sits among them. It adjusts. The adjustment is too small for you to hear, a pressure of the leg, a tilt of the shell against the facet. It never stops adjusting.

You might wonder how anything reproduces in a place where nothing is allowed to change. Watch long enough, days perhaps, and you might see it happen, once.

A beetle on one of the middle crystals goes still. Its leg stops. For the length of a breath there is a hole in the chord, a missing tone, and the whole hollow seems to lean toward the gap. Every membrane on that pitch sags. Then, from inside the stilled shell, the note starts again. It is thinner at first and slightly sharp, then settling, then exact. The old beetle does not move. The young one is inside it. It was always inside, curled in the hollow of the parent's body, rubbing its own small leg against the parent's inner shell, practising. When the parent dies, the child simply keeps the post. It eats its way outward through the soft parts over a long time, and it does not leave. The dead shell becomes its shell. The note becomes its note.

So each beetle is not really a beetle. It is a lineage wearing one body, a single tone passed inward and down, generation within generation, like a word whispered into a closed box. The beetle on the lowest crystal may be the ten-thousandth to sing that note. It has never met another of its kind. It has never needed to.

## The Grazers

At first the pale many-legged things seem like the only idle creatures here. They are silent, slow, and wandering, with fine hairs standing up from their backs. They seem to be simply eating the moss. They are not.

Follow one. It moves across the dark mat at the pace of a shadow crossing a floor. It is about the size of a folded hand and flat, with too many legs to count, and each leg lifts and sets down without a sound. Its hairs are not uniform. They come in bands of different lengths along its body. When it passes close to a membrane-plant, some bands stiffen and some go slack. It is feeling the chord through its hair, the way a hand feels the grain of wood.

Then it finds something. A small membrane near the seam has grown lopsided. One of its ribs is longer than the others, and it trembles on a pitch a hair's width off from any of the chord's tones. You could not hear the difference. The grazer can. It changes direction and climbs the stalk with patient, many-footed care. It eats the membrane. It eats all of it, down to the rib-frame, then eats the rib-frame, and leaves a clean, beaded stump that smells of resin.

That is what they graze on. They do not graze on the moss. They graze on error.

Anything in the hollow that begins to sound wrong, the grazers take. It might be a plant growing toward a pitch that does not exist, a membrane torn so it rattles, or a vine whose spiral has slipped a turn. They are the reason the chord has never drifted in all the ages of the rock. They are also why the second ridge, the one scratched new into the crystal edge, has nothing on it. Look at its base and you will find a faint wet shine where many small feet have passed. Once, something tried to settle there. Some bright seed of a sixth note may have begun. The grazers came, and it is gone.

They are not cruel. They have no more intention in them than a comb has. But if you sit on the moss long enough, one will come and walk slowly over your hand. Its hairs stand up against your skin, reading you, and then it moves on, and you will feel, obscurely, that you have been judged and found not yet worth eating. Not yet.

## The Ones in the Ceiling

The seed-pod things hang from the roof in the dark between the corkscrew vines. There are perhaps thirty of them, each the size of a closed fist, brown and dry-looking and ridged lengthwise.

When one opens, it takes a long time. The wings come out folded like a fan, pressed flat and translucent, veined with fine ribs. They spread until they are wide as two outstretched hands, and then the creature holds them there. It does not fly. Flight would stir the air, and nothing here stirs the air.

The wings are not quite still, though. They tremble in the chord like the plants do, but not like the plants, because the plants tremble and then stop, and these wings tremble and then keep something. If you hold your palm very close behind one without touching it, you can feel the chord coming through the membrane faintly changed. It is slightly delayed, as if the wing were not passing the sound on but repeating it from memory.

They are recording. Each opening is a long, slow listening. The ribs of the wing thicken very slightly, a ridge here and a groove there, pressing the shape of the chord into their own tissue. Then they fold, and the pattern is folded into them, and they hang again in the dark.

What they are for becomes clearer when you find one on the floor.

It has fallen from the ceiling, or climbed down, and it lies near the base of the entrance crack, at the foot of the rope. Its wings are open and dry, and they no longer tremble. The ridges on them are dense and deep, like the grooves of a thumbprint, like the rings of a very old tree. It has crawled here to the only place in the hollow where anything goes out.

They are waiting for the crack. They are waiting, perhaps, for the cavity to widen by some slow shrug of the bedrock, or for a wind to come down through it for the first time. Then they will do the thing their wings are for and go up. They will carry the chord out folded inside them, into a place that has weather and change and no song at all. They are seeds after all, and what they are seeds of is the sound.

The rope hangs a hand's width from this one. You wonder whether it tried to climb.

## The Stone with a Mouth

Now the large one. It is grey and flat and heavy, half-sunk in the salt beside the red seam. It is warm, and it has the whole chord inside it.

It does not sing. Put your ear to it and there is no leg rubbing, no crystal ringing. There is only the chord, complete, every tone present and balanced, clearer than it sounds anywhere else in the hollow. The chord outside is scattered, coming from hundreds of posts and arriving at your ear from many directions, slightly smeared by distance. Inside the grey one it is gathered. It is the chord as it is meant to be.

It is not a singer. It is the one who hears.

Everything else in the hollow is tuned to something. The beetles tune to each other, the plants to the beetles, the grazers to their sense of what is right. What is the grazers' sense of right tuned to? What is the reference, the thing that decides which pitch is true when a membrane grows a hair's width off?

It is this. It lies in the warmest place and lets the chord pour into its body through the whole surface of its skin, and it holds that chord perfectly in the dark of itself. The grazers, if you watch them, pass close to it often. They touch it with their hair-bands, one by one, over long intervals, the way a musician touches a tuning fork before playing. Then they go back out onto the floor and eat what does not match.

It has a mouth. The accounts are right about that. A long lipless seam runs along its front edge, closed. It has never been seen to open. It does not need to eat, because what it takes in is the sound, and the sound is endless. Perhaps it opened once, when the hollow first cracked open and the first beetle rubbed the first note into the first crystal. It might have opened to take a breath of that note and close on it, and it has been holding that breath ever since.

Here is the thing about the tally marks.

Sit beside the grey one with your ear against its side, long enough that you forget the rope, the entrance, the country of stone above. Longer than that. Eventually you will feel, under the chord, something else. It is a single slow pulse, so slow that you cannot believe the first one when it comes. It is not a sound. It is a movement, a faint swell of the warm grey surface against your cheek, and then a slow settling.

It has a heart. It beats once in a very long time.

Go and look at the tally now, the rows of short parallel lines cut into the lowest crystal. There are not many. Twenty-three, in clusters of five. Whoever made them sat here, with their ear where yours was, and counted. They counted not days, since there are no days, but this. Each line is one heartbeat of the thing that holds the chord. Twenty-three lines is a very long time to sit in the warm still air, feeling the chord in your teeth, waiting for a stone to stir once more.

The accounts say it has not moved in the memory of anyone who has come here. That is true. It moves on a scale of memory longer than any visitor's. Only one of them stayed long enough to notice.

## The One Who Stayed Too Long

So you begin to understand who else has lived here.

It was not a group. The ring of stones near the entrance has room for one person sitting. There is one set of blades. There is one resin cup, shaped around one fist. Someone came down the crack, braided a rope from vine-skins, and stayed. They knapped glass for a while. They had time to learn which facets flaked cleanly and which shattered. They learned to walk carefully enough that their footsteps made no sound against the chord. They learned to sit on the moss where it is quietest.

At some point they did the thing that everyone who lives with a single sound for long enough will do. They tried to add to it.

The carving of a beetle beside the lowest post is careful. The new ridge in the crystal is careful too, cut to the right depth, at the right angle, in the right place for a leg to rub against it. It was a sixth post, an invitation. Perhaps they thought a young beetle, pushed out of its parent's shell before its time, could be coaxed onto it. Perhaps they meant to sit there themselves and rub one of the glass blades across the ridge, to put one human-made note into the chord and see whether it would take.

The grazers came. The note did not hold. The chord closed over it the way still water closes over a dropped stone, without a ripple, as if it had never been.

After that there is only the tally. The visitor stopped trying to change the song and started listening to the one who keeps it. They sat with their ear against the warm grey side and counted the long slow pulse. And, as the description says, somewhere in the counting they stopped wanting anything to happen.

What happened to them then is not recorded. The rope still hangs. It is stiff with oil. It would bear weight. The resin cup is still half full, and the oil in it has not skinned over, because nothing here does. They might have climbed up. Or they might have lain down on the moss beside the grey one, in the warm place by the seam, and stayed. Perhaps the grazers, walking over them with their bands of reading hair, found them at last to be exactly in tune.

Look at the grey one again. It is flat and heavy, half-sunk in the salt. It is about the length of a person lying on their side with their knees drawn up.

No. It was here before them. The accounts are older than the tally. But you notice now that it has room beside it, a warm hollow in the salt, the same shape, where a second one could lie.

## What Lives There

So what lives in the Held Chord?

The beetles live there, each one a lineage folded into a single shell and a single tone. The plants live there, eating sound and shining a little. The grazers live there, keeping it all true, eating whatever is off. The ceiling-seeds live there, pressing the song into their wings and waiting for a way out that has not yet come. The great grey listener lives there, holding the whole chord inside itself, its heart beating once in a stretch of time too long to sit through.

None of those is the true answer, or not all of it. What lives there is the chord itself. It is not made by these creatures. They are its body. The beetles are its voice, the plants its lungs, the grazers its immune system, the grey one its memory, the seeds its offspring. It is a single slow organism made of sound and the things that sound has grown. It has held itself exactly, without change, since the rock first split.

It wants something, too, if a chord can want. Everything in the hollow is shaped to keep it the same, except one thing: the seeds. They wait by the crack. They carry the song folded in their wings. They are meant to go up.

None of them has ever made the climb. The crack is too narrow, too still, too long. The one at the foot of the rope died there with its wings open.

But you climbed down. You sat on the moss. You laid your ear against the grey one and felt the chord in your jaw, and behind your ears, and in the soles of your feet through the rock. You held it inside your body for as long as you stayed, and it pressed its shape into you the way it presses into the ceiling-seeds' wings, a ridge here, a groove there.

Then you went up the rope, into the light and the wind and the enormous silence.

For days afterward you still hear it. You hear it exactly, every tone, in the space behind your ears, where nothing up here can reach to eat it.

The hollow does not need to wait for the crack to widen. It has found another way out.
