# Those Who Live in the Noise

## I. The Night Swimmers

Begin small, because the tableland is mostly small things multiplied past counting.

After dusk, when the cups have filled, hold a scale-cup up to starlight and look closely. The water is not empty. Something is turning in it, too small and too clear to see except as a bending of the light: a glassy comma that flicks, rests, and flicks again.

The people call them *dew-lice*, though they are not lice. They are crustaceans no bigger than a grain of sand, and they live exactly one night. Their cysts lie dry all day in the pale fur on the rims of the cups, baked and shaken by the chorus, inert as dust. When the evening vapor condenses and the cup fills, the cysts soften, split, and release them. They graze the faint resin-sugar that seeps into the water, and by midnight they are grown. In the small hours they mate, a slow circling pairwise dance that makes the surface of a full cup tremble almost imperceptibly. Before dawn they climb the fur at the waterline and lay new cysts there. Then the water drains back through the pores, and they go with it, down into the plant.

Every traveler who drinks at dusk swallows thousands. Part of the sweetness is theirs. The children say that if you drink from a cup late enough in the night, you can taste the difference: flatter, older, the lice already gone. Grandmothers say the plant drinks them back. Nobody has ever called this a tragedy. It is simply what the water is.

## II. The Tappers

Walk into a silent region and you will think, at first, that nothing lives there. The scales are grey and cracked. They ring under your feet like old pottery. Without the chorus, the air feels thin and wrong, like the air at the top of a climb.

But crouch down and wait, and you will hear a tick.

Then another, somewhere else. Then a scattered, patient ticking across the whole dead expanse, like rain beginning on a roof and never deciding to become rain.

These are the **tappers**, narrow black beetles as long as a finger joint, which live only on dead scale. They eat the silvery crust, the brittle stuff that peels in sheets, and they find their food by knocking. A tapper lowers its head and strikes the scale with a hard knob on its brow, then pauses with its forelegs spread flat and feels what comes back. Hollow scale is dead and edible. Scale with something moving beneath it is not.

The people have noticed two things about the tappers. The first is that the living plain regrows faster where they are thick. Where tappers swarm on a wound, the green edge creeps inward in years rather than decades. The second is that the old listeners, lying on dead scale with their cheeks pressed down, say the tapping is not random either. It clusters and then spreads, and it travels.

"They are small drummers," a grandmother will tell a child in the night-voice, the spoken voice. "They were here before us. We learned it from them."

Whether this is true is not the point. It is how the people understand themselves.

## III. The Burl-Dwellers

Now the people, and one family among them, because there are no villages and it is only ever one family at a time.

Their burl stands in the long mound-country northeast of the Lid, two days' walk from the nearest neighbor. From outside it looks like a bark-crusted hill the height of three people, humped over something the plant swallowed long ago. The door is a low oval facing away from the wind, framed by a heap of bleached swarm-husks that rattle faintly in gusts. Inside is one round room, smooth as the inside of a gourd, walls sealed in amber resin that has darkened with smoke to the color of strong tea.

Four people live here: a woman, her mother, the woman's son, and a man who arrived two years ago from the southern rim and stayed.

None of them has a spoken name. Names are signs. The grandmother's is a flat palm laid on the air and then lifted a finger's width: *the ground breathing in*. The woman's is two fingers drawn down the opposite wrist, the gesture for resin running. The boy is nine, and his sign is still a child's sign, a quick flick of the thumb like a shrike's beak, which he will lose when he is grown and given another. The man's is the sign for obsidian, a closed fist struck once against the chest. It was given to him at the rim gathering where he met the woman, and it is half a joke, because he is hard and dark and hard to get to.

They speak with their hands all day. Nothing else works. The hand-language is large in daylight: whole-arm gestures, signs made with the shoulders and hips, visible across two hundred meters of shimmering scale. Children learn it before they can walk, and they learn it loud, so to speak, sloppy and huge. It grows smaller and more precise as they age, until among the old it is almost a matter of fingertips.

Speech is a night thing.

When the chorus collapses at dusk, the silence afterward is so enormous that the first voices are always tentative. People speak quietly, even alone together in a burl, as though the silence were a sleeping animal. Spoken language on the tableland is intimate, and a little shy. It is used for stories, lullabies, grief, lovemaking, and the kind of confessions that need darkness. A child is sung to at night and signed to by day. Asked the difference, a child will tell you that hands are for *what*, and voices are for *who*.

This is why people weep when the song ends. They are not only relieved. For the first time all day they can hear the voices of the people they love, and the voices sound, for a moment, very far away.

The family sleeps on a shelf carved into the wall, lying close with their palms against the resin. The chorus does not reach inside the burl as sound, only as a deep, grainy thrum carried through the wood. They sleep through the day's heat that way when they have to, hands on the wall, feeling the world hum. The boy used to have trouble sleeping at night because it was too quiet. His grandmother would put his hand flat on the floor so he could feel the slow breathing of the plain and know it was still there.

## IV. How You Become a Listener

Deafness comes to everyone on the tableland, and it comes in stages, and the people have made the stages into something like a passage.

It starts in the twenties with the highest tones. Shrike calls vanish first, then the whine of the evening, then the thin edge of children's voices. By forty, most people cannot follow speech without watching the speaker's mouth, and many stop trying. They sign at night too, small and close.

For some, though, the loss stops partway. The high frequencies go and the low ones stay. These people are few, perhaps one in thirty, and the hearing they have left is strange. They cannot hear birds. They can hear the ground.

The grandmother is one of these. When she walks the plain now she moves differently from the others, head tilted, stopping often. Sometimes, in the middle of the chorus, she lies down flat on a scale with one cheek against it and stays there for an hour. Her daughter used to think she was sick. Now her daughter waits.

What the grandmother feels is not the chorus as the young hear it, a wall of noise. It is a slow weather of pressures. Below the chatter of individual singers is something like groundswell under a choppy sea: long, rolling pushes of low sound that cross the plain for kilometers. Some last minutes. Some last days. They have directions. In the morning most of them run toward the Lid, and in the evening they run away from it, as the old listeners have always said. But not all of them.

Some go elsewhere.

## V. What the Swells Want

Here the family's life turns out to be not quite what they believed.

They move three or four times a year. They have always said they move for the ordinary reasons: the cups are shallow here, the walkers have gone south, a borer has eaten out the singers under the burl and it's too quiet to sleep. Every family says the same, and every family is right.

But the grandmother has been counting.

She has lived in eleven burls in her life. She has walked with her family across perhaps a fifth of the tableland. She has learned, lying with her face on the scales, to feel the long swells that do not run to the Lid. They are slower than the others and deeper, and they seem to begin somewhere far away and travel in a single direction for days without breaking. When she thinks back over every move her family has ever made, she finds that they have almost always walked the way one of those swells was running.

And almost always, where they arrived, something was beginning to fail.

It was never a full silence. It was a faltering: a patch of scale going dull, a region where the chorus came up late in the morning and ragged, a borer-trail that had not healed. It was always a place where drums were needed, and always a place that was, in its way, generous: a burl standing ready and empty, deep cups nearby, a swarm due within the month.

She has not told many people this. She told her daughter one night, in the speaking voice, very quietly, and her daughter was silent for a long time and then asked, *Is it calling us?*

The grandmother said she did not know. She said that the plant had once been deaf too. Before the singers, when it was young and sat on a bare mountain, it could only breathe as slowly as its own tissue let it. Then something came that sang, and every singer that sang in its chambers made it breathe deeper and grow warmer. It rewarded them with heat, and with hollow rooms to sing in, and they multiplied. Over some span of time too long to think about, the plant and the singers learned each other. After that it could speak, in its way, across a whole tableland.

Then, much later, came people with drums.

*I think it is still learning us,* she said. *I think it is patient. I think it has had a long time to be patient.*

## VI. The Drummed Places

When a region falls silent despite everything, when the drummers have beaten the ground for weeks and the grey keeps spreading, people do eventually leave. Or they stay and die there. The rings of old drums left on dead scale are the marks of both.

The family knows a secret about those rings. Every family on the tableland knows it, though outsiders at the rim gatherings do not.

The grey regions heal from the edges inward, over generations. The tappers knock, the green creeps back, and the scales lift and warm. When the healing reaches a ring of old drums, it does not simply pass over them. It gathers around them. The new tissue thickens there, humps up, and grows bark-crust, faster than it thickens anywhere else. Within a lifetime the ring has become a mound. Within two lifetimes it is a burl.

The most prized burls on the tableland, the driest and densest, the ones with walls that stay cool at noon and thrum most evenly in the day, are these. When a family hollows one out, their adzes eventually strike something that is not wood: the rim of an old drum, or its chitin frame, grey and petrified, embedded in the heartwood. Sometimes it is a bone. The family leaves these in place, smoothed over and resin-sealed, part of the wall. You can find them in many old burls if you know where to look: a curve of drum-rim at the height of a sleeping head, or a hand-bone set into the lintel.

The family's current burl has one. It is above the sleeping shelf, a faint pale circle in the amber, about the size of a spread hand. The boy sleeps under it. When he was small he thought it was a window. His grandmother told him, in the night-voice, that it was: that someone had stood where the burl now stands, beating a drum against a dying floor until they could not, and the plant, which forgets nothing, had grown up around them to keep them. *That is why it is so quiet in here*, she told him. *Someone already did the drumming.*

## VII. The One Who Lives There

Now, finally, the thing that actually lives on the tableland, since everything else lives on it, or in it, or because of it.

It does not have a place the way the family has a burl. It *is* the place. It occupies the whole volume where a mountain used to be, four hundred meters down and hundreds of kilometers across: a single slow body, warm all the way through, burning something ancient at a rate that would be imperceptible in any one cell and adds up, across its whole mass, to a cloud standing on its back every afternoon.

It breathes on a cycle of minutes. Its day is a single long exhalation beginning at dusk. Its year is the rolling of the swarms across it, region by region, like a slow shiver. Its wounds heal over generations. What its thoughts might be, if it has them, would take place over spans no one on the tableland could stand still long enough to witness. A swell that takes three days to cross the plain might be, for it, a single gesture.

It hears in the only sense it has, which is pressure. The chorus is not noise to it. The chorus is its circulation, its lungs, perhaps its nerves. It has spent an unguessable age making itself into a resonant instrument, with hollow chambers, soundboard scales, and pores that open to the song, and then filling that instrument with players. It rewarded the singers with warmth. It rewarded the tappers with dead crust to eat, and was repaid in quick healing. It fed the swifts from the Lid and the walkers from its tender new growth, and it let the shrikes and the borers and the heat-eyes cull and be culled, because a floor full of hunters is a floor that stays loud.

And for some small fraction of its life, a few thousand years or a few hundred, it has been learning a new kind of player, one that is larger and rarer than the singers and can walk. This one can be drawn across long distances, and it strikes the ground deliberately, in rhythm, exactly where the ground most needs striking. It does not sing without being asked. But it can be asked, perhaps, by a swell rolling in the right direction beneath the cheek of a woman lying on a scale. And it can be thanked with a feast of swarm-meat, a burl standing ready, and cups that run deep, sweet, and full of small swimming lives.

Nobody on the tableland says the plant is a god. They don't worship it. They have no temples, since they build nothing taller than a burl, and no priests, since the only authority is the grandmothers who can hear the ground. They say only that it is very old, very large, and patient. They are afraid of its silences. They do not wish it quiet.

Once, at a rim gathering, a lowland trader, dizzy and bleeding a little from one ear, asked by clumsy signs why the tableland people did not simply leave, since they were so few and it was so hard. The answer came from an old man with the fingertip-small signing of the very deaf. It was two signs: the flat hand lifting, *the ground breathing in*, and then the fist against the chest.

The trader took it to mean *this is our home* and was satisfied.

It was closer to *it breathes because we are here.* Or perhaps *it keeps us.* The two signs, run together that way, can mean either. On the tableland people have never found it necessary to choose.

## VIII. Night

The chorus has collapsed. The plain is steaming gold, then grey. In a thousand million cups, glassy commas are beginning to turn.

Inside the burl in the mound-country, the grandmother is speaking aloud. Her voice is cracked and slow, because she uses it only a little each night. She is telling her grandson what to do when she dies.

*Not in here,* she says. *Take me to a quiet place, the nearest one, the next time the ground goes grey. Leave me my drum.*

The boy is nine. He puts his hand flat on the floor, the way she taught him, and feels the plain breathe under the burl, slow and deep and warm.

Far off across the dark, carried through the root-mass rather than the air, someone is drumming. Under the drummer, so slightly that no one awake could see it, the living floor is tilting toward the sound.
