# Who Lives in the Wax Hollow

## I. One of them

Begin with one, since the city is too large to take in all at once.

She is in the old quarter, on the cooling eastern side, where the chambers are thick and black and the lanes have worn so deep that walking them is like walking the bottom of a trench. Her back plates are darker than a young mason's. The green has gone toward the colour of old bottle glass, and there are fine pale scratches along the segments where ten thousand doorways have brushed her. Her flank patches still shift, but slowly, more slowly than anyone else's in the lane. A young mason's colour moves from green to bronze in two or three breaths. Hers takes seven or eight, and the others have learned to wait for her. They slow their own colour to meet hers, the way you would lower your voice to someone old.

She is a sealer. That is the work of the old quarter now. When a chamber goes too cold to live in, when its walls stop smelling of anyone, she and the others like her close it. They do not do it with rubble, and they do not abandon it. They close it with a thin skin of wax laid across the doorway and smoothed with the underside of the jaw until it is flat and faintly shining, like the surface of a candle that has just set. Behind the skin the chamber keeps everything it held: its dust, its smell, the particular resin blend of the family or the work-crew or the brood-line that lived there. It is not a grave. The masons do not seem to have graves. It is closer to a letter someone has folded and put away.

She has sealed, by a rough count, some hundreds of doorways. Every so often she stops at one that is not yet cold enough and lays her padded foot against the wall, and holds it there. Then she moves on, and comes back in a season.

If you watched only her, you would think the masons were a dying people tidying up after themselves. You would be wrong. But start there, with the old one closing doors in the warm dusk, the tallow-moth powder settling on her back like frost on a slate roof.

## II. The many

Widen the view, and she disappears into the files.

The masons are not really individuals in the way she seemed to be. They are not quite a single mind either. The nearest thing is a crowd that has been listening to itself for a very long time. Colour passes down a lane, and the masons in the lane pass it on. It changes slightly as it goes, the way a rumour changes, so that a message starting at the warm western cracks has shifted and deepened by the time it reaches the eastern wall. A mason who sees it is not simply receiving an order. She is receiving what the city has made of the order on the way.

They sort themselves by work rather than by body. The shapes are nearly identical. The difference is in which smells a mason has lived among.

- **Carriers** come from the warm quarters and smell of fresh wax and raw obsidian grit. They are the files you see moving on every surface.
- **Nurses** live deep in the cushion-trees, where the brood cells are built into the seams between leaf-pads and the trees exude that extra mortar of wax. The nurses smell of the trees. Some of them never leave a single tree in their whole lives. They are born in its seams and tend its seams and are sealed into its seams when they die. Each cushion-tree is a small parish, and each parish has its own dialect of colour, a little faster or a little bronzer than the next.
- **Tasters** are the rarest and the strangest. They spend their time with their mouthparts pressed against walls.

The tasters are the reason the city is not dying.

## III. The ones in the walls

Now the view shifts again, because the tasters, it turns out, are not masons at all. Not the large green masons, anyway.

They are the small dark ones, the "parasitic tenants" who build their cells inside the walls of the larger species and live in the gaps of the city. They look like freeloaders, and in a sense they are. They eat the wax from the inside of other creatures' walls. They are never seen hauling pellets up the lanes. They pay no visible rent, not even the lizard's rent of eaten mites.

But look at what they eat, and look at where they go.

They move from the oldest walls toward the newest. They tunnel through the black paper-stone of the eastern quarter, where the resin blends are centuries old, and they grow fat on it. Their bodies take on its smell. Then they migrate west, inside the walls, through the thickness of the city, the way someone might cross an old town entirely by attics. When they reach the pale new districts, they die there. The green masons, finding the small dark bodies in the fresh walls, do not remove them. They grind them into the next batch of paper-stone.

So every new wall carries a trace of the oldest walls. It is faint, but it is there. The scent of the first chambers built in the hollow, which are now sealed and becoming geology, is still present in the newest chambers going up on the warm side, carried through the city in the bodies of the tenants no one quite approves of.

The city's memory lives in its walls, and the dark tenants are how that memory walks. Without them the old quarter would freeze and everything in it would be lost. With them, the old quarter goes on being tasted in the new one, generation after generation, a little fainter each time but never quite gone.

The green masons seem to know this. When a dark tenant emerges briefly into a lane, the files part around it. Their colour does something it does for no one else. It goes very pale, almost white, and holds there for a moment. It might be deference. It might be grief. It might be what they do in the presence of the past.

## IV. The one who lies in the sun

Go slower still.

On the warmest chamber of the eastern cushion-tree, which is no longer very warm, there is a wall-lizard. It is larger than the others, with skin gone grey and loose, and it has not moved noticeably in the time you have been watching. It is not asleep. Its eye tracks the files of masons on the tree's pads with a kind of patient, reptilian indifference.

The lizards live much longer than the masons. Nobody knows how long, but longer. A mason might see a few dozen seasons. This lizard has lain on this tree since the tree was the warmest place in the hollow, when the eastern quarter was new and pale and tacky and the western side was bare rock with nothing on it but a few exploratory creepers.

It has been moving the whole time. It has not moved much, and it has not moved on purpose. Every so often, over years, the warmest spot has shifted a hand's width westward, and the lizard has shifted after it. If you could see its whole life laid out, it would look like a very slow slide, a grey smear drawn across the eastern wall from south to north and now beginning, just barely, to curve west.

The lizards are the city's clocks. The masons read them. Where the lizards lie is where the heat is. When a lizard finally leaves a ledge the masons built for it, the sealers come to that district within a season or two. The ledge is always left in place. The old quarter is full of empty lizard-ledges, each one a mark of where the warmth used to be, like the rings on a wall where pictures once hung.

This is part of the rent, then. The lizards eat the mites, and they also tell the time. There is another part of the arrangement that is not visible yet. Keep it in mind.

## V. The empty places

So far everything that lives in the hollow has been easy to point at: the old sealer, the carrying files, the parishes in the trees, the dark tenants in the walls, the grey lizard in the fading sun.

But there are the plazas.

They are open patches of felt, kept clear, where nothing is ever built. There are several of them. The oldest lies in the eastern quarter, now almost ringed by sealed chambers. A newer one sits in the middle of the city. The newest is on the western side, and the felt there is still soft and springs back slowly under the foot of a passing beetle.

At first they look like squares, places to gather. But the masons do not gather in them. They walk around them. The files split at the edge of a plaza and rejoin on the far side, and the masons at the edge do something odd with their colour. They let it go flat. They stop signalling. For the few moments it takes to skirt the open felt, they are simply green, the same green as the plants, as though they were holding their breath.

Beetles graze the felt of the plazas and are tolerated. Moths drift down onto it and are tolerated. But a mason carrying a pellet of paper-stone will never set it down there. When a gust drops a flake of obsidian onto a plaza, a mason will go out, very slowly, and carry the flake back off, and then return to the lane with her colour still flat.

The plazas are being kept for something.

## VI. What they are keeping it for

The masons came from somewhere else. This mountain did not exist a few centuries ago, and nothing on it is native. Everything arrived.

The masons remember arriving. That memory is in the oldest walls, and the dark tenants have carried its scent all the way across the city into the newest walls. What the scent says, as far as scent can say anything, is this:

The masons came by air.

In most of their generations they have no wings. But the brood cells in the deepest seams of the cushion-trees hold something that is not hatched every generation, and not every century. It is hatched only when the vent is dying. There are fat, dormant larvae there. The nurses tend them and never let them come to term. Their cells are resealed and re-waxed, season after season, and they are kept warm by the trees' own wax and the fading heat of the rock. They wait.

When the warmth finally reaches the far rim and has nowhere else to go, the nurses will stop holding them back. They will emerge winged: long, lacquered, the same resinous green, carrying on their bodies the smells of every wall the city ever built. They will crawl out along the lanes, and the files will part for them, and they will go to the plazas.

This is why the plazas are kept clear. The winged generation needs open ground and warm felt to spread and dry its wings. It needs a space with no building on it, because a building is a thing that stays, and they are the part of the city that leaves.

They will not leave alone.

The tallow-moths will rise with them. They always have. Their dusty wings will lift the frost of pale powder off the whole city in one breath. Each winged mason will carry a pellet in her jaws. It will be a small pellet, a bead of paper-stone ground from the oldest walls and full of the bodies of dark tenants. Folded inside a fraction of those pellets will be something the masons have never been seen to touch in ordinary life: a lizard's egg.

That is the other half of the rent. The lizards tell the masons the time. When the time ends, the masons carry the lizards.

A few of the pellets will also hold a scrap of felt from the oldest plaza. The felt in the oldest plaza is not quite the same as the felt everywhere else. It is slower, darker, and its filaments are finer. It may be the felt the masons brought with them on their arrival, the living scrap of whatever hollow they came from before this one. It may be older than that. It may have been carried from vent to vent to vent across a chain of mountains, each one born and cooled and abandoned in turn, the whole way back to some first warm crack in the rock that no one remembers except as a smell.

## VII. Who lives there

So what lives in the Wax Hollow?

The old sealer lives there, closing doors. The files of carriers, the parishes of nurses in their trees, the dark tenants in the walls, and the grey lizard sliding after the heat all live there too. So do the moths and the glass-backed beetles and the creepers that trace the vent's plumbing across the floor.

But the answer keeps widening, the longer you stay.

The city lives there, and the city is not its builders. It is the conversation among them, slow as pigment, changing as it travels. It is the memory in its walls, carried forward by the creatures it barely tolerates.

A journey lives there. The whole settlement is walking west at the pace of cooling stone, and the hollow is not its home so much as a place it is passing through. It is one stop on a very long road. The previous stops are all cold now, sealed and becoming geology on mountains no one has climbed. The next stop does not exist yet. Somewhere across the ranges, some mountain has not yet been born, or has just been born and is still sharp enough to open a palm. It is waiting, without knowing it, for a green cloud to come down out of the thin air and begin, very quietly, to build.

And something lives there that is not there yet: the winged ones in their sealed cells, fat and dormant, being kept. So do the ones who will hatch from the pellets on the next mountain, and the ones after them. The plazas belong to them. The whole city walks around the empty felt with its colour held still, out of respect for the ones who have not arrived yet and the ones who will leave.

That is perhaps why the masons are not concerned about the far rim. They do not live in the hollow the way a person lives in a house. They live in it the way a flame lives in a row of candles, each one lit from the last just before it gutters.

At dusk the lantern-stalks hold the sunlight a little longer than they should. The powder of the moths settles. On the eastern wall the old sealer lays her foot against a door that is nearly cold, and waits, and does not seal it yet. In the oldest plaza, at the centre of the ringed dark quarter, the felt that came from somewhere else gives very slightly under the weight of nothing at all, and springs back, over several minutes, in the silence.
