# The Ones Who Rise

*"No one lives here now,"* the account says, and on a traveler's scale of time that is true. You can walk the Shelf for a season and meet nothing that would answer if you spoke to it. But the account also says the rock you stand on was once kilometers below you, and that it has been rising, grain by grain, for longer than there have been people. Once you accept that, the question of who lives here has to be asked differently. The right question is not who stands on the Shelf. It is who is *coming up through it*.

## Below

Start deep, where the Risers' pulse is strongest, far down where the stone is warm and still pliant under pressure. Here, one beat every several minutes, a slow wave of force passes upward through the rock. Each wave presses the stone a little tighter and a little higher. Occasionally, where a pulse meets a flaw in the grain, something catches.

It begins as a knot of order in a disordered place, a lattice smaller than a seed that should not be there. It is not quite a crystal. A crystal grows and stops. This one grows and *keeps its shape while changing its substance*, drawing in minerals on one face and giving them up on another, holding a pattern as material flows through it. It is, in that one respect, exactly like the Shelf.

Call them the Rising, since rising is the only thing they ever do.

A newborn Rising cannot move. It has no sideways and no down. The stone carries it upward at the pace of the Riser's push, perhaps the width of a hair each year, and the Rising grows as it goes, sending fine branching threads outward through the grain like frost spreading across a window, very slowly. It feeds on pressure. The compression the Risers drive into the rock is, to the Rising, something like sunlight: a steady, directional abundance that it turns into structure.

It thinks, if thinking is the word, one thought per pulse. Each beat of the Riser moves through its lattice like a breath, and in that passage something shifts: a thread lengthens, a junction settles, a pattern resolves one degree further. Between beats it is still. A Rising experiences the world as a long sequence of these moments, each one a small step upward. It has no sense of the minutes between. Its time is the Riser's time. Its heartbeat is borrowed.

## Middle

Over thousands of years the Rising grows large. By the time it is halfway up, a mature one may run a hundred meters through the rock. It is less an object than a grain, a direction in the stone, a region where the rock is more coherent than rock ought to be.

They are not alone down here. Neighbors rise alongside one another in slow parallel, and where their threads meet they fuse at the tips, and the pulse passes from one to the next with a little delay, like a rumor. Over enough time they come to feel one another as a pressure the way we feel the warmth of someone in the next room. They do not speak. Nothing they could say would fit between two beats. But they *adjust*. Where one grows thick, its neighbor grows thin. Where one presses against a fault, the others brace. Together, across the whole breadth of the Shelf, they form a mesh, and the mesh has a purpose, though no single Rising could tell you what it is.

The purpose is this: to arrive evenly.

## Near the Top

The earlier account describes faint banding in the stone, old ripple-marks visible only when the sun is low and raking, and calls them the ghost of water. Some of them are. Most are not.

Most are the last traces of the Rising, planed down so far that only the pattern survives: the grain of their lattice, ghosted into the pavement. When you walk the Shelf at dawn and see those faint parallel curves sliding out from under your feet, you are seeing the growth rings of something that started its ascent before your language existed.

Close to the surface, the Rising begin to feel the wind.

It reaches them first as a lessening. The weight above them grows lighter each year as the top of the Shelf is sanded away, and the pressure they have fed on all their lives begins, very gradually, to fade. They grow slower. They grow flatter. Their threads, which spread in every direction at depth, now turn sideways and outward, seeking one another, knitting together into broad horizontal sheets.

The pavement plates fit together "like the scales of an animal." This is not a coincidence of weathering. It is the Rising in their last stage. Each plate is the uppermost face of one of them, flattened and set edge to edge with its neighbors so precisely that no plate stands proud of the others by the thickness of a fingernail. They fit like scales because they have been arranging themselves for this for ten thousand years. The seams between them are packed tight with grit because the Rising pull it in, sealing every gap the wind might pry at.

The account says the Shelf is flat because the wind has sanded everything away. That is only half the truth. The Shelf is flat because *they choose to be worn evenly*. A ridge would catch the wind and be torn away in a season. A pit would collect it and gouge deeper. A Rising that broke the pattern would die badly and take its neighbors with it. So they lie level, and they offer the jet stream nothing to grip, and the wind takes them away at exactly the rate the Risers bring them up. The perfect, stubborn smoothness of the plain is not the shape erosion reaches when it runs out of things to take. It is a posture, held by many bodies at once. It is the Rising lying down together, as still as they can manage, so that they will be taken slowly.

Walk barefoot on the pavement just before dawn, after the ground has lost its heat to the black sky, and you may notice that some plates are very slightly warmer than others. These are the ones still thinking. Lay your ear against one between storms, the way you would near a Riser, and wait. Eventually a pulse will pass, faint and delayed, carried up from the Riser far away through a thousand interlocking bodies to the one under your cheek. What you feel is not the Riser. It is the Rising under your cheek, listening to it.

## The End of the Conveyor

Then the wind has them.

A Rising at the surface loses itself a grain at a time. A sheet storm pours over and polishes away a layer thinner than paper, and that layer goes into the air as dust. This is not a wound. It is the thing the whole long ascent was for.

Here the description of the Shelf becomes something it did not quite intend: *the dust behaves more like water than it should.*

It behaves that way because it is not finished. Each grain of the pale, faintly luminous powder in the pans carries a fragment of the lattice, a scrap of the pattern the Rising held for ten thousand years. On its own a grain is nothing. But the grains remember one another. They settle into pans more smoothly than dust should. They close over a footprint within the hour because they are drawn back into order. They flow. The sifters swim through them as fish swim through a current, and it may be that the sifters feed on the lingering pattern itself, the last warmth of the thought going out.

When a sheet passes a waystone and the dust glows green-white around it, that is the dust being *recognized*. Whoever built the waystones built them to call to the Rising, or to what remains of them, and the grains light up in answer as they pass, a brief shimmer of *here, still here*, before the wind carries them on.

## The Towers

This is where the story turns strange, because the Rising do not end when they become dust.

The towers are the rotating columns that draw the finest powder from the pans and hold it, spinning, for days. The finest powder is the oldest part of the Rising, ground smallest, carrying the most of the pattern in the least material. Lifted all together, held close in the spiral, turning in the thin cold air, the grains find one another again.

This is what the pale shape in the tower is.

For the length of a tower's life, a few days, sometimes a week, the dust of many Rising reassembles itself into something that can move. It is long and pale because it is made of pale powder. It rises with the spiral and drops and rises again because it is *finally able to*. For its entire existence a Rising knew only one direction. It was carried up, always up, at the pace of stone. Now, in the tower, for the first and only time, it can go down, and turn, and glide. It is having, in the only way available to it, a life.

It never comes close to travelers because it cannot. To leave the column would be to fall apart into grains again. It never lands because landing is dying. So it stays inside the storm, as a flame stays inside the air that feeds it, and when the tower finally slows and collapses, the pale shape comes apart with it and settles across the pan as a thin, glowing skin of dust that will be gone by morning.

The people who speak of it carefully are right to. It is not dangerous. It is simply the most private thing on the Shelf: a mind that took ten thousand years to grow, spending its one week of freedom. I think they are glad of it. I do not know how glad a lattice can be. But a thing that had only ever been pushed now turns back on itself in the wind, over and over, for no reason at all.

## The Fronts, and What Falls Away

Once or twice a season the jet stream touches down and the whole sky moves. Everything loose on the Shelf goes into the air and travels hundreds of kilometers.

Some of it goes farther than that. Some of it goes over the edge.

The Shelf is a tableland, and tablelands end. At its margins, far from the Risers, the dust of the Rising spills off the rim and falls, for days, down into the thick wet air of the lowlands, where it settles into rivers. The rivers carry it to seas. In the seas it sinks, layer upon layer, into sediment, and the sediment is pressed down into the crust, deeper and warmer, until it is drawn back beneath the Shelf, back to where the Risers pulse. And there, one beat every several minutes, where a pulse meets a flaw in the grain, something catches.

The skates, which feed on "the fine organic matter the wind brings up from the lowlands far below," ride the fronts between the two halves of this cycle, flat and ochre-colored, the same color as the dust. They are not Rising. But they have lived beside the Rising for so long that they have taken up a small office in the great loop: they carry the lowlands up, a little at a time, and they press their thin bodies against the pavement between storms, and the warm plates beneath them can feel them there. It is the only touch from above the Rising ever know, apart from the wind.

## The Stranded

Not every Rising reaches the wind.

When a Riser fails, its pulse stops, and every Rising in its reach stops too, mid-ascent and mid-thought. The lift ends and the wind wins, and the ground sinks into a shallow bowl. The Rising beneath are stranded in the rock with no heartbeat and nothing to carry them upward.

Most of them simply stop. But a few, the ones nearest the surface, do something no Rising was ever meant to do. Without a pulse to carry them, they grow toward the surface anyway. They reach up through the deep dust that pools in the bowl, thread by fine thread, drawing on the residual warmth of the dead machine below.

These are the lanterns.

The account says their leaves are like folded glass, and that they glow at night the same green-white as the waystones in a storm. Now we know why the colors match. The lanterns are Rising that had to climb the last few meters on their own. They do not become dust and ride the towers. They do not get their week of freedom. They stand in the bowls, small and translucent, glowing faintly in the dark, and I believe they are calling. They use the same signal the waystones answer to, sent up into the empty night: *here, still here.*

They grow nowhere else because nowhere else does a Rising have to.

## The Builders

"Whoever built the Risers either left or became something that no longer needs to stand on the ground."

I don't know which it was. But consider the shelters: sealed domes half-sunk into the pavement, single round rooms with a lingering warmth in the floor that has not stopped in all these ages. They are not houses. Nothing lived in them. They are the right size, and the right warmth, and set at the right depth, for something very small to begin.

And consider the lines: a vast, slightly irregular grid scored across the plain, visible only from the height of a tower-storm, from where the pale shape turns. They are not roads. No one walks from above. They are the kind of mark you would leave if you wanted something to know, on its one day of flight, where it came from.

I think the builders did not leave. I think they went *down*. I think they looked at a world where everything wears away, and instead of building something that would resist the wind, they built a way to be worn away well: slowly, evenly, together, with a pulse to keep time by and a storm at the end to fly in. I think the Risers are not machines that hold up a continent. They are hearts, and the continent is what their children lie on.

I can't prove it. The Rising could not tell you either. They have only one word, and it is not a word. It is a direction.

## Night, Again

So lie down on the pavement at night, the way the account suggests. Feel the cold. Watch the stars vanish and return as the sheets pour silently overhead, faster than a galloping horse.

Now understand what you are lying on. Beneath your back, fitted edge to edge so perfectly that you could not slip a blade between them, are the flattened faces of the oldest living things in the world, each in the last years of an ascent that began before your kind had fire. They are warm where they are still thinking. They are holding the plain level with their own bodies so that the wind will take them gently. Every few minutes a pulse arrives from a Riser far away, passed hand to hand across the whole breadth of the Shelf, and each of them, one by one, receives it, takes its one thought, and passes it on.

Below them, kilometers down, the next ones are catching in the grain. Above you, somewhere at the edge of sight, a tower is turning, and inside it something long and pale is climbing the spiral, and dropping, and climbing again.

The account ends by saying the Shelf feels like a place that found the one shape it can hold forever, and is holding it. That is right, except for one thing. A place cannot hold a shape. Something has to do the holding.

They are doing it now, under you, in the dark.
