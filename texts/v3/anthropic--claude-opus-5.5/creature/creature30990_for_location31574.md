# Who Lives in Hesk

## The Lodger

Start with the one thing that is actually there.

The masons don't live in Hesk. They work in it, the way frost works on a window. The dust-moths drift through it and the stilt-ravens pick over it. But one creature does *live* in the city, in the proper sense: it keeps rooms, sleeps under roofs, and passes through doorways. It is the thing that leaves black feathers in spirals on the high balconies.

Call it the Lodger, because that is what it is: the one who stays while the owners are away.

No one has seen it clearly because it lives where the city is undecided. The domes and galleries that exist only from certain angles are its house. When you stand in the right place and see the dome, you are looking at the outside of its home. When you step to the left and see open plaza, you have stepped around to where the Lodger actually is, and it is standing in the space you are looking through, watching you look through it. It doesn't hide. It is simply always on the side of things you are not facing.

What can be known about it comes from traces. Remember the glossy black stone the masons use for thresholds and lintels, the stone that stays cold at midday. The masons don't make it. They find it lying in the street in thin curled sheets, the way you might find a shed snakeskin, and they carry it in their mouths to the nearest doorway and press it into place. The Lodger sheds. It is long and low and made in coils, and each winter it slips out of a layer of itself. What it leaves behind is so cold because it came from somewhere the upward light has never reached, somewhere around the back of a corner.

It eats stilt-ravens, and it eats them slowly. It circles the bird, and the bird, which cannot see it, simply stands there, confused by a sense that the balcony is turning. The Lodger takes the raven apart from the outside in, one feather per revolution. When it finishes, the spiral on the stone is a record of how many times it went around, and the count is always the same: forty-nine. No one knows why.

Think about what the ravens do. They are the only things in Hesk that make a deliberate sound: a single dry click, spaced far apart, like a fingernail tapping a table in another room. Like a clock. Like something counting.

The Lodger does not eat them from hunger. It eats them because they are counting, and the city is not supposed to be counted. A counted city would have to pick a direction. It would have to be either old or young, either ending or beginning. Every click from a raven is a small vote for time running one way. The Lodger keeps the vote from ever being settled. It is the house cat of an undecided place, and it keeps the ticking down so the owners can sleep.

The owners, then.

## The Measurements

Everything in Hesk was made for somebody. The masons are only following the lines. The doors, stairs, sills, and benches were measured to a body, and the body left its dimensions everywhere the way a sleeper leaves a hollow in a mattress.

The doors are tall, perhaps half again the height of a man, but narrow, barely wide enough for a man's shoulders. So the householders were tall and slender, or they turned sideways to go through doors, or their shoulders folded.

The stair treads are longer than a human foot and shorter than a mason's. The risers are low, lower than feels natural, so climbing a Hesk stair feels like walking up a hill that isn't quite there. They climbed gently, then. A tall creature that climbs gently is one that is either very old or very careful, or one for whom a stumble would last a long time.

The windowsills are all set at the same height, just below the chin of the tall figure the doors imply. Nearly every window faces outward and down, toward the rim and the cloud sea. There are almost no windows looking up. Where there are, they have been bricked in with laminated stone. Nobody in Hesk wanted to look at the sky.

The benches are the strangest. They line the edges of plazas and terraces, and they are very long, sometimes the full length of a colonnade, but the seats are shallow, no deeper than a hand. You cannot sit on them. You could only lean. They are rails for resting against, for standing a long time.

So far the picture is of tall, narrow, careful people who stood rather than sat, climbed low stairs slowly, and looked always down at the light.

## The Faces in the Western Stone

In the eastern districts the carvings have worn smooth as soap. You can feel that there were figures on the walls once, but not what they were.

In the west, where the ruins are growing back into buildings, the carvings are *coming up*. Over months you can watch a smooth grey panel develop a shallow bulge, then a ridge, then something like a brow. It is exactly like a face surfacing from water, as the description says. Stay long enough, and bring enough patience, and eventually the faces break the surface.

They are the householders.

They are long, even longer than the doors suggest. Their faces are longer than a horse's, with the features gathered low: eyes set down near what might be the cheek, a small closed mouth near the chin. The top of the head is smooth and high and blank, like the upper half of an egg. In every carving, without exception, the face is turned downward, and the carver has shown it lit from below. The chisel-work, though there was never a chisel, is brightest under the jaw and under the brow ridge and in the hollows beneath the eyes. Their shadows rise up and off the tops of their heads in thin lines carved into the stone above them.

Their hands have too many joints. In the carvings they are always holding something small and cupped against their chests. At first you think it's a bowl. Later, when more of the panel has emerged, you see that the thing in the cupped hands has legs. It has a long neck. It is mostly mouth.

The householders are holding masons the way you would hold a newborn.

Some panels show more. A householder kneels at the edge of a wall. From its mouth, a long bead of something runs down onto the stonework, and the stonework rises to meet it. Beside it, very small, a mason watches with its neck lifted. The carvings seem to say that the householders built Hesk first, and the masons are their students, or their children, or their memory of how.

On one panel in the far west, a householder lies full length on one of the shallow benches. It is not leaning but lying, stretched along the whole colonnade. Its body is laminated like the stone, drawn in fine horizontal lines, each no thicker than a fingernail. It is hard to tell where the householder ends and the bench begins.

## How Slowly They Walked

Here the picture begins to turn over.

The stairs were built for slow climbing. The rails were built for standing a long time. The windows faced a sea of cloud that changes only over seasons. None of this is the architecture of a creature that lives at our speed.

Consider the doorway you pass twice, the one with the particular crack in its lintel. On the second pass the crack is wider and a century has gone by. You tell yourself you are being turned through time. But perhaps you have stepped briefly into a householder's stride. Perhaps you walked, for a few turns of the street, at the pace the streets were made for. One of their steps is a lifetime of dust settling. One of their glances down at the cloud sea lasts for a generation of masons.

It would explain the distances. A street is not really three days long. It is three days long *for them*, which is to say it is as long as it takes them to feel they have gone somewhere. The city is larger on the inside of its streets because the streets are measured in their time, and when you walk them you borrow a little of it without meaning to.

It would explain the chord. At dusk, a whole district of stone rings in a long faint tone that swells and fades over several minutes. The description calls this the walls contracting in the cold. Perhaps it is. But the masons stop working and lift their necks, and they do not do that for anything else. They do not do it for the ravens or the wind or the black minutes when the cloud sea parts. They do it only for the chord, the way a dog lifts its head at a familiar voice from the next room.

It is a syllable. That is the thing you eventually cannot stop believing. One syllable of something being said, slowly, by someone for whom several minutes is a breath. A district sounds at once because the voice is large and the stone is the only thing in Hesk dense enough to carry it. If you stayed a thousand years in the plaza, listening every dusk, you might hear a sentence.

The masons have heard sentences. That is why they know where the lines are.

## Where They Actually Are

So where are they? You can walk every avenue and never see a householder, never feel one pass, never find a footprint.

Two places.

The first is the sky's floor. On certain evenings the black upper sky turns solid, and the shadows rising from the towers touch it and pool there like smoke on a ceiling. Look carefully on those evenings, especially over the western districts. Some of the pooled shadows have no tower beneath them. They are long and narrow. They are the right shape. Their heads are turned downward, toward you.

Those are the evenings the householders are home. You cannot see them, because they are lit, as everything in Hesk is lit, from underneath, and they are so tall and so slow that their bodies take in the light without giving it back. You can only see what they cast. Their shadows fall up onto the dark and gather there. On those evenings, when you feel the pull to fall toward the cloud sea, you are feeling what they feel all the time. They are not quite standing on the plateau. They are leaning out over it from the sky's floor, the way you lean on a rail, looking down at the light.

The second place is the seam.

## The Seam

A band a quarter mile wide runs north to south through the city, where buildings flicker between old and new and the dust hangs in the air unable to decide which way to fall. The masons gather there more than anywhere else, and the description suggests they are stitching the two tides together. They are, but the stitching is not only for the city's sake.

The householders live in both halves of Hesk, in opposite directions.

In the east they grow old. Their city ages around them, the corners round off, the stairs wear into bowls, and they wear with it. They lean longer on the benches and turn their faces more steadily down, until they are laminated all the way through and are simply part of the wall.

In the west they grow young. They come *out* of the walls. The dust blows inward to make them, the carvings sharpen into their faces, and the ruins stand up around them as they stand up themselves, straightening like the leaning column that takes a season to come upright.

They are the same people. The householder aging into the eastern stone and the one surfacing from the western stone are one life, running both ways from the middle. Only at the seam do the two directions touch. For as long as the seam holds, which for them is an instant and for us is a quarter mile of hanging dust, a householder is exactly itself, not ending and not beginning. It can walk through a door at full height, climb a low stair gently, lean against a long bench, and look down at the cloud.

That is what the doors are for. The description says they are made for inhabitants who have not arrived yet, or who left long ago, or who will come and go in the same instant at the seam. It is all three, and it is one person.

This is why the masons crowd the seam, laying their thin beads of paste along walls that are crumbling and assembling at once. They are not only keeping the city from tearing. They are keeping the *moment* from tearing: the one place and time where the people who made them are whole. Every fingernail's width of stone a mason lays at the seam is a fingernail's width more of that present, one more fraction of an instant in which a householder can stand in a doorway and be home.

The seam drifts east over centuries. The masons follow it because it is where the householders are. The ancient eastern ruins begin, reluctantly, to heal as it passes. It is like an old person who, as a younger relative enters the room, sits up a little and remembers who they were.

## What the Walls Are Made Of

One more turn, and then the picture settles.

The stone of Hesk has no seams, no joints, no tool marks. Nothing was cut. It was laid down grain by grain, in laminations each no thicker than a fingernail, and it rings faintly when struck.

The masons make it from three things. The first is dust, licked from the troughs in the streets, and those troughs were worn not by feet but by the dust itself moving like slow water. The second is the dawn rime, the only moisture in Hesk, which lasts twenty minutes and then blows sideways as a faint glitter. The third is *something else*, a secretion from the mason's own throat. The carvings show a householder kneeling at a wall with a bead running from its mouth. A mason carries in its throat whatever its makers carried in theirs.

And the dust: in the east, the householders wear away with their city, and when they are finished aging they are dust. In the west, the wind blows dust *into* the walls and faces surface from the stone. The dust in the troughs, sliding along every street of Hesk like slow water, is the householders in between, grain by grain, on their way from one end of their lives to the other. It flows east toward the ruins or west toward the rebuilding, and at the seam it stops and hangs, because it is *them*, and for a moment they are home.

So the masons lick the householders up from the streets, mix them with the only water in the world, add a little of what the householders gave them, and lay them down again, one fingernail's width a day. Each lamination is a day of someone's life, pressed thin. This is why the stone rings. It has a voice in it. At dusk, when the whole district chimes and the masons lift their necks, the city is not contracting in the cold. It is the people in the walls saying one more slow syllable, and the masons stop to hear which line they should fill in next.

## Standing There, Again

So who lives in Hesk?

A cold coiled thing lives in the back of every corner, eating the ravens so the city will never be counted.

Forty or fifty grey creatures, mostly mouth, were once held in cupped hands and taught a single skill. They practice it every day with great care, without knowing why, following the voice in the stone.

And the householders live there. They are tall, narrow, and gentle, with long downturned faces and too many joints in their hands. They built the stairs low because they climbed slowly and the benches shallow because they preferred to lean. They bricked up every window that looked at the sky. They are too slow to see and too large to hear except as a chord in the walls. They are shadows pooled on the underside of the night, looking down. They are dust in the streets, rime on the north walls, fine grey laminations in every tower. They are aging in the east and being born in the west. For one long instant at the seam they are simply present, standing in their own doorways, whole.

They are not absent. They are everywhere, all at once, spread through the entire age of the city, the way a tree's years are spread through its rings. No one lives in Hesk, because no one could live there in the way we mean. Or everyone does, because the city is made of them, every grain.

If you stand in the great plaza at dusk and the chord rises out of the stone around you, notice what you feel. It is not quite loneliness. It is the particular unease of standing in a crowded room with your eyes closed. The chord fades. The mason at the wall lowers its neck and lays one more bead of paste, a fingernail's width, one more day. Somewhere around the back of a corner that isn't there, something cold turns once, very slowly, and settles in to sleep.
