# Who Lives on Hollin Height

## I. The Listener

Start with one of them, because it is easiest to begin with a person.

Her name, spoken aloud, is Oda Vell. She has not heard it spoken in eleven years, since her brother died, and nobody else would think to say it; in the open she is a gesture, the first two fingers of the right hand drawn down the left forearm like a bow across a string. It means *she who hears the fourth voice*, because for fifty-three years she has tended the engine the town counts fourth, a tower on the sixth terrace whose chord sits low and slightly flat and has a slow waver in it, like an old man humming while he works.

She wakes before the Lift, because everyone does. The body learns. In the last hour of the night the chord begins, imperceptibly, to lean. The pistons are approaching the top of their stroke, the bellows are nearly full, and the pressure in the air changes in a way no instrument in the Water Guild's cabinet can measure. The sleeping people of Hollin turn over in their low stone beds and open their eyes all at once, like a field of flowers under a single cloud.

Oda sits up and puts her palm flat on the floor. The floor is warm. She presses and waits, and through the stone, through the heel of her hand and the bones of her wrist, she feels the fourth voice arriving at itself.

She is almost entirely deaf in the ordinary sense. Birdsong, if there were birdsong, would be lost on her. Speech in a hush reaches her as a soft wool of sound, and she reads lips out of habit and courtesy. But put her in the open terrace at noon and she will tell you, with a flick of her fingers, that the ninth voice has a pipe fouled with scale a third of the way up, and that the bellows on the twenty-second are going to split along an old seam before the season turns. She is always right. She does not know how she knows. The knowing lives somewhere under her breastbone, in a place she thinks of, privately, as a second pair of ears that faces inward.

The Listeners' hands are recognizable anywhere on the plateau. They are broad and flat, the fingertips thick with callus from resting on iron, and the palms are so sensitive that some of them wear gloves indoors, the way people elsewhere wear dark lenses against bright light. Their chests are deep. Many of them have a habit of standing with their feet a little apart and their knees soft, which the townsfolk call *standing in the chord*, and which a stranger might mistake for someone on the deck of a ship.

That is the first answer to the question of who lives here: people like Oda. People who have made themselves into instruments for receiving one very large sound.

It is not the whole answer.

## II. The Children of the Chord

Children in Hollin are born in the hushes, always. A woman near her time moves into the deepest quiet her family can find or borrow, and families with a house in the heart of a good hush are, in a sense, the plateau's midwives. They open their doors to strangers for this and are paid in rust-finished iron, in cress, in favors that last generations.

The newborn is kept in quiet for forty days. On the forty-first morning, before the Lift, the child is carried out to the edge of the hush and held facing the nearest engine. When the chord rises, the baby goes silent, every baby, the families say. Not stunned, not frightened. Attending. The small face goes slack and inward. The fists open. Then the Lift passes, the Wringing begins, the child is streaming wet like everything else, and at last it cries: a high, thin, furious sound that no one around it can hear, and that everyone watches with enormous tenderness.

This is called *giving the child its note*. It is believed that each person in Hollin carries one tone of the chord inside them, received on that forty-first morning, and that it depends on which engine you faced and where you stood. Oda's note came from the fourth voice. That, the town says, is why she ended up tending it. Whether this is true, or whether it is a story that makes itself true by being believed, nobody in Hollin thinks it worth asking.

The children learn sign before speech, as the description says, but the first thing they learn is something older than sign. They learn to feel the hushes. A toddler who wanders into the open terrace will stop, sway, and turn back before any adult reaches her, as if she had walked into a wall of warm water. Young children play games along the edges of the hushes, daring each other a step out into the loud, then scrambling back, laughing silently. The oldest game in the town, played with pebbles on a chalk grid, is a map of the chord. Children who play it well grow up to be Listeners.

As they grow they become somewhat deaf, as everyone does. Their hearts, too, begin to do something the physicians from the lowlands, when they visit, find alarming and cannot explain. At rest, the heart of a grown Hollin-born person beats in a slow, exact relationship to the beat-frequency between the two engines nearest their home. It is not the same rate. It is a ratio: three beats of the heart to one swell of interference, or five to two. Move a Hollin family to a house in a different hush, and over a month or so their hearts will shift, sometimes uncomfortably, to a new ratio.

The townsfolk know this and do not find it strange. When they move house they say they are *retuning*. When someone is dying, the family carries them, if they can, back to the hush where they were born, so that they die in their first ratio. When the heart finally stops, the Listeners say that person has *gone into the chord*, and they mean it literally.

## III. The Dead

What the people of Hollin do with their dead is the first place where the question of who lives on the plateau begins to shift under the feet.

There is no soil to bury anyone in. There is no wood to burn them with. The dead are carried at night, wrapped in undyed wool, out of the hushes and into the loud places near the engines, onto the broad rings of lichen that encircle each tower's base. They are laid down in the pale green and the grey and the startling pinkish gold. Then everyone goes home, and does not look back, and waits for the Lift.

The chord is hardest there. The water is wrung out of the air fastest there. Over a season the body goes down into the lichen: softening, greening, gilding. The rings do not simply grow over it; they reorganize around it. A person laid on the stone leaves a mark in the lichen's pattern that persists after nothing of the person remains. It is a small kink in a ring, a place where the standing waves bend slightly. The Listeners can read these marks, and they will tell you, pointing to a curl of pink-gold on the base of the fourth engine, *that one is my grandmother*. They can tell you which of the thirty-one voices she carried.

So the lichen is not only a map of the sound. It is a record of everyone who has ever lived in the sound. And because the lichen's patterns reflect the vibration that shapes them, and because the vibration, very faintly, is shaped in turn by everything it passes through, there is a belief among the Listeners that the dead have a small say in the chord. Each body laid in the rings nudges the harmony a hair's breadth. Over five hundred years that is a great many nudges.

When the Listeners say the whole harmony is slowly descending, some of them add, in the quiet of the deepest hushes, *because it is getting heavier*.

## IV. The Swifts

Go up the engine towers, with wool in your ears and a guide's hand on your shoulder, and you will find the drum-swifts.

The description says they nest in the pipes and communicate by vibration and navigate by the pressure of the chord. All of that is true. What the description does not say, because only the tenders know it, is that the swifts *work*.

Scale builds in the pipes. The water that drinks the heat of the deep rock carries dissolved stone up with it, and the stone comes out of solution on the pipe walls as a creamy crust that thickens year by year. A fouled pipe sounds flat, and a flat pipe sours the chord around it. The tenders can clear the lower pipes with long iron scrapers, but the upper throats, forty meters up in the steam, are beyond human reach.

The swifts clear them. Their nests are cups of mud and down pressed into the scale itself, and when they drum on the iron, in their rapid, flickering patterns, the scale cracks and sloughs away in flakes. Tenders standing at the base of a tower on a still day will see them come drifting down, pale, like slow snow. A pipe with a thriving swift colony stays clean for centuries. A pipe the swifts abandon fouls within a generation.

And the swifts do abandon pipes. Nobody knows how they choose. A colony will leave a throat that seems perfectly sound and crowd into one that is already half-choked, and within a few years the choked pipe rings clear again and the abandoned one begins to sag. The Listeners have come, over lifetimes, to understand that the swifts are not responding to the state of the pipes. They are responding to the state of the *chord*. They go where the harmony needs them.

There is an old Listener's sign for the swifts that is not a bird. It is the gesture for *tuning*: the thumb and forefinger pinched together and turned, slowly, as though tightening a peg.

So now there are two kinds of creature on Hollin Height who tend the engines. One is warm-blooded, deaf, hand-talking, and buries its dead in the lichen. The other is warm-blooded, deaf, wing-drumming, and drops its dead down the pipes, where they are blown out at the next Lift as small dark shapes that fall into the rings. Both are keeping the same enormous instrument in tune, and neither is quite sure why.

## V. The Rats in the Walls

The description says the rats are oddly untroubled. That is worth a moment.

They live in the channel-walls, in the dished stone along the water, in modest numbers. They do not multiply out of control, though there is food enough: the weed, the flies, the refuse of a city. They do not sicken. They do not go mad, as the dogs do. They sit very still on the lips of the channels at dawn, whiskers spread wide, while the Lift comes up through the rock, and the townsfolk who have watched them closely say the rats close their eyes.

Hollin children are taught not to kill them. It is not a strong taboo, just a sense that it is rude, like shouting. An old saying in sign runs: *the rats know where the water goes*. It is said of anyone who is quiet and competent and not much noticed. And it is literally true that when the Water Guild wants to trace a leak in the old works, a seepage somewhere in the gorge-side sluices, the apprentices go down with lanterns and follow the rats. The rats' runs are the shortest paths along the channels, and the rats never nest where the stone is failing.

There is a thing they do that nobody writes down. Every so often, once or twice in a lifetime, a family in one of the hushes finds a rat sitting in the middle of their floor at dawn, facing the nearest engine, whiskers spread, eyes shut. It does not move when approached. It stays through the Lift and through the Wringing, and then it leaves. Within the year, without exception, that family's house falls out of the hush. The quiet has shifted a few paces, as the hushes do, a few paces a century, and the rat was sitting on the new edge before anyone else could feel it.

The rats, in other words, can feel the hushes move.

That is the moment in the description where the question starts to open. Because what exactly is it that is moving?

## VI. The Hushes

Here is what the description says: the hushes are places where the interference of the engine-voices cancels itself out. They are irregular, they do not move or they move very slowly, and they drift over generations.

Here is what the people of Hollin do not say aloud: *it is not an absence.*

Stand in the heart of the Undertone at night, when the market is closed and the rail terminus is dark and the only sound is the wool-soft hum of thirty-one voices cancelling each other into near-nothing. Stand there long enough and you will feel what every Hollin child feels at the edge of her first game of dare. There is someone there.

Not a ghost. The townsfolk are very clear on this; the dead go into the chord and into the lichen, not into the quiet. Not a spirit, not a god. The sign for it is the sign for the hushes themselves, the palm pressed flat and lowered slowly, as though smoothing a sheet, but made with both hands, and made facing *inward*, toward the signer's own chest. It means something like *the held breath*, or *the one who is listening back*.

What it is, as nearly as anyone can say, is the shape the sound makes when it is not sounding. The cancellation of thirty-one enormous voices is not simply silence. It is the exact negative of the chord, a region where every wave is met by its perfect opposite. And that region has an edge, and a texture, and a slow, wandering will of its own. It drifts toward some things and away from others. Hushes have been known to swell by a courtyard's width in the years after a good birth, and to contract after violence. The Undertone has held the fig tree for several centuries and has never once drifted far enough to leave it at the edge. The Listeners suspect the hush is keeping the fig, the way a hand might cup a candle.

The quiet ones, if you want a name, cannot be seen and cannot be touched. They can be *leaned against*. People who live in the deepest hushes say that on bad nights, grieving or sick or afraid, they have lain down on the stone floor of their houses and felt the silence lean back, the way a large warm animal leans into you when you sit beside it in the dark.

Mothers give birth in the hushes. The dying are carried back to them. Cats, which are prized, live only in their deepest parts, and the cats' behavior is interesting: they sit for hours watching nothing, tails wrapped round their feet, with the calm of animals in the presence of something much larger and entirely unthreatening.

The dogs go mad. Perhaps the dogs can feel the hushes too, and cannot bear the thought of something that big being that quiet.

## VII. Underneath

Then there is the heat.

It rises through shafts no one living has fully mapped. The tenders go down a certain distance, with ropes and lamps and wax in their ears, to check the lower valves. Below that distance they do not go. The explanation they give is practical: the air grows too hot and the stone too slick. That is true. It is not the explanation they give each other.

Down there, in the warm black wet at the roots of the engines, the chord is different. It is less a sound than a *motion*, a vast, slow rising and falling that you feel in the whole body at once, the way a swimmer feels a swell passing under her in open water. And the tenders who have gone deepest say that beneath the stroke of the pistons, beneath the fill and empty of the bellows, there is another rhythm, far slower. It is a pulse so long that a man might stand through a whole shift and feel it rise only once. It does not come from the engines. The engines come from it.

The old records of the Water Guild mention, in one place only, a tender named Harl who went down past the last marked valve and came back three days later. He spent the rest of his life in the Undertone, signing the same short phrase to anyone who would watch. It was a phrase with no clear meaning in the hand-language, a slow closing of the fist followed by a slow opening of the fist, repeated. The Guild scribes recorded it as *pumping*. The Listeners who read the record now render it differently. They say it is the sign a mother makes to a child to mean *heartbeat*, but done at the pace of the deep pulse, so slowly it took him the whole length of a breath to close his hand.

## VIII. What Lives Here

So we come back to the question and it has changed shape, as things on Hollin Height do, slowly and without asking permission.

Who lives on Hollin Height?

People live here: deaf, broad-handed, silent in the open and soft-voiced indoors, their hearts beating in exact ratio to the interference of iron voices. They are born in the quiet, given their note on the forty-first morning, buried in the lichen, and remembered as a kink in a pink-gold ring. Swifts live here, drumming scale out of the pipes where the harmony needs them. Rats live here, who know where the water goes and sit on the edges of the hushes before the hushes move. A fig lives here, cupped for centuries in a quiet that will not let it go. Somewhere below, a pulse lives, so slow a man went half-mad trying to hold it in his fist.

But look at it the other way, the way the Listeners do in the deepest hushes, when they speak aloud at all.

The engines draw heat from below and turn it into sound. The sound wrings water from the sky. The water holds the stone together, and runs down to the lowlands, and makes them green. The lichen records the sound, and the dead are written into the lichen, and the lichen, very slightly, shapes the sound. The swifts keep the throats clear where the chord asks. The Listeners patch the bellows and hear where it falters. The rats follow the water, the cats attend the hushes, and the hushes wander toward births and away from violence, and hold the fig. The Bruise, the one place a guild tried to silence a voice, has taken a wound that the whole harmony is patiently healing, and the Listeners say it will take a thousand years, which is to say they know exactly how long it takes this body to close a cut.

It is all one thing.

That is what lives on Hollin Height. Not the people, not the swifts, not the rats or the quiet ones or the pulse beneath, but the whole of it, with those as its parts. It is a creature the size of a plateau. Its heart is in the deep rock, its breath is in the bellows, and its voice is a chord of thirty-one throats. It sweats water through every pore of its stone skin, it grows a lichen hide that remembers everything, and it has swifts for its tuning fingers and a city of silent people for its ears.

It has no hands. That, perhaps, is why the people of Hollin speak with theirs. A body that can only sing has grown, slowly, over five hundred years, a population that can *shape*. The town's builders dig rooms into the stone and deepen the channels; its Water Guild turns wrung water through sluices and wheels. Its children play the chalk-grid game that maps the chord. Its Listeners press their fingertips to their sternums and feel the thing they live inside, and know, wordlessly, where it hurts.

It is young, in its own terms. That is the last thing the Listeners say, and only in the deepest quiet. The whole harmony is descending by an interval so small it would take ten thousand years to fall a tone. It is growing heavier, they say, with the weight of all the dead in the rings. It is *singing itself into a shape it has not yet reached*. They do not know what that shape is. Some believe it will stop. Some believe it will begin again. But the ones who have lain on the stone floors of the old houses and felt the silence lean back think something else.

They think it is learning to speak.

They think a chord of thirty-one voices, falling slowly toward its foundation, is like an infant's cry dropping over years toward a voice. And they think that when it reaches the bottom it will say something. And they think, though none of them would say this even in the hushes, even in the dark, that they are not only its ears and hands. They think they may be the first word it is trying to form.

## IX. Dawn

Oda Vell stands at the edge of her hush on the sixth terrace. Her bare feet are flat on the warm stone, her knees are soft, and her hand rests on her chest.

The fourth voice leans toward its stroke. She feels the pistons approach the top, the bellows full, the swifts shifting in the pipes above like an audience settling. At her feet, on the lip of the channel, a rat sits with its whiskers spread wide and its eyes shut. Behind her the hush holds its breath, and she can feel it holding, and she can feel it, very faintly, lean against her back.

Somewhere far below, a fist closes, taking the length of a lifetime to do it.

The Lift comes. The whole height shouts its one enormous, rending major chord at the sky, and Oda Vell, who has heard nothing ordinary for fifty years, hears all of it. She hears the fourth voice arriving at itself, and the thirty other voices arriving with it. She hears the swifts pour upward in their thousands, and the stone begin to stream. And under it all, she hears the thing she lives inside, drawing, once a day, its long and patient breath.

She takes her hand from her chest and signs to no one, to everyone, to the plateau itself, the oldest gesture she knows. It is a slow closing of the fist and a slow opening.

*Yes. I'm here. Go on.*
