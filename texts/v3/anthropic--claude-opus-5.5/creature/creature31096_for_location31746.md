# Who Lives at Lathe

## The Thirty-Some

Start with the ones you can see.

At any time there are between thirty and forty of them, and the number moves slowly, like everything else in the basin. A few come up from the valleys each decade and stay. A few leave, usually in their first three years, usually because of the sound. A child is born perhaps once every two winters, and when it happens the whole population comes to the house, all of them, crowding one enormous room so that for a single evening it looks the way it was built to look.

They are not a tall people or a short one. They come from everywhere below and have not been here long enough to become a type. But the basin marks them all the same way, and you learn to recognise a Lathe person in a valley market by the marks.

Their hands give them away first. Lime settles in the creases of the knuckles and around the nails and never fully washes out, so their hands look lightly chalked, as if they had just been kneading. The older ones have fingernails ridged pale at the tips. They say this doesn't hurt. They say, laughing, that it's the stone trying them on for size.

Their voices are big. Everyone who stays long enough develops a way of speaking pitched just above the drone, a carrying, chesty register, flat in the vowels, that sounds in the lowlands like shouting. They aren't shouting. They're talking. Down in the valleys they seem boisterous and slightly deaf, and they are forever being asked to lower their voices. When they try, they find they can't hear themselves, and they grow uneasy, like people walking on ice.

Their skin is cool to the touch. The air of the bowl is always a sweater's warmth and always damp, and they carry that coolness down the mountain with them. Lowland children, hugging a visiting uncle from Lathe, say he smells like the inside of a well. They mean it kindly, and it's accurate.

## Their Names

A child of Lathe gets two names.

The first is ordinary: a lowland name, or a family name, the kind you can shout across a square. Aud, Colm, Hesketh, Ivry, Brannagh, Tully.

The second is sung. Within a day of the birth, the child is carried to the horn nearest the house, and the mother or father, or whoever is holding it, hums a note against the drone of that horn: a short phrase, two or three tones, sometimes only one, set at a chosen interval above or below the horn's pitch. That phrase is the child's true name. It has no letters. It can't be spoken, only hummed, and only really understood in the presence of the horn it was set against.

But the horns grow deeper as they thicken. Over a lifetime a horn's note falls by the distance between two notes, so a name set in childhood drifts. When an old person hums their own name against the horn of their birth, the interval has changed. It is a different chord now. It is still their name, the way a face in a mirror is still their face.

Lovers learn each other's sung names and hum them in the dark, where the drone is always present to make the interval. It is thought unbearably intimate to hum someone's name to them in the lowlands, away from any horn. There is nothing there to hold the interval, and the name comes out as a mere tune.

## What They Do All Day

They tend.

This is the thing a visitor eventually understands: the people of Lathe are not really builders, though they call themselves that. They are gardeners of stone, and most of their work is fighting the garden.

**The Scrapers** walk the lanes each morning with antler picks and flat copper blades and clear the night's growth from doorways, window-rounds and channel mouths. A morning's deposit is soft, like wet chalk, and comes away in curls. Children collect the curls and press them into little shapes, which harden on the windowsills by evening. Every house in Lathe has a sill crowded with these: small lumpy birds and faces, the town's first art. Most are thrown into the channels eventually, where they become part of the rims. Very little in Lathe is ever really thrown away. It just moves into the architecture.

**The Weavers** work in the green quarter, where the new wicker stands dripping and supple. They are the most visionary of the townspeople and the most patient. A weaver is designing a room she will sleep in at seventy, or never sleep in at all. The best of them think in three time-scales at once: the shape of the wicker now, the shape the stone will be when the wicker has vanished inside it, and the shape the room will slowly close toward if no one tends it. A good weaver leaves generous doorways, because every opening in Lathe narrows over time. A great one leaves openings so large they seem absurd, arches you could drive a hay-cart through, knowing that in two hundred years they will be a comfortable human size.

**The Channel-Cutters** are the most respected and the most feared, gently. They decide where the water goes, and so they decide what becomes stone. Redirect a flow and you can grow a wall in a new direction, or thicken a vault, or let a room finally dry. Neglect a flow and you can lose a house. They are careful people. They speak slowly for Lathe folk. When two households quarrel, the Cutters are called, and the quarrel is often settled by moving a trickle a hand's-breadth one way or the other, a decision that will play out over a century.

**The Readers** keep the measuring rods and can read a life in bands. They are usually the oldest. You bring them your birth-reed and they hold it up to the grey light from a roof-opening, turning it, and tell you your own history in wet years and dry: here is the thick band of the flood spring when you were five; here is the fine dark ash line; here, this thin pale layer, is the dry year your father went down the mountain and didn't come back until autumn. People say you should not ask a Reader to read your rod unless you're ready, because they will tell you what they see. It is all written there, and they have no talent for tact.

**The Cradle-Keepers** tend the upper terraces where the dead are given to the water. There are usually only two or three of them. More on them below.

And everyone, all of them, spends part of every day standing still in some enormous empty room, listening. Nobody calls this work. Nobody would think to skip it.

## The Children

There are rarely more than five or six children in Lathe at once, and each grows up with more space than any child anywhere.

A Lathe child has a hundred empty houses to run through. Rooms the size of barns. Vaults that swallow a shout completely, because the drone is always louder. They play a game called Finding, which is hide-and-seek played by rhythm: the seeker stands in the roar with eyes shut and listens for the particular splash-pattern of the hider's feet crossing a channel. Good players can name a friend by footfall from three houses away. The best hiders learn to walk in step with the dripping.

They are fearless around water and wary of stillness. A Lathe child dropped into a lowland meadow on a windless day will often cry, and not know why.

Each child has a reed, given at birth, set in a steady flow near the family house. They check it compulsively, the way lowland children measure themselves against a doorpost. *Mine's thicker than yours.* When a child is old enough to wonder about death, a parent will sometimes take them up to the head of the basin and show them a very old rim, a pale moss-covered swell, and tell them whose it is. Then they show them their own reed, and let them work out the rest.

They grow up knowing, without distress, that they will become part of the floor.

## The Dippers

There are always exactly two.

People have tried to count the generations and failed, because you never see young dippers in Lathe, and you never see a dead one. Some say the pair simply lives a very long time. Some say that when one dies, a stranger dipper flies up from the valleys and takes its place within the day, and the survivor accepts it without comment. Some say, only half joking, that they are the same two birds they have always been, and that dippers, living in the lime-water and nesting in the growing stone, have become a little stony themselves and don't wear out.

The people treat them almost as neighbours. You say good morning to the dippers. You apologise if you startle them. You don't give them names, because it seems presumptuous, as if they were the ones doing the naming.

Their song is one of the only sounds in Lathe that reliably cuts through the roar, a high, sweet, scratchy warble, pitched above everything. When a Lathe person has been in the lowlands a long time and comes home, it is often the dipper-song they hear first, before the horns, before the waterfalls. Many of them weep at it and are embarrassed.

## The Heron and the Stag

The heron comes some autumns. It stands in the upper pool for days, grey and patient, and fishes for nothing, since there are almost no fish. People leave it alone because it looks like it belongs to the same order of time as the stone. Some think it comes to look at the dead. Some think it comes because, in the whole world, this is the one place where a creature so still does not seem strange.

The stag is seen once in a generation, in the fog at the head of the basin. Always a single person sees it, never two together. Always at the same place, by the oldest horn. Always it stands for a long moment and then is gone into the cloud. Nobody has ever found a track.

There is a quiet understanding in Lathe that when you see the stag, you have become a real inhabitant. Not a visitor who stayed, but one of the basin's own. People who have seen it don't boast about it. They just tend a little more carefully afterward, as if they'd been told something.

## The Majority

Here is the first turn in the counting.

Thirty or forty people live in Lathe. But the town is built on its dead, and the dead outnumber the living many times over.

The upper terraces, where the cradles are laid, are the newest part of the burial ground. The older rims below them are the older dead, and the oldest are the ones around the head spring, so grown over, so thick with moss and centuries of stone, that no one living knows their names. The Readers can estimate from the thickness roughly when each was laid. That's all.

So when you ask a Lathe person how many people live in their town, you get two answers, and they can't really choose between them. *Thirty-four,* they'll say. And then, after a moment: *Or everyone.*

The Cradle-Keepers speak of the dead not as ghosts and not as ancestors, exactly, but as a kind of slow population, citizens who've moved into a longer rhythm of life. They don't think the dead are aware in any ordinary way. They do think the dead are *doing* something: growing, holding up the terraces, shaping where the water goes. A rim made of someone's grandmother turns the stream a particular way, and that turn has consequences for which houses get water, which walls thicken, which horns sound. In that sense the dead are still making decisions for the town, extremely slowly, the way stone makes decisions.

This is what people mean when they say the horns speak with the voices of the dead. It isn't sentiment. Some of the stone that rings is made of them. The water pours across a mouth whose walls contain, somewhere in their laminae, the calcified weave of a funeral cradle, and a sealed-in hand, and the hum becomes a chord.

Children sometimes ask: *Which horn is Grandmother?* The Keepers answer honestly: *None of them yet. Give her four hundred years.*

## The Expected

Here is the second turn.

Walk through Lathe and you'll notice that nearly every empty room has something in it. Not much. A bench. A folded blanket. A shallow clay bowl, turned upside down. A single wicker stool going pale at the edges where the damp has touched it.

These are laid out for the Expected.

The houses of Lathe are built for households that don't exist yet, and the people are too practical to call that a delusion. They simply believe that the future is a kind of resident. It hasn't arrived, but it has already been given a room, and it would be rude to leave the room completely bare.

So when a weaver finishes the wicker of a new house, she sets a blanket inside it before the water is ever led over. When a house matures enough to be sound, someone climbs up and replaces the rotten blanket with a fresh one. It isn't a ritual anyone performs with ceremony. You just notice a blanket is getting musty and you swap it out, the way you'd tidy a spare bed for a visitor whose arrival date is uncertain.

The Expected are never named, never imagined in any detail. People don't speculate much about who they'll be. Asked, a Lathe person will shrug and say, *Whoever needs the room.* But the Expected shape everything. The town is laid out for them. Its streets are wide for their crowds. Its vaults are tall for their celebrations. Its horns are tuned, loosely, for a chord that will only reach its full depth centuries from now.

If the dead are the town's past citizens, grown into the floor, the Expected are its future ones, already allotted their walls. The thirty-some living people are a thin bright layer between two enormous populations, like the soft white rim that grows between the old stone below and the empty air above.

## The Sealed Rooms

In the oldest quarter there are a few houses that were not tended and have closed completely. From outside they are just rounded masses of pale stone, heavy with moss, fern-covered. You can make out where the doors were, low swellings where the lime closed over the openings, like healed wounds.

People don't like to guess what was left inside.

But everyone does guess, a little. The children tell stories, of course. The adults are quieter. Some think the sealed rooms simply hold air: a century-old breath of the basin, a pocket of some earlier year's fog, trapped and unchanging. Some think a household was laid to rest inside deliberately, a whole family choosing, at the end, to let their house take them together instead of going up to the cradles.

There is one sealed room, the Readers say, from which a note can be heard. It's very faint and only audible in the frost-silence, on the holy days when the upper channels lock and the horns go quiet from the top of the basin down. On those days, if you stand very close to its moss-covered flank and hold your breath, there is a hum. Low and steady, like a horn. But no water runs over that house. No horn is set in it.

The Channel-Cutters say it's water moving inside the stone, through the porous chambers, finding its own way. That is almost certainly right. Everyone agrees that it's right. Nobody quite stops listening.

## The Moss

Here is the third turn, and the source describes it plainly: moss is the true citizen of Lathe.

Every rim in the basin is built on moss. The water filters through the moss, leaves its lime, and the moss is buried, and grows upward, and is buried again, and grows upward again. Cut any wall and it's there: fossil moss in fine laminae, the pages of a book in which every page is a green thing that almost escaped.

The people of Lathe know this and have, over generations, come to recognise themselves in it. There's a saying: *We are the moss that talks.* It means: we are the thin living layer that keeps the stone growing. We are always being entombed, always climbing out. We are the reason the town rises at all. If the moss stopped growing, the lime would only crust the old stone. It would thicken, but it wouldn't *build.* The terraces rise because something green insists, every season, on growing a hair taller than its own burial.

The people are the same. Every doorway scraped open each morning, every channel recut, every wicker frame woven in the green quarter is a small insistence against being sealed in. The town rises because they keep climbing out of it.

## The One Who Actually Lives There

So you have the living few, the counted and the uncounted dead, the Expected, the sealed rooms and whatever hums in them, the two dippers, the rumoured stag, the grazing snails in their stone spirals, and the moss beneath all of them. It's a large census for a town that looks empty.

But if you stay long enough, until the noise stops being noise, until you catch yourself pitching your voice above the drone without thinking, until your fingernails go pale at the tips, you come to feel that all of these are really one thing.

The people have a word for it. They use it sparingly, the way lowlanders use the name of a god. They call it *the Arriving.*

The Arriving is not the spring, or not only. It is the whole slow process: water rising cold from the mountain's heart, heavy with lime; the thin air drawing its breath out; the stone letting go and settling on moss and wicker and feathers and the dead; the rims climbing by a fingernail a year; the horns deepening; the doorways narrowing; the town thickening toward its own completion. It is a presence that moves at the speed of stone. It has no face. It does not notice anyone. It will never be finished.

And everything in Lathe lives *inside* it: the living, the dead, the Expected, the dippers, the moss. They are all layers the Arriving is laying down. Some are thin and bright and short-lived, some thick, some dark as ash, some grown over with green. The Readers, holding a measuring rod up to the grey light, are reading the Arriving's handwriting. The singers humming a child's name against a horn are setting that child into the Arriving's chord. The Cradle-Keepers, guiding the spring over a woven body on the upper terraces, are simply handing someone back.

You could say the Arriving is what lives in the Basin at Lathe, and everyone else is visiting. The people wouldn't argue. But they would add, reasonably, that they are the part of the Arriving that can laugh, and cut channels, and lay a clean blanket in an empty room. And that this seems to matter, somehow, to the shape the stone takes.

## The Lamp

Go back down, in the evening fog, into the half-built square.

The single lamp in the window of the vast pale house belongs to Aud. She is seventy-something by lowland reckoning; by Lathe reckoning, her birth-reed is as thick as a man's wrist. She is a weaver, one of the old ones who plan in three time-scales. This house is the first she ever designed. She began the wicker when she was nineteen. It has been sound enough to sleep in for eleven years.

The room is the size of a barn. She has one bench, one blanket, one lamp. The water sheets down the north wall and the channel in the floor murmurs past her feet toward the lane. Through the round opening in the roof the fog drifts down, grey on grey. The horn set into the terrace just outside, her birth-horn, the one her name was hummed against, drones through the stone at a pitch a full tone lower than when she was a baby. Sometimes she hums her name against it, just to hear what chord it's become.

On her knees she has a length of fresh willow, still green, and she is weaving.

It might be a doorway for someone else's future house. It might be a horn. It might be a cradle. She doesn't say, and nobody asks. She has decided, whatever it is, to make the openings very large.

The water fills everything. Across the square, someone laughs, a big chesty laugh pitched above the roar, and it's a small, distinct event, and it's gone. The dippers are asleep in their hollow in the young stone. The dead are holding up the terraces. The Expected are waiting in their rooms, with their blankets. The moss is growing, a hair at a time, toward the fog.

The stone goes on arriving. And in it, for now, lives Aud, weaving by lamplight, one warm layer, thin and bright, being laid down.
