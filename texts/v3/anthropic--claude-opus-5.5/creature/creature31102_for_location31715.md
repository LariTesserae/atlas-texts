# Who Lives on the Singing Table

## The Answer Everyone Gives

Ask what lives here and the first answer comes up through your boots: the *tsik*.

Pick one out of the mat and it sits in the hollow of your palm like a seed with legs. It is black with a sheen of bronze, its wing-cases ridged along the edges like the rim of a coin. It has no eyes to speak of. It knows the world by warmth, damp, and vibration, and above all by vibration. The fine hairs on its legs feel the song of its neighbors through the stems of the cushion plant, and it adjusts: a fraction faster, a fraction slower, until its own small rasp slides into place and it is no longer singing alone but *in* something. That is the whole of its life. It eats, it sings in step, it rises in the columns at dusk, and if it survives the swifts it falls back into the green, mates, and lays its eggs among the fungal threads. It lives one summer, and the song does not notice it go.

That is the first answer, and it is true. But it is the answer of someone who has only just arrived.

## The Answer the Herders Give

Stay a season and you might be taken in by a camp. Say it is a woman called Dorje-of-the-Third-Pool, though that is not her name, only the sign for her: a hand cupped as if holding water, three taps of the thumb.

She lives in a peat dome with her grandson, her two daughters, their husbands, and whoever else is passing. She is small, broad-handed, brown as the brick walls of her house. Her ears are sealed with the grey-white wax, and she has not heard the full roar of the table since she was four years old and her mother first pressed the plugs in. She has good hands. Her signing is so fast that the young ones watch her with open mouths, the way children elsewhere might watch a juggler. She can tell a joke with two fingers and a lift of the chin, and she can end an argument with the flat of her palm laid gently on the other person's chest, which means *enough, I have felt what you mean*.

She knows every pool within four days' walk and the name of every tooth. She knows which hummocks were houses and who was born in each. She lays her cheek against the mat each evening to listen for drums, and her face goes very calm when she does it, calm in the way of a person listening to a familiar voice in another room.

If you ask her what lives on the table, she will answer with her hands: *tsik, birds, grazers, us.* Then she will look at you for a moment longer than she needs to and add nothing. That is how you learn there is more.

She will not tell you in daylight. Nobody will. In daylight everything is signed, and there is one thing the herders do not sign. It has a sign, but using it by day would be like pointing at someone who is standing right behind you.

## The Quiet Hour

You wake in the coldest part of the night because the world has gone strange. The roar has sunk to a murmur, and the murmur is so much less than you have grown used to that it feels like falling. Dorje is already awake. She takes one plug out of her ear. Then she does something you have never seen her do. She speaks.

Her voice is low and rough from little use. She speaks the way people elsewhere might undress: carefully, knowing she is being seen.

"This is its hour too," she says. "It rests now. So we talk now. When it isn't working, it comes to listen."

Then she makes the sign. She holds her two hands flat, palms facing, a little apart, and brings them together until they touch. She holds them there. Then she draws them slowly apart, and her fingers tremble a little as they separate, like the edge of a wave.

You have seen the waves meet in the day: the two walls of sound rolling toward each other across the green, the collision, the sudden trough where the noise cancels and you can hear your own blood moving. The sign is that. Two songs meeting and making a hole.

The herders' word for it, spoken only in this hour, is the same breathed sound they use for a held breath. You might write it *hhn*. Most people who come to the table never hear it said.

## What It Seems to Be

At first it seems to be only that: the trough. A drifting place of near-silence that wanders across the table, the kind of thing a people might give a name to and then, over generations, give a will.

You might believe that for a while.

Then one morning the fog comes up over the lip, milk-white, and rolls inland, and you see the song made visible: bands of thicker and thinner mist, shivering in rows, marching with the waves. Far off, perhaps a mile, perhaps three (distance lies here), there is a place where the bands do not shiver. It is a pocket of still air in the moving fog, roughly round, drifting against the waves rather than with them. The fog around it trembles and the fog inside it hangs like cloth.

And in the middle of the stillness something is standing.

It is tall. That is the first shock, on a table where nothing rises higher than your ankle and your eye has forgotten verticals. It is pale, the white of the fog-catching hairs on the cushion rosettes, so that it looks at first like a place where the fog has thickened and stood up. It is thin, all long stalk and angle. It is bowed. Its head, if it is a head, hangs low toward the ground, the way a person bows over a sickbed.

It does not move for a long time. Then it takes a single step, very slow, and the pocket of stillness moves with it.

Dorje, beside you, does not make the sign. She lowers her eyes, the way you lower your eyes when you walk past someone praying.

## What It Is Made Of

What follows comes in pieces, over many quiet hours, from many mouths. No one herder knows all of it.

Its body is covered in the same fine white hairs as the cushion plant, only longer, trailing, so that in fog it beads with water like the rosettes do and glitters at sunrise. Some say it drinks this way, and has never needed the pools. Others say the pools are its doing: that the small round dark ones, no wider than a house and perfectly circular, are places where one of its kind once stood so long, so still, listening so hard, that the peat slumped beneath its weight into a bowl. The pools are warm because the rot runs deepest there. The pools are round because it turned slowly as it listened.

Its legs are jointed like a *tsik*'s. Along their length, and down the long bowed column of its body, run ridges: plates like the edges of wing-cases, like the rim of a coin. It sings with them. It rasps them together as the *tsik* rasp theirs, and that is what it does with the whole of its life.

But it does not sing *with* the table. It sings *against* it.

This is the thing the herders understood before anyone could say how they understood it. A *tsik* feels its neighbors and falls into step. The *hhn* feels the whole vast chorus around it, every wave of it, and sings its exact opposite: each swell answered by a trough, each rasp by a counter-rasp at the precise moment that erases it. It is not silent. It may be the loudest thing on the table. But its song is the mirror of everyone else's, and where the two meet, both vanish. The *hhn* walks across the world inside a room of quiet that it makes with its own body, working ceaselessly and precisely, never resting while the song is strong.

The meeting of waves that leaves a trough of stillness, the thing every traveler notices, happens by chance when two regions of *tsik* fall out of step. But the herders say some troughs are not chance, and that you can tell. The chance ones flicker and are gone in a few breaths. The others hold. They drift. They go somewhere.

The ground-runners go still when one passes. The lizards flatten themselves harder against the warmth. The *tsik* beneath it, finding their song answered and annulled, grow confused and falter, and for a while after it has passed there is a raggedness in the chorus, a trail of disordered rhythm through the grain of the waves, like a wake behind a boat. The falcons will not hover over it. Nothing hunts it. The herders say nothing ever has.

## Why

Here the pieces disagree, and here the herders grow quietest.

Some say it is simply an animal, a very strange one, that has learned to hide inside the noise. It hunts by hearing, and it needs silence to hear its prey, so it makes silence. That is the explanation a sensible person might give. Dorje, when you offer it, smiles and pats your hand.

"What would it hunt," she asks, "that it has to hear so carefully?"

The grazers? It has never touched one. The *tsik*? There are millions under every step; you do not need to listen for them. There is nothing on the table so rare or so faint that it would need to be tracked through silence. Nothing, she says, on the table.

Under it.

Every evening she lays her cheek against the mat and listens for drums. The low beat carries through the peat, below the song, from camps miles away. You have tried it and heard it: the faint soft thud of a neighbor's news. But lie there longer, the herders say, lie there the way the old ones do, the whole of a quiet hour, and underneath the drums there is something else. It is slower than any drum, so slow that you might mistake it for your own pulse and then realize it has not changed in a long time. A settling. A long, faint groan, and then a long pause, and then another.

It is the stone. It is the mountain.

The granite or basalt or whatever it was lies down there under ages of peat, and it is still, very slightly, moving. It shifts under the weight of the living and the dead laid over it. It cools, and cracks, and eases. It is the thing the table is lying on, and it is the last of the mountain, and it is not quite finished.

The *hhn* is listening to it.

That is what the bowed head means, the posture of the sickbed. It walks from place to place across the green, making silence as it goes, so that it can stoop and hear, far down through the warm dark, the old stone breathing. Sometimes it goes to the teeth. The shallow rubbed hollows on the crowns of the tooth-stones, which everyone assumed were worn by generations of hands, are too high on the stone for most hands and too round. The herders say they are where it rests its head against the only places the mountain still reaches the air, and listens with its skull pressed to bare rock, the way you might press your ear to the chest of someone you love to hear whether they are still there.

## Who Carved the Waves

At the teeth there are marks older than the herders' memory, and among them the one shape repeated again and again: the wavy line, rolling across the rock. *Whoever made them heard the waves too*, people say. Travelers find this touching.

Look longer at those lines. In a few places, not many, the wave is interrupted. It rolls and rolls, and then there is a gap, a stretch of smooth stone where the line simply stops, and then beyond the gap the wave resumes.

The herders say the gap is the *hhn*. Whoever made these marks knew what the herders know: that the song is not the whole of the table, that something walks inside it as a hole. They carved the song and then carved the silence in it, because the silence was the more important part.

Or perhaps, the oldest ones say in the quiet hour, the marks were not made by people at all. Perhaps they were scratched with the ridged edge of a pale jointed leg, leaning against the stone for a very long time.

## The Few

There are not many of them. No one agrees on how many. Some herders say a handful, spread across a hundred miles of green. Some say there is only one, very old, and that it is everywhere eventually. Some say there were more once, in their grandmothers' grandmothers' time. They say the table was quieter then, patched with walking silences, and that the teeth were taller and more of the mountain still spoke.

This is the thing Dorje tells you last, on your final night, in the coldest hour, with both plugs out of her ears and the murmur of the table around you like the sea heard from inside a shell.

"When the stone stops," she says, "it will stop."

You ask what she means: stop singing? Stop walking? Die?

She shakes her head slowly. "Stop listening. There won't be anything left to hear. It will lift its head for the first time. Then it will stand up straight, and it will stop singing against us." She is quiet a moment. "And then the table will be loud everywhere, all the way across, with nowhere quiet in it. And no one will be keeping the watch."

She says it without grief. She says it the way people speak of a death that has been coming for so long that it has become a kind of home.

## The Hour It Rests

The coldest hour, when the *tsik* sink to their uneven murmur and the whole table is as near to quiet as it ever comes, is the only time the *hhn* does not have to work. The world is soft enough then that it can hear without making silence. The herders say it straightens a little. It stops its ceaseless counter-song. It walks, and wherever it walks there is no still pocket in the fog, because everywhere is still enough.

In that hour it comes near the camps.

This is why the herders speak aloud only then. It is the hour when voices can be heard at all, and it is the hour when it listens to them. What you say in the quiet, you say in its hearing. That is why speaking is intimacy on the table. There is always one more listener in the dark, bowed and patient and pale. It has spent its whole existence attending, with complete devotion, to the smallest sounds of something very old and very tired. When it turns that attention for an hour toward a peat hut and the rough low voices inside it, it listens the same way. It hears the stone breathing and the grandmother breathing, and it makes no distinction between them.

Sometimes, the herders say, if you step out of the hut in that hour, you can see it at the edge of the camp, just at the limit of starlight: thin, white, dripping with the first gathering fog. It looks close enough to touch, or it is very far away and much larger than you can imagine. Its head is tilted, and it is not listening down. It is listening to you.

You must not make the sign at it. The sign is for talking *about* it. To its face, in that hour, you speak. The old ones say you should tell it something true, quietly, the way you would speak at the side of a bed where someone is sleeping and might not wake. Tell it the name of your child. Tell it which pool is fullest. Tell it that you hear the stone too, sometimes, when you lie down with your cheek against the green.

Then the east pales. The fog rises over the lip of the table like milk boiling over, and the *tsik* stir, and the first wave of the day comes rolling in from very far away. The pale thing bows its head again. It lowers its long body toward the ground and begins, very softly, to sing the song's exact reverse, and a small room of silence closes around it as it walks away across the warm and breathing green. It goes back to its vigil. It will listen until there is nothing left to hear.
