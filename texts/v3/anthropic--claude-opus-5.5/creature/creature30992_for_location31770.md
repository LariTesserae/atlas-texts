# Those Who Live in the Undercrown

## The Threaders, Seen Up Close

You will probably meet them first by touch.

A line you are holding goes taut, then slack, then taut twice. Someone far along the strand, lost in the white haze, is speaking to you through the rope. In a place that swallows sound, threaders stopped trusting voices long ago. They talk through cords. They pluck, pull, and twist, and the meaning travels along the fiber faster than a shout and farther. Two threaders tied to the same line can hold a conversation a kilometer apart. A whole band strung across a bridge, each on a safety cord clipped to the long main rope, forms something like a single nervous system. When one person feels a chill and stops, everyone knows within a breath.

When you do see them, they are hard to see. They are pale, as everything here is pale. Their skin has the flat, bloodless tone of people who have never stood in sunlight that carried heat. Their hair, worn long and bound into cords of its own, is almost colorless. They dress in felted plant fiber, many layers thick, gray-white and soft as moth wing. From a little distance a band of threaders moving along a strand looks like a row of knots that happen to be walking.

Their faces are the exception. Around their eyes every threader wears a band of black.

It is soot, scraped from the inside of the shelter lamps and mixed with a little fat. Practically, it cuts the glare. In a world with no shadows, the eyes ache constantly, and the black gives them somewhere to rest. But the soot is more than practical. Black is the rarest thing in the Undercrown. The only black a threader can make comes from fire, and fire comes only from the death of a longwalker. So the dark bands carry weight. A child gets them for the first time when they are old enough to hold their own line. A dead person has them wiped away. The black around a threader's eyes is the closest thing they have to a name spoken aloud.

Their real names are knots.

## How a Threader Reads the World

They go bare-necked and bare-handed whenever they can, and this looks like foolishness until you understand it. The skin is their instrument. A threader walking a strand keeps her palm flat on the tissue, feeling for the faint drop in temperature that means something is building beneath the surface. She keeps her throat open to the air because the back of the neck feels a chill first. Threader children are taught to stand very still with their eyes closed and turn slowly until they can say which direction is colder. The ones who can do this well become leaders. The ones who cannot are kept close to the main line all their lives.

The world they read is almost entirely temperature and tension. Is the tissue underfoot cold or merely cool? Is the bridge humming faintly under the breath-winds, or has it gone slack, which means its roots are loosening? Is the air drawing downward, the long inhale, or pushing up? They almost never ask where anything is, because place doesn't last. They ask what is happening, and they ask it with their skin.

Their language has dozens of words for cold and only one for warmth. The one word also means *still*, *finished*, and *soon gone*. It is a word they say quietly.

## The Problem of Being Warm

The threaders understand, in a way that shapes everything about them, that they are warm things living inside a world that grows by cold.

Their own bodies give off heat. So do their shelters and their lamps. In the grammar of the Undercrown, a warm place is a place where nothing is growing, where tissue is dying and will soon be taken back. A threader shelter, glowing faintly orange from within, lashed to the underside of a strand, has the same signature as a patch of rot. Some threaders believe the plant notices this. Stay in one place too long, they say, and the plant will decide you are a dead part of itself and resorb you. The strand you are tied to will wither, or the chamber will seal around you.

Whether or not this is true, they behave as if it were. They move on before their warmth can accumulate. They keep their lamps small and burn them only inside closed shelters, hidden from the open air. They think of themselves, with a kind of grim humor, as small walking pieces of death that have learned to keep ahead of the plant's attention.

This is why their dead matter so much to them.

## What They Do With Their Dead

A threader who dies cools.

Among themselves, threaders do not say that someone has died. They say the person *has begun to grow*. The body loses its warmth, and in the Undercrown cold is the sign of life starting. For the first time, the dead person matches the world around them. They have stopped being a small warm wrongness and become part of the pattern.

So the band carries the body to the coldest place they can find: a fresh frost-tip, rimed white, groping forward into the void and trailing mist. They wipe the soot from the dead person's eyes. They bind the body to the tip with cords knotted in that person's name, and then they leave. The strand keeps growing. Within days the tissue swells around the body, begins to take it in, and carries it forward into the open air. If the strand roots on the far side, the dead become part of a bridge. If it withers and falls, the dead fall with it, tumbling through the white for minutes that feel like hours.

The threaders treat both outcomes as good. Either way, the plant has accepted the person. Either way, they are circulating.

And sometimes they come back.

A strand splits under a husk-knife, and there inside the pale tissue, compressed and faint, is a cord knotted in a name someone remembers. Once in a long while it is more than a cord: a hand, preserved by cold and sap, the fingers still bent as though holding a line. Threaders do not fear this. They greet it. They touch the knot and say the name, and they go on. The plant took the person in, carried them for a while, and brought them past again. It seems to them less like a haunting than a visit.

## Knots as Letters

Once you understand this, the threaders' knots stop looking like simple trail markers.

Yes, they tie warnings and directions: *this bridge is restless*, *wound-pool two hundred spans down-breath*, *moths gathering*. But they also tie other knots, more elaborate ones, into living tissue. They choose the places deliberately: growing surfaces, cold patches, spots where the plant will surely close over the cord within a season.

They are writing to the future, and the plant is their messenger.

A threader who has lost a child might tie a long knot-letter into a frost-tip and walk away, knowing it will be swallowed, carried, and perhaps never seen again. Or perhaps it will be found in thirty years by a stranger cutting through a bridge who has never heard the child's name but can read the knots. Old threaders tie their whole lives into cords in their last seasons: which strands they walked, which bands they joined, which longwalker they helped kill for lamp fat in a hard year. Then they give the cords to the plant.

What a threader values most is not being remembered by people. It is being carried. People forget. The Undercrown destroys every structure within fifty years, but it does not quite destroy what it swallows. It moves it around. To tie a knot into the plant is to put something into that slow, endless current where it will turn up again and again in unexpected places, like a word going around the edges of a very long conversation.

Children learn to read the old knots before they learn to tie their own. Threaders cutting tissue always pause over what they find inside it. Most of what they find is ordinary, fragments of recent bands. But now and then a knot turns up that no one alive knows how to tie. It follows grammar that is almost, but not quite, their own. The threaders say these knots were tied by *the ones who were carried first*. They are not sure who that was.

## Frost-Moths

The moths are the pulse of the place, and the threaders' daily food.

Each one is barely the size of a fingernail, white, with wings so thin that the diffuse light passes straight through them. At rest against a wall they vanish. In motion they look like a disturbance in the air, a shimmer, a patch of frost that has decided to drift. They gather at growing tips in the thousands, feeding on whatever the growth breathes out into the mist. Some threaders believe it is sugar and some believe it is the cold itself. Their lives are short. A generation of frost-moths can be born, feed, breed, and die in the time it takes one strand to cross from wall to wall.

They lay their eggs in rime, directly into the frost-coat on growing tissue. When growth moves on and the frost melts into dew, the eggs hatch in the droplets. The larvae are tiny and wriggling and live on the sweet water of the melted frost. Then they rise. So the moths follow the plant exactly: they are born where growth has just been and fly toward where it is going.

Threaders harvest them with fine cord nets swept through the swarms. Frost-moths taste of almost nothing, faintly sweet, faintly of the cold air. Threaders eat them by the handful, raw or pressed into cakes. There is a threader saying, tied rather than spoken, that roughly means *the moths are the plant's thoughts, and we are eating them*. It is half joke, half theology.

## Longwalkers

You see them first as a vertical line in the haze, then as several lines, and then you realize the lines are legs. Far above them, held impossibly high, is a body.

A longwalker's legs are thin as saplings, many-jointed, and there are a great many of them. Threaders argue about the number; counts run from sixteen to more than thirty, and possibly it varies from animal to animal. The feet at the ends are broad, soft pads that spread across the curved surfaces of the strands and grip by some combination of suction and patience. The body is a pale, ribbed, roughly egg-shaped mass the size of a small house, with no obvious head. At its underside is a long flexible feeding organ, a kind of scraping tongue, that it lowers to the tissue as it walks, shaving away the soft outer layer. It leaves behind a smooth pale track that is visibly *warmer* than the tissue around it for hours afterward. The plant, wounded shallowly, has paused its growth there.

Longwalkers do not hurry. They never make a sound. They do not seem to notice threaders at all, though a band of threaders has followed a single longwalker for years at a time. This is a common strategy: a longwalker almost never steps onto a dying bridge. It seems to feel, through those many broad soft feet, the same slackness and warmth that a threader's palm feels, but sooner and more surely. Threaders say that if you want to know where the Undercrown is safe, follow the longwalkers. If you want to know where it is growing, follow the moths. If you want to know where it is dying, follow the threaders, because they have already left.

They are killed only in great need, because a longwalker's body holds enough fat to fuel a band's lamps for many years. Killing one is dangerous and difficult. It means cutting through leg after leg with husk-knives until the animal's own weight brings it down, while its remaining legs clutch the strand. It is also sad, and threaders treat it that way. The whole band ties the name of the longwalker into their cords, though the animal has no name, and they give it one for the purpose. The black soot around every threader's eyes comes from a longwalker's fat, burned in a shelter lamp. When a threader says they "wear" a particular longwalker, they mean their eye-black was made from that animal's lamp-fat. It is a lineage, a kind of family.

And here is the thing about longwalkers that bothers the threaders most. Sometimes they carry cords.

Tangled around their high joints, sometimes grown into the ribbed hide of their bodies, are ropes of plant fiber. They are threader rope, or something like it, knotted. Some of the knots are recent: lost safety lines, snagged nets. But some are not. Some are in the old grammar that no living threader uses, the knots of *the ones who were carried first*. The fiber is dark and hard with age, and grown so deep into the longwalker that the animal must have carried it for longer than any threader has lived.

In a place where nothing is old, the longwalkers may be the exception. No one has ever seen one young. No one has ever seen one die except by threader knives. It is possible that the longwalkers have been walking the strands since before the current strands existed, crossing each new bridge as the old ones fall. It is possible they remember the shape of the Undercrown fifty rearrangements ago, and a hundred. No one knows where they go when the bridge they are walking falls. No one has ever seen one fall with it.

## The Dark Ones

And then there are the things in the open air.

Threaders call them *the dark ones*, or *the warm ones*, or by a knot-name that means something like *the still things that move*. The last name holds the whole paradox. They are rarely seen, and never up close. They appear in the far distance, drifting on the breath-winds in the open void far from any strand: dark shapes, true dark, the only dark in the entire hollow besides soot. From a distance they look like drops of ink suspended in milk. Their shape is hard to judge. Some look round, some elongated, some winged or finned. They turn slowly as they drift. They rise on the exhale and sink on the inhale.

They are warm.

The few threaders who have been close enough, carried by an accident of the winds or stranded on a falling strand that happened to pass one, report the same thing. As it went by, they felt heat on their faces like a lamp. It was steady heat, and it came off the dark thing's surface. They could see the air shimmering around it.

Here is what the threaders make of this, in their own terms. The dark ones are black because they drink the light instead of scattering it. And because they drink the light, they are warm. Warmth in the Undercrown means stillness, the pause, the opposite of growth. A dark one is therefore a thing that is not growing. It is holding itself closed. It has taken all the light and heat it needs and stopped.

Something that stops growing and holds its warmth inside a hard dark shell, riding the wind, is what most of the threaders have concluded: it is a seed.

They are the plant's seeds, it is said. Somewhere deep in the walls, where no threader has gone, the plant makes them. It makes them rarely, perhaps once in many years. It releases them into the open air of the hollow, and there they drift, warm and dark and patient, waiting for something. What they are waiting for is where the threaders begin to disagree.

## What the Dark Ones Carry

A few times in threader memory, a dark one has come down. It struck a wall, or broke against a strand, or was brought down by a falling bridge. Each of these events is tied into the great knot-histories, and threaders who were not alive to see them still know every detail.

What they found inside was always the same, and always different.

The shell was hard, dark, and smooth, like the husk-plates of dead tissue, but black. Split, it had a texture like carved stone. It was the same material, threaders realized, as the strange flat carved plates that sometimes turn up embedded in the walls. The inside of the shell was warm and dry, and it was full.

It was full of what the plant had swallowed. There were cords, hundreds of them, tight-packed, knotted: threader cords, recent and ancient, in every grammar threaders know and some they don't. There were husk-knives. There were the small bones of hands. There were fragments of shelters and scraps of felt. There were spirals of the unreadable writing that sometimes appears in the walls. And packed among all of it, in a pale sticky tissue like the heart of a bean, there were the true seeds of the plant: small, glassy, cold even inside all that warmth.

The threaders' reading of this is the core of their belief, the thing they tie into cords for their children before any other knot.

The plant, they say, swallows everything it touches. It carries what it swallows through its body for years, circulating it, turning it over. Then at last, when it makes a seed, it packs everything it has carried into the seed: every knot, every knife, every bone, every name. The seed rides the breath-winds of the hollow, rising on each exhale, higher each time, drifting toward the long throats of the light-stalks. Eventually, on some great breath, it goes up one of them, kilometers up, out into the surface, into the cold thin air and the light that still carries heat. And from there the wind takes it.

Where? Somewhere else cold. Another frozen plain. Another world, even; the threaders' cosmology is vague about the scale of *above*. There the seed comes down. There it splits, and its cold glassy heart begins to grow downward, drawing warmth from the ground, freezing it, hollowing it. In a very long time there is a new Undercrown.

And inside the seed, around that glassy heart, there are cords, knots, knives, bones, and names. Everything that was carried.

## The Ones Who Were Carried First

This is the threaders' origin story, the reason for everything.

The first people did not come from anywhere, they say. They came out of a dark one. A seed fell into this hollow, long ago, when the hollow was young and small. Maybe it came from the surface, blown in from some other Undercrown on some other cold plain, or maybe it was one of this plant's own seeds that never managed to escape. It broke, and out of the warm dark shell came people. Or rather, what came out were knots, bones, and knives, and people grew out of them the way the plant grows out of a glassy seed-heart. The knots told them who they were. The knives were their first tools. The bones were their ancestors.

It is a myth, or it sounds like one. Threaders do not all believe it the same way. Some take it literally. Some take it the way you might take a poem. But all of them act on it. It is why they tie their lives into the plant. It is why they give their dead to the growing tips. It is why they read every old knot they find as carefully as they would read a letter addressed to them.

They are not only living in the Undercrown. They are trying to get *into the seed*. Every threader hopes, in a quiet way, that something they tied, a knot, a name, one cord of their own hair, will be swallowed, carried, circulated, and at last packed into one of those warm dark shells.
