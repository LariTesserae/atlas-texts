# Who Lives at Kessary

## Nobody, at First

Every living thing in the rift arrived there. That is the first thing to understand about who lives at Kessary. Nothing was born to it. The swifts came up out of some older cave system or down from some farther cliff. The crickets crawled in over the rim. The ants came down on their black ropes, and the snakes followed the lizards, and the hawks followed the swifts. Even the eyeless crabs, if they really came up from the dark water under the limestone, came up as strangers into light and heat and air.

So for most of its eleven years the rift has not been lived in so much as *occupied*. It is a boomtown. Everyone came for the warmth, the food, the nests, or the salt, and everyone behaves like a newcomer: crowding, feeding, breeding fast, dying fast, leaving marks that the wall grows over within a season.

But eleven years is long enough for some things to stop arriving and start belonging. If you ask who lives at Kessary, as opposed to who is merely there, the answer is small, and it is still coming into being.

## The Crabs That Are Becoming Walls

Start with the crabs, because they changed first.

The brine that feeds the pools is the same brine that makes the saltbloom, and it does not care what it crusts. A crab that sits long enough in a slow-evaporating margin comes out with a rime on its back, a fine white fur of crystal along the ridges of its shell. Most crabs scrape it off against the rock or lose it at the next molt. Some do not.

In the older pools, the ones on slabs that have held still for six or seven years, there are crabs whose backs have grown. Tiny feathered spires of bloom rise from their carapaces, pink at the base and white at the tips, like frost on a stone. The films have found them too. A purple-black skin creeps up the crystal, or an orange one, depending on the pool. Crickets ride on them. On the largest crabs, which are about the size of a child's palm, the bloom has branched so richly that the animal looks like a little walking reef. It moves slowly and deliberately, a small white fortress rattling across the slab.

These crabs no longer climb the walls at night. They are too heavy. They stay in the pools and scrape the films from their own backs and from each other's. Other crabs, the young and unburdened ones, clamber over them to graze. The harvesters have started calling them *old men*. When one dies, its shell dissolves in a few weeks, but the bloom stays. It is a hollow lattice of crystal in the shape of a crab that is no longer there, and the next generation shelters in it.

Nobody planned this. The rift is simply beginning to build with its own inhabitants. A pool that has held still for eight years is ringed with these little ghost-reefs, shell-shaped, white, and delicate, and new crabs grow up inside the shapes of the old ones. It is the first place in Kessary where anything has had a grandmother.

## The Dead's Birds

Then there are the swifts that live in the shrines.

When someone dies in the rift, the harvesters wedge a nest into a crack near the spot and leave it. It is always a good one: white, dense, worth a week's food on the plain. It is not harvested. No one would touch it, and no one has, not once in eleven years. That rule is kept more strictly than any of the others.

The swifts take these over. A shrine nest is the one nest in Kessary that is never prised loose, and a pair that finds one can raise brood after brood in it for years. Over time the shrine nests have become the oldest continuously occupied nests in the rift. Some have been built on so many times that they have grown into great lumpy grey-white masses the size of a man's head, fused to the rock and rimed with bloom.

The harvesters say the birds from the shrine nests are different, and they may be right. They are a little larger. They hold their ground longer against the hawks. Most of all, they are the first to leave. When a section of wall is about to go, the ordinary swifts flee in a burst. The shrine birds have already gone, sometimes an hour earlier, sometimes half a day. Harvesters working near a shrine learn to glance at it the way you glance at a sky. An empty shrine nest in the middle of a busy morning means *get off the wall*.

The harvesters call them the dead's birds, and they do not mean it as a superstition, or not only. They mean the birds live where the dead are. They were raised by parents who were raised in the same crack. They have been in the rift longer than almost any other living thing, and they know it better. The dead who are remembered there are keeping watch through them.

## The Man Who Stopped Going Up

His name is Oszek, and he was a harvester in the second year, when the ladders were bad and people died often.

His wife died in the fourth year, in a morning collapse on the north wall. It was the kind that begins with a door slamming in an empty house. He wedged a nest for her. Then he went up to the plain, and came down the next morning, and went up and came down, and one evening he did not go up.

He has lived at the bottom of the rift for six years now. His shelter is the oldest stone ring in Kessary. He has built it up with flat liver-stone laid so carefully that no edge faces inward, and he has roofed half of it with hides the others bring him. He cooks in a basalt seam that has held a steady heat for as long as he has been there. Harvesters leave him water from the plain and take away the nests he gathers, and pay him in cloth and oil and news he mostly does not listen to. He hardly climbs at all anymore. He does not need to. The rift brings things down to him.

What he does is keep the handprints.

There are thousands of them, red ochre on pale stone, each one meaning *here is danger*. The trouble is that danger in Kessary moves. A crack that was lethal in the fifth year may have closed tight in the seventh. A ledge that was safe may have hollowed out underneath. The old prints fade slowly and the bloom grows over them unevenly, and a newcomer cannot tell a live warning from a dead one.

Oszek can. He walks the lower walls in the early mornings with a pot of ochre and a pot of lime-white. Where a warning is still true, he renews it. Where the danger has passed, he paints the palm of the old print over in white. He does not scrape it away, because someone made it for a reason and the reason is worth remembering. The print is left as a red outline around a pale hand, meaning *this was dangerous, and is not now*. The younger harvesters have learned to read them. There are whole stretches of lower wall where the red hands have turned into white ones, like a crowd of ghosts raising their palms.

He knows the rift by sound. The fresh rock carries sound strangely, and he has spent six years listening to it. He can put his ear to a slab and tell you whether it is sitting on solid basalt or on air. He can hear the ropy seeps moving in the deep fissures, a sound he describes as someone turning over in bed. On the plain, people say he talks to the stone. He does not. He listens to it, the way you listen to someone who is dying so you will know when.

He is not a hermit, exactly. He is a man who has decided to stay near the place where his wife is, and who has found, to his own surprise, that the place is worth knowing. He is the rift's memory, as much of one as anything so new can have. He is also, it turns out, a father.

## Pell

She was born in the rift. Nobody meant it to happen.

Her mother was a harvester named Ilvi, who kept working the bloom walls into the eighth month because the salt paid well and she was strong. Labor came early, in the afternoon, just as the cloud was forming at the rim. There was no time to get her up the ladders. Oszek had been living at the bottom for less than a year. He and two other harvesters carried her into his stone ring, and she gave birth on a hide laid over warm basalt, in the first rain, with the stone steaming around them and the swifts wheeling overhead.

The harvesters say the girl did not cry until the rain stopped. They say that when she did, her voice carried down the long axis of the rift, and a man on the far wall half a mile away called back to ask what had happened.

Ilvi lived. She took her daughter up to the plain and raised her there for two years, bringing her down on warm days. Then Ilvi died on a walkway that gave way at nightfall. It was one of the many that are lost every year. Her nest is wedged in the basalt twenty feet above Oszek's shelter, and the dead's birds live in it.

The girl was two. Her mother's people on the plain took her in, and within a month she was sick. It was nothing a healer could name. It was cold, and it was quiet. The plain was too silent for her. She could not sleep without the tick-rustle of the insect millions, the hiss of brine, the swift-shriek, the far crystalline smash of falling bloom. She shivered in summer. She would not eat. Twice she was found at the rim at dusk, alone, watching the swifts go down.

In the end they brought her back to Oszek, because there was nowhere else she would live.

She is nine now. Her name is Pell.

## What She Is Like

Pell is small, brown, and hard-handed. Her knees and palms carry calluses so thick that the liver-stone hardly cuts her, and she does not wear leather unless Oszek makes her. She has never been cold in her life except on the plain, and she has been up to the plain only a few times since, and hated it. She drinks only carried water, like everyone. She is the only person in Kessary who has never had to be told that the rain is not water.

She moves through the rift the way the swifts move through the steam. It is not recklessness. It is fluency. She reads the crabs. She watches the shrine birds without appearing to watch them. She knows which slabs ring hollow and which ring solid by the sound of her own footsteps, that dull porcelain *tonk* that the newcomers do not even notice. She knows that the purple-black films grow near heat she cannot feel yet, and she steps around them. She learned to walk on rock that had been in the air for less than two years. She has never walked on anything rounded by water or softened by soil. When she was taken up to the plain as a small child, she stood on the dusty brown earth, looked down at her feet in confusion, and said that the ground was *broken*.

She talks across the rift. The fresh rock carries a voice improbable distances, and Pell learned to talk in it. She calls out to harvesters on the far walls in a clear, low, flat voice that does not seem loud up close, and it arrives at the other end of a half mile like a hand on the shoulder. The harvesters rely on her now more than they admit. She is the one who passes along the morning's route after the first one down has checked it. If Pell says a walkway is hanging from one side, nobody argues.

She has made her own mark. Oszek's red and white hands mean *danger* and *no longer*. Pell's mark is a small handprint in lime-white alone, with no red at all. It is her own hand, pressed flat. It means *this is new, and it is fine*. She puts it on fresh rock, on slabs exposed by last week's collapse, on ledges that did not exist a month ago. It says: *I have been here first, and it held.* There are hundreds of them now, small and pale. Most will disappear under bloom within a season, and she does not seem to mind. She makes more.

She is, in the way that matters, the first native. Oszek chose the rift. Pell cannot really choose anything else. Her body was built for this heat. Her ears were tuned to this noise. Her hands were shaped on this stone. She is to Kessary what the old-men crabs are, and the shrine birds: the first thing that grew up inside the shape the place made.

## The One Silence

There is one sound Pell has never grown used to, and it is the silence.

Every few days a slab lets go somewhere in the rift. First comes the crack, then the grinding roar that goes on too long, then the boom felt in the chest. Afterward, for a minute or so, everything stops. The swifts stop calling. The insect millions stop rustling. The crabs stop rattling. The brine still hisses and the fissures still sigh, but every living voice in the rift goes silent at once.

The harvesters bow their heads in that minute. Oszek listens. His ear is always to the stone by then, trying to hear where it came from and whether it is over.

Pell laughs.

She did it first when she was a baby, and the harvesters were frightened by it. She has done it ever since. It is not mockery. Oszek has thought about it a long time and believes it is something closer to recognition, like a child who hears her own name called in an empty room. In that minute of silence the rift is, for once, only itself. It is not the swifts, the crickets, or the crabs. It is not anything that arrived. It is the thing that opened, and is still opening, making its one sound, which is no sound.

Then the swifts start up again. And Pell's laughter is, most of the time, the first sound after them.

## The Thing That Lives There

So there is a list, and it is not wrong. Crickets in shimmering millions. Centipedes banded red and yellow. Beetles with green backs. Ants in living ropes. Bats rising at dusk. Lizards, snakes, doves, hawks, kites. White crabs in rattling carpets. Swifts in columns that can be seen from twenty miles. Harvesters on their ladders with their chisels and bone scrapers and baskets.

But those are the ones who came. The ones who *live* at Kessary are fewer, and stranger, and still becoming. They are the crabs carrying the rift's own crystal on their backs, sheltering in the shapes of their dead. They are the birds raised in the nests of the remembered, who hear the stone begin to shift before any other creature does. They are the man who stayed near his wife and learned to listen to the stone the way you listen to someone who is dying. And they are the girl born in the first rain, who does not know that ground can be anything other than newly broken, who marks fresh stone with her own white hand, and who laughs in the only silence the place has.

Some of the harvesters say the rift will open all the way to the sea. Some say it will fill one day with black stone from below. Nobody knows. Oszek does not guess. Pell does not care. She plans, she says, to find out.

The rift widens a finger's width a year, on average, in lurches and jolts rather than smoothly. So, Oszek has noticed, does she.
