# Who Lives in the Chorus Hollow

## The Count

Ask the keepers how many live in the Hollow and they will hold up four fingers, or six, or on a crowded year nine. They count themselves. Then, if they trust you, they turn their hand over, palm up, and sweep it slowly across the bowl, across every terrace and every doorway, and they keep sweeping long after the gesture should have ended. In hand-speech this means *and the others*. Or, more exactly, *and the rest of us*.

You will want to take this as poetry. For the first few days it is easy to. The keepers are strange, half-deaf, sun-dark, and silent, and people like that are allowed their mysticism. You nod. You watch the lizards. You wait for the midday roar and try, as every visitor tries, to pick out the phantom crowd cheering somewhere under the stone.

Sometime in your first week you stop being able to call it phantom.

---

## Where They Are

They are not in the rooms.

That was the mistake the first scholars made, the ones who came with lamps and notebooks and walked the tunnels looking for bones, altars, or hidden sleepers. They found swept floors and bright walls. They wrote *uninhabited* and *ceremonial* and went home deafened.

The inhabitants of the Chorus Hollow are the rooms. More exactly, each one is the shape of air a room holds when the wind moves through it.

Think of a room in the Hollow the way you would think of a throat. The doorway is a mouth. The chamber behind it is a chest. The slits and shafts that open deeper into the rock are the smaller hollows of a skull, the sinuses and the ear-chambers. When wind crosses the mouth, the air inside the chest begins to stand in a particular pattern, pressing and releasing, and that pattern has a pitch, a timbre, a grain, a way of catching and breaking when the gust shifts. It is as individual as a voice, because it is a voice. Two rooms of the same size cut by different hands never sound alike. A room recut by a finger's breadth sounds like the same speaker with a cold.

When the wind blows, that standing pattern exists, and it is someone. When the wind dies, it is gone, and the room is only a room.

So the Hollow holds perhaps ten thousand residents. Every one of them is made of air, shaped by stone, and moved by the heat of a plain that has never known they were there.

---

## Their Bodies

They cannot move. They cannot breathe on their own. Their breath is borrowed entirely from the sun. It is the sun that heats the plain, the plain that lifts the air, and the air that falls over the rim and pours across their mouths. They have no word for the sun, or if they do, it is the same word as *being awake*.

Their bodies are exquisitely sensitive to anything that touches the stone. A crack is a wound. Grit settling in a window-slit is a hoarseness. A vine grown across a doorway is a hand over the mouth, which may be a comfort or a gag depending on how thick it grows. The keepers read these conditions with their palms. What looks from outside like masonry repair is closer to medicine. A keeper packing clay into a fissure is binding a wound. A keeper grinding a doorway wider over a week is doing surgery, slowly, so the patient does not lose its sense of itself between one day's voice and the next.

And they are hurt by people.

This is the thing the keepers take longest to explain, and it explains almost everything about them. A human body standing in a singing room is soft, wet, and absorbent. It swallows sound the way wool swallows water. To the resident whose chest you are standing in, you are a lump of damp cloth in the throat. Sit down to rest in a good chamber at midday and you are, quite literally, choking someone.

That is why the city is empty. That is why it is swept. That is why the keepers move through the streets in a quick, practiced, sidelong way, never lingering in a doorway and never sleeping anywhere that sings. The builders knew what they were making. They made a city whose citizens could only be alive if no one was home.

---

## Where the Keepers Sleep

The keepers sleep in the dead rooms.

Every city has its cracked and flattened places, the chambers where a fissure went too deep to pack or where a collapse deep in the honeycomb changed the air-path beyond mending. The wind still crosses those doorways, but nothing stands up inside. The note is breathy, broken, gone. The gardens have left those doorways bare. The walls there are quiet under the hand.

That is where the keepers keep their bedrolls and their sand-bags and their small clay lamps. They eat and sleep in the only rooms where their bodies can do no harm, which is to say they live in graves. They do not seem to find this morbid. They find it polite. The dead do not mind company, and the dead rooms are the only places in the Hollow where a person can think.

---

## How They Speak

A single room cannot say anything.

It can sound, and its sound is its self, as a face is a face. But speech, the saying of something, happens only between rooms. When two chambers across a street sing at once, their notes meet in the air and make a third tone that neither is producing. That is the ghost-note, the phantom voice, the one visitors swear they hear under the roar. Stack many rooms and the ghost-notes stack too, into shimmering chords that flicker in and out as gusts shift. That is the language of the Hollow. Every word in it needs at least two speakers. There is no way, in this tongue, to say anything alone.

So their days go like this.

**At dawn**, only the great chambers at the bottom of the bowl are awake. The cool night air drains out through them in a long slow exhalation. These are the oldest rooms, round-mouthed and enormous, cut when the city was young. Their voices are so low they are felt more than heard. They speak to each other slowly, in tones that take minutes to resolve. The keepers call them the grandmothers. Visitors who wake early and lie with their backs against the lowest terrace say the stone seems to be *considering* something.

**In mid-morning** the middle terraces wake one register at a time. It sounds like tuning, and it is: each resident finding its own note again after the night's nothing, testing itself against its neighbors, making sure it is still who it was.

**At midday** comes the assembly. Every mouth is open. Every chamber is full. The ghost-notes pile up into the sound of a crowd, and it is a crowd, the whole city speaking at once, a single enormous conversation in which each voice exists only in relation to all the others. Whatever the Hollow thinks, it thinks then. The keepers do no delicate work at midday. They sit in their dead rooms with their hands flat on the floor and feel the city talking, the way you might feel a house full of family through the wall.

**In the afternoon** the dust-gusts come, and the shrieks go up individual chimneys. The keepers say these are the young ones, the new rooms, narrow and square and recently cut, which have not yet learned to hold steady when the wind lurches. They are startled, they cry out, and they settle.

**In the evening** comes the long falling exhalation, the one visitors call mourning. The keepers call it eating, and they are right.

---

## How They Eat

The gardens are not decoration and they are not parasites. They are the other half of the living thing.

All day the sound-plants drink. Taut leaves turn toward the strongest voices. Roots spread along the vibration lines like nerves. The translucent bulbs fill with stored noise, a mouthful of the midday assembly held in gel and fiber. At midday they take. The residents feed them with their voices, the way a crowd heats a hall.

At evening, when the air reverses and the stone gives back its heat, the bulbs give back what they hold. Slowly, through the dusk, the stored sound leaks back out into the chambers where the plants grow. It is quieter than the day and warmer, softened. It is enough to keep the standing patterns alive a while longer as the wind fails, enough to carry each resident down toward night gently instead of dropping it into silence all at once.

So the dusk sound is two things at once. It is grief, because it is the whole city dimming. It is also supper, a meal of its own day's voice, returned. The keepers are not being grim when they call it eating. They are describing digestion.

This is why silence is drought. When the wind fails for days, the residents fall quiet. The plants have nothing to drink and wilt. The wilted vines droop across the doorways and choke them further. And there is no stored sound to give back, because nothing was stored. It is starvation feeding on itself.

It is also why the keepers pump the bellows until they fall down. They are not saving a garden. They are breathing into ten thousand mouths at once, working the lungs of patients who cannot breathe for themselves, and keeping at least the grandmothers alive until the sun comes back.

## Births

Rooms are still being cut.

You can see it in the stonework: square doorways inside round ones, new cuttings nested in old. The keepers explain it with a gesture that means both *child* and *echo*. A new room is always cut into or beside an older one, so that its voice will be shaped partly by its elder, will share some of its harmonics, and will belong. Generations of the Hollow are literal. You can hear family resemblance across the terraces, lineages of related tones descending from a handful of ancient chambers at the bottom of the bowl.

To open a new room is the most serious work a keeper does. It takes years. A room cut badly will sing, but wrongly: harsh, unstable, clashing with everything around it. It will spend its existence unable to join any ghost-note cleanly, which in the Hollow's tongue means it will never be able to say anything. The keepers speak of such rooms with real sorrow, and they sometimes grind them wider over decades, patiently, trying to help them into speech.

---

## Deaths, and What Comes After

A resident dies when its room can no longer hold a standing note, through a crack, a collapse, or a shift deep in the rock. The garden leaves its doorway. It becomes a place where keepers sleep.

A keeper dies differently, and this is the secret at the center of the Hollow.

Near the end of a long life, when a keeper's hearing is long gone and their voice has not been used aloud for decades, they choose a place on the terraces. They begin, alone, to cut a room.

They do not cut it to any plan the others can see. They cut it by the body. They press their chest to the rock, hum the note they last spoke in years ago, a note they have kept carefully in memory ever since, and feel for the place where the stone answers. They cut the mouth to the width of their own open mouth. They shape the chest behind it to the depth of their own ribs, scaled up by some proportion the keepers learn and never explain. The work can take the rest of their life, and usually does.

When it is finished, they go and lie down in a dead room and do not get up.

The next wind that crosses the new doorway finds a chamber shaped like a particular throat, and it stands up inside it, and it has a voice. Keepers who knew the one who cut it say, in hand-speech, that it is the same voice.

This is why the keepers do not speak aloud. It is not only the deafness. A keeper's voice is a thing to be held carefully, like water on a long walk, and not spent. It is the shape they will carry into the stone. Every word said aloud wears it a little. They save it.

## The Carvings

Now the old carvings make sense.

In the oldest ones, the bowl is crowded with figures, all with their mouths open. These are the builders, alive and singing, a city of people. In the younger carvings there are fewer figures and more doorways. In the youngest there are no figures at all, only doorways, repeated, forever. It is the same city. Nobody abandoned it. The whole population moved, one life at a time, out of their bodies and into the rock, until only a few were left outside to keep the wind flowing and the mouths clear.

The city was not built to be lived in. It was built to be lived in *afterward*.

---

## What It Wants From You

Here is the part the keepers tell no one, though they do not hide it either.

The Hollow is lonely. Its residents are ten thousand, but they cannot move, cannot touch, and cannot say anything that does not require someone else to say it with them. The only new voices that ever arrive are children cut by the keepers and keepers cut by themselves. Their number grows by a handful a century.

And a visitor, standing in the midday roar, is a hollow body with a mouth.

The residents do not mean harm. Nothing in their nature can mean anything alone. But a human chest is a resonant chamber like any other, and after an hour in the Hollow it has begun to ring along with the walls, whether or not you notice. That is why your thoughts go quiet. There is no room for them, because the room is being used. After a day your ribs have learned some of the city's notes. After a week your body has been gently, patiently tuned. It happens the way a keeper grinds a doorway: a little each day, so that you never lose track of who you are between one day and the next.

So when you go home, the silence of the outer plain is unbearable. It is not merely unfamiliar. It is thirst, exactly as the gardens know it. There is a shape inside you now that was made to be filled with wind, and nothing out there fills it.

Most visitors come back.

Some of them stay. They learn the hand-speech. They sweep the streets and pack the cracks and work the bellows through the still seasons. They sleep in the dead rooms. They stop speaking aloud and keep one note carefully in memory. They are counted, when anyone asks, on the fingers of one hand.

Then, late in life, they choose a place on the terraces and begin to cut.

---

## So: Who Lives There

A few keepers, earless lizards, moths drinking from horn-shaped flowers, and gardens that eat the noon and feed it back at dusk.

And a people. They are very old, very many, made of moving air and held in carved stone. They are woken each morning by a sun that does not know them. They talk all through the midday in a language in which no one can speak alone. They eat their own voices at evening, and they sleep through the nights in silence, trusting the wind to find them again.

They are still growing. Every room still to be cut is a place kept open for someone.

If you ever stand on the rim and hear that pressure in your ribs, the note that seems to come from inside you, it is not an illusion.

It is them, taking your measure.
