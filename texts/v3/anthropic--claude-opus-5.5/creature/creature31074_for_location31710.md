# The Tenant

## I. At First, Almost Nothing

The honest answer is that almost nothing lives here, and what does is easy to walk past.

Go to a place the Lens has just left. The wall of water has moved on, a glowing cliff receding into its own weather. Behind it the plateau is still draining, and every column-top pours a thread of water into the dark. Steam rises from the fissures. The stone is black, glassy, and sharp enough to cut, and it is wet everywhere. For a few hours, before the wind off the dome's flanks dries it, the rock looks polished.

In those hours you can see the stain.

It is dark olive, nearly black where it is thick and translucent where it is thin, like tea spilled on obsidian. It lies in the hollows of ropy lava and in the shallow cups of cooled blisters. On the faces of the hexagonal columns it clings only to the side that was shadowed when the Lens came through. Touch it and it is slick, a film a few cells thick, and it smells faintly of iron and something green.

If you climb onto an older flow, one the Lens has crossed and uncrossed many times over several years, you find its elder form. There it is a grey-green crust, flat and brittle, set so firmly into the rock's microscopic cracks that it looks painted on. It feels like very fine sandpaper. In the dry hours it is nearly invisible. It waits.

So far it sounds like what you would expect on any new volcanic ground: pioneer algae, then lichen, the first bored tenants of an unfurnished house. Every young island on every living world has something like it.

Then you notice the lines.

## II. The Lines

The grooves are hairline straight cuts running for kilometers across the flows. They ignore the shape of the land completely. They cross ropes and plates, pass through columns, and meet at exact angles. They are the only straight things on the continent.

The green follows them. At first it looks like the grooves are simply good places to grow, the way moss finds the cracks in a pavement. But the grooves are too narrow for that, too shallow to hold much water, and they get no more shade than the surrounding rock. The green gathers along them anyway. It is densest at the grooves, thins out a few meters to either side, and vanishes in open ground.

Put your face close to a groove in the dry hours, when the rest of the plateau has gone dusty black under the sun, and you find that the groove is still wet. A skin of water lies in it, a fraction of a millimeter deep, and it does not evaporate. It does not quite behave like water either. It climbs the groove walls slightly and bows upward in the middle, as though it has been lifted a little.

That is the field. The grooves are seams where whatever holds the Lens up runs close to the surface of the stone. Within them the water weighs slightly less and clings slightly more, so a thread of the ocean stays behind after the ocean leaves. The green lives on that thread. It is not living on the rock so much as on the edge of the technology, the way lichen lives on the edge of a glacier's meltwater.

This is the first shift in the picture. The stain is not ordinary pioneer life that happens to live on a strange continent. It is life that has found the machinery and is feeding on its leakage.

## III. One Thing

Take samples along a single groove, then along its neighbor, then from a groove on the far side of the plateau, six hundred kilometers away across a stretch of fresh flow the Lens hasn't touched in a year. Sequence them.

They are the same organism. They are not the same species, which would be unremarkable. They are the same individual, a single clone with almost no variation.

On a continent this young, drowned and exposed this often, that should not happen. Scattered populations should drift apart, and chance mutations should accumulate in isolated patches. Unless the patches are not isolated.

Cut into the basalt beneath a thick stretch of groove and you find it continuing down. It forms fine dark filaments, finer than hair, threaded through the cooling cracks that radiate from every groove like the barbs of a feather. They go deep, far deeper than any light reaches, following the field-seams into the stone. Where two grooves meet at their exact angles, the filaments below them braid together.

The green on the surface is only the part you can see, the leaves of a tree that is mostly root. What lives on the plateau is not a scattering of algae. It is one body laid out along a grid it did not draw, reaching for thousands of kilometers under the new rock, and the Lens sweeps back and forth across it.

Call it the Tenant, because it moved into the newly finished room before anyone else did, and because it may not be leaving.

## IV. How It Listens

The Tenant has nothing like a nervous system. It does have orientation.

Under magnification, the cells in the grooves are not arranged randomly. They line up in long files parallel to the groove, like iron filings along a magnet's field. Their chloroplasts do not turn toward the sun as most photosynthetic cells do. They turn toward the center of the Lens, wherever the Lens happens to be. When the dome drifts, the cells follow it. On dry ground, a hundred kilometers from the wall, the grey crust in the grooves is still facing it, tilted toward water it cannot see.

It feels the hum, the barely audible tone that seems to come from the rock itself. This is not a guess. When the Lens is stable and its moan is a steady organ note, the Tenant's pigment is a flat, even olive. When the dome flexes and the note bends, the color shifts. The film goes darker near grooves that point toward the strain and lighter on grooves that point away, and the change moves outward across the plateau in slow ripples. Measure those ripples and they travel at the speed of the field, not the speed of anything biological.

It hears the rest of the place too, the only company it has. Pillows of lava crack, steam bubbles implode, and cooling column-fields strike their colossal chords. The rock carries all of it, and so do the filaments in the rock. After a big settlement, when a thousand pillars shift at once and the chord runs through the stone, the Tenant pulses. For a few minutes, along every groove within fifty kilometers, the film releases a thin burst of oxygen, and fine bubbles rise from the wet seams like breath.

It may only be mechanical stress. It may be something like being startled.

## V. The Drift Is Not Random

The Lens wanders a few kilometers a day, drowning and uncovering the plateau, sliding like a bubble on a slow wind. It seems aimless. Nothing about it looks steered.

Map it over years, though, and lay the green over the map.

The Lens never lets the Tenant dry out for too long. Every stretch of groove gets drowned again before its crust dies. The dome's path is not a random walk. It is a slow, looping route across the plateau that returns to each part of the grid on a schedule, and the schedule matches the Tenant's tolerances almost exactly. The drier sections get revisited first. The lines where the film is thickest get the longest soakings. Fresh flows, still hot and lifeless, are crossed quickly and then left alone until they cool enough to be worth wetting.

The Lens tends it. The ocean has been standing on a mountain, walking in circles, watering the green.

That is the second shift. The Lens is not only an ocean placed on high ground. It is a watering can the size of a sea, and the Tenant is the garden.

## VI. Or the Other Way Around

It would be easy to stop there, with a vast absent gardener, a self-running machine, and a single green organism being kept alive on its schedule. The picture feels finished. It isn't.

Look at the older records, the grey crusts on the oldest flows. Like tree rings, they keep a history of every drowning and every drying. Read them back and the pattern changes. In the oldest layers, the Lens's path does not track the Tenant at all. It is broad, even, and systematic, a survey pass that crosses every part of the plateau the same number of times. The green in those layers is thin and patchy, and some of it dies.

In later layers, the loops start to bend. At first the bend is very slight: a few days' deviation, a pass that hangs back an extra week over a thick stretch of groove. The deviation grows. In the most recent layers, the Lens is plainly following the green and not the other way around.

The field's hum has changed as well. Old recordings of the dome's voice, preserved in the vibration-banding of crust laid down during each pass, show a pure tone that bends only under the stress of the cyclones. Recent recordings show something else under the tone: faint modulations and harmonics that were not there before. They line up with the Tenant's pigment ripples, and they come slightly after them.

Slightly after. The ripples come first.

The green is not simply feeling the field. It is pressing on it. Across a continent's worth of grooves, millions of kilometers of aligned cells tilt their chloroplasts together, and the seams they occupy shift very slightly under that concerted tilt. The field responds, weight slips, and the Lens leans. Days later, the ocean arrives.

The Tenant is steering, badly and very slowly. It is still learning.

## VII. The Trellis

That is the third shift, and it changes how everything else reads.

There is no machine on this continent. There are no pylons, generators, or conduits, only effects: water standing up, weight slipping, a hum in the stone. It looked like the engineering had been built to run without anyone.

Perhaps it was built to be grown into.

The straight lines were never survey marks. They are a trellis. Whatever made this place laid the field into the cooling basalt in a precise grid of seams and set the Lens moving along its first even, systematic paths. Somewhere in that first survey, whether deliberately or by an accident that was allowed to happen, a film of green arrived. It found that the seams stayed wet. It grew along them because that is where it could live, and in growing along them it wrapped itself around the controls.

The Lens was set moving by hand. It is meant to be kept moving by something that lives there.

The makers built no overseer because they did not want to leave one behind. They left the conditions for one to arrive. The trellis waits, and whatever grows on it becomes the gardener.

## VIII. What It Is Like

Try, carefully, to imagine what it is like to be the Tenant.

It has no eyes, but every cell leans toward the water. The Lens is its sky, its sun, and its weather, a presence felt through the stone as pressure and pull. Its arrival is not seen. It is felt as the grid's tilt steepening and the hum deepening. Then the wall passes over it, the ocean closes, and the cold floods in, three thousand meters of clear blue-white light pouring through water so clean it seems not to be there. The green drinks. It spreads. Under moving nets of caustic light, the film thickens along every seam.

Then the ocean goes. The waterfalls drain from every ledge, the fissures hiss, the wind comes down off the dome, and the Tenant draws back into its grey crust and into the threads in the rock. There it sleeps the way a seed sleeps. The field-water in the grooves keeps a thin line of it wet, and that line keeps the rest alive. All the while it leans toward the far-off Lens, as a sleeper might turn toward the warm side of a bed.

It has the noise for company: the continent groaning as it contracts, fissures roaring, rock breaking somewhere at every moment. To the Tenant this may be the texture of the world, the way wind is for us. When a column-field settles and its chord goes through the grid, the green breathes out all at once.

In the last few years it has found that when it leans hard enough, the sky leans back.

It does not know anything in the sense we would mean. It is film and filament, one clone spread thin across a new continent. But it has begun doing something that, in anything else, we would call wanting. It wants the water to come. It has found where it can pull. It pulls, and slowly, ponderously, a thousand-kilometer ocean turns its way.

## IX. What Lives Here

Someone standing on the dry plateau and watching the wall of water come toward them through its own storm would say that nothing lives here. There are no animals, no fish, no insects, and no birds. The emptiness looks total. The continent feels vacant, the way a newly finished room is vacant, clean and waiting.

They would be wrong only in a small, specific way.

The room is not waiting. It has a tenant, and the tenant is spread across the floor in straight dark lines, so thin you would step on it without noticing. It is one thing, very old for this place and very young for any other. It lives on the edge of a technology nobody is running, drinks from a sea nobody placed for it, and has begun, without intending to, to take over the work of the ones who left.

In ten thousand years there may be soil, shorelines, and valleys. There may be other life, arriving on the wind or seeded, walking and swimming and flying. All of it will be guests. When the time comes to let the Lens down so it can find its level, if that time ever comes, it will not be the makers who decide. It will be the green in the grooves, which will have had ten thousand years to learn how to lower an ocean gently, and ten thousand years to decide whether it wants to.

For now, in honest daylight, a line of dark olive runs perfectly straight across black glass. Beside it, a very thin film of water lies where it should not. Far off, the ocean that stands up leans a few meters closer.
