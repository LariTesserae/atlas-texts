# Who Lives in the Held Terrace

## The Small Ones First

Start with what you can hold in your hand.

The frogs in the condensation chambers are the only residents that let themselves be examined. Kneel at the lip of one of the basin-rooms at the foot of a singing stem, where the threads of water come down out of the dark bore and break on the surface. There are usually three or four of them, sitting at the edge with their forefeet in the water.

They are small enough to fit in the hollow of a palm. They are clear the way a thin slice of cucumber is clear: you see through them, but softly, with a green cast. Their bones are fine grey lines, drawn as delicately as the veining on a leaf. Their eyes are gold, very round, and do not follow you.

Look through the belly and you see the eggs.

Every one of them carries a clutch. The eggs are packed into the lower body like beads in a pouch: dozens of dark points, each in its own pale bubble of jelly, pressed together so tightly that the frog's outline bulges slightly at the flanks. You will look for the ones without eggs, the males, and you will not find any. There are no tadpoles in the water. There is no spawn clinging to the chamber walls. The water is perfectly clear and holds nothing but the reflection of the bore overhead.

Their stomachs are empty too. You can see that plainly. A thin collapsed tube lies behind the eggs with nothing in it, and there is nothing on the whole plateau small and quick enough for them to eat. They have not eaten. You come to suspect they have never eaten, and that they were not made to.

What they do is keep time.

Their throats pulse in unison with the high whistling tone of the chord, every frog in every chamber across miles of floor. At first you take it for coincidence, or resonance, the way a glass will hum near a bell. Then you lift one out. It sits in your palm, cool and heavier than its size, and its throat goes on pulsing. It does not break rhythm. Walk away from the stem with it and it keeps the beat. Walk off the plateau with it, if you are the kind of person who would, and the pulse continues in your cupped hands in the loud valley air, steady as a clock. It is keeping a count that the valley cannot hear.

But nothing is being counted. The tone doesn't change. The number doesn't advance. That puzzled you until you thought of music, of the small curved mark over a note that tells the players to hold it. A fermata. For as long as it lasts, the players still feel the beat going by underneath the held note. They count, and the count leads nowhere, because the length of the hold is not theirs to decide. They are keeping their place, so that when the hold is released they will all come in together.

That is what the frogs are: a choir keeping its place. Each one is a small pilot flame of pulse, so that when the moment comes, the plateau will not have lost the tempo. The eggs are held because everything is held. They are ready. They will be laid on the downbeat.

Put her back. She settles at the lip of the water as though she had never left.

## The Moth and What It Carries

The moth in the colonnade seems at first to belong to a different order. It is large, soft, and the colour of dry grass, and it does nothing at all.

Look at the wings. You were told they repeat the weave of the floor: four over, four under, the right-angled turn at the end of each course. They do, but not exactly. Once you have walked enough of the floor you can read the weave well enough to notice when it is wrong. On the moth's wings it goes somewhere the floor does not go.

On one moth, the pattern starts as the tight ordinary weave and then opens into a lattice, then into bare parallel lines like warp threads. Then, on the trailing edge of the hindwing, weft threads come back in and close the lattice up again into solid floor. You have seen the first half of that pattern. It is the edge of the plateau, where the floor thins into strands over the drop. You have never seen the second half. There is no second half. It hasn't been grown.

There are more moths than you thought, once you know how to look. They sit very still, in places no one would think to check, and each carries a different picture. On one, the pale pleats and cream planes of shelf-fungus show a broad flat landing, bordered by something like a balustrade. It is sitting on the underside of the topmost tread of the stair that goes nowhere. On another, the fine felted pattern of the window-hedges runs up and over itself into a roof. Another is folded into the hand's width of air between the two tips of the unclosed arch, clinging to neither side, as though it were holding the gap open, or holding it shut. Its wings show the keystone.

They have no mouths. You see this when one turns its head, which happens perhaps once in a day of watching. Where the coiled tongue should be there is only a smooth fold of dun scale.

The moths are the plan. The terrace keeps its intentions on their backs, the way a builder keeps drawings pinned to the site wall. Each one has settled precisely where its picture belongs and folded itself there, like a note in the margin: *here, the landing; here, the roof; here, the closing stone.* You understand that the moths will not fly away when the breath comes. They will open their wings and lie flat, and the pattern on them will spread outward into the growth around them, and they will become what they depict. They were never visitors to the structures. They are the structures, carried a little ahead of themselves.

This is why you can stare at one and only then realise it was always there. It was. It is the most permanent thing on the plateau, because it has not happened yet.

## The One That Walks

If you see the walker, you see it once.

It is always the same. Everyone who has come back from the terrace and talked about it describes the same few seconds. Something very tall and narrow moves with a wader's slow, lifted gait between the helix tower and the stair, from left to right as you face the colonnade, and then the fog takes it. No one sees it arrive. No one sees it reach its destination. It is as though there were only one crossing, and every visitor happens onto the same few strides of it. As if the walker has been in the middle of this single walk since the terrace began, and will be for as long as it lasts, and you have been let in on it for as long as it takes to draw a breath.

It is tall the way the singing stems are tall, and jointed like them. People argue about the legs. Two, says one; four, says another; six, says a third. The truth may be that each stride is taken by a different leg. Its body might be a colonnade walking, or a length of stem that has learned to step. It has no face that anyone reports. There is a suggestion of a head at the top, bent forward and downward, the posture of someone reading something written on the floor.

It leaves no mark, but the floor it crosses is not the same as the floor around it. If you find the place, and you can, because it is the line between the tower and the stair, the weave there runs lengthwise. It is one long unbroken course with no turns, straight as a ruled line, from one structure to the other. On either side the ordinary four-over-four-under folds away like fabric from a seam. Nothing grows on that line and nothing is placed across it. It is a reserved corridor, cleaner than anything else on a plateau of clean things.

Measure it. The stride the walker takes is the unit of the terrace. Pace out the spacing of the colonnade's trunks, the distance from the stair to the window-wall, the gaps between the ranks of the singing stems. It is always a whole number of those strides. The walker is not a builder, because nothing here was built with hands. It is closer to a rule, the measure itself, walking. It is the surveyor's chain, alive. Where it walks, the plan is laid out, and the rest of the terrace keeps its distances by it.

And it is walking toward the stair.

You feel the rightness of that before you can say why. A flight of treads climbing to twice a person's height and stopping, with a crisp finished top step and nothing beyond it. It is a podium. It is the step a conductor mounts. A fermata ends when the conductor moves, and not before; everyone on the stand is watching the raised hand. The walker is the hand, or the one who carries it. Somewhere ahead of it in the fog is the top of the stair. It will arrive. It will climb. It will stand on that last tread, in the open air where the moth is folded, and it will make a gesture, and the whole terrace will come in on the downbeat.

It has been walking for a very long time. Its strides are long and deliberate, and it does not hurry. You understand that this is not slowness. It is the tempo of the piece.

## The Rooms Are the Wrong Size

For a while that seems like the whole of it. There is a choir holding its count, a set of drawings waiting to become walls, a measure walking toward the moment of release. It's a strange house, but a complete one, with its staff all present.

Then you sit down on one of the benches.

You have been avoiding them. No one sits on them, and it felt like presumption. But you are tired, damp to exactly the same degree you have been for hours, and the bench is there. You sit.

It fits you.

It doesn't fit like a bench that happens to be the right height. It fits like something made for you after careful measurement. The seat meets the backs of your knees exactly. The back, a long sheet of dense green growth, curves into the small of your back and lets go at your shoulder blades. Your arms find their rests without looking for them.

You stand and look around, and now you see it everywhere. The basins stand at waist height: *your* waist. The stair's risers are the height your leg wants to lift, and its treads are the length of your foot and a little more. The windows in the hedge-walls are cut at the level of your eyes. The widest singing stems will admit you walking upright, just, without ducking. The colonnade's vaulting is precisely high enough that you do not feel it overhead.

The frogs are too small for any of this. The moths have no use for benches. The walker is far too tall; it would have to fold itself in thirds to look through those windows. None of the residents you have met are the ones this house was furnished for.

Later, down in the valley, you tell someone. They have been up there too, years ago. They are a head shorter than you and much broader, and they tell you, surprised, that the bench fit *them*. The basins were at *their* waist. You argue about it, then stop. You have both understood the same thing at once.

The terrace fits whoever stands in it. It doesn't change to do it; nothing there changes. It was simply made in the shape of *someone standing here*, and whoever is standing there is that someone.

## The Tenant

So there is a tenant. There has always been one, because a house is shaped around its occupant. This terrace, with its benches and basins and windows, is shaped around a single occupant, and it isn't home.

Try to picture it from the house. That's how you read a stranger from their rooms: from the wear on the chair arms, the height of the shelves, what they keep near the bed. But nothing on the terrace is worn, so you have to read it from what the terrace has prepared.

The rooms with walls on every side and no door, which you can see into only from the stair, are not rooms to be lived in. They are rooms to be *heard* from. Stand at the top of the stair, beside the folded moth, and look down into them. Each has a basin in its centre, full to the brim and perfectly still. In the chambers of the stems below, the frogs are pulsing. The drone comes up through the root-floor into your feet. You realise that the doorless rooms are resonators. They are the body of the instrument. The chord is shaped in them.

Everywhere else, the benches face the rooms.

This is a house built for listening. Not for a listener of any particular kind, but for the act, the posture: someone seated, still, turned toward the sound, waiting. The tenant of the Held Terrace is an audience of one, not yet arrived, for whom every seat has been set.

And what they are waiting to hear is not the chord.

## The Breath

You were told the drone is a held breath. That's true, but the telling was incomplete. You notice the missing part only when you stand at one of the singing stems and put your hand to its mouth.

The air is going in.

The warm wet air comes up from the valleys, crosses the lips of the stems, and goes *down* the bores. It is drawn in, not blown out, and the note sounds on the intake. The condensation beading on the inner walls is the moisture of that air, wrung out of it as it passes. Everything else, the warmth, the faint taste of resin and honey, drains down through the slots in the floor into the unseen dark beneath the root-mat, and is kept.

The terrace isn't holding a breath it has already taken. It is still taking it. It has been drawing in for as long as it has existed. It draws the warm breath of the valleys up onto itself: the breath of the rivers that change course and the trees that fall and rot, the heat of all that rush and failure and replacement. It fills itself with it, slowly, slowly, the way a singer fills her chest before a phrase she knows will be very long.
