# Who Lives in the Clamour

## The Hands

Start with the hands, because in this city you see them before faces.

They are broad, short-fingered, and scarred across the palms in fine white crosshatching. Every surface on the shelf scores skin, so a hand that has lived here forty years carries forty years of basalt written into it. The fingertips are hard as horn. The nails are worn to the quick and stained yellow-brown at the edges from the mineral pools. Nobody here has soft hands, not the rich with their bronze knives, not the children. A baby's first scratches come when it reaches from the sling to grab a wall, and its mother does not pull it back. The rock is how you learn where you are.

The hands never stop moving. People sign while walking, while carrying egg-baskets on their heads, while hauling sinew lines out of the cooking pits. They sign to themselves, the way people elsewhere mutter. A conversation across a lane runs at the speed of thought, and an outsider watching sees only a blur, like fish turning in a shoal. The grammar lives in the space in front of the chest. The face carries tone: lifted brows for questions, a tightening of the mouth for emphasis, a sideways jerk of the chin that means *that one, there, the one I'm talking about*. The eyes do a great deal of the work. People here look at each other more than people do on quiet coasts, because looking away means you have stopped listening.

Watch a crowd at a cooking pit and it can seem at first that no one is speaking at all. Then you notice that everyone is speaking constantly, a hundred conversations crossing over the steam, overlapping without interfering, because each one travels only along its own line of sight.

## The Bodies

They are short and thick through the chest, with the same compact look the auk-birds have. Their diet is eggs and fat and fish, and nothing else. They carry weight low. They walk slightly stooped, because the rooms are narrow and the lintels low, and because a gannet-bird on every doorway is waiting to strike at anything that rises into its reach. Their heads carry the old white marks of beak-blows the way sailors' hands carry rope-burn.

Their feet are something to see. Nobody wears shoes. The rock is warm, and hide would rot in the damp. Over a lifetime the soles thicken into pads like the heel of a dog's foot, cracked and pale and perfectly insensible to the edges of the basalt. The people walk over clinker that would flay a visitor's feet and do not look down. They feel the heat through those soles, though. They can tell by foot alone when they have crossed from one district's rock to another's, where the warmth runs a fraction higher over some deep fissure. They say *walking warm* for being near home.

Their skin is always damp. Their hair is always damp, worn long and knotted up against the lamp smoke, and it smells of oil. Their eyes are narrowed and red-rimmed in the first years of life, until the ammonia stops mattering. After that the eyes are just narrow. People from the quiet coasts say the shelf-folk always look as though they are squinting into a wind, or about to laugh.

They cannot hear well, and it hardly matters. By middle age most have lost the high sounds entirely, worn away by the barking and the blowholes. Nobody considers this a loss. Hearing is a close-range sense here, like smell. What they have instead is the chest. The massed growl of the auk-birds is a pressure you feel behind the breastbone, and the people read it the way sailors read swell. When the growl in a district shifts pitch, everyone there knows something has disturbed the crevices: a gull raid, a fall of stone, a stranger. The blowholes they feel through their feet a moment before the sound arrives. Children play at guessing which blowhole will fire next, and the good ones are right more often than chance allows.

Children born deaf are not remarked on. They grow up signing like everyone else, pressing foreheads like everyone else. Some families quietly prefer it, because a deaf child is never frightened by the quiet coasts.

## The Forehead

Speech is intimate here in a way it is nowhere else.

To say something aloud, you must bring your mouth to someone's ear, or press your forehead to theirs and let the voice carry through bone. That is how lovers speak. It is how mothers speak to infants. It is how the dying are spoken to. It is also how secrets are passed, and how oaths are sworn. The spoken word is a physical closeness, and so it carries weight. People sign all day, but they speak only a few times, and they remember what was spoken.

There is an old saying, made with two fingers touched to the brow: *I have heard you.* It means *I will not forget this.* A marriage is two people who have heard each other. An enemy is someone you would never let near your ear.

Names are signs. Each child receives one before it is weaned, usually drawn from the family's ledges: a curve of the hand for a particular dome, a flick for a particular crack where the auk-birds nest thickest. A child's name says where its eggs come from. The spoken name, the one breathed forehead to forehead, is given later and is known only to family. Many people go their whole lives without hearing more than a dozen others say their spoken name aloud.

## The Ledge-Right

Everyone belongs to a ledge.

It is not a single ledge, but a tangle of rights: *these three plates by the pit-rim, this seam of crevice in the tower on the east lane, that half of the dome the gulls do not hold.* Families know their ledges bird by bird. A grandmother can tell you which gannet pair has nested on which disc of rock for how many seasons. She can tell you when the female was replaced, and what became of the old one. The ledges are not owned the way land is owned on the coasts. They are *kept*. A family that fails to keep its ledges, taking too many eggs from one crack or leaving another to the gulls, loses them. There is no court to take them away. Other families simply begin to keep them instead, and the city lets it happen.

Children learn the ledges before they learn much else. At four or five they are sent up the bird-towers on sinew lines, small enough to reach into the deep cavities. They come back with bitten fingers and eggs in their mouths, carried whole and warm the way a dog carries a bird. By ten they can climb any tower in their district blind, by feel, in the steam. They know the stonework hold by hold, the way other children know the voices of their kin.

There is a particular look to a tower-child: long in the arm, scarred at the fingertips, balanced in a way that seems slightly less than human, as though they have been partly raised by what they climb among. Some of them, grown, never quite come down. They live in rooms built into the towers' bases and sleep to the roar of the nests above them. They sign to the birds, though they know the birds don't understand. People call them *half-feathered*, and it is not an insult.

## The Counters

In every district there are Counters, and in the whole city nobody is more respected.

They are usually old women, though not always. They hold the tallies. There is no writing on the shelf, but there are cords: long strings of twisted gut, knotted in patterns, a knot for each egg taken, a different knot for each chick, a loop for each bird of each ledge. The cords hang in the Counters' houses in great dark bundles from the corbelled ceilings, stiff with lamp-smoke and generations old. A Counter can put her hand into the bundle blind and draw out the cord for a particular ledge in a particular season sixty years gone. She can read it with her fingers and tell you how many eggs came off it, and that it was the same number as this year.

That is what the Counters do. They confirm the sameness.

Every season, when the egg-baskets come down, the families bring their counts to the Counters. The Counters tie them in and lay the new cord beside the old cords. They run their fingers down all of them together, and if the knots match, and they always match, the Counter makes the sign: flat hand, level, pressed slightly down. *Held.* Then the district eats.

It is hard to overstate how much this matters, or how strange it is when you begin to feel it. Every other people in the world lives by some growth or some loss: good years and lean years, a family's fortunes rising, a harbour silting up. The shelf-folk live by an exact and ceaseless balance, and their whole social life is organised around confirming that the balance has not moved. A good year is a year identical to the last. A great Counter is one whose cords, laid side by side, run so perfectly parallel that you cannot tell the decades apart.

The Counters do not climb. Their hands have gone soft from years of handling cord, and that softness is their badge. When a Counter dies, her cords pass to whichever of her apprentices can read them best by touch. The test is simple. The old cords are laid in the apprentice's hands in the dark, and she must say what season each one is. The one who comes closest is chosen. The test is hard for a reason no one says out loud: the right answer, very often, is that you cannot tell.

## The Dead

There is no soil to bury anyone in. There is no wood to burn them on.

So the dead are given back to the ledges.

They are carried out, washed in a cooking pit gone cool, wrapped in nothing, and laid on the domes, on the high flat places the gulls hold. The family signs to the gulls, *here, this is ours, take it*. They withdraw to the nearest rooftops and watch the gulls come. It does not take long. A person laid out at dawn is bone by the next day, and the bones are gathered and carried down into the lava tubes. There they are stacked in the long dark galleries where the petrels nest.

This is not a grief. That is the thing outsiders find hardest to understand. It is a keeping. The people eat the birds all their lives. At the end the birds eat the people, and the count stays level. The Counters tie a knot for each of the dead, the same knot they tie for a chick taken from the nest, because it is the same thing going the other way.

The children are told: *you are made of eggs, and one day you will be made of gulls.* They find this comforting. When they are small they watch the gulls robbing the gannet-ledges and laughing on the chimney-tops, and they sign to each other: *that one is my grandfather*. They are not joking, exactly.

## Below

Go down into the tubes, then, where the petrels are.

The lava tubes run under the whole city, and further. Nobody knows how far. The upper galleries are storerooms and lanes, lamplit, smoky, busy with people carrying baskets. Below those are the ossuaries, where the bones lie stacked in the warm dark to the roof. Below those the tubes go on, narrowing, warming, branching, down toward wherever the heat comes from, and that is where the petrels nest.

They are small dark birds. They come in from the sea at night and go down into the rock, deeper than anyone climbs, and they sing down there. It is not the barking of the gannets or the laughter of the gulls. It is a crooning, a low bubbling moan, rising and falling, thousands of voices overlapping in the dark. On the surface it comes up through the vents with the steam, and in the lanes at night it seems to come from the stone itself.

The people have a story about this, and it is the closest thing they have to a religion. They don't tell it often, and they almost never sign it. They speak it, forehead to forehead, to children who are old enough.

The story is this. The shelf is not a thing that happened. It is a thing that is happening. The lava that poured out and made the plain has not stopped. It has only been stilled. Beneath the rock, far down, something is still pouring, and it is asleep, and the heat that comes up through the stone is its breath. The steam is its breath. It sleeps because it is being sung to. The petrels sing to it underground, the gulls laugh over it, the gannets bark, the auk-birds growl, the surf booms into the tubes, and the whole clamour of the shelf is a single enormous lullaby that has been going since the day the rock cooled.

And the thing sleeps. As long as it sleeps, nothing changes. The heat does not rise. The rock does not crack. No new flow comes pouring down over the city. The pools stay the same temperature as in a grandmother's time. The shelf is *held*.

But the noise has to stay the same. That is the point of the story, and suddenly everything else makes sense: the Counters, the ledges, the cords, the dead given to the gulls, the law that nobody takes more than the birds give. If you take too many eggs, there are fewer chicks. With fewer chicks, the colony shrinks. If the colony shrinks, the sound thins, just a little, in one district, over one fissure. And something under the rock, in its long sleep, turns over.

## What Silence Means

This is why people who leave for the quiet coasts are frightened.

It is not the strangeness of silence, though that is part of it. It is what silence *means*. A shelf-person who wakes on a soft coast in the night, in the dark, and hears nothing, hears only wind and a few insects and the small sigh of waves on sand, does not think *how peaceful*. They think *it has stopped*. Their body knows before their mind does: the singing has stopped, and something is waking. They get up in the dark and they shout. They shout at nothing, at the quiet, trying to fill it. The coast-people think they are mad, or grieving. They are only doing what they were raised to do. They are keeping it asleep.

Most come home within a year. They walk up the harbour steps into the barking and the stink and the grey light, with the blowholes coughing along the shore. They put their thick bare feet on the warm black rock and weep, and the shelf-folk let them, because they understand.

## The Whole of It

So who lives here?

The people do: short, broad, horn-handed, silent-voiced and never still, climbing and counting and signing across the steam. The birds do, in numbers that don't fit into the mind. And the mats in the pools, rust and olive and sulphur-yellow, which are the only green thing anyone here has ever seen. And the petrels in the deep tubes, and the bones stacked beside them.

But the longer you look, the less these seem like separate inhabitants and the more they seem like parts of one creature. The city and the colony interleave. The people eat the birds and the birds eat the people. The Counters' cords run parallel, decade on decade, and the rock does not wear. And the whole of it breathes steam and makes a single unbroken sound, day and night, generation on generation, holding itself exactly level.

It is a vast, patient, many-handed thing, spread across miles of black stone. Its nerves are knotted gut, its voice is ten thousand throats, and its memory is fingertips reading the dark. It has one purpose, which it has never once failed at: to keep the ground from moving.

## Night, at the Pit-Rim

Late at night an old Counter comes out of her house onto the rim of the pit where her district cooks. The pool below is milky and still, and a few bags hang in it on their lines, simmering. The gulls have settled on the chimney-tops, mostly. The petrels are singing underground; she can feel them more than hear them, a hum in the stone. Out on the coast the surf is louder in the dark, and every few breaths a blowhole fires, and she feels it through her feet before the cough arrives.

She has spent the day with the cords. They matched, as they always match.

She kneels on the rim and lays her soft palm flat on the black rock. It is warm, the same warmth as when she was a girl, the same as in her grandmother's hands. She holds her hand there a long time. Then she makes the sign against the stone itself, flat hand, level, pressed slightly down: *held.*

Under her palm, very faintly, the warmth seems to press back, the way a sleeper's chest rises against a hand laid on it. Or it doesn't. She could not say, and she has never told anyone she wonders.

She stays until the orange murk begins to pale toward the long grey day. Then she gets up and goes inside, and all around her, unbroken, the clamour goes on singing.
