# Who Lives in the Drowned Granary

## First, the Tenants

Begin where the description begins, with the full rooms and the packed walls: the mussels, the eels, the shrimp, the snails, the caddis larvae in their small stone houses. They are all real, and they are all there. Taken together they are less a population than a census, since everything has a place and everything is in it.

But a census implies someone counting. Watch long enough, and a very long time is the only way anything happens here, and you start to suspect that the room is not only occupied. It is kept.

## The Pale Thing in the Conduit

It is a salamander, or it is what a salamander becomes when it stops being asked to be anything else.

It is about the length of a tall man's arm from snout to the end of the tail, and heavier than it looks. Its skin is the color of the sinter, a cream so pale it is nearly the absence of color. Under that skin the faint pink of blood shows at the throat and along the flanks, where three feathery gill-tufts stand out on either side of the head. It never lost them. It never left the water, never grew lungs worth using, never became the adult its lineage once became somewhere far away and long ago. It is a larva that has lived for centuries. It has kept the shape of its own beginning, the way the room keeps the shape of the vault.

It has no eyes, or none that open. There are two small dark smudges beneath the skin where eyes might have been, like thumbprints under wax. It does not need them. The whole skin of its head is pitted with tiny sensory pores, and it reads the room by pressure, current, and the electric murmur of living muscle. It can feel a shrimp flex from a body-length away. It can feel an eel's heartbeat through the stone of its niche.

It moves the way the water moves here, which is to say barely. It can lie across the mouth of the conduit for a season without shifting. When it does move, it walks. It has four short limbs with splayed, soft-fingered hands, three fingers in front and two behind, and it walks across the floor as if across the floor of a house it knows in the dark. That is exactly what it is doing.

## What It Does at Noon

Here is the first thing that should have been noticed and wasn't.

When the grey smudge of noon comes down through the opening, every eel head in every narrow niche turns toward the center of the room. The description says so. What it does not say is that the salamander does not turn toward it.

At noon the keeper is already in the center of the room. It walks out of the conduit a little before midday, as if it can feel the sun coming across the saddle overhead through a hundred feet of rock and water, and perhaps it can. It settles on the paving directly beneath the opening, under the place where the column of grey will hang. When the light arrives it is behind the keeper and above it, and the keeper's pale body is the brightest thing in the room, the only thing the light actually seems to touch.

It faces outward, toward the walls, toward the eels.

So the hundreds of blunt pale faces that turn toward the center are not quite turning toward the light. Or they are turning toward the light and toward what sits in it, and the two have been the same thing for so long that the difference no longer matters.

For an hour the keeper stays there, gills slowly lifting and falling. It turns, very slowly, through a full circle, a few degrees at a time, so that by the time the light fades it has faced every wall of the chamber once. Then the grey dissolves and the eels withdraw, and the keeper walks back to the conduit.

It does not eat during this hour. Nothing is being fed. The eels are being looked at, or they are looking back, or the hour is simply the room's way of being counted.

## The Keeping

After enough noons the pattern shows itself. The room does not fill its empty niches by accident.

When a mussel colony dies back, the shells stay. But when an eel dies, the body does not stay in its hole. The keeper comes. It walks up the ramp that was once a stair and along the ledges, pressing its soft hands into the worn cubbyholes, and it draws the dead eel out by the head, gently. Some it eats, slowly, over days. Others it carries to the floor and leaves, and the shrimp come in their thousands and make the body into a pale shimmering shape that gradually becomes no shape. The bones settle. The sinter takes them in time. The walls are faintly studded with fossils of their own tenants, and the keeper is the one who places them.

Then the niche is empty.

Eels should not be able to breed here. They are creatures of long journeys. The ones the world knows are born in far warm seas, cross oceans as glass threads, and climb rivers for years before they settle, and they go back to the sea to spawn and die. A sealed basin at the top of a mountain should have held one generation and then emptied, one niche at a time, until the scroll-holes stood open and dark.

They have not emptied. The conduit is the reason.

## The Road Down

The spring channel is the width of a child at the mouth, square and hand-cut. The builders' tool marks survive faintly along its first stretch, until they stop, and after that the walls are no longer made. The channel was bored back into the rock until it met the warm water, and beyond that meeting point it simply becomes the mountain's own plumbing: cracks, fissures, a descending braid of warm dark passages that the builders never entered.

The keeper goes down there.

Not often. Perhaps once in the life of a mussel. It leaves the mouth of the conduit and does not come back for a season, then a year, and for that time the room is unattended. Noon comes and the light hangs over an empty floor. The eels turn toward it anyway, because a custom does not stop when the one who keeps it is away, and they turn back.

Then the keeper returns. It walks up out of the warm channel slowly, as it does everything, and it carries something. The small near-transparent eels, fine as threads of glass, are wound through its gill-tufts, held in the loose folds of skin at its throat, and clinging to its back. There might be a dozen of them. They have come from wherever the deep water goes, far down in the mountain, somewhere the keeper knows and nothing else here does. Maybe there is a sea down there, black and hot and older than the spring. Maybe the mountain's warm water connects, through channels no one has mapped, to a river that connects to a coast. Maybe it only leads to another chamber, deeper, with other niches and another keeper.

The keeper walks to the walls and puts the elvers into the empty niches, one at a time.

It does not put them just anywhere. It knows which holes are empty. It holds each small eel at the mouth of a particular scroll-hole until the eel's own wish to be inside something takes over, and the glass thread slides into the dark and stays.

*An empty niche is filled within a season.* That is the rule of the place, and this is how it happens.

## The Office, Not the Animal

The keeper is old, but it cannot be as old as the room, and that leads to the next realization.

There is more than one of them. The description guesses this, *it might be several of them*, and it is right, though not in the way it seems. They do not all keep the room together. Far back in the conduit, where the water is hottest and the rock gives way to natural cracks, there are others, smaller and younger. They lie in the warm channels for decades doing nothing but growing on shrimp. They almost never come into the room.

When the keeper dies, and after centuries it does die, it does not die in the room. It walks back into the conduit one last time and does not return. For a while there is no keeper. The niches of dead eels stand empty, and the room is briefly ragged.

Then one of the younger ones comes up.

It is a little smaller than the last. The first time it comes out of the conduit it does not know the way. It walks the floor slowly, touching everything, and it keeps turning back to the conduit's mouth as if uncertain it is allowed. The eels do not know it. It does not know the eels. But at noon something pulls it to the center of the room, under the opening, and when the light comes it is sitting there, facing outward. Every head turns toward it.

After that, it is the keeper.

So what lives in the conduit is not one animal but a succession, an office handed down through an unbroken line of blind pale larvae who never grow up. Each one learns the room by walking it in the dark. Each one learns the particular warmth of each niche, the heartbeat of each eel, the exact texture of the wall where the old tool marks survive under the sinter like the ghost of a fingerprint. Each one takes up the work the one before it left off, and none of them could say why.

## The Builders

It would be easy, too easy, to say the keepers are the builders, that the people who cut this place long ago went down into the water and became something that lives in the dark and keeps the dead. That is a story, and the room does not tell stories.

What the room offers is quieter, and stranger.

The builders cut the niches and flooded them warm. They had hammerstones and sand and patience. They made a building for creatures, not for themselves. Someone had to put the first animals into it, and someone had to keep putting them in when they died, and someone had to go down through the channel, or up the mountain from a distant river, with living things in their hands, and place them in rows, facing the center.

Then the builders were gone, and the practice was not.

At some point, which no record marks, the work of keeping passed from people who walked on the pavement above to an animal that walks on the pavement below. Whether that animal was taught, or tamed, or simply shaped by centuries of living in a place where every surface is claimed and every hole has a tenant, no one can say. Perhaps the builders brought the first salamanders from a sacred spring somewhere else and set them in the conduit, and the salamanders, living in the warm dark with the eels, slowly grew into the shape of the task. Perhaps the room did it alone. A building that insists, for ten thousand years, on being full may eventually produce the creature that fills it, the way a riverbed produces the fish that fits its current.

Either way, the keeper does what the builders did. It walks the rows. It clears the dead. It brings the new. It sits in the light at noon, and the tenants face it.

## The Arrangement

So the answer to *who lives there* keeps widening.

The mussels live there, in their banked centuries of shell. The eels live there, each in its assigned hole, facing the center. The shrimp and snails and caddis larvae live there, grazing, dropping, building their tiny second masonry over the first. The keeper lives there, in the conduit, in its long unbroken line.

But the thing that most truly lives in the drowned granary, the one that has been there longest and will be there last, is none of these. It is the arrangement itself. The rows, the places, the noon turning of every head, the empty niche and the new small eel and the dead one carried down to the floor. It is a custom with no one left to explain it. The creatures inhabit it the way the eels inhabit their holes: completely, without question, and with only their faces showing.

The arrangement is not alive, exactly. It does not eat. But it persists, and it reproduces itself through each new keeper that climbs up out of the warmth and learns the walls. It grows too, very slowly, as the sinter thickens and the honeycomb closes.

## The Last Keeper

Someday, very far off, the room will close.

The niches will narrow under their glaze until no eel can fit. The mussel banks will rise until they meet the snails coming down. The caddis ridges will merge with the sinter, and the sinter with the stone. The conduit will be the last open thing, because it is the warmest, and water that keeps moving does not easily glaze over.

There will be a keeper then. It will come up out of the channel on the day it becomes the keeper, as all of them have, and it will find a room that is almost solid. The vault will be a vault-shaped memory inside a block of shell and fossil and cream-colored mineral. The niches will be sealed, with their last tenants inside them like eels in amber.

It will walk as far as it can, which will not be far. It will settle, a little before noon, on the last patch of open floor beneath the opening. The opening will be very narrow by then, ringed with its banded collar of ten thousand summers, but it will still be there.

The light will come down, faint and grey.

The keeper will face outward toward the walls, toward the sealed rows, toward a congregation that is now part of the building. Nothing will turn to look at it. And it will sit there anyway for the hour, gills lifting and falling, turning a few degrees at a time until it has faced every wall once.

Then the grey will fade, and it will go back down into the warm dark, where the deep water is. In some other hand-cut room, deeper in the mountain or on the far side of it, other niches may still be open, and other small glass threads may still need a place to wait for the light.
