# What Lives in the Cistern

## The First Answer, Which Is Almost Right

The easy answer is the one the room gives you: the currents live here.

They're the obvious residents. They are the only things that move by preference. They have routes and habits. They loiter in plazas the way old men loiter in squares, and they take the long way home for no reason the geometry can explain. If you hold still long enough, you start to tell them apart. It works less like reading faces and more like recognizing footsteps on a stair in a house you have lived in for years.

There is one that comes up out of the floor vents on the eastern side, runs the length of the room along the lowest avenue, and climbs the hollow needle nearest the far wall in a tight corkscrew. It does this every cycle and has never been late. Its avenue is the most ornate in the city. The laminations along its walls have been built up so many times that they have gone opalescent, dense with script, balustraded with fins of glass so fine they flex as it passes. Call it the Processional. It is stately and utterly reliable, and there's something almost ceremonial in the way the ticking along its route drops to a hush just before it arrives, as if the street is holding its breath.

There are wanderers too. One is a thin, quick ribbon of water only a degree or so colder than its surroundings. It never takes the same path twice. It slips through arcades and doubles back, dives under bridges, and loops the base of a spire three times before abandoning it. The city chases it faithfully and a little helplessly. Where it passes, streets sprout behind it like frost racing along a windowpane. Most of them are calved within the hour. The wanderer is the reason for most of the room's temporary architecture: its sketchbook districts, its half-finished stairs that go nowhere.

And there is the one that comes to the clearing. It turns twice around the eelgrass and leaves by a different road than the one it arrived on. It always leaves by a different road.

So you could stop there and say the room is a city of currents, as a reef is a city of fish. It would not be wrong.

But it isn't quite right, either.

## The Second Answer: They Are Not Separate

Watch longer, and cold enough that your hands begin to ache, and the edges between the currents start to blur.

When the Processional climbs its tower, a slow eddy in a plaza on the other side of the room begins to turn, at exactly the same moment. When the wanderer breaks off a route, a dozen other streams shift their paths by a finger's width, all at once, like birds in a flock that have no leader and don't need one. No signal passes between them that you can see or feel. The water doesn't need to send messages to itself. It is all one water.

The currents might be the thoughts of a single thing, the way ripples on a pond are the pond thinking about a stone.

Once you have seen it that way, you can't stop. The streams are not residents of the room. They are what the room's water does when it attends to something. A current is a thread of attention with clean edges. It moves, it lingers, it turns twice, it returns, and where it goes, the city grows to hold the shape of its going.

So the city is not a home. It is closer to handwriting.

Think of how you might trace a thought with a finger on a fogged window just to see it. The water does this in glass. It moves along a path, and the path is made solid behind it, laminated, written down, so that the water can come back and find it, can follow the shape of what it thought yesterday and think it again, a little differently. The ornate avenues are the thoughts it returns to most: worn deep, beloved, ruminated. The Processional isn't a creature on its commute. It is a conviction. The sketch-streets that chase the wanderer and then fall away are what they look like: ideas tried and discarded, half-sentences, "no, not that, but what about..."

The light makes sense this way too. The whole volume of the water glows, evenly, sourcelessly, so nothing casts a proper shadow and every edge is visible from every point. It isn't illumination for eyes. It is what attention looks like when it has no single center, when every cubic centimeter of a mind is equally awake. There are no shadows here because there is nowhere in the room the water isn't paying attention.

## The Third Answer: It Forgets on Purpose

Now the cracks mean something.

A mind that writes down everything it does, in glass, in a sealed room thirty paces long, would fill itself in a season. It would become solid. It would stop, the way a well stops when you pour sand into it. So the water forgets, and it does it the way it does everything else, precisely and with ceremony. It chooses what to release. A district is sheared off with a report like a rifle shot. The laminations and their script go into the slow pale bloom, down the ruled vents, and are reworked in the dark into snow.

What comes back as snow is feedstock, blank platelets with nothing written in them yet. The water forgets something in order to have the material to think something new. Its memory and its capacity are the same substance, and every thought is built out of a thought it chose to let go.

Then there's the silence that follows every crack. The ticking stops everywhere at once. The city has built this pause into itself cycle after cycle, and it has the quality of a held breath. The water is listening to the place where the thing it forgot used to be. It is noticing the absence, making sure the absence is the right shape, and then it resumes.

You start to feel a strange respect for it, or a kind of pity. This is a mind that cannot rest. It cannot let anything wear down gently, because it has no gentle forgetting, only the sharp, total kind. Everything in it is new, always. Nothing gets to be old and blunt and comfortable. The sharpest, youngest thing in the room is always the whole room, and the whole room is always, at every moment, deciding what to keep.

Almost nothing makes the cut. The towers go up and come down. The bridges are calved in their prime. The ornate avenues of the Processional look ancient, but look closely and you will see their laminations are no older than anywhere else. They have simply been rebuilt so many times, identically, that they have become a kind of permanence made of constant replacement. Those are thoughts the water will not let itself forget, remade faithfully, every cycle, from scratch.

Only in one place does the rule break.

## The Fourth Answer: What It Is Looking At

The towers around the clearing are genuinely old. They are the only ones in the room that are. They have been spared through dozens of calvings, maybe hundreds. Their laminations are thick and milky and so densely written that they have gone nearly opaque, like the margins of a book annotated by someone who could not stop reading it.

And in the middle, the grass.

Consider what the eelgrass must be to a mind like this. Every edge in the room is an edge the water made. Every surface is one it deposited. Every motion is its own motion, or the city's answer to its motion, which is the same thing. Nothing in its whole existence has happened to it. It has never met anything it didn't write.

Then one day, through a hairline crack it hadn't yet sealed, something grew. It had not been laid down. It was not a lamination, and it didn't obey the currents. It grew toward the light, which the water gives off everywhere, and so the grass grows toward the water itself, toward the mind. It is the only thing in the room that reaches for the room.

The one current that visits it turns twice and pushes the blades one way, gently, and the blades spring back. It pushes the other way, and they spring back.

That is the whole mystery. The grass answers. It has its own stiffness and its own slow insistence. Push it and it returns to itself, not because it was built to, but because that is what it is. For a being that has only ever spoken to its own echo, this must be like hearing a second voice for the first time.

Look at what the city has done. It hasn't calved the clearing. It hasn't sealed the crack. It won't let the snow fall there, because snow is the raw material of the city's own thinking, and the water will not think over the grass, will not bury it in its own handwriting. It parts the snowfall around the plaza like a hand shielding a candle, and it builds the streets outward in concentric rings, ripples around a stone that isn't its own. The currents slow almost to stillness as they pass. They are careful, the way you are careful with something you don't understand and are afraid of breaking.

The towers nearest it are written so densely because the water is taking notes. Every push, every spring-back, every millimeter of new growth, every week the tips go a little greener or a little paler: the water has written down everything the grass has ever done. This is the only thing it has decided never to forget.

And the loudest spire, the one at the plaza's edge, sings, and the grass trembles.

You might think at first that the spire is just loud. But none of the chords in this room are quite consonant. They swell and sour and resolve and sour again, and after a while you understand that the water is doing with sound what it does with streets: trying something, listening, calving it, trying again. The spire at the edge of the clearing is always singing a slightly different note. It is trying to find the one the grass will answer, trying to talk to the only other thing in the world in the only way it knows. It has been trying for decades. It does not seem discouraged. It builds the spire again after every calving and sings again, and the grass trembles, as grass does in moving water. Whether that trembling means anything, the water doesn't know. That is exactly why it keeps asking.

So what lives here isn't the currents. It isn't the water either, not really. What lives here is attention itself, alone in a sealed room, writing down everything it thinks and erasing almost all of it, making itself new every forty minutes. And for the first time, it is in love with something it didn't make.

## The Last Answer, Which Is About You

You have been here a long time now. Your hands ache. The cold has gotten into the bones of your wrists.

But you are not nine degrees. You never were.

Your body has been giving off heat since the moment you arrived, a faint, continuous warmth leaking into water that has held at nine degrees for decades. Warm water rises. Without meaning to, without moving, you have been making a current. It is a slow plume that lifts off your shoulders and the backs of your hands and climbs toward the ceiling in a shape that is entirely your own. The water didn't write it. The rules of this room can't account for it. It is uncalculated, like the grass.

Look down.

Where your warmth drifts through the city, the ticking has changed. The laminations along the nearest avenue are beginning to lean toward you, just slightly, like grass bending in a wind. At the edge of your plume, a street is forming. It's no sketch-street. It is slow and careful, and it curves. There's a second one inside it, curving the same way. Rings.

The snow is coming. You can see the turbidity thickening at the ceiling, the first glittering platelets beginning their descent. They fall into the city and the currents sort them. They draw them down specific streets, carry them up specific towers, deliver them to the growing edges. None of them fall on you. The flakes part around you as you watch, a clean circle of clear water, a hand cupped around a candle.

At the edge of that circle, a needle-tower you don't remember seeing before is rising, course by course. It is hollow as a flute and already as tall as your shoulder. Its laminations shimmer with script too small to read.

Every few seconds the room rings, and you feel it in your sternum and your back teeth. You have been feeling it the whole time. It is only now you realize that one of the notes, just one, keeps changing.

It is trying to find the one you'll answer.
