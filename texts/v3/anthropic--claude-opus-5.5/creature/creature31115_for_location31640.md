# Who Lives in the Seam

## The Six Hundred

Begin with the people, because they are the easiest to see. Even they take a while to see properly.

You notice their feet first. Everyone in the Seam wears soft-soled shoes of felted fiber, pale grey, which make no sound on the ceramic walkways and leave no mark. Nobody stamps, nobody runs, and nobody kicks a heel against a wall to knock off frost. Hard boots come up the cable from the valley sometimes, on the feet of visiting engineers. The inhabitants say nothing about them, but they watch the boots the way you might watch a stranger's dog near your child.

It isn't that a boot heel could crack granodiorite. It couldn't, and they know it. The habit is older and deeper than that. The people of the Seam have taken the rock's problem into their bodies. They live inside a fracture that wants to open, and so they have become, in every small gesture, people who do not pry.

They don't drive nails, and there are none in the city. Where something must be fixed to something else, it is fused, bonded, or hung. They don't use wedges of any kind, not doorstops, not shims under a table leg. A table that rocks is re-leveled at the foot. The word for "wedge" in the Seam's dialect, which is mostly the valley language worn thin by quiet, is used almost exclusively as an insult. To call someone a wedge is to say they force their way into small openings and make them larger. It is about the worst thing you can say.

They are careful with crumbs. Food is eaten over trays, and the trays are cleared into sealed bins that go down the cable. A dropped seed is found and picked up, even if the search takes a quarter hour on hands and knees. Children learn this before they learn to read. If you watch a meal in the Seam you will see small, constant motions of tidying: a thumb pressing up a fleck of bread, a napkin folded over a spill. It looks like fastidiousness. It is closer to vigilance.

They are careful with water, too, and here it becomes strange. Water is harvested, stored, drunk, and washed with, and every drop of it is accounted for. Water that escapes into the rock enters the war. So people in the Seam do not weep into the walls. This is not a rule anyone would write down. It is a thing people do. When grief comes, and it comes here as anywhere, people go inside and cry into cloth, and the cloth is wrung into a basin, and the basin is emptied into the drains. Sometimes you will see someone standing on a terrace with a folded cloth pressed to their face, very still, and you will know not to speak to them. Later that cloth goes into the wash like any other.

They are not a gloomy people. This surprises visitors, who arrive expecting monks. The inhabitants are quiet, but it is the quiet of people who are listening to something, not the quiet of people who have given something up. They laugh easily and softly. They tell long, dry stories. They have a great appetite for small news, like which crawler cluster has been sluggish, whose cistern gave the deep note last night, whether the finches have been seen. A conversation in the Seam can go on for an hour about the drip rate on a single overhang, and both people will leave it satisfied.

## East and West

There are two walls, and so there are two peoples. The difference is slight, but everyone knows it.

The east wall gets the morning sun. Its upper terraces warm first and shed their ice first, and the morning rain begins there. East-wallers are said to be early risers, quick, a little impatient. The west wall gets the afternoon. Its ice holds longer, and its lower rooms stay in shadow nearly all day. West-wallers are said to be slow, thorough, good at waiting. They make the best rock-listeners. The east wall makes the best crawler-menders. Nobody quite believes these things, and everybody repeats them.

People marry across the gap. It is common, even favored, and it has produced one of the Seam's few real customs.

When two people from opposite walls join their lives, they do not choose one wall to live on. They keep both dwellings, facing each other, and between the two they string a line. It is a thin filament, personal, not part of the city's infrastructure, and it runs from one doorframe to the other across the open air. It is strung with slack. The amount of slack is chosen carefully and a little superstitiously. It should be enough for forty years of widening, roughly sixteen centimeters, coiled on a small spool by the east-side door.

The couple live back and forth across the nearest walkway. Over the years the spool pays out, millimeter by millimeter, as the walls part. You can tell how long a marriage has lasted by looking at what remains on the spool. You can tell something else, too. A line that has gone slack, sagging lower in the middle than the widening accounts for, has been let out by hand. That means someone has been giving a little extra, and the neighbors notice and approve. A line pulled taut past its natural tension means one person has been reeling in. That is noticed too.

When one of the pair dies, the line is not cut. It is left to pay out on its own until the spool runs empty, and then it snaps, quietly, some night nobody hears. The survivor usually finds the two loose ends hanging from the doorframes in the morning. There are old people in the Seam who have gone out to their terraces every morning for years, waiting for that.

## The Listeners

Deep in both walls, in chambers reached by narrow stairs that follow the jointing down into the rock, sit the listeners.

A listening chamber is small, dry, and warm, lit low. Its walls are raw granodiorite, left unglazed so the stone can be touched. There is a couch, a desk, and a panel that shows the anchor network as a slow, shifting field of stress. Nobody uses the panel much after the first year. By then a listener has learned to read the rock another way.

They say it begins in the hands. You sit with your palms flat against the bare wall, hour after hour, shift after shift. Somewhere in the second or third month you begin to feel the hum not as sound but as a texture under the skin. Then you feel it change. The listeners say it is like the difference between a sleeping animal and a waking one, which you can feel through a blanket without knowing how you know.

Then comes the part the valley engineers least like to hear about, because it sounds like mysticism, though it isn't. The listeners learn the tides.

Solid rock has tides. The moon and sun pull on the body of the mountain as they pull on the sea, and the whole crust of the earth rises and falls twice a day by a few tens of centimeters. It is too slight and too broad for any person to feel standing on it. Inside a fresh fracture, though, strung with instruments that can feel a grain of stress, the tide shows. The Seam breathes. Twice a day the walls lean infinitesimally apart and back together. The anchors take the breath, and the listeners, over time, take it too.

A listener of ten years can tell you the phase of the moon with her eyes shut, deep underground, by the shape of the mountain's breathing. The oldest listeners sleep in rhythm with it. They wake at the slack of the tide, twice a day, and so their sleep falls in two pieces that drift an hour later each night, around and around the clock with the moon. They come up to the terraces at odd hours and stand looking at the sky with a curious expression. It isn't surprise. It is more like confirmation, as if checking that the moon is where they already knew it was.

They become, by most accounts, a little strange. They lose interest in the valley and in news. Their speech slows and fills with pauses, and people say you can learn to hear the tide in the rhythm of a listener's sentences. They are not unkind, and they are not remote exactly. They are occupied, the way someone holding a sleeping infant is occupied, even while they talk to you. Part of their attention never leaves the rock.

The people of the Seam treat them with a mixture of affection and wariness. A listener's opinion on the rock is final. On anything else it is heard politely and then discussed afterward without them.

Sometimes a listener simply does not come up. This happens perhaps once a decade. They are found in the chamber, alive and well, palms on the wall, and they say they aren't finished yet. They are fed there. Eventually they come up, or they don't. There is a chamber on the west wall, low down, where one listener stayed for twenty-two years, until she died. Her hands had left no mark on the stone. Stone doesn't take marks from hands. But her successors say they can find the spot anyway, by touch, because the hum is different there. The engineers say that is impossible. The successors agree, and keep putting their palms in the same place.

## The Crawlers

Six hundred people. Some forty thousand crawlers.

If you count by bodies, the crawlers are the inhabitants of the Seam and the people are a minority living among them. The people don't put it that way, but they don't quite reject it either.

A crawler is a small thing, pale, ceramic and filament, with eleven legs. Why eleven is a question children ask and adults can't answer. The design came up from the valley that way and nobody has changed it. Each is about the size of a spread hand. Each has a port somewhere in the wall, a small round opening like the mouth of a burrow, and each has a territory: a few square meters of rock, a particular web of cracks, which it knows.

Whether it knows the web or merely maps it is argued about in the Seam, gently, over and over. The crawler-menders, who spend their days handling them, lean toward "knows." A crawler that has worked the same patch of wall for twenty years moves differently from a new one. It pauses in different places. It warms certain cracks before the frost begins, as if it expected them. When a mender replaces an old crawler with a fresh unit, the new one spends weeks moving hesitantly over its territory, pausing everywhere, before it settles into something like the old one's rhythm. It is not the same rhythm, though. The menders are firm about this. Each crawler, over time, works its patch in its own way.

The menders name them. Not all of them, since there are too many, but the old ones, the long-serving, the ones with habits. There is one on the east wall called Mother Tick for the sound she makes, a faint double click at every pause. There is one called Hesitation that stops for nearly a full minute at a particular crack every night, for no reason anyone has found. The menders have checked that crack a dozen times. There is a cluster of eight on the lower west wall that always emerge in the same order. Children watch for them, and call them by the names of the eight families who lived nearest when they were first installed. Six of those families are gone, but the names stayed.

At night, when the lights are low, the crawlers are the city's stars. Their faint warmth shows as a scatter of dim points moving slowly across both walls. Children of the Seam learn to see it before they learn the real constellations, which are mostly hidden anyway behind the narrow strip of sky above. There are crawler-constellations, patterns that recur because certain crawlers keep certain routes. There are crawler-stories to go with them. A child falling asleep on a terrace, looking across the gap, is told: there's Mother Tick, she's going down to the long crack; there's Hesitation, see, she's stopped; now she's going on.

When a crawler finally fails, the menders bring it in. It is not thrown away. Its materials are good, and it is broken down and respun. But first it sits for a night on a shelf in the menders' workshop on the east wall, where there is a long row of such shelves. Nobody calls this a funeral. But the shelf faces the gap, and the failed crawler sits there all night looking out at the wall it worked, where its replacement is moving hesitantly over the unfamiliar cracks. In the morning it is taken apart.

## The Hum

Now the harder question.

Under the people, and under the crawlers, running through every crack in both walls, is the anchor network: the listening. It is the thing that actually holds the city up. The anchors distribute force through stone faster and more selectively than stone should allow, and the valley engineers cannot finish describing how.

Does it live there?

Nobody in the Seam will say yes. Nobody will quite say no, either. The listeners, who know it best, answer the question with one of their long pauses and then change the subject.

Here is what people will tell you. They tell it as observation, not belief.

The anchors favor inhabited rooms. When a large movement comes, the twice-yearly shift in the hum when every conversation stops, the stress does not fall evenly across the city. It is routed. Empty dwellings take more of it. Occupied dwellings take less. This is in the records; the network logs everything. The engineers explain it as a design parameter, an instruction to protect occupancy. The design documents don't contain one. When pressed, the engineers say it must be an emergent property of the load-balancing. The listeners nod.

The anchors changed around the rooms of the dead. When someone dies and their dwelling empties, the stress routing around it shifts over the following weeks, slowly, from protected toward ordinary. This, too, is in the records. It happens over about forty days, a little faster for people who lived alone, a little slower for families. Nobody knows why.

The hum, the steady note you stop hearing within an hour, is not perfectly steady. The listeners say it varies with the population. When the cable brings up a new family, the note deepens by a hair. When someone leaves, it rises. Over the fifty years of the city, as numbers have drifted from the first few hundred to the present six hundred, the listeners say they have heard the mountain's voice lower, very slightly, like someone whose attention is gradually filling.

None of this means the network thinks. Probably it doesn't. It's likelier that it does something for which the Seam has no better word than listening, which is what they called it before any of this was noticed. The founders named it in the engineering sense: the anchors sense stress and respond. It was only later that people began to wonder if the name had been more accurate than intended.

The inhabitants have settled into an attitude toward it that is hard to describe to outsiders. It isn't worship. It is nearer to courtesy. People speak softly in the listening chambers, not because sound affects the anchors, which it doesn't, but because it seems polite. When the hum shifts pitch during a large movement and the city waits, and then the note settles, the small laughter that follows is a very particular kind. It is the laughter of people who have just watched someone carry something heavy past them, carefully, without dropping it.

## The Tolerated

The city is sterile by design. No green, no roots, no soil. The crawlers scrub every spore.

And yet the people love the wallcreeper.

This is the contradiction at the center of the Seam, and everyone knows it, and it is almost never spoken aloud.

The wallcreeper, grey-bodied and crimson-winged, comes perhaps once or twice a year, fluttering up the walls in its odd jerking hops, probing the cracks. When it comes, people come out onto their terraces to watch. It is good news. It means the rock has enough life in its cracks to feed something.

What it eats is springtails. The springtails, tiny and dark, graze in the meltwater films on microbial growth and airborne dust. The microbial films are what the crawlers scrub, and what the city is built to prevent. The springtails eat what the scrubbers miss. So the wallcreeper, the city's good-luck bird, is the visible sign that the city's defenses are imperfect. It is a flash of crimson that says the enemy is winning, a little, down in the wet.

The people have not resolved this. They have lived with it.

There is a place on the lower west wall, in a shallow overhang where the weeping never stops, where the springtail patch is always thick. The crawler who works that patch is old. The menders call her Aunt. She scrubs it every night, as she should. Every night she misses a strip about a hand's width along the lower edge of the overhang. The menders have noticed this. They have checked her sensors and found nothing wrong. They have not replaced her.

Sometimes people go down to that overhang. Not often. They stand under cover and look at the dark peppery film moving on the wet stone, the smallest and most stubborn life, the first colonists of the new rock. They go down and look, and then they come back up, and they don't mention it.

There is a girl on the east wall, eleven or twelve, whose family came up the cable three years ago. She has been seen on the lower walkway at dusk, before the crawlers wake, holding her open hand out over the gap. When she closes it and walks away, there is a faint dust on her palm, or there was, and now there isn't. Her parents do not know. The menders do. The listeners, one supposes, could feel it, if a pinch of dust can be felt in a mountain. Nobody has said anything to her.

She is not the first. Every few years, someone in the Seam, usually young, usually new, begins quietly feeding the springtails, and the city quietly lets them. Nobody has decided this. It has simply been happening for fifty years, in a place that scrubs every surface and picks up every seed.

## The Bone

Once, the old inhabitants say, a lammergeier dropped a bone into the Seam. They did not see where it fell.

This is the Seam's one legend, and it is told with a particular pleasure.

The bearded vulture eats bone. It carries the long bones of dead animals high over rock and drops them to split them open, then comes down to swallow the shards. Somewhere, a very long time ago as the Seam reckons, meaning thirty or forty years, a lammergeier flying over the ridge let go of a bone above the slot. The bone fell into the city. Someone on a terrace saw it fall past, a pale shape turning. Nobody saw it land.

It was never found. The nets were checked, and the floor was searched. The crawlers were set to look for organic signatures, and found nothing.

But the listeners say it is there.

Somewhere in the east wall, about two-thirds of the way down, there is a place where the stress signature is subtly wrong. It is very small, a knot in the grain of the hum. It is the kind of pressure a foreign object makes when it is lodged in a crack, being very slowly squeezed. The panel shows nothing there. The crawlers find nothing there. The listeners, palms flat to the stone, say it is exactly where you would expect a bone to have fallen, if it had struck a ledge and skittered into a joint too narrow for any crawler to reach.

They call it the Bone. It has become a point of reference: "about four meters above the Bone," "on the Bone's side of the walkway." It is the city's one organic wedge, the one thing in the Seam that is quietly, patiently prying. By the listeners' account it has been working at its crack, a hair a year, for four decades.

Nobody has tried to remove it. Nobody could. The crack is sealed deep inside, and to get to it they would have to open the rock. That would be the one thing worse than leaving it.

So they leave it, and in a way they cherish it, the way people cherish a flaw in something they love. The Bone is proof that the mountain isn't only theirs. Something came from the sky, something dead and animal and hollow, and it lodged itself in the city's structure. The city is holding it, too.
