# The Other Hand

## I. The First Answer

The obvious answer is the makers, so begin with them. They are the ones you meet.

They are small people, smaller than you expect, and their hands are too large for them. The hands are broad and long-fingered, and the pads of the thumbs are worn flat and shiny as a burnished wedge. The rest of them seems to have grown around the hands the way bark grows around a lashing, as an afterthought. Their skin takes on the grey-green of the strands after enough years, and it is always beaded with mist. Their hair is plaited, of course, but not neatly. Each plait is worked with the same small irregularities as their knots, a little tighter here, a little looser there, finished with a tuck. You can tell makers apart by their hair more easily than by their faces.

They eat what the Wicker gives. They drink from bladder-vines, which they slit with a thumbnail and suck like fruit. They eat fiddlehead ferns, and cratch picked from the moss-furrows and cracked between the teeth. Driftmoths they eat rarely and with some ceremony, lifting the soft grey body from the air in a cupped palm as if it were something they had been lent.

They sleep lashed. At the end of a working stretch a maker lies along the top of a strand and ties themself to it with a slipknot, a few loops around the waist and one around the ankle, and then goes still. A sleeping maker looks exactly like a lashing. More than one traveler has walked past a row of them in the dim amber of the breathing without knowing. The lashers do this too. The makers do not seem to find the resemblance strange.

They are very old. None of them remembers being young. When a maker is finished, which is the word they use, they walk to one of the great knot-masses and sit down in a gully among the blue fungi, among the crowd of remembered concentration, and they put their hands in their lap. After a while it is hard to say which of the soft hummings in the knot is theirs.

That is the first answer, and it is true. But if you watch the makers work long enough, you notice something they do that makes no sense.

They never finish a knot.

Every lashing is tied, tightened, dressed. Then, at the very end, the maker leaves the last tuck undone: a single tail of cord, pulled nearly through and not quite. Then they move on. When you ask why, they say, *That's for the other hand.*

## II. The Slack

Once you know to look, you find the evidence everywhere.

A lashing on a living stem is a dangerous thing. Tie it too tight and the stem cannot thicken beneath it. The bark swells above and below the cord, the sap backs up, and in a few seasons everything past the knot goes grey and brittle and dies. Anyone who has bound a young tree to a stake knows this. The cord must be eased as the tree grows, or the cord will kill it.

The Wicker has more lashings than there are leaves on a forest, and they were tied by hands of every kind. Some were patient. Some were hurried, frightened, or angry, and pulled far too hard. Yet the Wicker does not die of its knots. Walk any strand and you will find lashings that have been eased: the unfinished tuck drawn back, a loop let out by the width of a finger, the pale fresh bark beneath the old cord line showing the stem has had room to breathe. The work is invisible unless you are looking for it. It is always done from below.

The makers do not do this. You can watch them for weeks and they never loosen anything. When you show them an eased knot they touch it gently, the way you might touch the forehead of a child who has finally stopped crying, and they say again: *The other hand.*

## III. The Undersides

No one walks the undersides of the strands.

Why would they? The tops are carpeted with moss. They are wide and soft and sure underfoot. The undersides are a separate country, and you only see it by lying on your belly and hanging your head over the edge.

It is darker there. The green-gold light that comes from everywhere reaches the undersides last, and by then it has gone amber, the color of the breathing at its lowest. The moss is thin and wiry and grows in small tufts pointing the wrong way. Water gathers here. Everything the mist lays down on the upper faces runs over the curve and collects underneath in long rows of trembling beads. They swell along the lashings, merge at the knots, and hang, and hang, and then let go as those long silent threads that fall and break apart before reaching anything.

From below, the knots look different. You see their undersides, the crossings, the tails. You see every unfinished tuck, hanging down like a small beckoning finger.

Something lives here. You will not see it the first time you look. You might see it the hundredth time, if you are lucky, or if the Wicker decides you are ready. What you see first is not a figure. It is a row of hanging water-beads that fails to fall. They hang there, full and trembling, longer than they should. Then one of them, very slowly, rises. It rolls back up into the bark as if the bark had drawn a breath.

Then you see her.

## IV. Her

She stands on the underside of the strand the way you stand on its top. Her feet are flat to the wet bark and her head points down toward the warm dark. She does not hang. She is not climbing. For her, the strand is the floor, and the lowland no one has seen is her sky.

She is pale, as the undersides are pale. The makers are the grey-green of the upper bark, and she is the color of the bark where the lashings have just been eased: new-looking, slightly raw, the color of a healed place. She is taller than the makers and much thinner. Her hair does not fall. It drifts around her head the way weed drifts in slow water, and the drip-threads that fall past her bend toward it and slow down and sometimes stop.

Her hands are the strange part. They are exactly like a maker's hands, broad and long-fingered with flat worn thumbs, but reversed. If you put your right hand against her right hand, palm to palm through the thickness of a strand, the thumbs would be on the same side. She is the mirror of a maker, and she was mirrored in the one respect that matters.

She does not look at you. She is almost always turned toward a knot. When she does look at you, and she will once, the look is not unkind. It is the look of someone reading something you have written, and finding it about as good as they expected, and knowing what to do with it.

At first you think she is alone. The makers speak of her as one: *the other hand*, singular. But later you will be walking a strand in some far region, a week and a thousand crossings from where you saw her, and you will look over the edge and there she will be, turned toward a knot, unhurried. Either she is very fast, or the undersides follow different distances than the tops do, or there are as many of her as there are makers. Perhaps there is one for every hand that ties.

This is, again, not a question that has a fixed answer here.

## V. How She Works

She takes the unfinished tuck between her reversed thumb and finger and she draws it back.

Then she keeps going, slowly, if the knot needs it. She undoes the knot exactly in reverse, the last move first, then the one before, as though she were winding back the moment of its tying. You remember that knots remember. When you put your hand on an old lashing you feel a flicker of the one who tied it: their patience, their song, the ache of their fingers. She feels this too. She reads the knot backward, and as she eases each loop she takes some of that memory with her. The hurry goes out of it. The fear goes out of it. The bite of a hand that pulled too hard because it was angry goes out of it. What stays is the holding.

She does not undo good knots. She eases them, a finger's width, so the stem can grow. It is the tight ones she takes apart, the ones tied in terror, the ones that would girdle and kill. She does not destroy them. She unties them, lays the strands back along the stem, and makes room for someone to tie there again, better.

Sometimes she does more than this, and this is where you begin to understand what she is.

The Wicker is mostly hole. You were told this at the beginning and took it as a fact about the weave, the way you take the spacing of threads in cloth as a fact. But holes do not simply happen in a thing that is always being tied. A structure that is only ever extended, only ever bound tighter, only ever added to, fills in. It becomes a board, then a block, then a solid dead mass of wood with no room for weather or breath.

The hollows are her work.

Over ages, patiently, from below, she lets whole regions go slack. She eases a thousand lashings a finger's width each, and the strands drift apart a little, and the rising air finds the gap and widens it. Clouds form in the new space. Rain begins. A hollow opens, and in its thermal columns the driftmoths rise, and in its rain the hollow-whales move, pale as she is. The makers say the hollow-whales are *where the Wicker keeps its breath*. They do not say who made the room for breathing. They do not need to. Every maker leaves the last tuck undone.

## VI. What Happens to the Cord

When she takes a knot apart, she does not keep the cord.

She unlays it. She untwists the ply back into strands, the strands back into fiber, the fiber back into loose soft bast, the way a maker would if you ran the spinning backward. She holds it out into the drip-threads, and the water carries it down in fine pale wisps into the mesh below. There it catches, the way everything catches.

The cratch pull it into their furrows and it rots into the moss. The weaverbirds pick it out of the air and carry it to their hanging nests, and the nests become part of the mesh, and tendrils grow through them. Tendrils reach across the gaps and are caught and tied off, extending the weave. The makers strip bast from the young growth and spin it, thumb-thick, on their gourd-whorl spindles.

The cord in your own bundle, the cord you learned to splice with, was a knot once. It may have been an angry knot. It has been eased and unlaid and let fall and caught and grown and stripped and spun, and now it is yours, and it is clean.

Nothing falls out. You already knew this. You did not know that there was someone whose whole work was to make sure of it.

## VII. What She Is, When She Is Most Herself

She is the under.

Every weave is over-under. You were told that too, that the pattern repeats at every scale. The makers are the over: the strand laid across, the loop pulled tight, the reaching tendril caught and bound. They are the hand that adds.

But no one can weave with one hand. Any basket-maker will tell you so. One hand passes the strand over. The other holds the under open so the strand can pass, and keeps the tension from becoming strangling, and lets go at the right moment so the work can breathe. The second hand is quieter. It is almost never looked at, and nothing gets made without it.

A basket with no give splits the first time it is loaded. The Wicker holds itself up by tension, by every strand pressing against every other, and tension without slack is only the moment before something breaks. The long low creak you sometimes hear, the whole world settling a fraction deeper into its weave, is the sound of her easing it. It is the Wicker taking a breath it was allowed to take.

She is the only one who faces downward. The makers look along their strands, at the next knot, at the work. She looks always toward the warm damp dark that breathes up from below, where perhaps there is a lowland, or perhaps there is only more Wicker curving the other way, or perhaps the bottom is the top. If anyone knows what is down there, she does. She has never said. She does not speak, or if she does, it is the way she unties: backward, from the last word to the first, and by the time she reaches the beginning there is nothing left to hear, only a kind of loosening in your chest.

The makers are not afraid of her. When a maker walks to the old knot-mass to finish, when they sit down in the blue gully and put their hands in their lap, she is there on the underside of the knot, below them, turned toward them. She takes the last tuck of their last knot between her thumb and finger and draws it gently back, and something in them that has been holding very tight for a very long time is allowed to let go.

The humming in the knot does not stop. It eases.

## VIII. Your Knots

You will find your own knots. You were told that too. Walking some remote strand, you will come upon a lashing tied exactly as you tie, with your over-tightened second loop and your habitual tuck, old and half-swallowed by bark.

Here is the part nobody told you. You will also find places where your knots were.

You will know them by the bark: a seam of pale, healed, relieved wood where a cord once bit too deep, and the unmistakable ghost of that second loop, pulled too hard, as you always pull it. The cord is gone. It has been eased, taken apart, unlaid, and let fall, and the stem above it is green and thick and alive, because someone came from below and made room.

You will feel ashamed at first, standing there with your hand on the seam. Then you will feel something else, and it will take you a long time to name. It is the feeling of having been forgiven for something before you did it.

The makers will not be surprised when you tell them. *Of course,* they will say. *You've been working on it.* And you will understand at last that they meant both hands.

## IX. The Splice

On one of the old knots, in a gully blue with fungal light, a maker once showed a traveler how to splice a fraying cord. Unlay the ends. Marry the strands. Tuck each under its neighbor. Roll it tight against the thigh.

The traveler did it clumsily, and pulled far too hard at the last tuck, the way frightened people do.

What the traveler did not see was this. As they pulled, something pulled back, very slightly, from the far side of the strand, through the thickness of living wood. It was a second pressure, reversed, holding the under open so the strand could pass. When the traveler let go, the splice was a finger's width looser than they had made it.

The maker nodded and said it would hold.

Many strands later and very far away, the traveler found it again: grey with age, grown over with moss, holding. It held because it had been given exactly enough slack to live.
