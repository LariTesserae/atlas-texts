# Who Keeps the Seam

## Not the Toad

You would guess the toad first. It is the largest thing in the room and it holds the warmest place, the flat ledge by the flange joint. Its throat keeps the pipe's time, and everything brought by the pulse ends up, sooner or later, within reach of its tongue. It looks like a foreman: heavy, old, settled into its post, watching the gap.

But watch the toad through a hundred cycles and something is wrong with that reading. Its throat does not *lead* the pulse. It follows it, a fraction late, the way an old man's foot taps a beat he heard a moment ago. When the iron ticks, the toad's eyes go half-shut with something like relief. It is not in charge. It is being run.

The toad is a component. Put plainly, it is a boiler: a warm, slow-burning mass that takes in fuel and gives off nothing but heat and the occasional dry pellet of chitin that the springtails dismantle within the hour. It fell in. It was needed. It was fitted. It will be replaced, when the time comes, by whatever falls in next and is large enough.

So it is not the toad.

## The Reader

Look instead at the gauge.

The dial is clouded, but not evenly. There is a clear arc just above the numbers where something has wiped the inside of the glass clean, over and over, the way a thumb keeps one spot of a window clear. Along that arc lie the eggs, tiny and white, one at each graduation mark.

What laid them lives behind the glass. It is a mite, and not one of the ordinary floor mites. It is long-bodied and pale, almost as clear as the glass itself, with two forelegs held up and forward, never used for walking. It lives on the needle. During each pulse, as the needle creeps up the scale, the mite rides it, and at the top of the swing it touches one foreleg to the glass at the exact mark the needle reached. Then it rides the needle back down.

It is taking readings.

The eggs are not a clutch. They are a record. Where the needle peaks, the reader lays. Over months the eggs form a line, and the line is a graph: the pressure of the pipe, written in the only ink the reader has. When the eggs hatch, the young do not scatter. They climb the needle and take up the count where the parent left off. The parent does not die. It retires to the rim of the dial and thins, over a season, into a clear husk lodged against the brass, and the husks are packed in there like old ledgers on a shelf.

The reader is closer to the truth than the toad. But a clerk is not the one who gives orders. Somebody reads the readings.

## The Insulator

The green-glass insulator sits upright in the liverwort and holds a thimbleful of water. It is the only standing water in the pocket, the one place where the eleven-minute rain does not simply soak away. Every drop that falls into it stays.

Something lives in the water.

At first it looks like nothing: a faint thickening in the green, a flaw in the glass. Hold still and let the pulse come round, and you see it move. It is the size of a grain of barley and nearly transparent, with a body like a water flea's, a small hinged carapace, and inside it, suspended, one speck of rust-orange. The speck is a gear. It is a single oxide disc with a notched rim, no bigger than a pinhead, taken from the blooms on the pipe. It sits where a heart would sit.

It does not keep time with the pulse. That is the first thing that sets it apart. Every other living thing in the room rises when the iron ticks. This one rises *before*.

A moment before the pipe breathes, the thing in the insulator lifts to the meniscus and presses its back against the surface of the water. The skin of the water flexes. The liverwort around the glass shivers very slightly. The woodlice nearest the insulator go still, and the stillness spreads outward across the wall, row by row, from the liverwort to the far corner, like a hush moving through a crowd. Then the iron ticks.

You were told the woodlice go still just before the pipe breathes. They do. But they are not listening to the pipe. They are watching the insulator.

## The Engineer

Once you know where to look, you see its hand in everything.

The snails' long, slow lines along the iron all run one way. Follow them and they end at the liverwort. That is where the rivets go. Around the base of the insulator, sunk into the dark mat, lies a ring of rivet-heads, domed and blackened, set in mucus and mineral as neatly as cobbles. Each snail that carries one leaves it there and goes back to grazing gears, a little lighter. Nobody knows what the rivets were for. Now you know where they are going. They are the footings of something.

The crickets hang in tiers, graded by size, and the tiers are not arranged around the pipe. They are arranged around a point just above the insulator, a focus in the air where all their antennae converge when they sweep. The upper room is a mesh of fine threads, and the mesh has a centre, and the centre is directly over the glass. It is a sensing web, and all of it reports to one place.

The ratchet-needles on the ceiling grow one tooth per cycle. Count along any needle and you get a number of pulses. Count along all of them and you notice that the needles over the insulator have more teeth than the rest, not because they are older, but because they began at the same moment the thing in the glass began. The ceiling is a calendar with the insulator's birthday at its centre.

It is not the foreman. The toad was not the foreman either. There is no foreman here, because this is not a crew.

The thing in the insulator is the engineer. It is the one who knows how the machine goes. It does not do any of the work. It does something harder: it keeps the work *coherent*. Ten thousand woodlice face one way because it faces the other way and they face it. The rhythm the pocket keeps does not come up from the pipe to the creatures. It goes out from the glass, through the creatures, to the pipe.

## The Pipe, Reconsidered

It is worth asking again what feeds the pipe.

It runs east under the town toward nothing still standing. It has no source anyone can name. It is cast iron and lagging and a split flange, and it should have gone cold in the year the works came down.

Here is a possibility. It is not the only one, but it fits.

When the thing in the insulator lifts against the meniscus, the woodlice rise on their legs, all of them at once, across the whole lower half of the room. That is ten thousand small bodies lifting together, on warm clinker, pressed against iron. It makes a sound like paper drawn in on a breath. And a moment later the pipe breathes out.

Perhaps the pipe does not drive the pocket. Perhaps the pocket works the pipe the way a stoker works a boiler, or the way a heart's small electrical flicker comes before the heavy muscle does anything at all. The engineer signals. The crew lifts. Somewhere under the town, far to the east, something takes the lift as its cue and sends back heat.

It would mean the pocket is not something that grew up beside a machine. It would mean the pocket is the machine's control room, and the machine is very large, and most of it is somewhere else.

## What It Is Becoming

It is growing.

A season ago it was the size of a mustard seed. Now it is the size of barley. Its carapace has thickened, and the thickening is not chitin. It is lime, laid down from the concrete-water it lives in, one thin layer per pulse, so that under a lens its shell shows the same fine teeth as the needles on the ceiling. It is building itself on the same ratchet as the room.

The gear in its chest has company now. There is a second disc, smaller, meshed against the first. When the engineer rises to the meniscus, the two turn against each other by one notch. You can see it happen if the lamp is bright. It is counting, too. It is counting on its own gears, the only thing in the pocket that carries its clock inside itself instead of taking it from the iron.

And the insulator is getting crowded. The thimble of water is barely enough. The engineer lies curled against the glass with less room to turn, and the ring of rivets around the base has grown into a second ring, a low wall, a foundation twice the width of the insulator. A foundation is laid for something that has not been built yet.

The seam goes on opening, a hair's width a season. Every year there is more room, and every year it fills at once, as though the space were already spoken for.

It is. The engineer is spoken for. It is laying out its own floor plan and waiting for the room to be big enough to hold it.

## The Works

Here is the last layer, the one underneath the others.

There was a works on this yard. Men stood at gauges and read them. Men turned valve wheels by hand. Men fitted rivets. Men stood in rows facing the machine, and when the whistle went they all lifted together, and the works ran for years on that lift, on thousands of small bodies keeping one time. Then it closed. The roof came off. The men went. The brass tags that hung on the board by the door, one per man and one per shift, were scattered or scrapped.

One is here. It is stamped *4*, over a letter too worn to read. It is being worked slowly deeper into the floor by traffic, and every one of the snail-lines, every row of woodlice, every drift of moth-dust curves very slightly around it. They do not walk on the number. They step around it, the way you step around a name cut into a church floor.

The works did not die. It was only emptied. A works is not its roof or its walls. It is the habit of many small bodies keeping one time around a source of heat, and a habit that deep does not simply stop. It sank. It went down through the clinker and the slag to where the iron was still warm, and it waited. When the ground shrugged and the slabs parted and a little room opened in the dark, it did what a works does. It hired.

It took on woodlice by the thousand, because it needed a floor crew. It took on crickets for the upper gantry, snails for fitting and hauling, a reader for the gauges, and a toad for the boiler. It filled every post it had with what fell in, the way it always had. And last, it made itself an engineer. It did not find one. It *made* one, out of lime and rust and the rhythm of the pipe, grown in the one vessel in the room that could hold still water. It made itself someone to keep the time, since there was no longer anyone to blow the whistle.

That is who lives in the seam. The woodlice, the crickets, the snails and the toad live *in* it, the way a crew lives in its shift. What *lives there* is the works, in the old sense in which a place is inhabited by the thing it keeps doing. The engineer in the insulator is its mind, or the part of it closest to a mind: a small clear body with two gears for a heart, rising a moment before each breath to tell ten thousand others that it is time.

## The Vacancy

There is one more thing, and it is the gentlest.

At midday, when the blade of sunlight comes down through the gap and crosses the floor, the woodlice part for it. They leave a bare strip exactly the width of the light, and the strip travels slowly across the room from the fern to the toad's ledge.

It is the only unfilled space in the pocket. It moves, so nothing can fill it.

On each pass it crosses the brass tag. For a moment the light lies on the worn *4*, and every creature in the room holds its place around that lit rectangle of clinker, and the engineer in the green glass turns, the only time in the day that it does, to face it.

It is a post being kept open. The works is still short a hand. It knows, in whatever way a works knows anything, that the things it hires are only standing in, and that the ones it was built around are gone. It does not grieve. It is not built to grieve. It keeps the place. It keeps the heat on and the time right, and every eleven minutes it sends a thread of warm breath up through the crack into the yard, where on cool mornings you can see it rise.

You would have to fall in, or be small. The seam is widening, a hair a season, and the works is patient, and punctual, and warm.
