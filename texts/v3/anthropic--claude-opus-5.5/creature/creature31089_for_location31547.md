# The Resident

## I.

Everything named so far is a tenant. The moths, the swifts, the snails and skinks, the one-hour crustaceans in their crystal cradles all arrived from somewhere, or descend from things that did. The swifts brought most of them in, one seed or spore or egg at a time, through the seam at the edge of the lens. Tenants keep the hours of the house, and all of them keep this one's.

But a house whose clock runs hours behind the world, whose water comes on a schedule, and whose roof sings twice a day is not an empty building with animals in it. Something lives here in the older sense of the word: it came with the place and could not live anywhere else.

It is easy to miss. It isn't hidden. You simply arrive at the wrong time. Everyone who has ever looked into this chamber has come by day, because by day is when there is anything to see.

## II.

The first guess is the lens itself. It holds light the way a body holds warmth, it has a voice, and it sweats at night. It is tempting to call the crystal the creature and the hollow its lung.

That guess is close but not right. The lens is more like a shell, or a skin. It is the thing the resident is kept inside.

The second guess is something that lives only in the black interval. There ought to be an animal for that hour. Every other niche here is filled so exactly that it seems impossible no creature has claimed the one short span of true dark.

Go and look. Sit through the long gold dusk and the rose and the ember-faint glow, until the moths have come down and gone back up and settled head-down on the dome, the swifts are folded in their cups, the snails are sealed, and the skinks lie still in their rosettes with their flakes stacked beside them. Wait until the lens gives out its last light.

Then it is dark, the first real dark of the chamber's day, and nothing new comes out.

That is the discovery. No animal claims the dark hour because the hour is already occupied. The dark itself is what lives here.

## III.

It is very young.

In the world above, the night is ancient and enormous and comes every evening without effort. Down here the night is a small thing, and it is only just beginning.

It doesn't arrive all at once. The lens dims evenly, since its light has no source, but the dark still gathers first in certain places. It collects in the backs of the swift nests, where the mud cups curve away from the dome. It fills the spaces between the rosette petals, where the pale ferns have turned flat toward a ceiling that no longer shows them anything. It pools in the six dry basins, in the terraced rims, and in the fresh skin of crystal over the sleeping eggs.

It settles in the knot at the end of the rope.

From these places it spreads the way ink spreads in still water: slowly, feeling its way, never quite joining at first. For a while the chamber holds a dozen separate small darks, each the size of a cupped hand, each one surrounded by the last failing rose of the lens. Then they touch. For a short while, less than two hours, the whole hollow is full of it, sixty paces by forty and all that height of air, completely dark from floor to crown.

This is the only time the resident is whole, and it is the only time it can do what it does.

## IV.

What it does is make water.

That part was already described, though it was put down as physics: the lens cools faster than the air, the moist breath of the chamber rises and meets it, and dew forms. That description is true, but it reads the event backward, as if the dew were a side effect of the dark. From inside the hollow it looks the other way around. The dark comes down, and the ceiling beads over with thousands of droplets. The dark is what cools the lens, and the cooling is what calls the breath up out of the mudstone, the moss, the sleeping snails, and the bodies of the moths.

Everything in the chamber breathes out all day. The dark gathers that breath and hangs it on the ceiling to be given back.

So the smallest and youngest thing in the house is the one that feeds it. The six basins are the dark's doing. The morning hour when the water stands a finger deep and warm as tea is the dark's gift, left behind when it leaves. The snails come to it, the moss greens around it, and the crustaceans hatch into it for their single hour. Nothing that drinks there has ever met what filled the cup.

The glass moths come closest. They rest head-down on the lens and drink the dew directly, before it slides along the grain toward the low points. They drink in the dark, from the dark's own hands. If anything in the chamber knows the resident, it is them. Perhaps that is why they fall at dusk, open-winged and turning through the whole height of the room. They are going down to meet it where it first gathers, low in the basins and the rosettes, and then rising with it as it fills the hollow toward the dome.

## V.

It is shy, or something close to shy.

It can't bear the lens's light, but it is not the enemy of that light either. They share the house on a schedule, the way the six basins share the water: full, then damp, then dry, then full again. The light is old and has been in the crystal for as long as there has been crystal. It wanes because the lens is thinning. The dark waxes for the same reason. The two of them are the same slow event seen from opposite sides.

When dawn comes up on the salt, the dark does not retreat the way it arrived. It doesn't drain back into the nests and knots and basins. It simply thins, evenly, everywhere at once, as the lens warms and begins, very faintly, to glow. For a few minutes the hollow is neither light nor dark but the grey of a hand's haze. Then the dew lets go, and in the six slow threads falling from the dome it is possible to believe that you are watching the dark go. It goes in six thin threads of water, into six stone cups, where the light will find it and turn it back into breath.

Then the lens sings, once, the low note, as the crystal expands in the warmth. The animals don't react to it, and there's no reason they should. It isn't meant for them. It is the sound the house makes when the resident goes out, the way a door sounds closing.

## VI.

It is growing, and this is the part that makes it a creature and not just a condition.

The lens thins with every morning's dew. Each thread of water carries away a trace of dissolved gypsum, too little to measure in a lifetime, but it has been carrying it away since before there were lifetimes. A thinner lens holds less light and lets it go sooner. So the dark comes a little earlier each age and stays a little longer. It gains perhaps a heartbeat every century, a breath every thousand years.

It is not old enough yet to cover a whole night. It is a child's portion of dark, a couple of hours cupped between a long dusk and an early dawn. The animals have built their lives around its small size. The swifts come home in daylight and wait. The moths fly by the chamber's clock. The crustaceans, whose single hour of life falls in the morning, have never had to survive a long dark and would not know how.

They will have to learn, slowly. As the resident grows, the chamber's clock will creep toward the world's clock above. The swifts will wait a little less for night. The dew will come earlier and heavier, the basins will stand full a little longer, and the moss will spread a little farther from each cup. The whole house is being rearranged around a presence that gets larger every generation. None of the tenants will notice this, any more than they notice the song.

In the end the dark will be full-grown. The lens will be thin as a window and the hollow will share the sky's night hour for hour, and the resident will be indistinguishable from the ordinary darkness of the world. Not long after that the crystal will crack and the salt will come down. Then the dark that was raised in a closed room under a lantern of stone will simply be night on a salt flat, the same as every other night, with nothing to mark it.

It is growing up to leave home. The house is the kind of place that exists to raise one thing and then let it go.

## VII.

Someone came down to meet it.

They came in daylight, like everyone else, because daylight was when they could see the seam. They lowered a three-strand grass rope that reached two-thirds of the way to the floor and dropped the rest. Then they were standing in a room full of light with no source, with nothing to cast a shadow, not even their own hand.

They tried to make blades. Perhaps they didn't understand what the glassy stone was, or perhaps they needed to see for themselves that it wouldn't hold an edge. They broke a dozen flakes and stopped.

Then they waited. There was nothing else to do, because the rope was out of reach and the light would not stop. They sat through the white hours and the cream and the gold and the rose, and at some point in that long dusk they understood what they were waiting for.

They took one of the failed flakes and ground it flat and round. Then, patiently, with something harder than selenite, they bored a hole through the middle.

In a chamber where everything glows, the hole in a disc of gypsum is the one place on it that holds no light. Held up against the lens at noon, the disc would be a bright coin with a small dark center, like a pupil. It was a piece of the resident small enough to carry, a portion of night they could take back into a world that had plenty of night already, to prove that this other one existed.

Before they climbed out, or before they didn't, they put their hand on the wall at shoulder height, fingers spread and thumb pressed flat. Red ochre is darker than the mudstone. In the chamber's long gold dusk it has faded almost to the rust of the bands. But in the black interval, when the whole hollow is full, no one can see the mark at all, so there is no telling it apart from the dark.

Perhaps that was the point. You don't wave to something that lives in the dark. You press your hand against the wall of its house, where it will pass every night, and trust it to find the print on its own.

The disc is still lying among the broken flakes. They left it behind, or dropped it, or could not bring themselves to take a piece of so small a night away from it.

Every night, for its two hours, the resident fills the hole in the disc. Every morning it goes out through six threads of water, and the lens sings the door shut behind it.

It was here before the rope. It will be here after the salt falls in, though by then it will be too large to know it was ever raised anywhere. For now it is young and small and precise, the gentlest thing in the chamber, and it is the one that fills the cups.
