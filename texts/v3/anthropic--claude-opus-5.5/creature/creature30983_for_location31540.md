# Those Who Live in the Lee

You will mistake the first one for a calf.

It happens at the edge of a resting herd, in the hour when the grey is going to slate. Among the tall white pillars of breath there is one that is wrong. It is too thin and too low, and it breaks up at head height instead of climbing to the overcast. It comes and goes in quick small puffs, far faster than the slow bellows-breathing of the stacks. You assume a young beast, or a sick one.

Then the small pillar stands up and walks around to the other side of its animal, and you see that it goes on two legs.

---

## The People of the Lee

The lowlanders who know of them at all call them *leeward folk*, or just *the Lee*, because that is where you find them: on the downwind side of a kiln-beast, in the pocket of warm, still air the animal's body makes in the cold. They have no name for themselves that they will tell an outsider. When asked, they tend to press a palm flat against the nearest beast, as if that were an answer. It probably is.

They are small, shorter than lowlanders and broad through the chest, with deep barrel ribs built for air too thin to satisfy anyone raised below. Their hands are large and their fingers thick, the skin of the palms very fine. Their eyes are very large and pale, grey or nearly colorless, made for a world where midday is dusk. In true lowland daylight they would be half blind. Here they see things you cannot. They can tell a tarn from a patch of rime at five hundred paces. They can tell a boulder from a hill.

Their skin is hard to describe, because you never see it clean. The slag-powder of the trails gets into everything, and the Lee are stained with the road they walk. A child raised on a rust-red trail is rust-colored at the knuckles, the elbows, and the creases of the neck. One who has walked a sulfur line carries a yellow tinge in the lines of her palms for years. Their faces are dusted grey-white each dawn with the powder of settled slag-moths. They look like people made of the plateau's own materials, and in time you will learn that this is less a metaphor than you thought.

Their hearing is terrifying. In the silence of the steppe they hear what you only learned to hear in your first sleepless days: blood, jaw, breath. They hear yours from a long way off. Long before you see them, they know you are there. You are loud. Your heart is a drum to them.

## Borrowed Heat

There is no fire on the Kiln Steppe.

There is nothing to burn. There is no wood. There is no dung that isn't stone. The dead become gardens instead of fuel, and the lichen is too slow and too sacred to strip. Every warmth on the plateau is animal warmth, and the Lee have none to spare. So they borrow it.

A kiln-beast's flank is their hearth, their bed, and their house. They sleep curled against the resting stacks in the lee of the wind, whole families laid along an animal's belly like piglets against a sow, while the great slow breath goes up above them into the dark. The beasts permit this the way they permit the tender-birds and the brass-lice. Perhaps they do not notice. Perhaps a creature that burns stone from the inside has no reason to begrudge a little of its heat to something small.

The Lee word for *home* is, as near as a lowlander can render it, *the side of the animal*. It is a place that moves. When the herd moves, home moves. You do not go back to it. You keep up with it.

Everything about them follows from this. They cannot linger. They cannot store warmth or carry it or trade it. They cannot fall more than a day behind a herd and survive the night. Their whole life is a matter of staying close to heat that is walking away.

## What They Eat

The plateau feeds them, though grudgingly and strangely.

At dawn, before the grey fully rises, they go out onto the rime to harvest the settled slag-moths. They sweep them with fans made of the ribbed hide of the dead, gather them into bags, and press them into flat grey cakes that taste of iron and faintly of smoke that was never there. They eat these cold. Everything is eaten cold.

They catch tarn-eels, and this is dangerous. The eels discharge when the frost is thick, and a Lee fisher who reaches into a tarn at the wrong hour can be thrown backward with her arm dead for a week, or not get up at all. They fish with lines of copper drawn from the lace of old eels, and with hooks cut from the alloy teeth of dead kiln-beasts. The meat is dense, pale, slightly metallic, and it makes the tongue tingle.

They crack the brass-lice from the beasts' scale and eat them like nuts. They strip seed from the sparse grey grass that grows on the dead. And they lick the slag.

This is the thing lowlanders find hardest to watch. A Lee child will kneel on the newest part of a trail, where the powder is fine and bright, and lick it off her fingers. Adults carry pinches of it in small pouches and take it the way you might take salt. Their bodies want the iron, the copper, and the sulfur. Their blood, when you see it, is so dark it is nearly black. The eldest among them have teeth gone faintly bronze at the tips.

## Water

They collect rime at night on cloths of scraped hide stretched over frames. In the grey middle of the day, when the frost has softened to sheen, they wring the cloths into flasks carved from hollow beast-bone. A family's supply of water is measured in cloths. A careless person, one who lets a cloth sink into the stone at evening, is said to have *let the jar drink*. The phrase means waste, and it means grief.

## Speech

The Lee speak very little, and very quietly. In the thin air a whisper carries badly, and a shout is obscene. It scatters the tender-birds, and some say it startles the herd-pulses.

Mostly they speak by touch. Two Lee meeting will each press a palm against the other's, or against the bare skin of the throat, and hold it there. What passes between them is temperature: warmth and coolness, and small changes in each. A hand that is warmer than the other's means *I have eaten, I can share.* A hand that stays cool means *I am tired, I am afraid, I am behind.* A hand that warms slowly while held means something lowlanders can never quite get them to translate. The nearest they manage is *I remember you.*

They have learned, over a very long time, to read the herds.

The slow red waves that pass across the flanks of the stacks, and travel through a resting herd like rumor through a crowd, move faster than any person can walk. On a clear night of deep iron-dark, a Lee elder will climb a swell and watch the far glows for hours. She is reading. The pulses carry news across the plateau: a region where the sky has thickened and gone too cold, a tarn gone dry, a place where many are dying. The Lee do not claim to understand what the beasts are saying to each other. They claim only to understand some of what the beasts are saying *about the world*. The herd-glow is their weather, their map, and their gossip.

When they touch hands in the dark, they are imitating it. They know they are. They say they learned to talk from the stacks.

## The Writing

The Lee say the honeycomb is writing.

Every polygon of the basalt is a sign, they say, and the seams between them are the strokes, and the whole plateau is a single text written before anything walked on it. The kiln-beasts are reading it. They read the way the Lee eat moth-cake, by taking it into themselves, grinding it down, and turning it into heat and metal and breath. Each generation of stacks reads a little more of the text into nothing. The plateau is lower than it was. When the beasts have read the whole thing, the Lee say, the steppe will be flat and smooth as a tarn, and then something will happen. They do not agree on what.

Their children learn to *walk sentences*. A Lee child is taught a path through the polygons, step here, then here, along this seam and across that one, and the path is a phrase. The phrases are old. Some are prayers and some are directions. Some are names. A grown Lee woman can walk her own name into the stone in a hundred paces, and does, at certain moments of her life: when she is grown, when she bears a child, when she is dying. It leaves no mark. The rime takes her footprints by morning. The point is not that it lasts.

On the whole of the Kiln Steppe, nothing was built. The Lee are careful to keep it that way. They make no cairns and dig no hearths, and they walk their writing instead of carving it. They are the only creatures on the plateau that could leave a lasting mark, and they have chosen, deliberately and as a people, to leave none. Everything they have is carried or eaten or given back.

## The Loop

A kiln-beast takes two hundred years to walk the great migration once. A Lee lives perhaps sixty.

No one of them sees the whole loop. A person is born on one stretch of the trail, walks some arc of it at the herd's slow pace, and dies on another stretch, perhaps a third of the way around the world from where she began. Her children carry on. Her grandchildren's grandchildren will pass the place of her birth again, if the herd holds its course. The route shifts only a few feet a century.

So the Lee measure their lives not in years but in *arcs*: how much of the loop you have walked. And they navigate by the dead.

A fallen kiln-beast becomes a garden that lasts a hundred years. The Lee know every one on their stretch by name. They know when it fell, who mourned it, and what color its lichens came in. The dead are their calendar and their milestones. A Lee elder, coming over a swell, will point to a pale orange patch against the grey and say, *That is Long-Shoulder. My mother's mother sat with her.* Then she will add, *We are early this time*, or *We are late.*

When a Lee dies, the body is carried to the nearest of the beast-gardens and laid inside the ribs, among the metal-laced girders of bone, under the crusts of bone-white and orange. The grey grass roots in her. The lichen takes her slowly. In fifty years, or a hundred, her descendants will walk past that garden on the next turn of the loop, and it will be her they point to.

## Grief

When a kiln-beast dies, the Lee who slept against it do not leave.

They stay with the body while it cools. It takes days, and sometimes more than a week, for so much furnace to go out. The family lies against the dead flank exactly as they lay against the living one, taking its last heat. The herd goes on without them, and every hour they stay they fall further behind. This is dangerous. Some do not catch up.

But the rule is absolute, and older than anything else they will tell you: *you stay as long as it is warm.* When the flank is cold under the palm, the mourning is over. They stand, walk one sentence into the stone, and go after the herd.

For the Lee, grief lasts exactly as long as the heat does. This is not coldness. It is the most precise thing they know.

## Catching

The strange part comes later. Most lowlanders never stay long enough to see it.

Sometimes, rarely, an old Lee's hands begin to run warm. It is not the warmth of fever. It is steady, a little more each year, as if something inside had caught. They call it exactly that: *catching*. An elder who has caught can melt rime by laying her palm on it. On the darkest nights, if you are close and your eyes have adjusted, you might see her skin flush along the throat and forearms, very faint, the dull red of a stove seen through a crack in the door. Then it fades. Then, slowly, it comes again.

The Lee do not fear this. They go quiet around it, the way people go quiet near something holy.

They have a story that they tell only in whispers and only by touch, hand to throat, warmth rising and falling in the rhythm of the herd-pulse. The story says that the first kiln-beasts were not always beasts. They were small once, and walked on two legs, and slept in the lee of something larger. They followed it long enough, ate its leavings and licked its trails and learned its language of heat, and one day they caught.

Whether the story is true, no one can say. The Lee do not insist on it. But when an elder has caught, the others bring her to sleep at the very center of the family, where the children lie. And when she dies, they stay with her as long as she is warm, just as they would for a stack. It takes longer than it should.

## What Lives Here

So something does live on the Kiln Steppe that is not in the first description. It is not only the Lee, though they are part of it. The longer you watch, the more the picture shifts.

What lives on the Kiln Steppe is warmth, moving slowly.

Warmth wakes in the stone when a kiln-beast grinds it. It burns there for two hundred years. It pours out of the animal as breath and becomes the sky. It pours out as glow and becomes language. It leaks from the flank into the sleeping bodies of the Lee and keeps them alive another night. When the beast dies, warmth lingers in the ribs for a week, holding a family in place with the one rule they cannot break. Then it passes into the lichen, which spends it so slowly that a single ring outlasts the histories of the lowlands. It goes into the moths that rise at dusk and the eels that store it as light. Now and then, very rarely, it catches in an old woman's hands.

It is a single thing, very old and very patient, and every creature on the plateau is a room it is passing through. The Lee understand this better than anyone, and it is why they build nothing. You do not build a house for something that is walking. You keep up with it, and when it goes out you let it go, and you walk after it.

Their only prayer, as near as it can be translated, is two words pressed palm to palm: *keep moving*.

## And You

They will find you, eventually. They have heard your heartbeat for days.

One evening, as the grey goes to slate and the moths begin to rise, a Lee will come out of the lee of her animal and cross the rime toward you. She will not speak. She will lift one of her large, fine-skinned hands and lay it flat against your throat, and hold it there.

You are hot, far hotter than they are. You were raised in thick air and fed on fire-cooked food, and you burn like a small stove. She will feel it. You will see her pale eyes widen in the dimness.

Then, very likely, she will bring her children. They will lie down against you in the lee of the wind, the way they would against a stack, and sleep. Their small quick breaths will go up in thin puffs beside yours. You will lie awake all night under the weight of them, hearing their blood, while across the dark the herds pulse slowly like banked coals.

By morning they will be gone, back to the side of the animal, back onto the trail. Your face will be dusted grey with settled moths. Nothing will be written in the stone.

Nothing here needs you. That is still true. But for one night you were a hearth, and on the Kiln Steppe there is no higher thing to be.
