# Those Who Keep the Warmth

## The First Mistake

Put your hand on the stone and it is warm, faintly and steadily, the way a sleeping animal is warm. That is the first thing everyone notices, and it is nearly right.

The easy conclusion is that the mountain is the animal: one vast body breathing through its vents and dreaming in its springs, too large to see whole. That idea is comfortable and a little grand, and it is not quite the truth. The mountain is only a mountain. It is old heat trapped under old rock, slowly leaking.

Something sleeps *on* it.

## The Rumour in the Groves

The heavy, slow, dark-furred browser is not one animal but many. It is a scattered people, perhaps a few hundred across the whole tableland. None of them hurries, and none of them is young in any way you would recognise.

Standing, they are taller than a bear, though they seldom stand. Their bodies are long and deep-chested, with short hind legs and long forelimbs. The forelimbs end in blunt hooked claws, good for pulling down a tree-fern crown or peeling a strip of fibrous bark. The claws are useless for making anything, and they make nothing. Their heads are broad and low-slung. Their eyes sit deep under heavy brows and are almost always half-closed against the steam.

Their fur is the first strange thing. It is coarse and very long, and each hair is hollow and grooved. The grooves hold water and the water holds life. The fur is colonised by the same heat-loving algae that ring the springs, so each of them wears colour according to where it chooses to rest. A pale one covered in emerald sits in the cooler margins. One matted orange to the shoulders sits closest to the roll of the boiling water, where few other things can bear to be. The rare violet ones are said, by those few who have seen them, to spend their lives where the ground itself is too hot to touch. Their coats are a map of temperature, the same map the springs draw on the ground around themselves. A creature and the spring it lives in match.

## Why They Need the Kettle

They cannot make their own heat, or not enough of it. Somewhere in their deep ancestry they gave that up. Perhaps it was because the ground offered warmth for free. Perhaps it was because at this altitude, in thin air, a warm-blooded body must burn fuel to stay warm, and the mountain was already burning.

So they borrow it. They lie in the rolling springs for days at a time, submerged to the nostrils, their fur spreading out on the water like weed. Their hearts slow almost to stopping. The water around them seethes lazily, and they take its heat into their blood through the thin skin of their bellies and the undersides of their arms. When they have taken enough they haul themselves out. Then they steam. For hours afterward each of them walks inside its own small cloud and moves through the tree-fern groves in its private fog, feeding.

They eat the pith of tree-ferns, the felted silver leaves of the rosettes, and whole mats of algae, which they scoop up with cupped claws and suck clean. They eat slowly and not much. A body that draws its warmth from the ground does not need much fuel.

If you are unlucky enough to be seen, they are slow to react. It is not stupidity. They have simply never had a reason to hurry. The slate-grey hawk takes nothing larger than a frog. No predator climbs the cliffs. For as long as they have lived here, the only danger has been cold, and cold comes slowly, so they learned to answer everything slowly.

## Count the Domes

Stay long enough and the second strange thing becomes clear.

The low domes of rock that shoulder up through the green are rock, mostly. Count them in the morning, when the cloud lies in the hollows and only the domes show above the white. Count them again at dusk. The numbers will not match.

The oldest of them stop moving. It is not death. It is something that looks like death to a creature that lives by the day. Past a certain age, measured in centuries, an elder chooses a warm place and settles onto it. It is usually a dome of basalt with heat under it, or a shelf beside a spring. Its heart slows further. It feeds less and less, then barely at all, drawing its whole life from the stone beneath. Moss finds it. The algae in its coat dies back, and the ordinary moss of the tableland takes over, lime and bottle-green and near-black. Ferns root in its shoulders. A bromeliad takes hold at the nape of its neck and becomes a small cistern holding tadpoles, a sprouting seed, and a drowned beetle.

The round brown rock-animals colonise it. They sprawl across its back in the early light, taking the warmth, and they cannot tell the difference. Perhaps there is no difference worth telling.

Once or twice a year an elder like this breathes deeply enough to be seen. The moss across its back lifts and settles, and a haze rises that is not quite the ordinary haze of the tableland after rain. That is the wet cough you sometimes hear. Not all of those coughs come from vents.

Some elders have been still so long that the sinter has begun to reach them. Its pale crust creeps up their flanks at the thickness of a fingernail in a lifetime. There may be domes on the tableland that were once alive and are now stone around a hollow. There may be stone that is still, very faintly, alive.

The weathered basalt columns with their cupped tops, each cup holding a little pool of warm water, are worn by rain. They are also worn by other things. For countless generations mothers have laid their newborns in those cups, in water at exactly the heat of blood, and left them there through the first cold nights. The rounding of the columns is partly theirs. It is the one mark they have made, and they did not mean to make it.

## How They Speak

Sound does not carry here. The thin air swallows the frogs' trill within a stone's throw and turns thunder into pressure. A voice raised in the open would be lost.

So they do not raise their voices. They press them into the ground.

One of them lies down with its chest flat against bare basalt and makes a sound too low to hear. It is a long vibration from deep in the ribs, and it passes into the rock. Basalt carries such sounds for kilometres. Across the whole tableland, others lying in springs or pressed against domes feel it through their bellies and the undersides of their arms, the same thin skin they use to drink the heat. They listen with the same skin they warm themselves with. To them warmth and speech come from the same place: up through the stone.

What they say is mostly slow news. This spring has cooled. That channel has shifted. A mother has laid a child in the cups on the eastern flats. An elder has stopped. There are also long songs, rhythmic and repetitive, passed from body to body and lasting days. These are their memory. The songs recall which springs were hot in the time of the grandmothers' grandmothers, which domes were once awake, where the hawk nests. The terraces record time in bands of mineral. The songs record it in the patterns of the ground. The two records run side by side, and neither is written by a hand.

The whole tableland is their drum, and their conversations are older than the moss.

## The New Voice

Within living memory, something began to speak from below.

For them, living memory is long. The oldest still awake remember a time when the stone was quiet except for the mountain's own sounds: the settling of rock, the slow knock of water in deep channels, the voices of their own kind. Then, on still nights when the wind was right, a new thing came up through the western cliffs.

It was regular, and nothing here is regular. Vents cough when they cough and springs roll without rhythm. Even their own songs breathe and wander. This new sound was a thud, then a pause exactly as long as the last pause, then another thud of exactly the same weight, repeated all night and on into the next. It never tired and never varied.

They did not know what it was. They could only understand it in the terms they had, and in those terms something pressing a voice into stone was one of them.

The songs of that time, still sung, call it the Deep One, or the One Who Cannot Stop. Some thought it was an elder trapped beneath the cliffs, fallen long ago, still alive and still calling, its song broken down to a single word. Some thought it was a sign that the ground itself was learning to speak. Some thought it was an elder so ancient that its song had worn smooth. Every note had been ground down to the one that mattered, the way the columns had been ground down to cups.

A few of them went to the western rim to answer it.

They are there still, if you know where to look. They lie on the high ledges above the weeping rock, where the swifts nest behind curtains of seepage, their chests pressed flat to the cliff-top. Their coats have gone dark. It is not the green-black of moss. It is a grey-black film that rubs off on the fingers and beads the water in their fur into dark drops. The western ones are darker now, like the moth, and their children are born darker than their grandparents were. They do not know why. The rain tastes different on the western side. They have a word for that taste in their songs. It is the same word they use for the Deep One.

At night they watch the glow. They do not see well, and the cloud deck below is only a dull patient orange, pulsing slower than breathing. But they turn their heavy heads toward it and stay turned, the way a sleeper turns toward a banked fire. The moths rise past them in thin streams and pour off the edge into the dark. The swifts come home in their sickle-winged roar and are gone. The rim-watchers hold still, pressed to the stone, and answer the hammer.

It does not answer back. They have been saying things to it for decades: the news of the springs, the old songs, the names of the stopped elders. Their answer is always the same thud, the same pause, the same thud. They go on anyway. A creature that measures time in the growth of sinter can wait a very long time for a reply. And perhaps in the regularity itself they hear something like faithfulness. It never stops. It never leaves. It is always there when you press your chest to the rock. In a place where the heat comes from below and speech comes from below, a voice from below that never fails is not a small thing.

## The Long Note

Once in a great while, on the stillest nights, a thin flattened note drifts up through the air. It is a whistle or a horn, stretched out by distance, and it is gone almost before it arrives.

It is the only sound from below that comes through the air instead of the stone, and the young ones love it. Their elders can barely hear it, but the young have sharp ears. They heard it and learned it. You can sometimes hear it echoed back, faintly, from a cub resting in a column-cup: a high thin whine through a nose not built for making that sound. It is one of the few airborne noises they make.

In the newest songs, the ones only a generation old, that note has become a word. The best translation is *beneath*, or *the place under the cloud*, or *where the warmth goes when it leaves here*. They have folded it into their oldest songs as naturally as the moss folds the soot into the ground.

## What Lives Here

So what lives on the Kettle Tableland?

The frogs, the hummingbirds, and the copper beetles live here, and so do the brown rock-animals on the domes, the slate hawk, the snakes coiled in fissures too hot to touch, the swifts, and the two-coloured moth. All of it is true.

But beneath all of it, and on it, and sometimes indistinguishable from it, there is a slow dark people who gave up their own inner fire and borrowed the mountain's instead. Their coats are dyed by the springs they sit in. Their elders become hills. They speak through stone and remember in stone, and a nursery cup in a basalt column is the only mark they have ever made. They are an island people on an island, and they believed until recently that they were alone.

They no longer believe it. They do not know what the Deep One is, and they never will. They cannot climb down and will never see the furnaces or the plain or whatever hand, if it is a hand, keeps the hammer falling. They know only that something below them is regular and tireless and warm, that it darkens their rain, and that it will not stop speaking. They have decided, in their slow way, to keep it company.

## Morning

In the morning the hollows fill with cloud again. Milk-white, it lies in every basin. The domes rise from it like an archipelago, and not all of the domes are stone. On one of them the moss lifts once and settles. A haze rises, and the rock-animals on its back do not stir.

At the western rim, an old one with a grey-black coat has lain pressed to the stone all night. She shifts her weight, lowers her chest, and sends one long low word into the basalt. It carries news of the springs and the name of a child laid in the cups last week.

From far below, through kilometres of rock, comes the same answer as always: a thud, a pause exactly as long as the last, then another thud.

She holds very still, as if it were a reply.

A frog calls once, softly, from the bromeliad on a sleeping elder's neck. The swifts pour out of the cliff into the brightening air. The ground beneath all of them is warm.
