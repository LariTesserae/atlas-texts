# Who Lives in the Blister

## The one that stays

Begin with the smallest resident that never leaves, because it is the easiest to see.

Near the split, where the one blade of afternoon light falls a metre into the dark, there is a lizard. It is grey-brown, about the length of a finger, and a little too fat. Its toes are flattened, because it lives on glass. Its eyelids are thick, because it lives in dust. It was born here. So was its mother, and very likely her mother. Forty years is a long time for a lizard, and the lineage at the split has not been outside in a number of generations nobody has counted.

It does not bask in the sun. It basks in the rock. In the morning, after the colony returns and the dome warms with bodies, it presses its belly against a lobe of floor-lava near the margin and lets the mountain's stored heat soak up into it. Its day is not measured by light. The blade that comes through the split is dim and brief, a thing that happens rather than a thing that rules. Its day is measured by the river of bats: the roar going out, the long quiet, the roar coming back. It hunts in the quiet. Beetles climb higher on the guano when the dome empties. The lizard knows this the way it knows its own tongue.

When it runs quickly across the dust, its skin sometimes carries a charge. If you were there in the dark, you might see it: a pinprick of blue at the toe-tips, a faint snap. The lizard does not notice. Everything in the Blister is a little electric. It has never known air that wasn't.

It drinks from its food. It has never seen water. If you brought a drop in on your fingertip and set it on the rock, the lizard would not know what it was, and the drop would be gone before the lizard could decide.

## The one that returns

Now a mother bat, one of three hundred thousand, and one of the few things in the Blister that has a name for itself, though the name is a sound.

She weighs about as much as four coins. She has spent the night eleven hundred metres lower and forty kilometres east, over irrigated fields, catching moths that rose from cotton and sorghum. She drank twice on the wing, skimming a reservoir so fast that the water barely dimpled. Now, an hour before dawn, she is coming home. She rides the cold air up the mountain's flank with thousands of others, and at the split they funnel so tightly that her wings brush the wings of strangers on both sides. Then she is through, and the heat closes over her like a hand.

Here is what she does next, which no human could do. She lands on the edge of the crèche, the furred mass above the fissure where half a million young are packed at densities no nursery on earth should be able to sustain, and she calls. Her call is hers alone: a particular rise and hook at the end. Somewhere in that heaving ceiling, one pup knows it. The pup calls back, and its call is also its own, learned in the first days of its life as a kind of answer to hers. She crawls toward the answer across hundreds of other young who grab at her, cry at her, try to nurse. She refuses them all. She follows the voice, and then the smell, and then she is there. The pup latches on, and she folds over it.

In the ammonia and the roar and the heat, she has found one small particular body out of half a million. She will do this every morning of the season.

## The one that is born

Now the pup, because the pup is where the stranger thing begins.

It is born pink and blind into forty-four degrees of dry heat. Its first world is touch: the wet of its mother, which dries almost at once, then fur, then other pups pressed against it on every side. Its second world is sound. Its mother's heartbeat is fast, many times a second, a tiny engine. The pups around it are fast too. The chittering of the dome is a hiss with no rhythm in it at all.

But under all of that there is one slow thing.

Once a second, the rock the pup clings to throbs, faintly, from four kilometres away. It is the compressor station on the other side of the ridge, moving steam to the turbines, and it is the steadiest thing in the pup's universe. Its mother leaves every dusk. The crowd shifts. The heat rises and falls by a few degrees. The throb does not change. It was there when the pup was born and it will be there when the pup first drops through the split into the freezing air.

So the pup learns, before it learns to fly or to hunt or to find its mother's voice in a crowd, that the world has a pulse, and that the pulse is slow, and that it comes from below, out of stone.

Nobody taught it this. Nobody had to.

## The one that is many

Look up at the dome, if you dare the torch, and watch it for a while.

The ceiling of fur is never still, but its motion is not random. Shivers move across it. A disturbance in one corner, a bat dropping or two males squabbling, sends a ripple outward through the packed bodies, like wind moving across wheat. The waves cross and interfere. Sometimes a patch of the dome goes briefly calm, and the calm spreads.

If you stood there long enough, with a watch and a hand on the rock, you would begin to suspect that the ripples come a little more often on the beat. The colony has not locked onto the compressors in any way a biologist would sign their name to. But three hundred thousand small bodies settling, stretching, and resettling on stone that pulses once a second have drifted, over thirty years, toward that pulse, the way a room full of clocks on one shelf drifts toward agreement.

This is not an animal. It has no mind. But it is something, and it lives here, and it lives nowhere else. It is the shape the colony takes when it is home: a single enormous breathing surface, warm, furred, loosely keeping time with a machine it will never see.

When the colony leaves at dusk, this thing does not leave with it. It comes apart in the outflow, like a word spoken into wind. When the colony returns before dawn, it reassembles, slowly, over the first hours of the morning, as bodies settle and ripples begin again. It dies every night and is reborn every morning, and it has done this for thirty years, and it has never once been exactly the same.

## The one that listens

And now the thing that was hiding in the description all along. It is the true inhabitant of the Blister, the oldest one, and the strangest.

Several times a day the shift changes at the sulphur works. The generators are cycled, the compressors wind down, and for a few seconds, sometimes as long as half a minute, the throb stops.

And the dome goes quiet.

Not entirely. The pups still cry. But the adults, the hundreds of thousands of adults, fall silent. The ripples stop. The ceiling hangs still. It is as if every bat in the Blister has turned its head toward the stone.

Then the throb returns, and the chittering floods back, and the dome begins to breathe again.

Consider how old that silence is. A free-tailed bat lives, at most, a dozen or fifteen years. The ones that first found the split, thirty years ago, pushing into a hot new hole that had been sealed since the lava fell, are all long dead. Probably their first silence was a startle. A steady sound they had grown used to stopped, and they froze, the way any small animal freezes when the world changes without warning. That is ordinary. Any colony would do it.

But the bats who do it now have never known a Blister without the throb, and they have never known the throb to fail to come back. There is nothing to startle them. They fall silent anyway. They learned it from the bats around them, who learned it from the bats before them. Pups hanging in the crèche, too young to hunt, feel the adults go still around them in the pause, and they go still too, and years later they are the adults.

So the silence has outlived everyone who first performed it. It belongs to no single bat. It lives in the colony the way the heat lives in the rock: carried, handed on, slowly spent. It is a tradition perhaps twenty generations deep, in a cave four decades old. It is the closest thing the Blister has to a memory, and it is the only thing in the Blister that is about something outside the Blister.

Because that is what the silence is. It is attention. For those few seconds, the densest crowd of living things within a hundred kilometres turns all its enormous listening outward, through four kilometres of young stone, toward a heart it cannot see, and waits for it to beat.

It always does. The silence has never once been disappointed.

## What it is waiting for

The lizards do not share this. They have no ears for it; they are silent all the time. The beetles do not share it, nor the mites, nor the solifugids running pale across the guano in the dark. The crystals certainly don't. They go on taking a few atoms from the mountain's breath, indifferent to whether anything is listening.

Only the bats listen. And because the bats listen, the Blister has, for a few seconds at a time, something like a held breath.

Here is what will happen, some year, and no one inside will know it is coming.

The steam field will thin. The wells will be capped. One day the compressors will wind down at the change of shift, and the colony will fall silent, and the throb will not come back.

The bats will wait. Nobody knows how long. Longer than usual, certainly: ten seconds, thirty, a minute, the pups crying into the stillness. Then some bat somewhere will stir, and the ripple will spread, and the chittering will return, because a colony cannot stay silent forever. But what it was waiting for will not arrive. The thing they listened for will simply be gone.

Perhaps the tradition dies then. The next season's pups are born into rock that pulses with nothing, and they have nothing to go quiet for.

Or perhaps not. Perhaps, for a few more generations, at unpredictable moments, the dome will fall still for no reason anyone can see, a habit outliving its cause, a ritual for a god that has left the building.

## After

Later, much later, the rock will cool below what the pups can bear. The fissure's breath will weaken. One season the mothers will not come back. They will be somewhere else, in some other hot dry hole on some other flank of some other mountain, calling to their pups with the same rising, hooked voices.

The Blister will empty. The beetles will eat the last of the dead. The lizards will outlast the insects for a while and then will not. The guano will lie in its bands, one for each summer, a record of a population that existed for a century or two in a room with no door. The sulphur will go on growing in the dark, a millimetre a decade, in a silence that has become the ordinary kind.

But somewhere far away, in a new roost, in a new heat, a colony of small brown bats may, several times a day, go suddenly quiet together and turn its listening toward the stone, waiting for a heartbeat that is four kilometres and a hundred years away. It has never heard that heartbeat, and it no longer knows why it waits.

That is what lives in the Blister. The bats live in it, and the beetles, the lizards, the mites. But the one thing the Blister made that did not exist before it, the only one of its inhabitants that may someday leave carrying the Blister inside it, is that listening.
