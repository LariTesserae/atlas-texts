# The Tenant of Hallow Deep

## The Prints

The men who walk the green found them first. That was in the fourth year of pumping, when the water had dropped a little more than a foot and a half and the newest ground was still a narrow grey beach at the foot of the collared pillars.

A pair of walkers was working the edge with poles and lanterns. The older man stopped and held up his fist, which in the sign of the place means *hold* or *wait* or *don't*. In the silt ahead of them, on floor that had been underwater an hour before, there was a line of impressions.

They weren't boot prints. The silt on the green takes a mark the way wax takes a seal, perfect and permanent until the water fills it, and these marks were perfect. Each was broad and shallow, longer than a man's forearm, a flattened oval with a fringe of fine grooves along its front edge, as if something soft had been pressed down and then drawn slightly forward. The marks came in pairs, left and right, a stride apart. Between each pair ran a furrow as wide as a barrow, smooth-bottomed, faintly ribbed, where something long and heavy had been drawn along the floor.

The trail came up out of the retreating water, ran along the new beach for some forty yards, and went back in.

The silt had never been exposed to air, so nothing could have walked there since the water fell. Whatever made the trail had made it underwater, walking the floor of that warm unsuspected ocean, perhaps a week before, perhaps a century. In still water, in silt like flour, there is nothing to say which. The pumps had simply lowered the water off it the way you lift a cloth off a table and find the rings of old cups.

They marked it with red rag, the way they mark everything, and told the foreman, who told the engineers, who came down and looked and measured and went back up and wrote nothing down.

## The Trails

After that the crews looked for them, and they found them everywhere.

They didn't turn up every week. But every year the green gave up more of them, as regularly as it gave up collars on the pillars. The trails crossed the new ground in long lazy curves. They wound among the stalagmite forests, where the furrow squeezed between towers that couldn't have been more than a yard apart, so the thing that made it was narrower than it was long. They went around the rimstone pools, never through them. They circled the bases of the largest pillars, sometimes several times, the way a dog circles before lying down. In a few places the silt was pressed into a great shallow bowl, twenty feet across, with a smooth hollow at its centre, and the trails led into those bowls and out again.

Over the years the walkers learned to read them the way a shepherd reads sheep paths on a hill. Old trails were softened, their edges slumped by the slow settling of the silt, with a fine new skin of sediment in the bottoms of the prints. Newer ones were sharp. The walkers noticed, and it took them some years to be sure, that the trails near the waterline were always the sharpest.

Nobody found this frightening at first. Then everybody did. After a while it became simply a fact of the place, like the ammonia and the cockroaches.

What it meant was that the thing still walked. Every week the water gave back another finger's width of floor, and along that edge, in the last shallow margin before air, something had been moving recently. It kept to the shore of its shrinking sea. It walked the edge of what was left.

## What the Crews Call It

Down here there is no speaking, so it has no name that can be said aloud.

There is a sign for it. You hold your right hand out flat, palm down, fingers together, at about the height of your belt. Then you press it slowly downward, a little at a time, the way you might ease a lid onto something that mustn't be woken. It is almost the same as the sign for the water level, and deliberately so; the difference is in the slowness. Someone who doesn't know the language would think a man was telling another man to calm down.

On the surface, in the pub at the pithead, where men can talk again and are loud with the relief of it, they call it the Tenant. Nobody remembers who started that. It stuck because it was right. The miners own the coal and the company owns the mine, but the ocean was there first, and something was living in it, and every week the pumps serve it notice.

The naturalist had a name for it too, in Latin, built on the assumption that it was an amphibian. He had seen the prints and a cast of one, and in his fifth year of visits he claimed to have seen a little more. Nobody uses his name, and he left no drawing.

## The Eleven Hours

The pumps have stopped twice.

The first time was a fault in the line from the power station. It came in the ninth year, in winter, when snow on the surface brought down a pylon and the diesel generators took three hours to start in the cold. The second time was in the twenty-second year, a fire in the switchgear, and that one lasted eight.

When the pumps stop, Hallow Deep does not go quiet. The bats still roar and the rivers still pour, and the cascade still hammers its plunge pool. But the lowest layer of the noise, the one you feel in your knees and have felt every hour of every shift for years, simply goes. The men describe it as being like a floor dropping out. Some of them were sick. Several took out their earplugs without meaning to, as if the plugs were to blame.

Then, in the dimness, with only the sodium lamps and the hand lanterns, the water began to come back.

It rose too slowly to see. But the crews know the green better than they know their own kitchens, and inside the first hour of the first stoppage the walkers saw the newest cairns go wet at their bases. A red rag that had been lying on silt began to lift and stir. The sea was taking back its finger's width.

That was when it came up.

Accounts differ, as they would. The walkers were at the far edge of the lamps, where the lantern light fails a few yards out over the water. What they agree on is this. The water, which is always still at the edge, bulged. A long low swell moved toward the shore without breaking. Then something lay along the waterline, just under the surface and just above it, in the margin that had been dry an hour before.

It was pale. Everything down there is pale, but this was a different paleness, not bleached but deep, like the inside of a shell, or like tallow. It was long. They could see perhaps twenty feet of it, and it went on into the dark. It was broad and flattened, like a great salamander or like something that has lain on the floor under the weight of water for so long that it has grown to fit the floor. Along its flanks, where the lantern caught it, were ridges. They looked like ribs at first. Then the men saw they were not in the skin but on it: hard, pale, flaring shelves, one above another, like the collars on the pillars.

It had grown collars. It had lain so still, so long, in water so saturated with stone, that the stone had begun to grow on it. It had marked the levels of its sea on its own body.

It didn't come further. It lay along the new edge of the water, which was also the old edge, exactly as the water rose, keeping pace with it by a finger's width. When the generators caught and the pumps came back and the knee-deep hum filled the floor again, it withdrew with the water, drawing back into the dark without hurry.

The second time, in the twenty-second year, men who had heard the story went down to the edge on purpose, and it came again, and this time some of them saw its head.

## Its Head

It had no eyes. They hadn't expected any. Nothing born in that water has eyes.

What it had was a broad blunt snout, wider than a cart, fringed along the front edge with a hundred soft pale filaments. Those were the grooves at the front of every print, the men realised; it walked with its face down in the silt, combing. The filaments moved all the time, even when the rest of it was still, rippling and tasting and reaching a little way above the water into the air and then drawing back in, as if the air were a surprise, or an insult.

Where a fish would have gills it had a frill of something like feathers, like the fronds of the pale ferns around the lamps, a great deal of it, dark red with blood. It breathed with them in the warm water and the frill pulsed slowly, far slower than a man breathes, perhaps twice a minute.

The man who had been standing nearest, a pumpman called Aled Pryce who had worked in the Deep since the second year, did something nobody told him to do. He took his hands out of his pockets and made the sign, right hand flat, palm down, pressing slowly toward the water.

The filaments stopped moving.

That is the only part of the story that the men who were there refuse to argue about. For about the length of a long breath the fringe went still. Then it resumed, and the pumps came back, and it went down with its sea.

## What It Eats, and Where It Sleeps

The crews have built up some understanding of it, in the way men who work beside a thing for thirty years come to know it without ever quite deciding to.

It eats what the colony gives the water. Twelve million bats drop their dung and their dead into the sea beneath them, and have done so for longer than there has been a mine. The guano dunes on the dry ground are only the part that fell where the floor was high. Far more fell into the water, and settled, and fed the eyeless fish and the crayfish and the translucent shrimp, and these fed the white eels. Out in the deep middle of that water, in the rooms beyond the lamps, something vast and patient walked the bottom with its face in the silt, combing all of it back up again.

The white eels are frightened of it. The men are sure of this. When the long swell comes toward the shore, the eels, which rise freely to take fallen bats, stop rising.

It sleeps in the bowls, the great shallow hollows pressed into the silt among the pillars. Some of the old ones on the higher ground are half-filled now with drifted guano, and cockroaches seethe in them, and a fern has grown on the rim of one that happens to sit under a lamp. The newest ones lie under the water, which is why the walkers find them only when the pumps give them up.

And it has rooms.

## The Rooms

The green hides holes. Every walker knows it. The silt lies over them like a sheet and the rafts of calcite bridge them, and twice a man has gone through and not come up.

The engineers call them solution pockets. The walkers call them doors.

The trails go to them. When a walker finds a sharp new trail at the waterline and follows it back along the beach, very often it ends at a place where the silt dips slightly, a faint shallow funnel. When they probe it with a pole, the pole finds nothing and goes on finding nothing. A pole twelve feet long goes in to the hand.

Below the floor of Hallow Deep there is more of Hallow Deep. There are galleries in the limestone full of water still warm from the depths, where the pumps' reach is weaker and the level falls far more slowly. The thing goes down into them. As its sea shrinks above it, it has been moving house into the rooms below, where the water will stay longer.

The first man who went through was Emrys Hale, in the twelfth year. The second was Tom Wystan, in the nineteenth. Tom is the man in the photograph in the biscuit tin, and the biscuit tin sits on the lip of the door he went through, which has long since been capped with a slab of concrete and a painted cross.

What the walkers never mention on the surface, and seldom even in the sign, is that the trails near the shrine are not like the others. They don't curve past. They come to the concrete slab and stop, and there is a hollow, a smaller bowl, pressed into the silt right alongside it, as if something had lain there with its face against the capped door for a long time.

Twice, in the years since, the walkers have found the candle stub moved. It hasn't been lit, because matches fail down there. It has only been shifted a few inches, turned, and set down again, its wax dimpled faintly by something soft.

## The Collars

Watching it with the patience the Deep teaches, you begin to understand that the thing is not really an animal in the way the bats are animals, or the frogs, or the men.

It is something the water has been doing for a very long time.

The stone grows on it. The collars on its flanks are exactly like the collars on the pillars, and for the same reason. They form wherever it has lain still at a level long enough for calcite to crystallise, and it has lain still for a very long time. The crews who saw it in the twenty-second year say the collars ran all the way along its back. They were packed close together near the spine, like the growth rings nearest the heart of an old oak, and widely spaced near the flanks, where the newest had formed. The oldest ones must have grown when the ocean was higher than anyone has measured. Some of them, a man said, were so old they had fused, and its back was less like a back than a ridge, a low long pale wall of flowstone that breathed.

The men of the old walkways think about this more than they say. Somewhere under the water there may be stalagmites that aren't stalagmites. There may be ridges on the floor of that shrinking sea that are only lying still. Perhaps one day the pumps will lower the water off a great pale wall ringed with shelves, and the walkers will mark it with rag as safe ground, and it won't be ground.

Or perhaps it's the other way around, and that is what the stalagmites were. Perhaps the ocean of Hallow Deep has always made these things, slowly, out of stone and warm dark and bats falling like snow, and most of them finished long ago and stopped moving. Perhaps only one is left that still walks.

The men think about this too, and then they go back to their pumps.

## What It Waits For

It isn't fleeing. That is the last thing the crews have come to understand.

A creature that fled would go down into its rooms and stay there. This one comes back to the edge, week after week, year after year, and walks the margin. When the pumps stopped, it didn't cower. It came up with the water, keeping exact pace, lying along the line of its returning sea like a hand laid on a sill.

It is waiting, the way the water waits.

The engineers talk of a seventh pump, and an eighth. Every year it takes more power to hold the level. The miners say the water is patient, and they are right. It has been there longer than the coal. The seam above is thinning, and the mine will not last forever. One year the company will decide that the Hallow is no longer worth the cost of the pumps. The cables will come up the shaft, the diesels will be sold, and the last crew will walk back along the walkway, past the slab and the biscuit tin, through the gate of riveted plate, which they will leave open because there is no reason to close it.

Then the lowest layer of the noise will stop, and it will not start again.

The water will come back at the height of a man a month. It will cover the green and all the red rags and the cairns. It will cover the pale ferns around the dark lamps and drown the old gauging rod. It will lift the walkway off its stilts, plank by rotting plank, and float it among the pillars. It will rise over the pumps on their plinths and fill the pipes and climb the raise, and it will come up the timbered tunnel as far as it can.

And the thing will come with it, a finger's width behind the edge, its face in the silt, combing. It will walk the green that was its floor. It will come back to the bowls it slept in. It will lie down beside a concrete slab with a cross on it, beneath a biscuit tin gone to rust. The stone will begin again to grow on its back, a new collar, at a new level, which is also the oldest level.

Above it, the colony will go on, twelve million strong, pouring out through the chimney at dusk and back before dawn. The chittering will come down to it through fathoms of warm water, softened and changed, the way rain sounds to someone lying awake under a roof. It was the only sound it had ever heard until thirty-one years ago.

On the surface, perhaps, a man who once worked the Deep will be standing in the gorse in grey rain, with his ears still ringing from a noise that stopped years before. Out of habit, and without meaning to, he will hold out his right hand, palm down, and press it slowly toward the ground.
