# Who Keeps the Lowworks

## The Wrong Question

The first answer is that no one lives here, and the first answer is nearly right.

You can search the basin for a long time and find nothing that could be called a resident. The plants grow, but they are not tenants. They are more like the plaster that closes over a wound. They are what happens here, not who. The machines run, but a machine is not an inhabitant. It is a verb somebody left running.

Still, the machines are *maintained*. That is the detail that eventually snags.

The grease on the beam engine's pivot is fresh. It is amber and clean, with no grit in it, though the water is thick with silt. The splices on the live cables are new wraps of tarred cord over old breaks. The furnace grate of the great pumping station should have choked on clinker a thousand times over, and it is raked clear. Rivets that the cavitation should have shaken loose are tight. Somebody is doing this work. Nothing in the basin is worn, and part of the reason is that something keeps un-wearing it.

You cannot catch them at it by watching. You catch them by looking where time stutters.

## The Hands

Stand in the lee of the great engine house, where the flower opens and closes and opens, and wait for the downstroke.

On the downstroke the pressure wave arrives. It is the thud you feel in your teeth, and in the trough behind it the water tears into those bubbles that come from nowhere and hang for an instant. In that instant, sometimes, the bubbles hold a shape. It has a back and a bent arm, and something like a head turned toward the work. Then the wave passes and it is gone, and the grease on the pivot is fresher than it was.

Watch the loop. Watch the same three seconds repeat. The figure is there every time: kneeling at the pivot, reaching, applying the grease with a thumb. It is the same gesture and the same figure, and it is also, each time, entirely new.

These are the Hands. That is not their name, because a name is a thing you carry forward from yesterday, and they have no yesterday to carry it from. But it is what they are. They are the maintenance of the Lowworks, and they are the only thing here that works.

At first they seem to be made of whatever the moment provides. A Hand near a frayed cable is mostly blue-white flicker, a crawling arc that happens to have fingers. A Hand at the furnace is heat-shimmer and a dull red glow from inside, so that it looks like a figure drawn in the convection column. A Hand in the moss meadows is green, a gesture of stems. Look more closely and a pattern emerges. They are always roughly the height of a person. They always have two arms, though often more than two hands. And they always have a face, of a kind: a flat, pale disc where the head should be, on which the silt draws itself into rings and starbursts, the same lattices that form on the flat stones when the engines change pitch.

The face is not for looking out. It is for reading in.

## How They Arrive

They come with the bore.

The next time the flood wall rolls down from the north, watch its front rather than its force. It is a churning face of foam and torn leaves and silt, and it is full of figures. They tumble end over end in the surge, hundreds of them, pale and half-formed. They are not struggling. They are reaching. Even as the bore throws them against the engine houses, their arms are already out, their fingers already closing on tools that are not there yet, the way a sleeper's hand closes on a railing in a dream of stairs.

The bore strands them all across the basin, and each one lands exactly where it is needed. One lands at a cracked flange. One lands at the grate. One lands among the vines strangling a pylon, and it begins cutting before it has finished condensing.

A Hand is born knowing its job perfectly. This is the most important thing about them, and the strangest. There is no apprenticeship, no first fumbling try, no slow accumulation of skill. Skill is a kind of memory, a residue of all the times you did it wrong, and the Hands have none of that. They have the competence without the history. They arrive with the whole trade already in them, the way the basalt arrives already glassy and already cracked, and the way the trees arrive already tall.

They are the Lowworks' product in its purest form: a worker on the first morning of the job, in that bright, frightening, capable moment before anything has gone wrong. They are youth at industrial scale, the thing the factory makes, and they are also the factory's staff.

## What They Do

Each Hand has exactly one task. It tightens one rivet, rakes one grate, splices one cable, or carries one lava pillow.

That last is how the furnaces burn underwater. The Hands go down to the fissures, where the lava muscles out in lobes and skins itself in black glass, and they break off the fresh pillows while they are still molten inside. They carry them cradled against their chests, glowing through the fingers, across the basin floor to the engine houses, and feed them through the grates. The trails where they have walked are the sulfur-yellow lines in the moss. The heat of what they carried scorched the moss into a different color, and the plants grew back to match.

When a Hand finishes its task, it does not die. Death is an ending, and an ending requires something to have gone on for a while first. The Hand simply completes. It lets go of the wrench, and whatever held its shape stops holding. Its bubbles rise. Its shimmer disperses. It drifts up into the canopy, then into the foam, and the suction of the nearest outflow pipe draws it in. It goes up the great riveted throat and out into the sky, and it falls back as rain. Somewhere north, in the rain, the bore is gathering.

That is why there are no bones. Nothing here has ever been left over.

## The Silt Script

A Hand learns its task from the stones.

When the dominant engine shifts pitch, the silt on every flat stone rearranges itself into those concentric rings and lattices. They are not random. They are work orders. Each pattern is the engine's report on its own condition, and the pitch of a thudding piston changes very slightly when a bearing begins to dry, a flange begins to leak, or a grate begins to choke. The Hands read the patterns on the stones, and the same patterns form on their own blank faces, and then they know what to do.

So the engines tell the Hands what the engines need, and the Hands keep the engines running, and the running engines make the rain that carries the Hands back down in the bore to read the silt again. It is a closed circuit, like everything else here. No one gives the orders. The orders give themselves.

## If They See You

They will see you, if you stay near the engines long enough, and it is worth knowing what happens.

A Hand whose task has brought it near you will turn its silt-face toward you, and the patterns on it will scatter and re-form into something you have not seen on any stone. It is a single bright starburst, and it means *oh*. It is surprise, pure and entire, with nothing behind it.

Then it will do the only thing a being with no past can do with a stranger. It will assume you are new. It will assume you have just arrived, as it has, and that you are there to work, as it is. It will hold out whatever it is carrying: a rake, a coil of tarred cord, a glowing pillow of rock. It is offering you the first half of its task. It is not being generous. It cannot imagine that you might want anything else.

In the stutter-zones this happens over and over. The loop brings the Hand back around, and it sees you again for the first time, and the starburst blooms again. It is the same *oh*, as fresh as the first. It will greet you with total astonishment forty times in two minutes, and it will never tire of it, because it has never done it before.

Some visitors find this unbearable. Others stay for hours.

Do not take the tool. A person who takes a task from a Hand begins, slowly, to lose track of whether they have been here before.

## The Wagons

For a long time the Hands seem to be the whole answer. They are what lives in the Lowworks, if "lives" is the word: a population of first moments, endlessly replenished, keeping a youth-machine young.

But there is the rail line.

It comes down the rift wall on its trestle, crosses the floor, and runs into the deepest fissure, and the rust-red wagons on it are loaded with basalt blocks. Look at the blocks closely. They are not like the rest of the basin's stone. Their edges are softened. A face has been rounded by current. A corner carries a scratch that the plants did not cover. One block has a little crust of dried mineral on it, the kind that takes years to form. Another is nothing but a section of fallen column that somehow stayed fallen: it did not stutter back upright, and it grew no vines.

These are the parts of the basin that began to have a past.

It happens. Even here, even with the bore and the engines and the loops, something occasionally starts to accumulate. A stone gets worn. A mark stays. Most of the Hands' work is keeping the machines fresh, but some of it, and it is quiet, careful work, is finding those places. A Hand cuts the aged rock out of the floor, squares it into a block, and loads it onto a wagon. The moss grows into the hole. Nobody can tell anything was there.

The Lowworks is a factory, and a factory has waste. Its product is newness. Its waste is age, and the rail line is how the waste goes out.

The wagons do not come back up empty. They do not come back up at all. There are always wagons on the line, and every so often one of them is further down than it was.

## The One Below

At the bottom of the deepest fissure, past where any glow from the vents reaches, something receives them.

It is the only animal in the Lowworks. It moves, slowly. It has a yesterday. In fact it has all of them.

It is hard to describe its shape, because its shape is made of what the wagons bring. Every worn block and scratched stone and smoothed facet the basin has ever shed has been delivered to it and kept. Its body is an accretion of weathering. The fissure is not a crack with something at the bottom. The fissure is the gap around it, the outline it has pressed into the new rock by being so much older than everything else. It fills the dark the way a hand fills a glove.

It has no silt-face, and it does not need one. It reads nothing, because it already knows. Every scratch it holds is a record, and together they are the only history the basin has. Its skin is covered with them: rivet-marks, tool-marks, the smooth patches where a Hand's thumb pressed the same spot a thousand looping times.

So it remembers the Hands. It is the only thing that ever has. The Hands cannot know one another. Each one is alone in its single perfect moment, and none of them has ever met a Hand that came before. But the One Below has their marks, all of them. It knows the Hand that greased the pivot, and the Hand that greased it before that, and before that, back and back. It holds a crowd of millions who never stood in the same instant. It is their archive, and their only witness.

When the basin floor jolts, several times an hour, that is the One Below shifting its weight. The movement cracks the young rock above it. The plants close over the wound. Even its restlessness becomes, up there, more newness.

## What It Was

There is a story in the scratches, if you can read deep enough into them. It is the oldest mark of all, at the very center of the thing.

The engines were built to drain the basin. Whoever built them, and there is no mark of them, only of what they made, meant for the flood to be pumped out and the basin to be emptied, the water sent up and away. The deepest fissure was the drain. A Hand was sent to open it.

It was one of the first Hands, back when there were only a few. It read its task off the silt, went down the rail with a wrench, and reached the drain. And then it did the one thing no Hand has ever done since. It did not finish.

Perhaps it looked up. From the bottom of the fissure it could see the whole basin: the green cathedral dusk, the black glass, the engines thudding, the rain that was also the sea. It was all in its first moment, all sharp-edged and unbitten and roaring. The Hand understood, as only something standing still for a second moment could, what opening the drain would mean. The basin would empty. Then it would dry, and weather, and wear. Things would come to live in it, things that needed yesterdays: fish in the cracks, snails on the leaves, worms in the moss. They would eat. They would build nests and leave tracks. And they would die, and leave shells in the silt, and bones.

The basin would get a past. And a thing with a past gets old.

The Hand did not open the drain. It did not let go of the wrench, either, because letting go would have meant completing, and completing would have meant forgetting what it had just understood. So it held on. It stayed. It was the only Hand to have a second moment, and then a third, and every moment it had after that it kept, because it had nowhere else to put them.

When the first worn stone came down the rail, it took that in too. Then it took in the next.
