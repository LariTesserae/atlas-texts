# Who Lives in the Settling

## A First Draft

The answer came the way everything there comes: several times at once, each version slightly rotated from the others.

In the first version, the one who lives there is an old woman on the leaning bench in the square, with her hands folded in her lap and a rosette of pale green lichen on the shoulder of her coat.

In the second, it is a boy of about ten, standing very still in the northern lanes with his palm held flat and open under a ledge that is not quite there.

In the third, it is nobody. There is only a heavy coat on a peg by a door, and when you put your hand inside it, it is still warm.

All three are faintly true. Keep them in mind. By the end we will see which one holds weight.

---

## The Coats

You learn to recognize the people of the Settling by their coats before you learn their faces.

The coats are enormous. They are felted wool over quilted wool over something softer, lined with pockets inside pockets. They hang to the shin and button to the chin, and the collars fold up high enough to breathe into. Because the people here heat themselves and not their houses, the coat is the house. It is where the warmth lives. It is the one room in the Settling where it is safe to have a fire.

That has a consequence nobody warns you about. The coat is the warmest made thing in the town, so it is also the least settled. A coat worn close against a living body for twenty winters begins to loosen. Its pockets multiply. A lining appears under the lining. You reach for your gloves and find a second pair, or a pebble, or a folded note in handwriting that is almost your own. The warm cup of tea that people sometimes arrive holding usually comes out of a coat. Residents treat their coats the way other people treat a slightly unpredictable dog: fondly, and with some attention to what it might have picked up.

Coats are handed down. An old coat has been so many possible coats that it is almost soft with alternatives. Children are proud to inherit one.

Inside the coats, the people are quiet. They move slowly, in part because the lanes are narrow and the walls are close, and in part because haste generates heat. They wear gloves out of courtesy. Laying a bare hand on a young wall is rude, like breathing on someone's spectacles. Only the old lichen walls can take it, and those are touched constantly, stroked in passing, for luck.

They drink their tea scalding, in three swallows, and set the cup out on the sill to cool. A slow cup of tea makes a warm room. Everyone knows someone who once lingered over a pot with a friend on a long evening and woke to find the kitchen had a second door in it, opening onto a hallway neither of them owned.

They have their own words. A wall is *set* or *loose*. A lane is *thin* when the drafts are faint and *thick* when they crowd. *A lane of three* is a lane that has not decided. Directions are given in convergence: *two settlings over, past the thick bit, you'll know it by the lichen.* Nobody says how many steps. Steps don't mean anything here.

## Three Ways to Arrive

The people of the Settling came in three ways. You can still tell which way, if you know what to look for.

### Those who came

Some walked in. They followed the thread of meltwater down from inland, where there are ordinary hills and settled towns, and arrived on the flat carrying heat in their bodies like lanterns.

Newcomers are unsettled for a while. In their first winter, their outlines double in doorways. Their voices thicken in the old streets, not just the young ones. Locals keep them out of the outskirts, the way you'd keep a child away from deep water. A newcomer walking into a lane of three can make it a lane of five.

Some leave within a year. The ones who stay settle, as everything settles. The long cold works into them, the doubled edges fade, and they become singular again. But they are not always quite the person who arrived. Relatives who visit sometimes notice it and can't name it. It is one choice's difference, a road not taken that has quietly become the road taken. A woman named Hesk came to the Settling with a limp from a childhood fall. She settled without it. She also settled without any memory of the fall, and she says she misses it the way you'd miss a scar: not the pain, but the proof.

### Those who were born

Children born in the Settling come into the world unsettled.

A newborn here has a soft edge, like the haze in the hollows of the ground. When you hold one, it seems both heavier and lighter than a baby should. They are kept cool, swaddled in wool with their faces bare to the cold air, so they can set properly. A baby kept too warm stays blurred too long and grows up uncertain in a way that goes deeper than temperament.

Families do not name their children for a year. They wait to see which child settles. Until then the baby is called *the little one*, or *the draft*, and relatives visit and say things like *she's taking after her mother today.*

Almost every child born here has a twin that does not quite exist. You see it best in the summer lanes, when the town loosens: a faint second child playing beside the first, slightly out of step, stacking the same pebbles in a different order. Parents in other places would call it an imaginary friend. Parents here know better and set out a second cup of milk, which is never drunk, but is somehow always a little lower by evening.

Around the age of seven or eight, the twin fades. Children grieve it. Some grieve for years. A few adults will admit, late in the evening and in the singular streets, that they aren't certain they were the one who was meant to stay.

### Those who condensed

Then there are the old families.

They live in the oldest quarter, where the walls are thick with lichen and the lanes run single and clean. Their histories go back sixty or seventy years and then thin out, the way the lanes thin at the edge of town. Ask how their grandparents arrived and you get three answers, told with equal confidence by three members of the same family at the same table. None of them seems troubled by the disagreement.

Take the Askes. The eldest, Tove, remembers her wedding in a chapel with a green door. She also remembers it in a chapel with no door at all, only an arch open to the sea. Neither chapel exists. Her son remembers growing up in a house with a courtyard where he kept a rabbit. The Aske house has no courtyard. There is a place in its back wall where the grain of the porcelain swirls, as if something round had been packed into it long ago.

Their oldest belongings are not made of wood or cloth. They are made of wall. A comb. A set of teacups, faintly translucent, that ring when you set them down. A doll with a face like frost on a window.

The condensed feel the cold least of anyone. Their coats are thinner. They can stand in a young lane without doubling, and they can put a bare hand on a loose wall without loosening it. People say, carefully, that the condensed are what the town decided a person should be: its opinion about who ought to live here, precipitated out of the air along with the stairs and doors. The condensed don't argue with this. They say that everyone, anywhere, is a decision something made, and at least they know where theirs came from.

## The Work

There is not much to do in the Settling, and all of it is done slowly.

**Gutter-scrapers** go out at dawn with fine picks of bone and clear the frost from the gutters, which are no wider than a line of engraving. The work is close and patient, done with a lens. A good scraper can clear a whole lane's roof-edges in a morning without once breathing on the stone.

**Cistern-keepers** lift the lids of the town's hundreds of cisterns, some as large as a bath, some as small as a teacup. They check the water level and count the pale shrimp. They keep ledgers of the doubles: how many shrimp have a faint companion, in which cisterns, in which lanes. The ledgers go back decades. Nobody quite knows what they are for, only that the numbers mean something, and that you would want to notice if they changed.

**Stream-walkers** follow the thread of meltwater through the town in all its revisions at once. Where it runs as three interleaved streams in the loose lanes, they walk all three, which is exhausting and takes great concentration. They say that if the stream is not watched, it might settle into a bed that floods the old quarter, and that being watched helps it choose. Nobody is sure whether this is true, but nobody wants to stop and find out.

And then there are the **sitters**.

A sitter is someone who wants something from the town: a window, a step, a door between two houses. They go and sit beside the place where they want it, every day, through a whole winter or many winters, in their coat, with their breath hanging in front of them, and think about it. They don't pray or chant. They just attend. They look at the blank stretch of wall until they can see the window in it the way you can see a face in a cloud, and then they keep seeing it, day after day, until the wall begins to agree.

Old Imre sat for ten winters for a door between her kitchen and her sister's. It came in the eleventh, small at first, the size of a playing card, complete with a tiny stoop. The next year it was waist high. Now the two old women pass soup through it, and neither has to go out into the cold.

There are rules. You may only sit for your own walls. You may not sit against a neighbor. When two people want different things from the same stretch of stone, the wall sometimes gives both, in drafts, and then neither settles for a generation. Everyone agrees that is worse than either one getting their way.

And no one sits at the hollows at night.

Someone did, once. A young man named Aud sat beside a pool in the ground where the reflection showed a tower. He sat the whole night, and then another, and then all winter. There is a tower now, at the northeast edge of town, tall and thin and so loose that you can see the low roof it grew from through its walls. The two are still arguing, thirty years on. Aud left the Settling long ago. The tower stays, a monument to what happens when wanting outpaces patience.

## The Others

You will meet yourself in the loose lanes. Everyone does, eventually.

It happens in the young branches at the outskirts, where the drafts have not converged. You turn a corner, and there you are, a step to the left, slightly rotated, wearing your coat with the collar turned differently, holding a cup you didn't pick up. Sometimes that version of you has a scar you don't, or is a year older, or is walking the other way, with somewhere else to be.

The etiquette is simple: nod, and step aside. Don't speak. They would answer, and then you would both have said something, and the lane would have one more thing to decide.

Residents call them *the others*, or *the unchosen*, and not unkindly. Every person in the Settling has some number of others in the outskirts, the way every lane has its drafts. You have more of them in summer. You have fewer as you age and settle. The very old have almost none.

What happens to them when a lane settles?

Look closely at the walls in the old quarter, the thick singular ones patched with lichen. Put your eye right up to the grain on a bright day. You will see faint shapes packed into it, like leaves pressed in a book: the curve of a sleeve, a hand raised to a window that is no longer there, the line of a face in profile. The unchosen press into the stone as the stone sets. The old quarter is built of them.

*The walls are full of us,* people say, in the tone other towns use for *the churchyard is full of us*. It is a statement of belonging, not of horror. Every lichen wall you stroke for luck is partly made of neighbors who might have been.

The doubles of the shrimp go the same way, mostly. But not all of them. In the northern lanes, the doubles vanish from the cisterns and don't press into anything. The ledgers record that too.

## The Wren
