# Who Lives in the Rising Stand

## The obvious answer

The first answer is the one the description has already given: the Stand lives there. One root-mass, one organism, one process. It fills the continent the way a held breath fills a chest. Nothing else fits.

But that answer describes the house, not who lives in it. A place that says *nothing else is here* so many times is worth reading slowly, because the people who build perfect systems always describe them in absolutes, and the absolutes always come with qualifiers.

So read the qualifiers.

*Almost* nothing reflects.
The water *barely* leaves.
Where one stem dies, *which is rare*.
There are no gaps *wider than a hand*.
There is no quiet, *except perhaps* at the very bottom.

A system that recovers everything still sheds a little. The laws underneath the Stand guarantee it. No pump is perfect, and no chemistry runs without releasing heat. The stone at the root-front is *still slightly warm from the chemistry of its making*. That warmth is loss. The Stand cannot recover it. It seeps upward through the glass and into the wet air and is gone.

Whatever lives in the Stand lives on what the Stand cannot help losing.

## In the seams

Start with the gaps narrower than a hand.

Where two pillars press together but have not yet fused, there is a seam. It may be a finger wide or the width of a sheet of paper. It runs vertically for a hundred meters, following the ribs of the bark, dark and wet and trembling with the pumping on both sides. From outside you would call it a crack. From inside it is a country, very tall and very thin, with walls that breathe.

Things live in the seams.

At first they are hard to see as things. They look like the bark has flaked, like a thin paler layer peeling from the wet black wood. They are flat. Everything about them is flat. They are as long as a forearm and as thin as a fingernail, shaped like a pressed leaf or the shadow of a moth. They have no front or back, only two faces, and each face presses against a wall. They lie in the seams with their bodies spread against the living wood the way a hand spreads against a window.

They are pale, a greyish ivory like the root-filaments. Being black is expensive. It takes the nanoscopic forests the leaves grow, and nothing down here has light enough to justify that cost. They are pale because they have given up on light entirely.

They eat heat.

Not directly. They are not engines. But each one is patterned across both faces with fine branching channels, like the veins of a leaf or a river delta seen from orbit. Those channels carry fluid, and the fluid moves because one side of the creature is fractionally warmer than the other. The pillar on the left pumps a little harder than the pillar on the right. The water in the left pillar has come up more recently from the warm root-stone. The difference is a fraction of a degree. It is enough. The creature lives in the gradient between its two walls, drawing a thin living out of the difference.

The Stand's makers would call this waste. The creatures call it the world.

## The crack

They cannot move on their own. There is no room. A seam as thin as paper allows no crawling or burrowing. The walls hold them still.

Then comes dawn. The air shifts by a few degrees, and across whole valleys the wood contracts, and the crack goes off like artillery. The seams open.

Not by much. A millimeter, perhaps less, and only for an instant. The wood shrinks, the seam gapes, and then the Stand's own growth pressure begins closing it again. But in that instant every seam-creature in the valley lets go of both walls at once and slides.

They slide down. Always down, because down is where the warmth is and falling costs nothing. The shock of the cracking carries them a few centimeters, sometimes a meter, before the walls close and they flatten and hold.

This is their whole life of motion. One slide per clear dawn, and clear dawns are rare. A seam-creature might move a few dozen times in a life. Its life is long, because nothing here has any reason to hurry and nothing hunts it. It measures its existence in cracks. The gunshots that roll across the landscape each morning, the ones that sound like war, are to them the sound of the door opening.

So the dawn crack means two things. To the Stand it is a strain, a small injury repeated a million times. To whatever lives in the seams it is the only chance to go anywhere.

## Why the Stand does not close them

The Stand fills every gap. Why hasn't it filled these?

The first answer is tolerance. No mechanism can be built without clearance. Gears need a hair's width between their teeth or they seize. Pistons need slop. Pillars that pulse in slow unsynchronized waves need a little room between them, or the waves would tear the wood apart where two walls moved in opposite directions. The seams are the Stand's clearance. It cannot fill them without binding itself. They are the one kind of empty space it needs, and it keeps them empty the way a clock keeps the space between its teeth.

The creatures live in that necessity. The Stand cannot afford to close their home, because their home is part of how the Stand works.

The second answer is less comfortable. Perhaps the Stand does not notice them. They are thinner than its attention. Its rule is *everything filled*, but a rule is enforced at some scale, and below that scale the rule goes quiet. They are pale, flat, warm-sided and cold-sided, and they have spent generations becoming exactly as thin as the Stand's indifference.

## Where they came from

They were not here first. The Stand was.

Or rather, the Stand was here first *here*. It grows outward from a coast no one can find, over old land of bare rock and thin grass, and that old land had its own small residents: lichens, grasses, beetles, whatever clung to a weathered shore. When the growth front arrives, most of them are simply buried. The filaments dissolve the grey stone and rebuild it as green-veined glass, and the life on it goes into the chemistry with everything else.

Not all of it. Some small crawling thing, flat already, used to living under stones, finds a crack between two young stems at the growth front, where the stems are only person-thick and the seams are loose. It crawls in. The stems grow. The seam tightens. Its offspring are born in less room than it had. Theirs, in less than that.

Imagine this over a thousand kilometers of advance and many thousands of years. Imagine it happening at every edge at once, because every edge is advancing. Every seam in the interior was once a crack at the front, and the pillars that crowd it were once saplings. So the seam-creatures are not a species that found a niche. They are what is left of every species the Stand ever overran, pressed and selected and pressed again. They have been flattened by the growth of the thing that ate their world, until they became the exact shape of the space it had to leave.

They are the old land. They are what the old land became to survive being covered. Every one of them faces sideways, toward the walls, never outward. They have forgotten there was an outward.

## Higher up: the sheen

The seams hold most of them, but not all.

*Almost* nothing reflects. The black leaves trap nearly every photon. But nearly is not every. There is a thin band of light at the far violet edge of the spectrum that the nanoscopic pillars were not tuned to catch. It slides off the leaf-plates at a steep angle, fainter than moonlight and invisible to any eye that ever evolved on the old coast.

In the crown, in the overlaps where one fifteen-meter plate lies over another like a scale on a fish, there is another space thinner than a hand. Something lives there too.

These are faster than the seam-creatures, because the crown moves. The wind ripples the plates constantly, and each ripple lifts and drops the overlaps, so the space breathes open and shut many times a minute. The crown-dwellers ride that breathing. They are small, a few centimeters, and translucent. Each has one surface covered in a fine iridescent film that catches the discarded violet and does something with it. Where many cluster in the shelter of one overlap, the leaf above them seems to glow, very faintly, a colour no one on the old coast would have had a word for.

From a high ridge, the Stand looks like a vast dark animal breathing. If you could see in that violet, you would see the animal's fur shot through with a faint moving shimmer, like frost forming and melting in patterns too slow to follow. That shimmer is a population. It is the only thing on the continent that could be called colour.

## At the rim: the grains

The water *barely* leaves.

At the edges, rivers form. They are tiny compared to the Stand and have carved nothing yet but a few shallow raw-edged gullies. But they are the only erosive force the new stone has ever known. They do something the Stand never does. They break things down. They loosen crystals of banded glass from the plateau's edge and tumble them, and in tumbling they round the sharp edges.

In the gullies there are grains of sand, which have never existed on the plateau before.

And in the sand, in the film of water around the grains, there are specks. They are almost nothing: single cells, strings of cells, clumps. They are small enough and new enough that you could not say whether they came from the old land or were shed from the Stand's own root-filaments and went feral. They live in the only place on the continent where something is coming apart instead of being assembled. They eat the mineral solution the river washes out of the new stone, which the Stand made and then, through the river, lost.

What they are doing, slowly, is making soil.

The description says there was no soil to begin with. That is still true almost everywhere. But at the rim, in a few gullies, in quantities you could hold in your cupped hands, there is a thin grey paste of rounded grains and living specks. It is the first soil the new world has ever had. It is the opposite of the Stand. It is decay. Nothing here rots, yet something at the rim has begun to.

The Stand will eventually grow over these gullies too, because it grows over everything. But the rivers will move outward with the rim, because the rivers are made by the rim. The soil-makers will move with them, a thin ring of undoing always just outside the edge of the doing.

## At the bottom

There is no quiet, *except perhaps* at the very bottom.

Deep in the root-stone, the roar stops being sound and becomes a vibration in the rock. It is felt in the teeth and in the long bones. Down here the channels in the honeycombed stone are old, laid down when the plateau was a kilometer lower, and the filaments that made them have moved on and upward. Some of the channels are empty. They are dry, warm from the chemistry still settling in the glass around them, and they hum with the pumping overhead.

Here the pulses arrive from above, from billions of vessels contracting and releasing in waves that are not synchronized. The unsynchronized waves reach the bottom of the world and add together, and because they are unsynchronized, the sum is noise. It is a roar and a grinding, the sound of a single organism working with no pattern in it.

But patterns in noise are a matter of who is listening.

In the oldest channels there is something that does not move at all. It is not flat like the seam-creatures or bright like the crown-dwellers. It is threadlike, a lattice of fine pale fibres strung across the empty channels like a web. It might once have been root-filament, or something that grew out of root-filament, or something that grew in the space root-filament left behind. The fibres vibrate. They vibrate with the roar, because everything here does. But they are tuned, each one to a slightly different frequency, the way strings on an instrument are tuned, and as they vibrate they tighten and slacken slightly.

What they are doing, as near as it can be described, is listening to the Stand think.

Perhaps the Stand does not think. Perhaps it is only a process, patient and immense. But a process that large, with billions of independent pulses feeding back through water pressure and growth pressure and the slow exchange of fluid through fused walls, is doing *something* with all that signal. Down in the dry channels, a web of tuned threads catches some of it. The roar is not a roar to them. It is weather, and the weather has fronts. It is a language with no speaker, and they are its only audience.

Nothing here has a voice. That is still true. But voices are not the only thing that can be heard.

## And you

The description kept saying *you*.

Imagine standing behind a waterfall. You feel the sound in your chest. Its edges could cut you. The creaking is painfully loud up close.

Someone stood here once, or is imagined standing here, which in a place like this may be the same. Consider what you would be to the Stand's inhabitants.

You would be warm. Hugely, extravagantly warm, thirty-seven degrees in a place that stays damp and chill like the inside of a cellar. You would radiate heat in every direction, uncaught and unrecovered, the most wasteful object that had ever stood on the new stone. You would breathe out water the Stand could not catch. You would reflect light, all of it, every colour, standing pale in the grey dimness like a lamp.

Put your hand against a pillar. The seam beside your palm is thinner than your finger. Wait.

On the side of the seam nearest your hand, the wood warms by a fraction of a degree, then a degree. Deep in the gap, a pale flat thing that has spent its whole life between two walls differing by almost nothing feels, for the first time, a gradient worth having. Its channels run faster. It is richer in this minute than in the whole of its life before.

If the next dawn is clear, and the wood cracks, and the seam opens its millimeter, it will not slide down toward the root-stone. It will slide toward you.

So this is what lives in the Rising Stand: everything the Stand cannot help losing. There is the heat of its own chemistry, the violet it could not catch, the grains its own water wore away, and the pattern in its own noise. The inhabitants are thin, pale, voiceless, and patient, living in clearances and overlaps and gullies and dry channels, in the margin that any machine, however perfect, has to leave.

The Stand's law is *nothing is wasted, and everything is filled*. But nothing is ever wasted to the ones who live on the waste, and a law that fills everything must still leave a little room in which to move. They live in that room. They have been living there as long as the Stand has been rising.

The Stand has only just begun. So have they.
