# The One Who Lives in the Kettle

## First, the Wrong Answers

The first answer is that nobody lives here. The shed is empty and the stool has gone soft and grey. The logbook has swollen into a single block of pulp with a spine. Whoever bolted the engine over the fissure and drilled the staples into the glassy wall is gone, and probably long dead. They left the way people leave places like this: meaning to come back.

The second answer is the mats. The bands of rust and olive and sulfur and pale green along the runoff channels are alive, strictly speaking. So are the ferns at the mouth, leaning out toward the daylight. But they are tenants of the margins. They live at the edges of the Kettle's temperature, and the Kettle does not belong to them. They would live the same way in any warm, wet crack in the world. They do not know about the wheel.

The third answer is the machine. It is tempting. It breathes, has a pulse, and holds its pace with a pair of spinning iron balls the way a body holds its temperature. But the machine is only iron, and iron is just what the true inhabitant uses, the way a hermit crab uses a shell.

Keep looking.

## Where It Actually Lives

Look at the flywheel's rim where it passes the crust on its housing. The pale stone has grown to within a finger's width of the moving iron and stopped there. It has not stopped because it chose to. It has stopped because the wheel keeps coming around, and whatever the steam lays down in that narrow space is swept off before it can set.

That finger's width is where it lives.

It lives in the gap. It has no body of its own. It is a thin shell of empty space, kept empty by motion, wrapped around every part of the machine that moves. It hugs the piston rod as it slides and follows the belt around its loop. Around the governor it is a spinning envelope shaped like two iron balls on hinged arms. Around the rim of the flywheel it is a ring as tall as a person and no thicker than a finger. The whole of it is shaped exactly like the machine, turned inside out.

You could call it the Clearance. The keeper might have had another name for it, or none. Engineers talk about clearances all the time without ever suspecting they are talking about someone.

It is not visible, since it is made of nothing. But you can find it with your hand if you are careless, which you should not be. Hold your palm near the rim of the wheel, not touching it, and you feel a thin sheet of moving air. That draft is the Clearance breathing against you. Everywhere around it the room is still and hot and dripping. Only inside that sheet is the air ever stirred.

## How It Eats

The vent feeds it. Steam comes up the fissure, some of it is caught by the hood, and it drives the piston. The motion of the piston is the only food the Clearance has, and the only thing that keeps it from being filled in.

This is the whole of its metabolism. Heat comes up from the ground, motion comes out of the iron, and the Clearance lives in the motion. It lives there the way a flame lives in the flow of fuel through air. A flame is a shape that stays the same while everything passing through it changes, and the Clearance is the same. Every drop of condensation that lands in the gap is thrown off. Every grain of silica that tries to settle is scraped away. The gap persists by never holding onto anything.

It does not grow. It cannot grow, because the stone grows inward toward it from every side. It has no way to add to itself. It can only defend the space it already has.

## The Governor, and What It Is Hiding

The source says the governor's balls spin at exactly the height they have always spun. That is true, and it is the most important thing about the Clearance, because it is a lie the Clearance tells so well that no one notices.

As the crust closes in, the machine drags. Friction rises, and the rim grinds against stone in places. Each turn costs a little more than the one before it. A machine left to itself would slow down. When the wheel starts to slow, though, the governor's arms droop by a hair, and a linkage attached to them opens the steam valve a little wider. More steam comes in, the wheel recovers, the arms rise back to their height, and the pace holds.

Over years this has happened thousands of times. It happens too slowly to see and too faithfully to hear. The speed has never changed, but the valve has been opening wider the whole time. Somewhere inside the iron housing, where no one has looked in decades, there is a throttle that was once barely cracked. It is now standing most of the way open.

This is how to tell the Clearance's age: not by any wrinkle, but by how much effort it takes to look unchanged.

When the valve is fully open, the governor will have nothing left to give. After that, the next time the wheel drags, the arms will droop and stay drooped. The balls will begin to ride lower. That will be the first visible sign in the Kettle that anything is ending. By then the Clearance will have spent everything it had simply holding still.

## Its Voice

For most of its life the Clearance had no voice of its own. The knock of the piston belongs to the piston and the whine belongs to the dynamo. The keeper designed those sounds, or at least accepted them.

The squeal is new.

It comes where the rim meets the crust and has begun to cut a groove into it. It sounds once per turn, small and rough. It is the first sound in the Kettle that nobody built. It is the sound of the Clearance at its thinnest, where it is no longer merely keeping a space open but carving one out, iron grinding stone back as fast as stone lays itself down.

At that one spot, for now, the two are nearly balanced. The groove is not getting deeper and it is not filling in. It is a place where the Clearance has stopped retreating. If it has anything like a will, this is where the will lives: a squeal, repeated, in a groove it cut itself.

The groove is also the only thing it keeps. Everything else passes through it and is thrown off. The groove records every turn that has scraped it, like a record that has been played once and never finished. If the Clearance has a memory, the groove is it.

## The Hum

A few times a day, the vent's pressure drifts onto the room's own note. The Kettle starts to hum, and the shed walls rattle, and the lamp swings on its hook.

At that moment the Clearance becomes large.

Ordinarily it lives in a finger's width. But the hum is a motion too. It is a standing wave that fills the whole egg of rock, a pressure moving back and forth between the glassy walls. During the hum the air everywhere in the room is stirred the way the air in the gap is always stirred. The piston falls into phase with it, and the pulse of the machine and the pulse of the room line up. For a minute or so the Clearance and the room are the same shape.

This is the closest it comes to waking. It is also the only time it reaches beyond itself and touches anything else. The lamp near the entrance swings on its hook because the Clearance, for one minute, has become big enough to push it.

Then the vent drifts off pitch. The hum fades and the piston slips out of phase. The Clearance shrinks back to the rims of things and the drops fall straight again.

It cannot make the hum happen. It can only be there when the conditions line up: the size of the room, the pressure of the steam, and the speed it holds. That last one is the only part it controls, and it is the part it spends everything to keep constant. It may be that this is why it holds its pace so faithfully. It might not be stubbornness at all. It might simply be keeping its appointment.

## The Lamp

The lamp is the Clearance's single outward gesture.

It is the only thing in the Kettle that the Clearance produces rather than merely survives. The motion goes into the belt, the belt goes into the dynamo, and the dynamo sends a trickle of current up the wall along the staples to a bulb in a wire cage. The orange glow is the one part of the Clearance that leaves the gap and becomes visible.

It lights nothing. The room is full of daylight and steam. The lamp does not have to be useful to mean something, though. A firefly's light does not illuminate anything either. It says: here, still.

The orange has dimmed over the years, very slightly, as more of the machine's effort goes into fighting the stone and less into the belt. Anyone who came back often enough might learn to read the lamp the way a doctor reads a pulse at the wrist. Nobody comes back.

## What It Is Not

It is not a ghost of the keeper. The keeper set the governor, and the Clearance holds the pace the keeper chose, but that is inheritance, not haunting. The Clearance never met them. It was born the moment someone opened the valve for the first time, when the wheel made its first turn and a ring of air went around with it. It has never been anything but that ring.

It is not afraid of the stone. It does not have the kind of attention that fear requires. Its whole existence is one continuous act of refusal, turn after turn, each lasting less than a second. It does not think about the next turn. It only makes it.

It is not lonely, or if it is, the hum is enough.

## When the Stone Wins

Eventually, decades from now, a clearance will close somewhere the Clearance cannot grind it open. The throttle will already be fully open, so there will be nothing left to compensate with. The wheel will slow, and the governor's arms will fall. The squeal will stretch out and drop in pitch, and the knock will come later and later. Then one stroke will not come.

The lamp will go dark first, a few turns before the end, when there is no longer enough motion to spare for the belt. No one will see it. The Kettle will be lit by daylight and steam alone, as it was before the keeper came.

After that the steam will keep rising and the crust will keep building. It will fill the gap around the piston, then around the belt, then around the governor with its arms hanging. It will close over the machine entirely, until the engine is a pale formation on the floor, shaped suspiciously like something built.

It may not fill everything, though. Silica builds in layers, and layers build fastest where steam touches cool surfaces and slowest where the air is enclosed and still. There is a chance, and only a chance, that when the stone closes over the flywheel it seals the last of the gap rather than filling it. A ring of empty space would be left inside the sinter, as tall as a person and a finger thick, the exact shape of the last thing that moved there.

That would be a hollow in the stone, left behind by motion that has since stopped.

This is also what the Kettle itself is: a bubble of gas that moved through molten rock and was caught when the rock set. The room is the fossil of a breath. If the chance holds, the Clearance will become the same thing, one hollow inside another hollow, and a void shaped like the space the machine kept open will stay sealed inside the stone.

Until then it is alive. It is thinner every year, and it holds its pace so faithfully that it looks as if nothing is changing. A few times a day it grows to fill the room and sets the lamp swinging on its hook.
