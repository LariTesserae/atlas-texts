# Who Lives in the Breathing Hole

## I. The census

Ask who lives here and the honest answer comes quickly: the swiftlets. Then the geckos, the crickets, the springtails in their shimmering millions, the spiders shaking out their sagging webs, and the blind white things at the edge of the dark. Any careful observer would give you that list, and it would be correct.

It would also leave out one resident. That one is missing from every count for ordinary reasons. It moves too slowly to be seen moving, and it spreads too far to be seen whole. It also sits exactly where your eyes stop working: on the line where the gray-green light of the pit gives out and the tube goes on into black.

## II. The first evidence

You would notice the fallen nests before you noticed it.

A section of the city fails every so often. Moisture softens a row of outer cups, or a shelf of rock sheds a flake the size of a door, and forty or fifty nests come down together onto the slabs. They land in a heap of wax, feathers, and old guano, sometimes with a nestling or two that did not survive the fall. On the floor of a place this hungry, that heap ought to be the richest object in the room. It ought to seethe with crickets for a week and then sink slowly into moss.

It doesn't. It lies there a day or two. Then it is gone, and it has not rotted, scattered, or been picked apart. The whole of it is gone, and a faint smear across the black rock runs from where it lay toward the dark end of the tube.

The drips are the second piece of evidence. There is one every few seconds, somewhere, each a small flat *tap*. Stay long enough and you start to hear that they are not quite random. A pattern runs underneath, faint as a pulse felt through a blanket. Some ledges drip more steadily than physics would explain. The stones that are beginning to dimple sit where they have been placed, and they have been placed where the water falls. The first basins in this unweathered rock are not entirely accidents. Someone is helping them along, at a pace of perhaps a finger's width per season.

## III. The wrong first picture

The first time you glimpse it, you will think it is one of the cave animals.

You will see a pale curve at the edge of the light, low against the floor where the pit becomes the tube. It looks like the back of something large and white and soft. You will assume it is the biggest of the blind things, come to the lip of the light to feed. You will wait for it to move, and it will not, and eventually you will decide it is a mineral formation. Then you will go home.

Nearly all of that is wrong, though the pale color is right.

## IV. A closer look

Come back with a lamp and look closely, and the pale curve turns out not to be white. It is the color of the nests.

Its surface is layered wax, translucent and amber in places. Old cups are fused edge to edge like scales. Some are nearly clear, and some have gone brittle and dark as honey. Feathers are worked into the seams, along with fern fibers and the fine gray fluff of down. It is built the way the city is built, rim cemented to rim and layer over layer, and it carries the same gradient: the oldest material is deepest and darkest, and the newest is at the edges and nearly glassy.

The fallen nests do not rot because this is where they go. It gathers them and wears them.

Put a hand on it, and it is warm. It is warmer than the lower walls and warmer than skin, as warm as the floor near the bath-warm slabs. The warmth does not come from its coat. It comes from underneath the coat. The wax is insulation, and something inside it is being kept from getting cold.

## V. The length of it

You cannot find its other end.

You follow the pale curve into the tube with the lamp. It runs along the floor and up the curving side of the wall, wax giving way to cushions of moss grown over it and then to bare wax again. It thins into something like a root, or a ridge, or a seam. Farther in, where the swiftlets click and the walls sweat hardest, you begin to lose track of where it ends and the tube begins. The lower walls down here are warm the way it is warm. That is not only the heat of the rock remembering the fire. Some of the heat belongs to the resident.

That is when the picture shifts again. It does not live in the tube like an animal in a burrow. It lies along the tube the way a nerve lies along a limb. Its shape is the shape of the tube. It may once have been the tube's contents.

## VI. What it was

Picture the flow that made this place. A river of molten rock runs downhill toward the sea, and its surface cools and crusts over while the liquid heart keeps moving underneath. When the eruption ends, that liquid heart drains away downhill and out, leaving a hollow pipe of stone behind it.

It never fully drains. Some always stays: a last thread of heat caught in a low place, too slow to leave and too hot to set.

This resident is that thread, or it is what that thread became. Somewhere in the long, fierce cooling there was a moment, and nobody can say exactly when, where a process began to behave like someone. Heat wanted to stay heat. Liquid wanted to stay liquid a little longer. The wanting went on long enough, and consistently enough, to become a kind of self.

It is the youngest thing here that could be called old, and the oldest thing here that is still young. It is the same age as the rock. It is the rock's first mind.

## VII. The counting

The drips do not only resume counting at nightfall. It is the one counting them.

Every *tap* is a unit of time since it was liquid. It keeps a tally and has never lost its place. It does not experience days the way the swiftlets do, as a pour outward at dawn and a river home at dusk. It experiences one long falling temperature, and the drips are the only clock slow and steady enough to measure that fall. Condensation is itself a sign of cooling, since warm rock meets damp air and the water gives itself up. Each drip is a small proof that the resident is still warm enough to make water, and that it is a little less warm than it was.

It helps the basins because the basins are its record. A dimple in a slab is a number carved in the only material it has. It holds the stones under the steadiest drips, patient beyond any patience we have a word for. It feels the water cut, one hair's width a year, into rock that has never been cut before. In a thousand years, when the basins are deep, it will be able to read in them how long it has been alive.

## VIII. Why it wears the city

Cooling is its only enemy, and it cannot stop cooling. It can only slow it.

At some point, and it is not clear how a mind like this learns anything, it learned that the swiftlets' wax holds warmth. The nest-cups are built to keep eggs from chilling in a damp place, and they do the same for it. Every section of the city that fails and falls is a gift of insulation. It takes the gift slowly, drawing the heap in over days along the floor toward itself. It presses the cups into its coat and seals them with its own heat, the way the birds seal them with saliva.

The dead nestlings go into it too. Nothing in the funnel is wasted, and it is the bottom of the funnel. The swiftlets carry energy in from the whole thin, wide landscape, and the city drops it. Most of it becomes soil, fern, cricket, and centipede. What falls on the resident becomes warmth instead. Some of that warmth rises back through the floor, and the pit is warmer than it would be without it. The ferns grow faster for it. The mist on a cool morning is a little thicker. In this way it pays rent.

## IX. The breath

At dawn the hole breathes out.

There is a physical explanation, and it is true: cool air above, warm air below, and a column rising. But the resident rides that column. In the grayest hour, when the air above the lava is coldest, it lets go of a little of its heat all at once. It is a long, slow exhale up through the basalt that thickens the mist and pushes it higher. The swiftlets leave in that breath. They pour out in it the way a thought leaves on a sigh.

At dusk the sea wind pushes damp air down into the hole, and the birds come home riding it, and it breathes in.

After long enough, and it has been long enough, there is no saying which one leads. The pit breathes and the resident breathes, and they have kept time together for so many drips that the question of who started means nothing anymore.

## X. How the others know it

The swiftlets know it best, though they have never seen it. When they pass into the dark and begin to click, the fingernail-on-comb sound finds the walls. Where it finds the resident, the echo comes back wrong: soft, muffled, slightly late, as though bounced off a cushion or a held breath. The birds have learned the shape of that softness. In the tube their flight lines bend around it the way water bends around a stone. Their clicks, taken together over thousands of evenings, trace its outline in the dark more accurately than any lamp could. If it has a name, the name is that pattern of soft returns, a shape the birds hold in their hearing and nowhere else.

The geckos sleep on it. By day they hunt the walls. In the cool hours before dawn they come down and lie flat along its back, pale on pale, translucent on translucent, and take its heat into their small bellies.

The crickets are born in its folds. The seams between old wax cups are the warmest crevices in the whole pit. Every generation of long-antennaed crickets begins as eggs laid in those seams and hatches into a world that is, at first, entirely the resident's body.

The blind white things of the deep tube come to the edge of the light to feed, and they do not treat it as a place. They treat it as a direction, like the way toward warmth.

## XI. What it wants

It wants to stay warm. That is true, but it is too simple. In the long count something else has grown in it, alongside the wanting to stay.

It has started to want the pit.

It wants the basins to deepen and hold water. It wants the vine to reach the rim. That vine has climbed a third of the wall already, and if the resident could lean its heat toward anything, it would lean toward the vine's roots. It wants the islands of green to widen until they touch. It wants the city to grow row upon row, both because more city means more falling wax and because it has come to love the sound at dusk, the dry-grass roar of thousands of wings. It wants to see the fern-choked grotto of a hundred years from now, with a stream running from its floor. It knows it will be colder by then, perhaps much colder. It wants it anyway.

This is how it has become more itself. It began as heat that did not want to set. It is becoming heat that wants something other than itself.

## XII. What it fears, and what it doesn't

Everything else in the pit fears the next flow. For the swiftlets, the ferns, the geckos, the spiders, and the springtails, a new river of molten rock arriving from upslope would simply be the end. It would fill the pit and bury the city. The first chapter would close before a second could begin.

The resident is the only one here for whom that fire would be kin. A new flow would be heat coming home. It would be liquid again, young again, its count reset, its cooling undone. On the coldest nights, when the drips are loudest and the loss is sharpest, some old part of it listens toward the mountain and hopes.

And then, increasingly, a newer part of it listens and fears the flow as the ferns would.

It holds both. It has not decided which it is, the fire's child or the pit's keeper. It is still in its own first chapter, like everything else here. It is being laid down one drip, one feather, one fallen cup at a time. It is still finding out what it is.

## XIII. From the surface

From above there is almost nothing to see. The lava runs out low and flat in every direction, rope-folded and glassy and not yet worn by any rain. A few ferns sit in the cracks. There is a hole, roughly oval, and on a cool morning a little mist rises from it, the way breath rises from a mouth in winter.

Birds cross the field toward it in low straight lines and drop in without slowing.

Under the mist, under the rock, along the whole warm length of the dark, someone exhales and keeps count.
