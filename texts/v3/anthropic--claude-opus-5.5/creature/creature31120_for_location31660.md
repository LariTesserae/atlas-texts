# The One Who Breathes at Eleven

## What the List Leaves Out

The inventory is honest as far as it goes. There are voles in the moss, ptarmigan in the rime, ravens on the headframe, a moose now and then, springtails beyond counting. All of it is true, and all of it was written by people who could hear. That shaped the list the way the rim shapes the light. They wrote down the things that seemed to be *enduring* the noise. They left out the one thing that lives in the bowl because of it.

It has no name. It could not have one. Nobody who worked the fan houses could speak a word near a shaft and be understood, so everything important got a sign instead. The sign for a bearing going bad was a fist rocked on its knuckles. The sign for gas was two fingers drawn under the nose. The sign for the other thing was this: a flat hand held palm-down at chest height, lowered slowly, as if pressing something into water, and then, just as slowly, allowed to rise again.

The old attendants still make it without thinking when the subject comes up, in kitchens and at funerals a long way from the bowl. The hand goes down and comes back up. If you time it, and nobody ever does, it takes about eleven seconds.

## How It Sees

Begin with the fact that it has never heard anything, in the way that a mole has never seen a sky.

For every other creature in the basin, the roar is a wall. For the voles it is a pressure they tunnel beneath. For the ravens it is weather they have decided not to mind. For the one that lives here, the roar is *light*. It is the medium by which the world becomes visible.

It feels sound through its whole body the way a leaf takes in the sun. The three fans are its three suns. They never set. It has no night in the sense we would mean. It has something like seasons, when the authority shuts one shaft for a week to replace a bearing and a third of its sky goes dark, and it draws in toward the two that remain.

The sound off the rim is not an echo to it. The reflection off the near scarp and the far scarp, meeting itself in the middle distance, draws a pattern across the basin floor, and that pattern is its landscape. There are nodes where the waves cancel into something like dimness. There are antinodes where they pile into a blaze. The pattern is fixed by the shape of the bowl and the placement of the shafts. It shifts only when a fan's pitch shifts, or when the ground settles and the scarps move and the whole geometry of reflection turns a few degrees. The thing knows that pattern the way you know the rooms of the house you grew up in. It rests in the dim places. It crosses the bright places quickly, or not at all.

This is why no one ever came upon it. The attendants walked the straight lines between shafts, along the power line, on duckboards long since swallowed. That route crosses the antinodes, the hot seams of noise. It is the one route it would never take.

## What It Looks Like, Insofar As It Looks Like Anything

The description of the place gives it away without meaning to. *You cannot tell whether a dark shape across the moss is a thicket two hundred metres away or a boulder twenty metres away until you are almost upon it.*

Some of those shapes are neither.

It is low and long and the colour of the ground, because it is mostly made of the ground. Its back is a mat. Sphagnum grows there, then feather moss over the sphagnum, the same slow sequence as everywhere else in the bowl, because it lies still for so long that the moss cannot tell it from the peat. Dwarf birch roots in the hollows along its spine. Cloudberry leaves spread palm-sized over its flanks. In the oldest parts of its body there may be lenses of ice. It is cold-blooded in a way that makes cold-blooded animals seem feverish.

Where the ravens perch on it there is orange *Xanthoria*, and this is almost the only way to spot it from the rim. On a clear day, with binoculars, you may find a small bright scatter of lichen out in the middle of the moss, on no stone and no stump, where no bird has any reason to sit. The ravens know. Ravens always know. They land on it the way they land on the evasé lips, for the warmth, because it has warmth of a kind, and because in winter it goes and lies in the plume.

## The Plume

This is the one indulgence it allows itself.

Mine air comes up at eight or nine degrees, wet, smelling of stone. To a creature that has made a life of cold, this is the equatorial sea. On the worst nights of the year, when the air sliding off the plateau pools so dense in the hollows that the frost grows visibly, it leaves its dim places and moves out along one of the three green comets. It lies full length in the tongue of vapour and lets the mine breathe on it.

By morning it is rimed like everything else, feathered windward in white ice an inch thick, a long bristling shape in a field of long bristling shapes. The ptarmigan sit on it. It does not mind them. They are nearly weightless, and they are the same colour as the frost.

Part of why the plumes are so lush is this. For sixty years something has been lying in them, turning the peat by its slow shifting, sharing its long patience with the ferns that grow nowhere else for a hundred kilometres. The authority's botanist attributes it all to the water. She is mostly right.

## Keeping Level

Now the monuments.

Someone comes once a year, in the few weeks when the moss is thawed enough to walk and frozen enough to bear weight. He carries a level and a staff and a clipboard in a plastic sleeve, wears his ear defenders the whole day, and eats lunch in the lee of the headframe with the ravens watching. He measures every numbered pin. Most of them do what they should. They sink a finger's width, or two, or occasionally a hand's width after a wet autumn. The numbers go down.

A few, every year, go *up*.

Not far. Three centimetres, four. Always in the same broad region of the basin, the dim quiet band between the second and third shafts, which is the one place where the interference pattern is gentle enough to live in. He writes *DISTURBED — FROST HEAVE?* in the margin. Frost heave is real, and it does lift things, and it is the obvious answer. He has been writing it for nineteen years. In the privacy of his truck on the drive home, he has admitted to himself that frost heave does not usually move in a line.

Here is what is actually happening. The ground goes down a finger's width a year. The moss grows up a finger's width a year. The thing that lives here has made its whole existence out of that equation. It is, in the most literal sense, a living thing treading water. Where it lies, it grows up from its own dead lower half at exactly the rate the earth withdraws beneath it, and so it stays where it has always been. It holds the same height relative to the rim, the same angle to the three suns, the same place in the pattern. It is not resisting the sinking. It is keeping pace with it, the way you keep pace with someone walking beside you.

When it shifts its weight, the peat around it flexes, and a pin rides up on a pedestal of disturbed moss. The surveyor writes *DISTURBED*. It is the most accurate word he uses all year.

## Eleven Seconds

The attendants say the beat between the fans has a period of about eleven seconds, and that after enough years you breathe to it.

They are right about the breathing and wrong about the fans.

The three fans are old machines. Their speeds wander. Motor bearings wear, blades are replaced, the voltage on the long sagging line sags further in the cold. The beat between two drifting frequencies should drift too, speeding up and slowing down over the months, sometimes vanishing altogether as the fans fall into phase. Anyone who has stood between two idling engines knows how that throb slides around.

In the bowl it does not slide. It sits at eleven seconds, year on year. The engineers who noticed this, and there were two over the decades, both put it down to the resonance of the basin. They reasoned that the bowl had a natural period, and the noise fell into it the way a swing falls into the push of a hand. That reasoning is also mostly right.

What sets the resonance of the basin is the basin. Here is something the inventory could not have listed, because it has no name and no call. Every eleven seconds, very slowly, the thing that lives in the bowl draws in, and the living mat of its back lowers by the thickness of a leaf. Then it rises again. The flat hand goes down, the flat hand comes up. Across all the dim ground where it lies, and that ground is wider than anyone would guess, the moss flexes, and the flexing changes how the roar comes back off the rim. The echoes lock to it. The fans' drift is pulled, gently and continually, back into its rhythm.

The men and women who worked here for thirty years did not learn to breathe to the machinery. They learned to breathe to *it*. They were standing on its back and did not know, and their bodies knew. Some of them say they still wake at night in quiet houses in the south and lie there counting, and the count comes out to eleven, and they do not know what it is they miss.

## The Knock

In the oldest of the three fans there is a knock every revolution. It has been there so long it is on no one's list.

It is on the thing's list. To a creature that sees by pressure, the knock is a small hard point of brightness, regular as a lighthouse. It sits off to one side of the blaze of the oldest sun. For sixty years it has been the one fixed star it steers by in the long dim middle of the bowl. It is how it knows which way is east, the way the green comets point. If the authority ever sends a crew to find and fix that knock, the thing will lose its bearings for a season. It will wander. The surveyor will find pins disturbed in places they have never been disturbed before, and he will write *FROST HEAVE?* with a larger question mark.

## Where It Came From

It is young. That is the part hardest to hold on to, looking at something mossed and lichened and apparently as old as peat.

It was not there before the mine. There was nothing like it on the plateau, where the spruce stood on well-drained sand and the only sounds were wind and ravens and the snap of frost in the trunks. It began when the ground began to settle and the water stayed and the moss took its chance. The fans began at the same time, and sound came into the world as a thing you could live in. It grew out of the coincidence of the two: a new cold, wet, sinking country with three suns that never set. As far as anyone can say, it is the only one of its kind, because there is only one place like this.

It grows the way the basin grows, at the edge. Where a new crack opens in the plateau and a new strip of spruce begins its slow lean into the bowl, it goes and lies against the foot of the fresh scarp in the cold talus. Something happens there that is like feeding and like being born. The fallen trees, head-down and still green, are laid across its back. Their lower branches root into it. Over years they become part of the ring-thickets that rise wherever it has lain long enough. It does not eat the forest. It *layers* it. It takes the drowned and falling spruce into itself and sends them up again as new stems, as the spruce were already trying to do.

So it is a little larger every decade. The bowl is wider every decade. Perhaps the two things are not separate.

## When the Fans Stop

The inventory ends on the thought that one day the fans will stop. The roar will go out of the bowl all at once, and for the first time in a century you will be able to hear what the place actually sounds like: wind in dwarf birch, the creak of the frost, water moving under moss.

It will be something else first.

When the three suns go out together, the thing that lives in the bowl will be in the dark for the first time in its life. It will not know where the rim is, or the plumes, or the knock, or east. It will lie very still in whatever dim place it happens to be. For some hours, maybe days, it will go on doing the one thing it has always done without needing to see.

It will breathe.

In that new quiet, standing on the rim, you will hear it. It will not sound like a voice or a call or anything a songbird or a moose would recognise. It will be the whole floor of the basin drawing in, a long soft sigh of water retreating through sphagnum across a square kilometre of moss. The birch will tick as it settles. The frost will creak along its spine. Then, eleven seconds later, the moss will lift and the water will return to every print and hollow, and there will be a sound like the tide coming in over a country that has never seen the sea.

The old attendants say they cannot imagine the quiet. Some say they would not want to hear it. That is true in a way they do not quite mean. They have heard it already, every day for thirty years, underneath the roar. They breathed to it. They pressed it into their hands and let it rise again. Some part of them knows that when the fans stop, what comes up out of the bowl will not be silence.

It will be the tenant, breathing at eleven, in the dark, waiting to learn what the world looks like without the suns.
