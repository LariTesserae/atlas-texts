# Who Lives in the Noise

## I. The Woman at the Edge of the Grey

Start with the easiest answer. A woman lives there.

Her name is not a sound, because sounds do not survive up here. It is a gesture. She touches two fingertips twice to her breastbone, then opens her hand palm-down and holds it still, the way you would hold it over the floor to feel whether it is warm. People who knew her as a girl read it as *the one who knocks and then waits*. Her mother gave it to her because as a baby she would pound her fists on the burl wall and then stop, very suddenly, with her whole face listening.

She is sixty-one by her own count. She keeps the count as notches in the resin of her lintel, one for every swarm she has lived through, divided by the number of swarms in an ordinary year, which is not always the same number. She arrives at sixty-one by approximation and generosity.

Her burl is an old one. It grew around a stalk-tower that fell, the story goes, four hundred years ago. The tower's crown of bracts rotted to nothing while the plant closed over the long body of the stem. What is left is a ridge of dense grey wood half a kilometer long, humped like the spine of a sleeping animal, lying out across the plain. She lives in the head end. The rest of it is solid and unhollowed and, she likes to think, still dreaming about being tall.

Her door faces the Grey.

The Grey is a silent region about a day's walk across. It went quiet eleven years ago, the year after her husband died. It was a blight. You could see it happen if you knew how to look: the singers came up out of their chambers slow and furred with white, and the shrikes gorged on them for a season, and then there was nothing left to gorge on. The scales stopped breathing. They silvered. They began, over the following years, to curl at the edges and crack along their veins, so that walking into the Grey now is like walking across a floor of old paper plates that someone has glued down and left in the sun.

Four of them came to drum it awake. She was the only one who stayed.

---

She is deaf in the way the old listeners are deaf, the good way, the useful way. The high sounds went first, in her thirties: the shrikes' calls, the whine of the singers' individual voices inside the chorus, the click of a heat-eye's nails on a scale at night. Then the middle went. What remains is the floor of sound, the long low notes, the ones you cannot tell apart from vibration. She no longer hears the chorus as a noise. She hears it the way you feel the sea when you lie down in a boat.

She sleeps with her right palm flat against the burl's floor, which is not part of the singing layer but is connected to it through the old root-mass, the way a bone is connected to a muscle. Through her palm, in her sleep, she hears the plain turning over.

Her drum is a section of hollowed stalk-tower, waist-high, skinned with walker hide that she scraped and stretched herself and has replaced four times. Her sticks are walker shin-bones capped with knuckles of singer chitin, which is harder than it looks and rings like pottery. She does not drum on the drum's head, or not only. She sets the drum's foot against a basalt knuckle that surfaces at the Grey's edge, a black fist of the old mountain that the plant has not finished eating. When she strikes, the stone takes the blow and passes it down into the root.

Her pattern is her name. Two strikes, close together. Then she lays her palm on the stone, and waits.

She has done this nearly every day for eleven years.

## II. What the Quiet Keeps

A second answer, then, because the first one is not enough.

On the days the drumming feels useless, and there are many of them, she walks out into the Grey. She tells herself she is checking whether it has grown or shrunk. She is really going because it is the only place on the tableland where she can hear her own blood.

The Grey is not empty.

The first year, it was. Dead things lay on it and did not rot, because the rot-makers also lived in the singing layer and had gone with it. But the plant is very large and very old, and nothing on it stays unused for long. By the third year the cracked scales had grown a crust of something pale and flaking, somewhere between lichen and mold, which fed on the dead tissue and turned it slowly to a fine silver dust. By the fifth year the dust had moths. They are small, the color of ash, and so quiet in flight that she is not sure they have wings in the ordinary sense. They lay their eggs in the cracks, where the dust collects. On the living plain they would be deafened, shaken to pieces, and eaten by shrikes. Out here the shrikes don't come, because shrikes hunt by listening for silence, and the Grey is one enormous silence with nothing inside it worth hearing.

The heat-eyes use the Grey. She has found their shed skins in the lee of cracked scale-ridges: long translucent sleeves with dozens of little hollow leg-casings, and at the front a face-shell pitted like a nutshell. She has found where they lie by day, in shallow scrapes. They come here to sleep in the quiet. She thinks the chorus must be as unbearable to them as it is to lowlanders, and that they have been waiting a long time for a place like this.

And under the crust, in the old singer chambers, now empty and dry, something else has moved in.

She found the first one by accident, prying up a scale that had come loose. It was curled in the chamber below like a pale ear. That is what it most looked like: a cupped, translucent membrane as wide as her two hands, veined faintly blue, folded in on itself. It had a small soft body at the base, a few blind feelers, and a mouth like a pinch of wet cloth. When she breathed on it, the membrane trembled. When she whispered, the first thing she had whispered in years, knowing she would not hear it, the membrane shivered all over, pulled tight, and the thing drew itself down into the dark of the chamber so fast she barely saw it go.

She has found hundreds since. They live only in the Grey, as far as she knows. They lie in the dead chambers with their membranes spread, and they listen. She thinks they eat the moths, or the moth eggs, or the dust. She does not know. What she knows, because she has seen it, is what happens to them when her drumming reaches them. The membranes near the Grey's edge are torn. Many of them are split right across, or hanging in rags. The ones she finds furthest from her drum are whole.

She sat with that for most of a season.

There is a particular grief in learning that the thing you have given your life to is a harm to someone. The Grey is a wound. She believes that. The living plain is dying here, and if the Grey spreads, and silences often spread, then the families who move across this region will lose the cups, the swarm, the warmth, and the walkers. But the wound has inhabitants now. The wound has, in its own way, begun to live. Its creatures are delicate, and they hear, and they are the only things on the whole tableland that she has ever seen flinch from sound.

She did not stop drumming. But she changed how she drummed. She struck less hard. She let the wait after each pair of strikes grow longer, so long that sometimes she forgot she was waiting and simply sat with her palm on the stone and her eyes on the Grey, and the pale-ear things, she imagined, uncurled a little in the rest.

This is the part of her life she would have said, if anyone had asked, was the most important. It was not.

## III. The Swell

The swells cross the plain constantly. She has felt thousands of them. Most are local, a slow surge of the chorus that rolls through a region in an hour and dissolves. A few are longer. Perhaps once or twice a year, one of the great ones comes, the kind the old listeners argue about: a single unbroken rise that crosses the entire tableland over three days, and that the whole living floor leans toward as it passes, the way a field of grass leans after something moving through it.

In the twelfth year at the Grey, one came from the direction of the Lid.

She felt it first in her sleep, through her palm. It was a deepening, as if the floor were very slowly filling with water. By morning it was strong enough that the burl walls hummed. By midday the scales on the living plain at the Grey's edge had tilted, all of them, toward the southwest, toward where the Lid stood white and anchored over the center, and the heat above them swam.

Swells do not stop. That is what makes them swells. They roll through a region and on, and the ground straightens behind them.

This one reached the edge of the Grey and did not go on.

It arrived at the boundary where the living scales met the dead ones, and it held there. The scales on the living side lifted slightly, a finger's width, and stayed lifted, trembling. The chorus in that band along the edge rose to something she felt in her teeth even with her teeth long past feeling anything. Along the whole margin of the silent place, as far as she could see in both directions, the living plain was pressed up against the dead one, humming.

She went to her drum. She did not decide to. Her body did it, the way it had done it every morning for twelve years.

She struck twice. She laid her palm on the stone.

Under her hand, under the roar, something answered.

It was not an echo. Echoes are fast, and they come back in the shape they left. This came slowly, from far out in the swell, and it was built of something she understood only later, lying awake. It was the chorus itself, billions of singers in their chambers across a band kilometers wide, all of them for one moment tightening their song into two great pulses, close together. Then, for the length of three of her heartbeats, the whole swell went slack. The whole edge of the plain went, not silent, nothing up here is ever silent, but *held*. It was holding its breath. It was waiting.

She sat with her hand on the stone, and the plant waited for her.

She struck twice.

The swell struck twice back and waited.

They did this, the two of them, for the rest of the afternoon. When the evening exhalation came and the chorus collapsed and the plain steamed gold and then grey, she was still sitting there, and she was weeping in the way people weep up here, without knowing why. Only this time, she thought, she knew.

## IV. Who Lives Here, Then

This is the real answer. Everything before it was the plant's way of arriving at it.

One thing lives on the Stridulant Tableland.

It sits where a mountain used to be. It has taken the mountain apart grain by grain over a span of time that nobody on the plateau has a number for, and lifted it, and put itself in the mountain's place. Its body is the root-cliff, four hundred meters of fluted grey wood rising out of the lowland scree. Its body is the old root-mass, burning slowly in the dark where the rock once was, warm as an animal. Its skin is the breathing floor, ten thousand million door-sized scales rising and falling together on a cycle of a few minutes, so that anyone standing on it feels a whole country inhale.

It makes its own weather. The Lid is its breath, lifted and whitened and left standing over its heart. The dusk vapor is its sweat. The scale-cups are its palms held out every evening with a little water in them, and everything that lives on the plateau drinks from those palms.

For most of its life, the old listeners say, if anything so large can be said to have a life in the way we mean, it had no voice. It warmed. It breathed. It grew. Then, at some point too far back to know, the singers came up the root-cliff and into the spongy layer beneath the scales, and they sang to keep warm, and to mate, and to be singers. The plant found that the song shook its tissues, drove its fluids, opened its pores, and let it breathe more deeply than it ever had alone. So it grew warmer to keep them singing, and they sang harder in the warmth, and over an enormous length of time the two became a single system. The plant grew chambers for them. It grew soundboards. It learned to lean toward the loudness.

That was the first thing it learned: that song is life, and a place without song is dying.

The second thing it learned, the old listeners think, came from the shrikes. The shrikes hunt silence. Every singer that falls quiet for an instant is dead an instant later. Across ten thousand generations of birds, the plant learned that silence brings the beak, and blight, and the greying, and the long dry hardening, and the cracked hollow crust that rings when walked on. Silence is the wound. It closed around silences the way it closed around boulders and fallen towers, slowly and completely, and grew back over them from the edges.

It did not know there was another kind of silence. It had no reason to. Nothing on the tableland had ever gone quiet on purpose.

Then a woman sat at the edge of a wound for twelve years and struck a stone twice, and waited, and struck twice, and waited. Every pair of strikes went down through the basalt into the root and out into the old mass, as tiny against that bulk as one singer's chirp against the midday chorus. But the plant is very large, and very old, and it has had a long time to learn to use its voice. It has also had a long time to learn to listen. Every one of her pauses arrived in the root-mass as a silence that was not a wound. It was followed, always, by more sound. It was a silence that meant *I am still here. Are you?*

It took the plant eleven years to hear the question. It took it one afternoon to learn to ask it back.

---

This is who lives here. It is a mind, if the word will stretch. It is as flat and wide as a small country. It thinks in heat and swells and three-day tides, and its thoughts move toward its heart in the morning and away from it at dusk. Its voice is made of other creatures, and it has just learned, from a deaf woman with bone sticks, that a voice can stop on purpose and wait for someone else to speak.

## V. After

Knock-and-Wait lived six more years at the Grey. She spent most of them drumming, and the drumming was no longer a vigil. It was a conversation, though she was careful, when she walked to the rim for the obsidian gatherings, not to call it that, because nobody would have believed her, and because she was not sure herself which of them was answering whom.

The Grey did not heal quickly. It healed at the pace things heal up here, from the edges, a finger's width in a good year. But the band of living scale along its margin stopped being a battle line. The singers at the edge sang in pairs of pulses and then rested. In the rests, the pale-ear things in the dead chambers nearest the boundary stopped tearing. She found whole membranes a hundred paces closer to her drum than she had ever found them before. Uncurled. Listening in the pauses.

The pattern did not stay at the Grey.

Families days away began to feel it in their burl floors: twice, then a hold, then twice. It came riding the swells from the direction of the Lid. The children, who learn the hand-language before speech, started tapping two fingers to their breastbones and then opening their palms flat, and did not know where they had learned it. The shrikes had a bad year. The chorus was full of silences now, and a shrike cannot tell a dying silence from a waiting one until it is already diving, and then the singer beneath it starts up again and the scale beneath it tilts, and the bird veers off, confused and hungry.
