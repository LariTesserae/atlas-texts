# Who Lives in the Upper Reach

## The Tenants

Start with the people. They are the easiest to see, and if you ask, they will tell you they are the ones who live here. They are wrong, but only partly.

There are not many of them. A few hundred, perhaps, though counting is difficult for reasons that will become clear. Below the cloud they are called the white-eyed, or the Reach-folk. Among themselves they have no name, because a name is a thing you call out, and nothing called out travels here.

You know them first by their eyes. Everyone who stays grows the squint, and the squint never lets go. The skin at the corners folds into deep, fine creases, pale inside where the sun can't reach. The irises fade over the years, as though the light has been slowly rinsing them. An old Reacher's eyes are the grey of the lichen, and they look at you through a gap no wider than a coin's edge. Their faces are shaped by it. They seem always to be wincing at something just behind your shoulder.

They wear the dust. It gets into everything, the fine white powder of fresh castings, and they gave up fighting it generations ago. Their clothes, their hair, the creases of their knuckles are all the same bleached nothing-colour as the slopes. Standing still against a cliff face, a Reacher nearly vanishes. Only the dark of their mouths gives them away, and the small wet gleam where their eyes should be.

They do not build. That is the first thing to understand about them. Everything here is already built, three and four layers deep, and the Reachers regard building the way you might regard shouting in a library. They occupy. They live in the gaps. A family might take the hollow beneath a pipe-bundle, where the curve of the pipes makes a roof and the water inside keeps the air cool and faintly murmuring. Another lives on a catwalk platform beneath a stairway that climbs to nothing, and they have hung cloths along the rails. The cloths hold no walls. They catch the morning sweat. Nobody owns anything larger than they can carry. They are tenants, and they know it, and they are careful.

## How They Speak

Sound dies at fifty paces, so the Reachers have never had a crowd.

Think about what that means. No one has ever addressed more than the people standing near them. There are no speeches, no proclamations, no voice that carries across a square, because there are no squares, only the packed dense spaces between castings where no one is ever more than an arm's length from something made. Every conversation is close. Every word is spoken near enough that you could touch the speaker.

So all their speech is intimate, whether they mean it to be or not. A Reacher telling you where the good lichen grows speaks in the same low register a lover would use. Their language has adapted. It is full of small sounds, clicks and breaths and a soft percussive tapping against iron that carries better than voice through the cast rock. They say that if you put your ear to a pipe-bundle in the early morning you can hear the whole valley talking. Taps travel along the metal for miles, one family passing news to the next, a slow ripple of gossip moving at the speed of patience.

They have no leaders, because a leader would have to be near everyone at once. Decisions travel like the taps, hand to hand, whisper to whisper. Sometimes a decision reaches the far end of the valley changed, reversed, or answered before it was asked. They find this unremarkable.

## What They Eat, and How

They eat the ibex. They eat the hares from the pipe-bundles, and sometimes lizards, and the dense moss of the gutter-pools, boiled to a green paste. They drink the sweat of the iron. Each morning, before the beads run together, the children go out with rags and wipe the cool faces of the mold-halves and wring the rags into jars. It is the first chore anyone learns. The water tastes faintly of metal and faintly of the kiln, and a Reacher who goes below the cloud will complain for weeks that the water there tastes of nothing.

When they butcher an ibex, they open it along its seam.

This is the rule, and it is kept without exception. The seam runs from the crown of the skull down the spine to the tail. A knife laid into it parts the animal cleanly, the two halves falling open like the mold they came from. Inside, the ibex is real. That is what unsettles the Reachers still, after all these generations. The meat is red and warm. The heart is a heart. The lungs are pink and fold like wet cloth. They expected, perhaps, something pale and fine-grained all the way through, and every time they find blood, and every time they are quiet for a moment before they go on.

No one cuts across a seam. Asked why, a Reacher will look at you through the coin's-edge gap and not answer. Then they will touch, briefly and without seeming to notice, the back of their own neck.

## How They Find Their Way

Landmarks lie. The Reachers learned this before anything else.

The peak you camped under comes around again ahead of you. The lichen sigil on the cliff repeats a hundred times, so seeing it tells you nothing. The towns across the valley are always across the valley. So the Reachers do not navigate by what they see. They navigate by count.

Every Reacher carries a knotted cord. The knots mark forty-pace intervals, the length of the repeating texture on the slopes, the one unit of distance the Reach itself seems to respect. You walk and you count, and at every forty you feel for the next knot. A route between two tanks is not *past the notched peak and left at the gantry*. It is *three cords and eleven knots, bearing into the light*. The cords are passed down. An old family's cords are dark with handling, the knots worn to beads.

They say that if you lose count in the Reach, you do not become lost in the ordinary way. You become repeated. You find yourself arriving at a place you have already left, over and over, each time a little more certain you have never been there.

## The Second Time You Meet Them

Here the tenants begin to be less easy to see.

You meet an old man at a collecting-tank. He is mending a cord, working a new knot with fingers so dusted they look cast. You speak with him a while, close, the way one does here. He tells you where the hares run. Three days later, a valley over, you meet him again at a different tank, mending the same cord, working the same knot. He does not know you. Or he knows you a moment late, the way the shadows here know the things that throw them: his face changes a full second after it should.

The Reachers do not talk about this among themselves. It is not quite a taboo. It is more that there is no way to talk about it that goes anywhere.

Ask a Reacher how they came to the Reach and they will tell you they climbed up through the warm cloud, as a child, or as a young woman, or following a brother. But ask for details and the story thins. They remember the cloud. They remember the warmth of it, and the first shock of white light above. They do not remember the climb. They do not remember the country below with any clarity: the name of a village, a mother's face. What they remember begins with the light, the way a casting begins with the opening of the mold.

There is a courtesy among them, older than anyone can say. You do not look at another person's back. Clothes are cut high at the neck and full down the spine. When they bathe in the collecting-tanks, which they do rarely, they bathe alone, and they keep their backs to the dark still water so they will not see their own reflections from behind. Some of them, it is whispered, have caught a glimpse anyway. There is a faint line there, pale and straight, crown to tailbone. Some of them have felt, running a hand up under their shirt in the night, a little fin of something like glass.

Nobody checks. That is the courtesy. In the Reach, it is considered a great unkindness to find out what someone is.

## The Lying Down

When a Reacher grows very old, they go to the edge of a new casting.

It has to be new, still warm, the flashing still standing proud along the crest. They walk out along it, slowly, and the family follows at a distance of forty paces exactly, one knot on the cord, near enough to see, too far to hear. The old one lies down on the warm flashing. They settle as the ibex settle, legs folded, head down. And they hold still.

The family stays. They sit on the cast rock and wait, sometimes for days, bringing water in the mornings. Over those days the old one grows paler. The dust settles on them and does not stir with their breath, because their breath grows so slow that it is hard to see. And at some point, no one can ever say exactly when, the seam closes. There is no longer an old person lying on the ridge. There is only the ridge, with a shape on it that a stranger would take for a boulder.

The Reachers do not have a word for this that means *death*. The word they use is the same one they use for a mold swinging shut. It is also, depending on how it is said, the word for a knot pulled tight.

Soon after, a scaffold goes up over the spot, and a small mold is clamped over it, and there is a slow pour, and a fresh sharp surface. The family goes home. They do not come back to look.

## The Ferns

There is a story that the first Reacher, the very first, brought the ferns up from below in the tread of a boot. Spores caught in the mud. They fell into the deepest tank and took, and grew, and that is why the ferns are the only living green in all that white and iron.

The Reachers tend the ferns with a devotion they show nothing else. They carry spores between tanks on wet cloths. They sit with them. They talk to them in the close low register, which is the only register they have. The ferns spiral, confused by the light that comes from everywhere, and the Reachers say this is right. They say the ferns are trying to turn toward the place they came from, and there is no direction to turn in, so they turn toward all of it.

They believe the ferns are the only things in the Reach that were never cast. They need to believe this. When a fern dies, a Reacher will sometimes tear the frond open along its central rib, very carefully, and look. Then they fold it closed again and bury it in the moss, and do not say what they found.

## Behind the Walls

Now the harder part. The Reachers are not the only ones who live here, and they are not even the ones who live here most.

The small square buildings with no doors stand everywhere, wedged between pipe-bundle and pylon, and from inside them come the scratchings. The Reachers have lived beside these sounds for generations. They have theories.

Some say what is inside is failed. A pour went wrong, or a mold was flawed, or something was filled that no one meant to fill: an ibex with its legs cast in the wrong order, a crow without the notch, a person poured before the mold was finished, still soft, still lacking a face. The works, which keep everything young and sharp and corrected, did not correct these. They put a box around them instead.

Others say what is inside is not failed but undecided. Castings the works have not yet chosen a shape for, pale slurry that has set into something without committing to being anything. Drafts. The scratching is the sound of them trying out edges.

Whatever they are, they are the only inhabitants of the Reach who have never been in the light. Inside those walls is the only true dark on the whole plateau. The Reachers are aware of this with something close to envy. They are also aware of it with something close to pity.

They leave wet cloths at the base of the doorless walls at night. In the morning the cloths are sometimes dry, and sometimes gone. Once, a cloth was found folded neatly at the foot of the wall, very small, into a shape like a fern.

And the scratchings have a rhythm. If you listen long enough, pressed close against the cast wall, you begin to hear it. Scratch, scratch, scratch, in sets of forty. They are counting. The Reachers say the things inside learned it from them, listening through the walls to the tapping on the pipes. The old ones say it is the other way around.

## The Shadows

Here the question of who lives in the Reach turns over, the way the valley turns over when the mist burns off the high saddle.

Everything in the Reach is new. The rock is months old, or weeks. Whatever wears is repaired. Nothing lasts long enough to be old. Except the shadows.
