# What Lives in the Mound Country

At first the answer seems to be *everything*: the ants, the termites, the bats, the sleeping birds, the crabs at the pool margins. The basin is so crowded with small lives that asking who lives here sounds like asking which grain of sand makes the beach.

But there is something else. You would not find it by looking, because in this dark you cannot look. You would not find it by listening either, because it makes almost no sound. You would find it the way you find the mounds: by touch, or by warmth. And you would probably mistake it for a mound.

## The Sleepers

Among the living mounds are the dead ones, the slumped humps described already, softened by rain and claimed by figs and thickets. Most are what they seem: low piles of rich earth with roots running through them.

Some are not. Put your hand on one of these and it is cool, not warm. It is cooler than the air and cooler than the ground. The surface is cracked into plates like the channel mud, curled slightly at the edges. Under your palm, very slowly, the whole hump rises and falls.

It is breathing. One breath takes about as long as it takes you to count to forty.

If you had a light, you would see that the hump is a body lying on its belly with its limbs drawn under it. It is long and low, broad across the shoulders, and about as heavy as a small ox. What looks like mud is mud. The creature plasters itself with laterite paste mixed with something it secretes from glands along its flanks, and the paste dries into a hide of red tiles. In the dry season the tiles crack and shrink, and the animal inside them shrinks as well. It has been lying in this spot for weeks, perhaps since the last pool on this stretch of the channel dried out.

Call these the sleepers, for now. That is all you would know of them on this night.

## The Face

The head lies flat against the earth. It is mostly snout: a long tapering tube, blunt at the tip, whose nostrils can close to slits. Behind it, where you would expect ears, there are none. There is only a smooth swelling of the skull on either side, and beneath the jaw a broad pad of bare, rubbery skin, pale grey, pressed to the ground.

That pad is how it hears. The sleeper's hearing lives in its chin. Through the hardened soil it takes in the seething of the nearest mounds, the scrape of ant columns on the move, and the footfalls of antelope a long way off at the pool. The sound travels up the jawbone into the swellings of the skull, where a dense mass of bone does the listening. Its eyes are small and set far back, with a milky film over them. They can tell dark from darker, and not much more. They are not needed for anything else.

When the mouth opens, and it rarely does in the dry season, it is only a narrow slot at the end of the snout. The tongue inside is coiled back into the chest, anchored somewhere near the breastbone. Extended, it is longer than the animal's forelimb, thin as a finger, grooved, and coated in a saliva that dries tacky in air and stays slick in the damp of a gallery.

So it is a termite-eater. That is the first shape the idea takes, and it is not wrong. But it is not finished.

## The Claws

The forelimbs are short, massive, and turned inward. Each foot carries three claws. Two are ordinary and blunt. The third, on the inside, is enormous: curved, as long as a man's hand, heavy as a stone adze. The animal cannot walk on it and has to fold it back against the wrist, so it moves on the knuckles of its forefeet with a rolling, deliberate gait. It is slow on the ground and not built for flight. It relies instead on the mud hide, on stillness, and on a body so close to the temperature of the earth that anything hunting by warmth passes over it.

That claw opens mounds. Nothing else in the basin can. The laterite walls of a large mound are hard enough to turn a spade, and the sleeper goes through them the way a lever goes through a crate lid. It sets the point in a seam, braces, pulls, and a plate of wall the size of a shield comes away.

Look again at the living mounds, the old cathedral-sized ones, and you will find that many of them carry seams. There are long vertical scars, healed over, where the wall is a slightly different colour and grain from the rest. Some mounds have dozens of these, layered like the rings of a tree. Every one is a wound that was opened and then sealed again by the colony.

That is the second shape. The sleepers do not destroy the mounds they feed on. A single opening lets them take what they need, and then they stop.

## The Diggers

They move only in the right season, in the long middle of the year when the rains have ended and the ground is still soft enough to work.

A digger's life is a circuit. It does not wander. It keeps a round of mounds, several hundred of them, spread over a stretch of floodplain a day's walk across, and it visits them in a fixed order that takes most of a year to complete. At each mound it stops and presses its chin to the base. It listens for a long time, sometimes most of a night. Then it either moves on, or opens the wall.

What it is listening to is the colony's health. A strong colony has a particular hum. Workers crowd the combs, the fungus is fruiting well, and the chimney draws steadily, so the whole structure vibrates at a pitch the digger knows. A colony that is failing sounds different. Perhaps its queen is aging, or its fungus has sickened, or ants have broken in. The hum thins and wavers. You can hear the same thing in a machine with a bad bearing.

From a strong colony the digger takes a little. It opens the wall, runs its tongue down the galleries, eats perhaps a few thousand workers, and then does something odd. It pushes its snout into the nearest fungus chamber and eats the comb. It takes a mouthful of the pale sponge and the white fungus furring it, then withdraws. By morning the workers are already sealing the breach, and within a month the seam is hard.

A failing colony it takes entirely. It tears the mound open, goes down into the chambers, and eats the queen. That clears the ground. The mound will slump in the next rains, and something will grow there.

The digger has no dens of its own. It sleeps in dead mounds, in the old galleries of colonies it finished years or decades before. Over a lifetime it widens and joins these galleries into a network of low passages beneath the thickets, wide enough for its body. This network is one reason a heavy footstep in the basin can break through into a hollow. Not every hollow in the mound country was made by termites.

## The Dung

Here the shape shifts again, and becomes larger.

The fungus a digger eats does not all digest. It passes through the gut and emerges in dung, and the digger does not leave its dung just anywhere. At certain points on its circuit, always near the base of a young mound or on bare ground between thickets, it scrapes a shallow bowl with its hind feet, squats over it, and fills it. Then it plasters the heap over with the same laterite paste it uses on its hide and leaves it to harden.

These dung-heaps are nurseries. They are dense, moist inside, and full of living fungal spores, carried from the best combs of the strongest colonies on its round. When the winged termites pour out of the mounds on the first night of the rains and come down again in pairs, shedding their wings, many of them do not burrow into bare soil. They burrow into the diggers' plastered heaps, which are soft now in the rain. They mate inside, and they found their colony there, with the fungus already waiting.

So the pattern of the mounds across the basin is not random. Look at it from above, if you could, over a few centuries, and you would see the mounds arranged along curving lines that loop and cross and double back. Those lines are the diggers' circuits. The oldest mounds sit where diggers squatted three or four hundred years ago. The thickets that crowd around them grew from fig seeds the diggers also carried, from fruit they ate when the dry season was worst and termites ran deep.

*Nobody built them*, the mounds, in the sense the word usually carries. That remains true. But the mound country was laid out by these animals, one bowl of dung at a time. The whole termite district runs on fungal lines that the diggers have been carrying from colony to colony, mouth to gut to nursery, for longer than the oldest mound has stood. In that sense the diggers are its founders, and they are still at work.

Call them the keepers, then. That is closer.

## The Keepers in the Dry

In the last weeks before the rains, the keepers stop.

A keeper is not a thing that burns hot. Its heart beats a few times a minute in this season, and its body holds the temperature of the ground it lies on. As the pools draw down, each keeper finishes its circuit wherever it happens to be, finds a dead mound or a gallery near water, lies down, and plasters itself in. Then it waits, as everything waits.

The musky animal smell that thickens under the gallery forest, which you took for the bats, is partly theirs. The females den under the evergreen trees along the old channel, where roots reach down to water and the soil stays cool. Each lies in a chamber dug beneath the root plates, and each carries a single young, pressed against her belly in a fold of skin. The young is pale and soft, with no hide of its own yet, and it feeds on a paste of fungus and milk. A female bears one young in perhaps eight or ten years. A keeper lives, as far as the mounds can say, for a century or more.

The heavy thing that moves in the reeds at the pool and then goes still is a keeper come to drink. It is careful. It comes at the darkest hour, drinks long and slowly with the tip of its snout, and then stands without moving until whatever it sensed through its chin has gone. It does not flee. It cannot, so it waits.

Lie with your ear to the ground, as you did to hear the mound-sound, and you might catch something else beneath it. It is lower than the seething of the combs, and it is not continuous. It comes as a slow pulse, a deep throb in the earth several seconds long, then silence for a long time, then another pulse some distance away.

That is the keepers talking. It is the only sound they make. They produce it somewhere deep in the chest and press it into the ground through the chin pad, and it carries through the baked laterite a surprisingly long way. It tells the others where each one lies, and that each one is still alive.

## What They Hear

A keeper hears the rain before anything else in the basin does.

Thunder too far off to hear through the air still runs through the ground. So do the first heavy storms falling on country at the far edge of the plain, and the change in the soil as water begins to soak through it somewhere distant. Weeks before the first drop falls here, the keepers under the gallery trees have felt it coming. Their slow pulses through the earth grow more frequent. Their breathing quickens by a few beats an hour. Under the plaster, their bodies are beginning to wake.

So when the rain does come, and the soil softens, and the workers open the mounds, and the winged ones rise from every chimney across the basin in their smoky plumes, the keepers are already moving. Every other animal comes to feed. The birds wake in the figs, the bats pour out of the gallery forest, and the toads and scorpions and frogs break out of the mud. The keepers do not feed. They do not even lift their heads toward the swarm.

They walk out into it with their hides softening in the rain, streaming red, and go to their nurseries. At each plastered dung-heap they stop and rake the crust open with the great inner claw so that the rain can reach the soft centre. Then they move on to the next. All night, all across the basin, in the loudest night of the year, they are opening the nurseries while termites come down around them like falling ash. By morning the fallen pairs are crawling in their thousands into the broken heaps, wingless, looking for somewhere soft and dark with the fungus already in it.

Over the following weeks, as the mounds and the thickets and the grass all surge back into green, pale mushrooms begin to push up out of the ground wherever a keeper has walked. They sprout in rings around old mounds and in lines between thickets. They come from the fungus the keepers carried, fruiting at last, and for a few days you could trace the circuits by them.

## Tonight

None of that is happening yet.

Tonight the keepers lie under the gallery forest and in the hollows of the dead mounds, plastered in red earth gone colourless in the dark. They breathe once to every forty of yours. Their chins are pressed to the ground. They are listening to the colonies seething around them, to the ants carrying, to antelope stepping carefully at the edge of the shrinking pools. From a long way off, below any sound you could hear, they catch the faint shudder of a storm breaking somewhere far across the plain.

Now and then, one of them pulses: a single deep note into the earth. After a long while another answers, somewhere far down the channel. Then the silence closes again.

The mounds breathe around them. The basin keeps its heat. Everything is quiet, waiting, and the keepers are waiting too, very close to the ground, for the night the rains finally reach them.
