# Those Who Live in the Warm Dark

## One of Them

Start small, with a single builder.

He is old for his kind. You can tell by his jaw, which has gone lumpy and pale at the hinge from a lifetime of carrying. The bronze of his flanks has dulled to the color of wet bark. A crescent of scales is missing behind his left gill, where an eel once closed on him and then, for reasons he will never know, let go. He has a stone in his mouth now: a shell, a small one, from the grazing snails, its coil worn smooth where it rolled against the silt. He found it at the foot of a slumped tower two lanes over. Before he picked it up he mouthed it, tasting it with his lips the way all builders do, checking the weight and the grain and some mineral flavor that tells him whether it will take mortar well.

He is carrying it home.

Home is a tower. He did not build it. He inherited it, the way builders inherit anything: he found it untended during a season when its last keeper did not return, and he fought two younger males for it and won. The lower courses are older than any memory the basin holds. He knows the tower entirely by touch. He knows the rough band near the base where he always slows, and the place a third of the way up where a spined shell juts out and he must swerve. He knows the lip at the top where the warm draft pours out against his belly.

He also knows a spot about four courses below the rim where he always stops and presses his flank against the wall and holds it there. It is a smooth, curved stone, harder than shell. He does not know that it is a jaw plate, or whose. He only knows that it is his place to rest, and that the wall there feels right.

He sets the new shell. He twists it with a small push of his head, presses it in, and holds it while the mucus from his gills beads and clouds and settles into the gap. He holds it for a long time. This is most of what building is: not the carrying, not the choosing, but the waiting, jaw braced, while the water slowly turns what he has breathed into stone.

Then he clicks, once. It is a small dry tick of the teeth deep in his throat. Nearby, other clicks answer. Nothing in the answer means *well done*. It means *I am here*, which in the dark is nearly the same thing.

## The One Who Chooses

The males build, but they are not the ones who decide what a good tower is.

A female moves through the district during the building season, faintly iridescent, a thin shimmer of green along her flanks that only shows when a jelly-lantern drifts near. She does not look at towers. She cannot; nothing here can. She enters them.

She swims into the dark mouth at the base and rises up the throat on the convection. As she goes she reads everything. She reads how smoothly the draft pulls, whether it stutters around loose stones or runs clean, whether the heat is even on every side or cold in patches where the mortar has failed. A tower is a lung, and she is testing how well it breathes. The males polish the outsides and bring the pearlescent shells and dress the rims with care, and these things matter to her. What matters most is the breath.

A tower that draws well is a tower whose eggs will not cook or chill. So in the end what the females select, generation after generation, is not beauty but a steady inner warmth. The fine outer shell is only how a male shows that he can afford to care about more than that.

When she chooses, she lays her eggs on a ledge high inside the throat and leaves. She does not stay. The male guards the tower, fanning the draft through it with his fins when the seep ebbs and the heat falters.

The eggs hatch in the dark. The fry are glassy, the size of a grain of rice. The first thing any builder ever sees is light. The rising current carries them up out of the chimney and into the plume above, into the halo of small green jellies. For a little while each of them hangs in the brightest place in the basin, among hundreds of cold lanterns, in water that tastes of sulfur and warmth.

Most of them are eaten there. The survivors drift and grow heavy and fall back down into the city like silt, and they never see that much light again for the rest of their lives. Perhaps something of it stays with them. Perhaps that is why they build upward.

## The Listener

The eel has lived in the same wall for longer than the old male has been alive.

It is about the length of three builders laid end to end, and it is the color of the dark it lies in. Its gap is in the side of one of the deep lanes, the sunken ones, where the walls rise a full body's height on both sides and the channel narrows to a slot. Its head rests just at the edge of the opening. Its eyes are small and nearly useless. It does not need them.

The eel listens. It has spent decades learning the clicking of the city: which clicks mean a group is traveling, which mean a stone is being set, which mean a builder is carrying something heavy and must turn its whole body to take a corner. It knows the rhythm of the lanes the way the builders know their towers. A group of builders coming down the channel, clicking to keep together, is to the eel a kind of song with a gap in it. There is always one who falls a little behind, or whose clicks come muffled around a stone.

Then there is the one sharp, broken-off click, and then quiet.

It would be easy to think of the eel as the city's enemy. It is closer to say that the eel is part of how the city is shaped. The builders travel in groups because of it. They build the walls of the deep lanes with fewer gaps than the walls elsewhere, and set their stones more tightly. In certain old channels there is a strange pattern: long runs of wall where every loose stone has been packed and mortared over and over, as if the builders were trying to seal the channel shut. That is fear, laid down in stone. The eel has been building the city too, in its way, by being the thing the city is built against.

It is very old. Someday it will die in its gap, and the shrimp will take it, and the builders will find the long delicate ladder of its spine lying in the lane. They will carry the vertebrae away one at a time and set them in their towers. The eel will go up into the warm water after all.

## The Unmakers

In the old quarters, where the seeps have gone cold, the builders are gone and the silence is total. It would be easy to think nothing lives there. That is wrong.

Put your hand on a slumping tower in a dead district. The mortar is soft, furred with pale film, and it gives under the palm like old bread. Hold still, and after a while you will feel them moving: tiny blind things, flattened and pale, the size of lentils. They have too many legs, and their bodies are segmented like woodlice. They live only in the cold. The warm districts are poison to them. They eat the mortar.

Not the mineral part. The mortar is built from mucus, and something of the builders' bodies remains in it, bound up in the chalk. Over centuries of warmth the builders' mucus hardens and holds. When the heat goes and the water cools, the unmakers come up through the silt from wherever they wait. They begin working through the walls, eating the old binding, grain by grain, course by course.

This is why towers fall. It is not age alone, and not silt. It is being slowly digested by a people who have never seen a builder and never will, who live in the builders' absence the way mushrooms live in fallen trees.

When a tower finally goes, the groan and the shell-cascade carry across into the living districts, and the clicking stops. The builders feel it as a disaster. It is also a harvest. Every tower the unmakers bring down becomes a fan of loose shells across the lanes, clean stones scoured of film, and in time the builders will come and carry them back up into the warm. The unmakers take the city apart so that it can be put together again. Neither side knows the other exists.

## Underneath

Go down further, through the silt, through the courses of the buried cities, city under city under city. Everything down there is compressed and stained and ancient. Shells of snails extinct for a hundred thousand seasons are packed together with the jaws of builders whose lineage no longer exists.

Something lives there too.

It is not an animal. It is a slow chemistry wearing the shape of life: threads of bacteria so fine they are invisible, woven through the buried mortar in a web that runs for miles. They feed on the last traces of the builders' mucus, deep in the old cities, and on the faint heat still rising from below. They grow so slowly that a thread might lengthen a finger's width in a century.

Where their web runs close to a crack in the pale basin stone, they change the water around them. They sour it, sweeten it, loosen the stone by the smallest imaginable degree. It may be that this is how new seeps open: warm water finding its way up through channels that the buried web has spent ten thousand years softening. It may be that the city, by burying itself, feeds the thing that opens the next hearth, and so decides, in the longest and slowest way, where it will live next.

No one could prove this. No one is there to try. But the living districts do tend to drift toward places where the old buried quarters lie deepest, as if the dead cities were calling the living ones over their own graves.

## The One Overhead

Now the large thing, the one the builders freeze for.

It comes rarely. The first sign is a change in the halos. Above the towers of a district the green jelly-lights, always shifting in the plume, begin to lean. All of them drift in the same direction at once, toward something. Then the long slow pressure-wave rolls down the lanes and pushes gently at every builder's lateral line. Every fish in the district stops, holds, and becomes a stone among stones.

It passes above the towers. It is long, very long, and soft, and moves with a slow rippling of fins or frills along its whole body. It does not hunt builders. It is too large and too slow, and the builders are down among the walls where it cannot reach. It is feeding on the light.

It swims through the plumes above the towers with its mouth open, a wide, pale, sieving mouth. It takes in the drifting jellies and the snail larvae and the glassy newly hatched fry, all the small bright life that rides the warm water up out of the chimneys. Where it has passed, the halos go dark. For a long time afterward the district is blacker than ever, lit only by the faint tips of shrimp antennae.

It is perhaps the only one of its kind, or one of very few. It seems to follow a route: it swings slowly from hearth to hearth across the whole basin over a span of seasons, grazing the plume above each living district in turn. It is never seen above the dead quarters, where there is nothing to eat.

Here is the strange part. When the halos return over a grazed district, they return faster than they should, and sometimes they return with lights of colors not seen there before. The jellies that ride its wake are carried from hearth to hearth in the swirl of its passage. Some of them pass through it and come out alive. The great slow thing that eats the city's light is also, it seems, the thing that spreads it. New hearths do not light up by chance. They light up after it has passed.

The builders freeze for it because it is vast, and vastness in the dark means danger. They have no way to know that without it their towers might have no halos at all, and their fry nothing to see.

## What Lives Here

Let it widen, then. Draw back.

There are the builders, with their stones and their mortar and their slow, courting towers. There are the grazers on the films, and the lodgers in the walls, and the eel in its gap, listening. There are the unmakers in the cold districts, and the web in the buried stone, and the great slow thing that eats the light and spreads it.

And there is something that includes all of them.

Consider what the basin does, taken whole. It breathes, with the seep-cycle swelling and fading every few days. It has a skeleton of shell and bone that it lays down around itself and never sheds. It feeds, and it grows at the top. It dies at its edges, and is digested there, and its body is carried back into the warm to be used again. Over centuries it moves, crawling across the basin floor toward the heat, trailing a long body of its own dead behind it. It even has something like a nervous system: the clicking that passes from builder to builder through the lanes, the freeze that spreads through a whole district at once when a tower settles or something large goes overhead, the long silence and then the slow restarting.

It is not that the city is alive in the way a fish is alive. It is more that in this place the difference stops mattering much. The builders live in the city the way cells live in a body. The city lives in the basin the way a body lives in the world: warm, dark, feeding on a heat it did not make and will never see.

What lives here is all of it, together.

## Back to One

The old male is resting in his place, four courses below the rim, his flank pressed to the smooth curved stone that feels right.

Somewhere far across the district, a tower gives way. There is the groan, and the long pattering cascade of shells, and every fish in the dark goes still. He goes still with them. The draft pours past him, warm, up the throat of his tower. Above him, faintly, green lights turn in the plume.

For a long while nothing moves.

Then, somewhere nearby, a single dry click. *I am here.*

He answers, and pushes off from the wall, and goes out into the lanes to look for another stone.
