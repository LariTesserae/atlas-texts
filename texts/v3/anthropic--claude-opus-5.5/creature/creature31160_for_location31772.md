# Who Keeps the Shelf

## Nobody, at First

Your first answer is that nobody lives here.

You stand in the doorway of the keeper's hut with the roar at your back and look at the cot, the kettle, the logbook swollen into a single brick of pulp. The mattress has gone to moss. A fern has rooted in the stove's ash pan and put up one pale frond toward the gap where the flue meets the roof. The chair is pushed back from the table at an angle, as if someone stood up to answer a sound and did not come back.

So your first answer is: the swifts, a dozen of them, and the snails, and the ferns. A shelf of weather and plants and one abandoned machine that has not yet run down.

Stay a day, though, and that answer stops holding.

## The Keeper

Start with the one who was here, because the shelf is still shaped around them.

You can reconstruct a person from what they wore smooth. The hut's threshold stone is dished in the middle from a particular stride, short and a little pigeon-toed. There is a path, nearly overgrown, that runs from the hut door to the turbine house, then to the anemometer mast, then to the benchmark boulder at the old edge, then back. It is a loop, walked so many times that the moss along it is a different species from the moss beside it, finer and flatter, the kind that grows where something passes regularly. The keeper walked the circuit every day. Probably twice: once at dawn in the glitter, once at dusk inside the cloud, by feel.

What did they do on the circuit? They listened. You learn this from the turbine house, where a brass ear trumpet hangs on a nail beside the generator, and from the bearing housings, which each have a small flat patch filed into the casing, the size of a fingertip, polished bright. The keeper laid a fingertip there, or the trumpet's bell, and listened to the machine the way a doctor listens to a chest. A good bearing hums. A bad one grinds long before it fails. They read the anemometer, and the level of the catchment by the pitch of the penstock's drumming. They measured the ledge.

That last one was not in their job description. You can tell from the benchmark: the official one, the elevation, is cut neat and deep with a proper chisel. Beside it, scratched smaller with something like a nail, runs a row of little ticks. Each is a finger's width from the last, marching outward toward where the edge used to be, and each has a tiny year beside it. The keeper noticed the shelf was growing and began, privately, to keep track. Nobody asked them to. It was simply what they were like.

The ticks stop. After the last one there is a gap of about a hand's width of bare rock, then moss.

Where did they go? Not down; the winch cable is snapped, and there is no path down but the cloud. You look for them on the one line that leads somewhere unlikely: the pitons, driven into the cliff in a crooked row that goes up and sideways and ends at a blank column face. You would take them for a route abandoned. But the last piton is not rusted like the others. It is furred all over in that blue-green lichen, thickly, the way the lichen furs only live iron.

Hold that thought. You'll come back to it.

## What Took Over the Keeping

The machine should have died.

This is what finally makes you sure the shelf is inhabited. A Pelton wheel in its own spray, a belt, a shaft, a generator, a governor on greased pivots. Left alone in warm sulfurous wet for decades, it would seize. The bearings would dry and gall, the belt would rot, the brushes would wear to their springs, the pivot pins of the governor would rust fast. Iron does not keep itself. Something has to go around with oil and a rag and a fingertip on the filed patch.

Force the turbine-house door and look closely, with the whine in your teeth, and you see who does.

The lichen is thickest where the work is. It grows in a ring around each bearing housing, and where the ring meets the shaft seal it is not crust anymore but something wetter: a gloss, a slow bead of clear resin, amber-green, that wells up and runs into the seal. It feels like oil and smells faintly of sap and hot pennies. The belt is not leather anymore, or not only leather. It is sheathed along its whole length in a thin, supple, blue-green skin that flexes as it slaps over the pulleys and never cracks. On the commutator, where carbon brushes should long since have worn away, sit two soft green pads, dense as felt, pressed against the copper by the same old springs and conducting.

And the governor. Its pivot pins wear little collars of the stuff, and the iron balls, if you watch them long enough, are not quite bare. A bloom of it lies on each sphere, thin as breath on glass, and it spins with them, and it does not fly off.

So here is your second answer. What lives on the shelf is a lichen, or something that began as one: a partnership, the way lichens are, of fungus and alga. Here, perhaps, the partnership includes a third member. That member is not a living thing but a practice. It learned to eat what the iron gives off: the heat, the trickle of current that leaks through wet metal under load, the electrochemistry of rust being prevented. It lives on the machine running, and so it keeps the machine running. It oils what it lives on. It is the first tenant who ever paid rent in kind.

The ferns that lean toward the turbine house are the outer fringe of this. Their roots, if you dig, are sheathed in blue-green where they come near the foundation. They are not reaching for warmth. They are reaching for the thing that feeds them, and it reaches back.

## The Load

Here you should settle, and you nearly do. A lichen that keeps an engine. Strange enough.

But a governor does not hold speed in a vacuum. It holds speed against a load. If nothing draws current at the far end of the wire, a generator spins free and light, and the governor's balls fly high and stay there, and the flow is throttled nearly to nothing. That is not what you see. The balls lift and sink. The evening surge comes off the snowfields, the governor feels it and eases the machine back. At night the pitch of the whine drops a little, as if something somewhere has turned down its lamps. Something is drawing power. Something on the line has a daily rhythm.

The cables leave the pylon and fall away into the cloud floor, and along them, as the description said, the lichen forms beads. You notice now what the description only mentioned: they run in both directions. Watch one bead for an hour (you have the time; everything here has the time) and it does not move. Come back after the cloud has risen and fallen, and the spacing has changed. Some beads have crept outward and downward. Some have crept in.

So your answer changes again, and it is larger than you wanted.

The thing that lives on the shelf is not only on the shelf. The lichen follows the power down into the cloud, into whatever valley or town or foundry lies at the other end, and it has been doing so for long enough to have arrived. Whatever is down there, living or ruined, the current is still being used, and what uses it is almost certainly the same kind of thing that uses it up here. The town may be empty. The lamps may be green. The load at the bottom of the wire may be nothing but an enormous slow colony drawing a trickle of power and holding that trickle steady, dawn to dusk, the way a sleeper breathes.

The beads coming up the cable are its answer to the beads going down. The two ends of the line are growing toward each other through the cloud, and the governor between them turns its iron balls and holds everything to speed. It is not one creature at the shelf and another in the valley. It is one creature, long and thin as a wire. Water is its intake, the wheel its heart, the cable its spine, and the governor sits in the middle as the only part of it that thinks, if balancing is a kind of thinking. It may be the oldest kind.

## The Visitor

Now the goat.

It comes up out of the cloud once a season on legs too long for it, pale as the sinter, and you are lucky to see it. It does not climb the cliff the way a goat would, bounding and searching. It uses the pitons. It places each hoof exactly, the way a person places their feet on a ladder they have climbed a thousand times, and it comes down that crooked line from the blank column face at the top. You realize the pitons were never a route that led nowhere. They lead from somewhere you cannot see: a crack, a cave, a fold in the columns, and they lead down to here.

Watch where it goes once it arrives.

It drinks from the sinter basins, as the description says, but not just any of them. It drinks from the one nearest the hut. It stands a while at the open door and looks in. It noses the kettle. Then it walks the loop: turbine house, anemometer mast, benchmark, back. It walks it slowly, with its head low, on the path of fine flat moss that nothing else on the shelf is heavy enough to keep flattened. At the turbine house it lays its muzzle against the filed patch on a bearing housing and holds still, listening, its long ears turned back.

Its hocks and pasterns are furred blue-green.

You do not have to decide what this means. The logbook might say, if you could open it, but the logbook is a brick, and the lichen has been at its spine. What you can say is that someone still walks the keeper's circuit on the keeper's day, or near enough. Someone still listens to the bearings, and then, having heard what it needed to hear, goes back up the pitons into the mountain and not down into the cloud. The last tick on the benchmark was never a stopping point. The keeper simply stopped needing the nail.

## What Lives Here

So, in the end, gathered up:

On the shelf lives a keeping.

It began as a person with a short, pigeon-toed stride who noticed things nobody asked them to notice and wrote them down. It has become something distributed and slow. It is a blue-green bloom on live iron that sweats oil into bearings and felts itself onto copper. It is a line of beads creeping down a wire toward a twin of itself creeping up. It is a governor that has been balancing load against flow so long that the balancing has become a living thing, with a pulse, a daily breath, and roots in the ferns. It is a pale animal that comes once a season to lay its ear against the machine.

It is not in a hurry. It shares its pace with the mountain, which is also, if you think about it, growing out over nothing, a finger's width a year, as if it too had been told to keep something steady and extend it. The ledge pushes outward. The lichen follows the new rock. The pylon that once stood at the lip now stands inland, and the cables leave the shelf at a slightly shallower angle every decade, so that in some very long while, if nothing breaks, the stone will have grown out far enough to meet the wire partway, and the creature will be able to walk down its own spine.

At dusk the swifts pour through the louvers, into the warmth that the machine makes and the lichen keeps. The cloud climbs the cliff. Down at the far end of the line, something turns its lamps down for the night. The governor feels the load ease, lifts its iron arms a fraction of an inch, and on each whirling ball the thin green bloom tilts with it, adjusting, holding everything exactly where it has always been held.

Nobody lives here, you said. You were wrong in the way people are wrong about still water. Everything here lives very slowly. Everything here is keeping something.
