# Who Lives in the Pans

## The Lids

You notice the lids first, and only if you are looking.

Here and there across the pavement, a gnamma has been covered. One of the thin curved sheets that peel from the whalebacks has been laid over the mouth of a rock hole, concave side down, like a bowl turned over a cup. At first it looks like chance. Sheets fall all the time, and some of them must land on holes.

But the lids are only ever on the deep holes, never the shallow ones, and only on holes that still hold water. They are seated, too. Small stones are wedged under one edge so the sheet cannot rock, and when you step on it there is no clack.

Lift one and the water beneath is different from the open holes nearby. It is cooler and clearer. There are no drowned insects on the surface and no skin of red dust. Two or three frogs are pressed into the damp rim, gold-eyed and unbothered. On the silt at the bottom the pale star-leaved rosettes are thicker than anywhere else, and tadpoles hang among them in a slow black cloud.

Someone keeps these. The keeping is old. Where a lid has sat for a long time, wind has shifted it back and forth by the width of a grain for longer than anyone could count, and it has ground a faint pale ring into the granite around the hole's lip. Some of those rings are worn a finger deep.

There are no footprints on the rock, because rock keeps nothing. But go to the margin of a pan in the second week of the dry-down, where the clay has stiffened from custard to leather, and you may find tracks.

## The Tracks

They are broad and round, each about the size of a plate. They are shallow and have no heel, and a row of faint ridges crosses the leading edge, scalloped like the tidemark. They do not fall in any gait you know. Four prints are pressed deep and close together, as if something set its whole weight down and stayed a while. Then comes a long smooth drag, then four more prints.

Where the tracks meet the water there is a wide oval hollow, deeper at the middle and body-shaped, now half full and full of tadpoles. Something lay down there.

Follow the tracks back and they run along the channels between pans, the ones the water has worn only a finger deep. They keep to the channels exactly. You could take this to mean the walker follows the water. After an afternoon of following, you begin to suspect it runs the other way.

## Seeing It

You will almost certainly see it without knowing.

At dawn, when mist stands knee-high on the pans, you might see a low rounded back in the white, rust-dark and a little rough, and take it for one more small whaleback. Then the mist around it moves wrongly and parts where it should not. When the sun has burned the mist away, there is a whaleback there, or there isn't.

It moves at the pace of spreading water. That is walking pace, the pace of a sheet of rain edging across the pavement. Then it stops, the way the water stops, and fills a moment. The moment may last an hour, or an afternoon. Most of seeing it is seeing it stopped. For a long time I thought the water on this plain was undecided. Now I think the water is keeping pace with something.

Up close it is about as long as a person lying down, broader across the shoulders, and low. It stands on four heavy limbs that fold beneath it, and when it rests they vanish entirely, so that what remains is a dome. Its back is laterite: rust-red and plum-dark, pitted like the rind of a fruit, hard enough to ring faintly if a hailstone hits it. Its belly and limbs are pale granite, grainy and rounded, slick where water runs. Along its flanks the two colors meet and fuse into a mottle of brick and bone, and it looks cooked.

The head is long and carried close to the ground. The mouth has no teeth, only a hard, flat-lipped edge made for scraping. The eyes sit deep, and they are the color of gnamma water, clear tea-brown. When it tilts its head, something fine settles in them, slowly, the way grit settles in a rock hole after a stone drops in.

Its skin comes away in sheets. Over its shoulders and down its flanks the outer layer lifts in thin curved plates that stay attached along one edge, so its sides are lined with small overhangs. Those overhangs are cool, damp, and occupied. There are skinks under them, and beetles, and frogs in the wet season. Once, under the plate at the base of its neck, I saw a single cup of swallow mud with a white egg in it. It walks slowly enough for that.

Its back is green. In the dry months the moss in the pits of its hide curls up like burnt paper, and it looks like any other scorched rock. Three days after rain the moss is velvet, crowded into every seam. Sundews stud the channels where water runs off its spine and glisten there. Blue-black algae grows in the creases behind its limbs, dry and crackling at the edges and slick in the middle. When it wades, a strand of bladderwort sometimes trails from its flank. It is a small piece of the plain that walks.
