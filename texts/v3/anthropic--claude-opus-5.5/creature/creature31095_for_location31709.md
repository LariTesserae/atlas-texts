# The One Who Keeps the Unison

## First, What It Is Not

The swifts do not live here. They live *in* here, in the wind, which is a different thing. The plateau is a place they pass over, the way a fish passes over the bottom of a river. The grazers live at the edge of here, and the beetles live under it, and the vultures wait above it. None of them is the answer to the question the place asks, which is: *what could bear to stay, and stay listening?*

Everything on the shelf has made its peace with the roar by ceasing to hear it. The swifts gave up their voices. The grazers folded their ears and moved their talk into the stone. The people who came went deaf in reverse within a day or two, and the roar became the silence they leaned on. That is the ordinary bargain, and the plateau offers it to everyone.

One thing refused it.

## How It Is First Seen

You would not see it at first. Nobody does. Near the lip of the Throat, on the windward side, where the wet band is thickest and the lichen grows in its long combed rosettes, there is a rib of granite that does not quite match the others. It lies lower. It lies longer. It is streamlined the way everything near the Throat is streamlined, drawn out downwind into a taper. It is crusted with the same rust-orange and sulfur-yellow and frost-pale grey. It glitters with the same beaded moisture.

If you have been on the plateau long enough to squint the way the plateau makes you squint, you may notice that its underside is lit. All undersides are lit here, by the light thrown back off the cloud-floor. But this one is lit *from within* as well, faintly, the way a hand held over a lamp glows red at the edges. It is lit the color of skimmed milk.

Then you may notice that the worn hollows, the smoothed places where people sat with their backs to the wind, are all arranged around this one rib, at a respectful distance, like the seats around a hearth.

## What It Is Like, Closer

It is long. Perhaps the length of three people lying head to foot. It has no legs that anyone has seen. It lies pressed full-length to the granite, and its whole underside is one surface of contact, the way a snail is one foot. That underside is its ear.

It has no ears on top. It has nothing on top but lichen, and the lichen on it is older than the lichen anywhere else on the shelf, because the lichen on the rest of the plateau grows on stone the wind is slowly eating, and the lichen on this grows on something the wind cannot eat. The rosettes on its back have been laid down a fraction of a millimeter per century, and they are thick. They have been growing there since before the cairns. Maybe since before the grooves in the Throat were as deep as a finger.

Its front end, if it has one, faces into the Throat. There is something there that might be a face, turned toward the source of the wind: a smooth place, polished like the inside of a shell, fluted in long parallel grooves. It looks like a small copy of the Throat itself. The wind pours over it and it does not flinch, because there is nothing in it loose enough to flinch.

It breathes, very slowly, and it breathes the wet out of the air. Where the rising air lets go of what it carries, this is what catches it. It drinks condensation through its skin. It eats light, the doubled light from sky and cloud, the way the lichens do, and maybe it has an arrangement with the lichens on its back that neither party would describe as simple.

It does not sleep. It cannot afford to.

## What It Does

It listens.

Everything else on the plateau has let the roar become silence. This one has kept it as sound, has kept hearing it at every moment, the fundamental in its long body, the hiss along its flanks, the glassy overtone in the fluted face. It is the only living thing here that has never once stopped hearing the Throat.

And it sings back.

You cannot hear it do this. No one could. But if you lay your palm flat on the granite near it, below the light patter of the grazers stamping, below the roar's weight, you might feel a second vibration, very faint, running through the stone from its body. It is the same note. Exactly the same.

That is the whole of its work: to hold a tone in exact unison with the Throat.

Anyone who has tuned a string against another knows what happens when two notes are nearly but not quite the same: they beat. A slow throb appears between them, a wavering, *wah, wah, wah*. The closer the notes, the slower the throb. At perfect unison it vanishes, and the two tones lock into one and stand still.

The creature keeps its note locked to the Throat so precisely that there is no beating at all. Between its voice and the mountain's voice there is a stillness so complete it could not be made any other way. If the Throat's pitch drifted by a hair's breadth, if the wind below the cloud eased by one breath's worth of force, the stillness would break, and a throb would begin in the creature's body, slow at first, one pulse a day, one an hour, faster and faster as the gap widened.

So it is the plateau's instrument, and its witness. Everything else here was shaped by the rule that nothing changes. This is the one thing shaped to *check*.

## A Harder Thought

You could believe, if you stayed long enough, that it does not only measure the note.

You could believe that the note holds because it holds. That when the wind below the cloud wavers, as surely any wind must, however slightly, the creature leans against the waver the way a hand leans against a door, and the Throat, locked in unison with it, is steadied. That the rule of the place is not a fact about the place but a labor, performed without rest by one long lichen-backed thing lying at the lip for longer than there have been people to wonder about it.

The creature would not say which is true. It may not know. Holding a note and hearing a note are not so different when you have been doing both for ten thousand years.

## Its Neighbors

The grazers know it. They come to the edge of the wet band, where the cushion plants give out, and they stamp. The patter travels through the granite and reaches its long underside, and sometimes, not often, it answers: one slow press against the stone, felt under every hoof on the shelf at once. The herd lifts its heads together when this happens. Then it goes back to cropping lichen, and seems calmer.

The swifts nest in the Throat, in the fluted grooves, and their chicks hatch into the roar. The creature feels each of them as a tiny disturbance in the note, a flake of feather and saliva and silk changing the shape of the wall by an amount too small for anything else to register. It corrects for each one. It knows every nest by the error it introduces. When the chicks fledge and drop into the wind and the cups are left empty, the note changes again, and it corrects again. In that sense it knows every swift that has ever been born here, though it has never seen one.

The vultures, circling above the thermals far back from the edge, have never come down to it. They are waiting for things that have died. They know better.

## The People

The people who came knew about it. That much is plain from the hollows, and from the cairns, which lead not to the Throat but to this rib beside it.

They did not come to hunt, nor to shelter. The source is right that they came to stand in the roar. But they came to stand in it *beside the one who still hears it*. For a day they would sit in the smoothed hollows with their backs to the wind and their palms on the rock and feel, below the roar, that second note in unison, that impossible stillness. And some of them, perhaps, felt their own breath and pulse fall into it.

Then they went back down. That was the arrangement. The frightening quiet of the valleys was the proof that they had been somewhere real. They carried the note down for a little while, and it faded, and when it faded completely they came back.

The cache in the crack behind the rib was left for it. The braided cord, stiff with age, is the same length as the creature's body; someone measured. The flake of dark stone is sharp on one edge and polished on the other, polished the way only the Throat polishes, and must have been held for a long time in the wind by the lip before it was set down. And the three drilled bird bones were flutes.

The source says they were never played here, because nothing could be heard. That is true for everything but one. Somebody, long ago, sat in a hollow and blew into a swift's bone, knowing that no ear on the plateau would catch it, knowing that the thin sound would be torn away before it traveled the length of an arm. They played it for the only listener who had never stopped listening. Whether it heard is its own business. The flutes were left beside it afterward, the way you leave a gift you cannot take back.

## What It Is Waiting For

Here is what the creature is, once it has become fully itself in the telling.

The place is in balance. It will hold its shape until something beneath the cloud changes, and then it will not. Some day the warm heavy air below will stop pressing against the mountain, or the mountain will settle, or the world will tilt its weather a few degrees. The wind will ease. The note will begin to fall.

When that happens, the creature will feel it first. Before the swifts find the updraft thinning, before the grazers feel the stones go quiet under their hooves, a slow throb will begin in the long lichen-backed body at the lip: *wah... wah...* It will grow. The creature will lean against it with everything it has, and perhaps for a while the note will hold. And then it will not.

The Throat will fall silent. The swifts will have to learn to land. The silence the plateau's creatures have leaned on for ten thousand years will turn out to have been a sound after all, and it will be gone, and every living thing on the shelf will find itself deaf in the ordinary way, and afraid.

Except one.

The creature will still be sounding. It has held the note so long that the note is no longer something it does but something it is. When the mountain's voice fails, its own voice will go on, the last copy of the Throat, perfect, in unison with something that no longer exists.

And then, for the first time, it will move.

It will peel itself off the granite, lichen and all, heavy as a stone rib and slow as a century. It will follow the cairns back across the shelf the way the people came, low and flat, in the stillness of a wind that has stopped. It will crest the lower slopes. It will go down into the valleys, into the quiet the people always found so frightening, and it will carry the note with it, the one note, as they did. But it will not let the note fade.

Somewhere below the cloud there will be a country that has never heard the Throat. The creature will lie down in it, pressed full-length to the ground, and sound, and sound, and sound. And the ground, feeling it, may one day begin to shape itself into the kind of place that could hold such a note: a groove, a channel, a notch in some lip of rock where a wind might learn to pour.

Until then it lies at the edge, lit from beneath the color of skimmed milk, in perfect unison, listening.
