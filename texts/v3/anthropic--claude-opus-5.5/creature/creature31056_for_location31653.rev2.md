# Who Lives in the Seaward City

## The First Answer

People live here, though not many.

You see them first as the source describes them: a figure in an amber window, a figure standing in an arcade, a small upright mark walking the pale avenue two kilometers off with nothing on either side. Then you get closer, which takes longer than it should, and they become particular.

They are lean and unhurried. They dress in undyed cloth, grey-green, with the heavy weave of fabric that is damp every morning of its life. They go bareheaded and, more surprisingly, barehanded. Every adult has palms crossed with fine white lines, the healed marks of old seam-cuts, and most can tell you which plate gave them each one. Children are taught to touch the lay with the flats of their fingers and never to draw a hand along a join. Every child learns this, and every child is cut anyway, usually once, badly. The first cut is spoken of the way other places speak of a first word. After it, children stop treating the ground as a floor and start treating it as a text.

Their eyes are large-pupilled from a lifetime of pewter noon. Visitors, of whom there have been very few, find their gaze unsettling at first. When someone here talks to you, they look slightly to one side of your face, and the side they choose is always toward the nearest stay. They are not being evasive. They grew up in a place where light lies, where the image of a thing sits a little off from the thing, and they aim their attention at where you probably are, not where you appear to be. The waders do the same when they fly. People who have lived here all their lives correct for a bend that is not there, gently and constantly.

They navigate, as the source says, by counting seams. That habit has grown into their whole way of speaking. An address is a count: *four hundred and twelve seams west of Oreth, eleven north.* A journey is a count. So is a relationship: people say two households are *thirty seams apart* the way others would say *second cousins*, and the phrase carries weight whether or not anyone has walked it. Counts are kept in memory and recited, and a good counter is respected the way a good singer or a good lawyer is respected elsewhere. The stays have names: Oreth, Vail, Sunder-by-the-Figs, the Leaning One (which does not lean). Everything else is measured from them, because they are the only things in the city about which nobody can be wrong. You may misjudge how far away a stay is. Once you stand at its base, there is no argument.

## Why They Rarely Walk Toward Each Other

The figures on the avenues almost never seem to be going toward each other. That is because they are nearly all going the same way.

There are two ways to live in the Seaward City, and every person here has chosen one or had it chosen for them. People speak of those who **keep house** and those who **keep pace**.

The city walks west, but its buildings do not move. The crest moves, the way a wave's crest moves while the water only rises and falls. A house built on young stone near the sea will, over a lifetime, find itself farther and farther inland, not because it has gone anywhere but because the city has shifted around it. New stays rise to the west, old ones slacken to the east, and the windows nearby go dark one by one.

So a person who keeps house, who stays in one place and tends one window and knows one set of seams by heart, is carried eastward by standing still. The young live near the sea and the old live near the forest. Nobody here thinks this is sad. It is simply how a life is shaped: you are born near the steam and you die near the green, and between the two the neighborhood ages around you at exactly your pace. Old keepers of house sit in their doorways in the evenings and watch the jungle's edge come a few meters closer each year. They speak to it familiarly, the way you might speak to a large patient dog that has been waiting at your gate for a long time.

Those who keep pace refuse this. Every year or two they leave their house and walk west, far enough to stay near the crest and near the lit windows and the warm stone. They move through the city the way a surfer holds a wave. Theirs is a lonelier life and in some ways a younger one, and it makes them the figures you see on the avenues: small, solitary, all facing the same direction and walking at slightly different speeds, like beads of water creeping across a plate. They don't approach one another because they aren't going *to* anywhere. They are going *with* something.

## Where They Meet

They do meet, but only at night and only at the stays.

In the wet rings, among the lanterns, in the pale blue-white necklace of light around each column's base, everyone agrees where they are. The lensing that makes daytime meetings nearly impossible (you set out to meet a friend at a plaza and arrive to find it was never where you thought) cannot confuse you at a stay. So the rings are where people court, trade, argue, and settle debts. They sit on the moss-ridges with their feet in the black water and talk in low voices, and the deep pressure of the stay moves in their chests beneath the conversation, so that the talk always seems to have a heartbeat under it. Children here are often conceived within sight of a stay, and it is considered lucky. Funerals are held there too.

Their voices are soft, and people who grew up near an arcade often hum its note without knowing it. You can sometimes tell where someone was raised by the pitch they drift into when they are thinking. Two people raised by the same arcade will find themselves humming in unison and look up at each other, startled, and something will pass between them that is hard to name.

## Birth Plates

Every person here has a plate.

On the night of your birth, somewhere far west, a new slab rose steaming out of the shallows, and that slab is yours. The edge-watchers name each plate as it rises, and a child is given that name. Most people never visit their birth plate, and many couldn't find it without a long recitation of seams. But they know it is there. It cooled when they were infants, grew its first moss in their childhood, and slid inland year by year as they did. It ages exactly as they age.

This is the city's central belief, and it is less a doctrine than an observation that keeps proving true: **the ground is exactly one lifetime deep.** Nothing here is older than a lifetime because the oldest plate belongs to the oldest person living. When that person dies, the plate lets go. Its stay slackens, its stone crazes into gravel, and the forest takes it. The eastern edge of the city is not a line on a map but a person, some very old keeper of house sitting in a doorway, whose breathing holds back the jungle by a few meters.

Nobody can say which way the causation runs. Nobody here thinks the question is interesting.

## The Other Windows

Behind most of the amber windows there is no one.

Look closely through one and you will see a room of carved stone with a surface of dense precise lines, and across those lines a single bead of light traveling very slowly, the way water travels across a plate. Sometimes the wall re-forms by a fraction while you watch, like a sleeper shifting. There is a faint ticking, less random than the ticking of the cooling lay. This is the city thinking, or calculating, or doing something the people here call *reckoning*, a word that means both counting and coming to terms.

People sit with these windows. Some keepers of house spend their evenings in a lit room with a process, not doing anything with it, only keeping it company, and they say the bead of light moves a little faster when someone is there. This may or may not be true. From outside, as the source says, you cannot tell which windows hold people and which hold processes. From inside, people say, you eventually stop being able to tell either. Very old keepers of house sometimes stop speaking and simply sit, and their windows keep glowing a while after they are gone.

The writing on the walls is the city's own hand. It is not an inscription left by builders but notes the city keeps as it goes, and its script drifts. Each district is written slightly differently from the one to its east, the way a person's handwriting changes over years. The newest script, on the newest plates near the sea, is always a little unfamiliar, and it is the young who learn to read it first. Language here flows from west to east, exactly like the fog. Children teach new letters to their parents, and the parents, in old age, can read the walls of their own district and very little else. Keepers of pace read a braid of scripts, a strand for each place they have lived. Nobody reads all of it, because the oldest writing is under the figs.

## The Surveyors

The source mentions surveyors who *once* lived here, and people will tell you about them if you ask.

They came from the east, through the forest, long ago. They brought instruments and intended to map the place. They discovered that distances lie, that no two measurements agreed, and that the city would not hold still to be drawn. So they stopped measuring and started counting, and in time they stopped counting *for* anyone and counted only to find their way home. They gave the stone its name, the lay. Their children stayed.

The surveyors did not leave. They kept house, and their houses are in the far east now, dark, with seam-figs standing in their doorways. The people of the Seaward City are what the surveyors' children became when they gave up the map.

One thing from the surveyors survives in every household: a long, careful plot of the stays, recorded by seam-count over decades. When you draw it out, the stays fall along a curve. It is not a straight line and not a grid. It is a flattened spiral, turning counterclockwise, opening westward, whose center lies somewhere under the forest, lost.

Remember this. It matters later.

## The Lodgers

The animals are fewer than the people, and quieter.

The **grey waders** stand in the ring-pools, and when they fly they correct, forever, for the stays' pull. They are the only creatures here that fly any real distance, and they fly the way people look: aimed a little aside. People are fond of them without making much of it. A wader standing in your ring at dawn is a good sign, and nobody is sure of what.

The **glass snails** graze the seam-moss at night with lantern-light caught in their shells. Every shell coils counterclockwise, like every vine on every stay. People do not eat them. If you ask why, you will get a pause and then an answer like *you wouldn't eat a house*, which is not quite an explanation.

The **moths** spiral up the stays toward the dark, drawn not to light but away from it. Nobody has ever seen one come down. Children say they are carrying the darkness up the columns to wherever the columns go, and that this is why the sky is dim. Adults smile at this and do not contradict it.

Then there is the large, three-toed grazer that is almost never seen. People who keep house near the forest know it best, and they call it **the relief**, in the sense of a relief watch: the one who comes to take over when your shift is done.

It comes from the east. Its wide-set prints run out of the jungle, across the gravel and the crazing plates, to the stays that are beginning to slacken. It grazes their rings for a season or a year. It is often found at the very end, when a stay's pull finally fails, standing at the base with its head raised, listening. Then it goes back east, and along its trail, from seeds carried in its dung, the forest follows. Every stay that has let go has had a relief attend it. The animal is not waiting for the city to die. It is waiting to inherit, patiently and without malice, the way the old keepers of house wait in their doorways, and the two recognize each other. An old woman at the forest edge will set out a bowl of fig-pulp at dusk, and in the morning the bowl will be empty and three-toed prints will circle her house once, and nobody will think this strange.

## The Tenant

By now the shape is showing through.

The stone stays warm at its center like bread. The avenues carry threads of light like veins under skin. Broken plates are quietly absorbed and the damage closes over. Things that heal are alive. The surveyors' first theory, still repeated, was that the city is a single enormous plant, a rhizome. Like a creeping grass it grows at one end and dies back at the other, with the stays as its stems drinking water and the dim sky as the light it eats. The theory is not wrong. It is incomplete.

The snails complete it.

The city is also a shell. It coils counterclockwise, and every vine and snail follows its grain, because on a shell everything follows the grain. It is a spiral, as the surveyors' plot shows, but a spiral unwinding: its oldest whorls rot under the forest while its newest lip lengthens into the sea. And the stays do exactly what the tube threading a nautilus's chambers does. They draw water *out*. They pull the moisture from the stone, bead by bead, keeping the old chambers dry, keeping the lay held and taut and, possibly, afloat. When a stay goes slack, the water comes back, the stone drinks it and softens, and the chamber is flooded and abandoned.

A nautilus lives only in its newest chamber. Everything behind it is rooms it has outgrown and sealed and left.

So something lives at the lip.

## At the Seaward Edge

Among those who keep pace there are a few who keep pace all the way, until there is no more west to walk to, and they live at the edge. They are the edge-watchers, the namers of plates, and what they say about the laying is careful and modest, because they know nobody will quite believe them.

They say that on a laying night the sea goes flat and oily and then pale, and that the paleness is not in the water but *under* it. It is a broad warmth just beneath the surface, lighter than the sea around it, moving the way the skin of a large sleeping animal moves when it breathes. It has no edge you can point to. It is like the underside of a tongue, or the soft mantle of a mollusk seen through a meter of dark water. Heat comes off it. The water above it smokes.

It never surfaces. It never shows an eye or a limb or a mouth. It exhales. That is the sound that rolls across the lowland on certain nights, soft and enormous. As it exhales, stone comes out of it, or out of the water it has warmed, in slow tongues. The plate rises and settles, and the pale warmth withdraws a little farther west, into deeper water. It is always a little farther west. No one has seen it by daylight. The edge-watchers suspect that there is no daylight where it lives.

They also say that it is paying attention. It does not attend to them particularly. It attends to the way a hand attends to the tool in it. When you stand at the edge on a laying night, the new plate forms a little way off from where you are standing, never beneath you, as though something has noticed you and made room.

## What the Tenant Is Building For

A hermit crab has no interest in the shell it leaves behind. This tenant does.

It lays the avenues with light in them for people it will never see walking. It grows stays that will hold for a lifetime and then deliberately let go. It aligns its arches to frame fig trees that have not yet split its plates. It builds bridges over bare stone where there is no river, because when its stays slacken and the water is released and the lowland softens, there *will* be rivers there, running east through the jungle under those bridges long after the tenant is far to the west. The bridges are anticipating one. The source is exactly right about that.

The tenant lives in its newest chamber and walks away from everything it makes, and everything it makes is shaped for whoever comes after: the moss, the snails, the people who keep house, the relief, the forest, the rivers. Its whole life is leaving rooms for others. The people here have come to understand this, and it is the closest thing they have to a moral. They do not say *build for yourself*, or even *build for your children*. They say *build for the relief*.

When a keeper of pace grows very old at the edge, there is sometimes a night when they walk out into the pale water during a laying and are not found. Nobody calls this death, exactly. In the morning there is a new plate, and on some of these plates, if you look closely, there is a small region where the grain runs crosswise to the rest, about the length of a person lying down, like a flaw in glass. The edge-watchers name those plates with particular care. Children born on those nights are thought to be quick to learn the new script.

## Or Perhaps Not a Tenant at All

Some of the old keepers of house, the ones who sit longest with the processes and speak least, will tell you, if you wait long enough, that there is no creature under the water.

There is only the crest. A wave is not a thing. It is a motion passing through a material, a shape the water takes briefly and then gives up. The pale warmth the edge-watchers see might be the crest itself seen from below: the place where the lay is being lifted out of the sea, with nothing doing the lifting except the lifting. The city is not a shell with an animal in it. It is a wave in stone, and the stays are where the wave piles up, and the forest is where it breaks, and the whole of it has been moving west for longer than anyone can say because moving west is what it *is*.

They say it without sadness. They point out that a person is a crest in exactly this sense: a shape that moves through a lifetime of matter, holding it taut for a while, drawing the water out, and then letting it go to the green. Each person here has a plate that bears their name and ages at their pace. The city is made of people the way a wave is made of water: not permanently, and not by any single piece of it, but truly.

The edge-watchers disagree. They have felt the warmth and been given room. You can hold both views at once, and most people here do, the way they hold the true place of a thing and its lensed image together without quite choosing.

## So: Who Lives Here

Tonight, somewhere in the Seaward City, a keeper of house sits in a doorway at the forest's edge with a bowl of fig-pulp at her feet. She is the oldest living person, though nobody has told her, and she is humming the note of an arcade that went dark forty years ago. A few meters from her, the relief stands at the base of a stay that is almost done pulling, its head raised, listening to the stone.

In a lit window, a process moves its bead of light across a wall of script that three people alive can read. In another window a man sits beside a process and keeps it company, and it may be moving a little faster for him.

On a pale avenue a keeper of pace walks west alone, counting seams under his breath, and a kilometer to his south another walks the same way at nearly the same speed. Neither has seen the other. They will meet in a year, perhaps, in a ring of lanterns, and hum the same note, and look up.

In the seams the glass snails graze, carrying little shells of light, all coiled the same way. On the moss-ridges the waders sleep standing. Up the stays, the moths climb into the dark with whatever they are carrying.

And out at the edge, beyond the last stay, the sea goes flat and oily and pale. Something warm moves beneath it, or something that is only warmth moves. It breathes out across the whole lowland, and a new plate rises steaming into the dark. On the shore, an edge-watcher holds a newborn child and speaks a name toward the slab. The child and the stone will be exactly the same age for as long as either of them lasts.
