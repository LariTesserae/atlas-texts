# Those Who Keep It Sharp

## The First Thing You Notice

The first thing you notice about the people of the Seam is their hands.

They are always touching the rock. They don't grip it or lean on it; a palm rests flat against a wall in passing the way you might touch a sleeping animal to be sure it is still breathing. A woman crossing a walkway lets her fingertips trail along the ceramic rail and then, where the rail meets the living wall, along the stone. A man waiting outside a door stands with the back of his hand pressed to the granodiorite beside the frame. Children do it without thinking. Their parents taught it by doing it, and their parents learned it in the first years, when nobody yet trusted the anchors, when every tick in the night meant *is this the one*.

It is a greeting, too. When two people meet on a terrace, each lays a palm on the wall before speaking, so that the first thing they share is the rock between them. When they meet across the gap, on facing terraces forty meters apart, both reach back to their own wall at once. The gesture says: *we are both still held.*

They speak softly. Sound carries in the slot. A word said at normal volume on the east wall arrives, faint and clean, on the west, and a raised voice goes the full depth to the rubble floor and comes back up changed. So conversations are close and low. Arguments, when they happen, happen in whispers, which the inhabitants say makes them shorter. It is hard to stay angry at a whisper.

They wear grey and undyed wool, hooded, damp at the hem. Almost everyone has a slight stoop of the shoulders from the hooded walkways, which were built for the ice rain and are a little lower than they should be. Their faces are pale from the brief light. Their eyes are good in the dark.

## How They Count

A child in the Seam is born at a distance.

On the doorframe of every inhabited dwelling there are two columns of marks. One is the child's height. The other is the width of the gap, measured from that door to its counterpart on the opposite wall with a fine calibrated line that the water-keepers lower and draw taut once a year. Beside each mark is a date, and beside some of them a note: *the year the wallcreeper came twice. The year of the long fog. The year Anselm's leg healed wrong.*

So when you ask someone in the Seam how old they are, they may tell you in years, but they are just as likely to tell you in width. *I was born at forty and a hand,* says a young woman, meaning forty meters and ten centimeters, a hand's breadth being their unit for twenty-five years of widening. *I'll be old at forty and three hands.* Nobody expects to see four.

Marriages are made across the gap when they can be. A couple from facing walls strings a single thread between their two terraces, filament-fine, visible only in fog, and each year on the anniversary they let out four millimeters of slack together, one of them on each side, in silence. The thread doesn't carry anything. Some old couples have a small coil of spare line on each terrace, and when they are asked what they will do when it runs out, they say they measured it for the lives they expected to have. A few have been let out to the end, and the thread hangs taut and humming in the wind until one of them dies, and then the other cuts it and lets it fall, and it drops the whole three hundred meters so slowly and lightly that it is sometimes still falling at dusk.

## The Listeners

Deepest in the walls, where the anchor network gathers into chambers the size of a small room, sit the rock-listeners.

The chambers are dim and warm and always faintly wet. There is nothing in them but a bench, a lamp, and the wall, which here is left bare granodiorite with the anchor threads running through it like silver roots. The listeners work barefoot. Most keep one hand on the stone and one on a slate where they record what they feel, though the best of them stopped writing years ago.

Oskar Venn has listened for thirty-one years. He is the oldest listener and the only one left of the first cohort. He says the hum is not one note but several thousand, braided, and that after long enough you can hear individual cracks the way you can pick out one voice in a quiet crowd. There is a joint two hundred meters down on the west wall that he calls *the complainer*, because it takes every small stress personally. There is a long seam near the north end he calls *the sleeper*, which has not moved in eleven years and which he watches more carefully than any other, because he does not believe in sleep.

The younger listeners bring him tea and ask him questions they would not put on a slate.

The question is always the same, in different words. The lowland engineers wrote that the anchors move force faster than stone allows. The listeners know something the engineers only suspect: the anchors answer *before*. Not by much. A fraction of a second, sometimes half a breath. The hum changes pitch, and *then* the mountain moves. Every listener notices this within their first year, and every one of them has lain awake over it.

Oskar's answer never changes either. *It's listening,* he says. *So are we. You don't have to know which of you heard it first.*

The inhabitants call the network itself *the Listening*, a noun, the way you might say *the Tide* or *the Weather*. They do not claim it is alive. They are careful people. But they speak of it the way you speak of a thing with preferences. It favors the east wall, slightly, and nobody knows why. It dislikes sudden loads, and the cable crews learned in the first decade to lower cargo in stages, pausing, so it can take the weight in its own time. It does not like to be hurried. It seems, the listeners say, to prefer the city lived in. The rooms that have been empty longest hum a little lower, a little slower, like a held breath.

That is part of why people keep the empty rooms.

## The Room-Keepers

Everyone in the Seam tends empty rooms. It isn't a profession. It is simply expected.

Each household keeps a handful of vacant dwellings near its own: opens them every few days, checks the seals, runs a hand along the walls, sits for a while. The official reason is maintenance, a room occupied now and then catching seepage or strain early. The real reason is harder to say, and they rarely try.

What they do in those rooms has become its own quiet custom. They leave things. A cup on a shelf. A folded blanket. A smooth grey pebble from the valley, the only smooth stone in the Seam, brought up in a sealed crate by someone who wanted the later ones to have something that had already been worn. A note, sometimes, tucked into a niche: *The morning ice is worst in the third month. Stay under the hoods. The wallcreeper likes the ledge below this window, if it comes.*

They are writing to the eight thousand. To the people the Seam was built for and who have not come, and may not, and will need to know where the cold pools and which bridge sings in a west wind. There are rooms in the upper city that hold forty years of notes from a dozen different hands, stacked in a niche like a slow correspondence with no one. Some keepers answer each other's notes. Some argue in them. There is one room on the east wall, fairly high, where someone left a question in the first decade: *Do you remember us?* It has been answered two hundred and six times.

## The Crawlers

At dusk the crawlers come out, and for a few hours they are the most numerous inhabitants of the city.

They are not supposed to have personalities. They are pale, hand-sized, many-legged, each one identical when it leaves the fabricator. But the Seam is old enough now, and the crawlers long-lived enough, that wear has done what wear does. Joints loosen differently. Sensors cloud. A unit that took a falling ice-blade across its back in year twelve walks with a slight lean ever after, and a leaning crawler reads cracks from a slightly different angle, and so, over years, it develops a route of its own.

The inhabitants name the old ones. They pretend they don't.

There is Stitch, on the lower west wall, who lost two legs on one side and now travels in a slow clockwise spiral, working the same forty square meters every night with a thoroughness no intact crawler matches. The repairers could fix Stitch in an afternoon. They have not. People on the lower west wall step around its route. There is the Widow, who has come back to the same empty port every dawn for nine years, though the port was sealed after a fault and the Widow must now cross twenty meters of open rock to reach a working one. There is a unit on the east wall called Slow Ingrid, after a woman who died in year twenty-three, because it pauses at every crack precisely twice as long as the others do, and Ingrid had been like that too.

On nights when the city lights are low, children lie on their terraces and watch the faint warm points of the crawlers creep across the opposite wall, and they make constellations of them that never hold. *There's the Widow crossing. There's Stitch going round.* In the lowlands, children learn the stars. In the Seam, the stars move, and the children learn them by their limps.

## The Water-Keeper

Mira Kesh tends the deepest cistern, the one the inhabitants call the Well, cut a hundred and ninety meters down the east wall where the fog-water gathers after it has run the whole length of the mesh.

The Well is a chamber of perfectly black, perfectly still water, so clear that when Mira lowers her lamp on its chain, the light goes down and down past fracture faces that look as sharp under the surface as they did the night the mountain split. It is, she says, the only place in the Seam where you can see the rock as it was on the first night. Up in the air, even here, the frost has begun its patient work: a hairline, a faint rounding, a thin grey bloom of mineral from the weeping. Underwater, nothing has touched it.

Her work is to watch for change. Once a month she lowers the lamp to its full depth and looks. She is looking for films, the first dim sheen of microbial life that might settle on the submerged faces and one day feed something larger. If she finds it, the protocol is to drain and scrub. She has found it three times in eighteen years. Each time she followed the protocol, and each time she sat on the ledge afterward for a long while in the dark, feeling as if she had turned someone away from a door.

She is the one who hears the Well speak. Every few weeks, as water shifts in the rock around the chamber, the cistern gives out a single deep note, like a drop into an enormous jar. Mira keeps a record of them: time, temperature, the pitch as near as she can sing it back. She has no reason to believe the notes mean anything. She has been keeping the record for eighteen years. She would like, before she dies, to give it to someone who will keep it for eighteen more.

## The Child

Pell is eleven, born at forty and a hand and a finger, and she has never seen a tree.

She has seen pictures. She has eaten fruit that came up the cable in sealed crates, and she knows the fruit grew on something, and she has tried to imagine a thing made of wood standing in open air with nothing holding it but its own roots, and her imagination goes cold at the thought of those roots. Roots are wedges. Every child in the Seam knows this before they know their letters. She has heard of gardens the way lowland children hear of sea monsters: a place where people *deliberately let things grow into the ground*.

She is small and quick and not as careful as she should be, and she is the one who found the bone.

It was on the netting at a hundred and sixty meters, spring of last year, on a sheet the crawlers could not easily reach because the ice rain had torn the adjacent mount. She was not supposed to be out on the net-ledge. She saw it because the morning sun came down the slot at just the right angle, and there, in the grey mesh, lay something that was not stone.

A long bone, a sheep's or an ibex's, split lengthwise the way the lammergeiers split them, dropping them from a height onto rock to get at the marrow. It was old. It was the color of weathered ivory. And in the long groove where the marrow had been, protected from wind and ice, was a crust the size of a thumbprint that was *orange*.

Pell had never seen orange that wasn't in a picture or a fruit.

She knew what it was. Everyone in the Seam knows lichen the way sailors know rot. She knew the protocol. She knew exactly how many years it would take a patch of lichen to start its acids working at a crystal face, how many more before the first grain loosened, how many more until the crack. She knew she should report it, and a crawler would come, and the orange would be scrubbed back to bone and the bone sent down the cable in a sealed box with the rest of the city's organic waste.

She put it inside her coat.

## The Secret Everyone Keeps

It is in an empty room now, high on the north end of the west wall, near where the snow finches nest. It sits on a ceramic shelf, not touching the rock. There is a small dish of fog-water beside it, which somebody refills.

Pell told one person, which was a mistake, and that person told one person, and so on, the way these things go. By autumn half the city knew. By winter the rock-listeners knew, and Oskar Venn climbed the whole height of the west wall, which took him most of a day, to sit in the room for an hour with his hand on the wall beside the shelf, listening. When he came down, people asked him what he had heard. He said the Listening hummed a little higher in that room than in the empty rooms around it, the way it does in the rooms where people live.

Nobody has reported it. Nobody has scrubbed it. The crawler route that passes the window has been quietly adjusted by a repairer who will not say which one. The notes in the niche of that room are different from the notes in the other empty rooms. They are not written to the eight thousand. They are written to the lichen.

*You are not allowed to touch the wall,* says one. *We are sorry about this. It is a good wall.*

*You are the first,* says another. *We know what you are for. Not yet.*

And one, in a hand Mira Kesh would recognize as her own, if anyone asked her, which no one has: *We turned three of your cousins away from the Well. This is the best we could do.*

## Who Lives Here

So who lives here?

Six hundred people who touch the rock when they greet each other. People who measure their lives in the widening of a wound, who marry across a gap they know will outgrow them, who spend their nights fighting ice for a few millimeters of sharpness they cannot hope to keep forever. People who keep eight thousand empty rooms warm for strangers and write them letters. Listeners who sit barefoot in the dark with their hands on a hum that may be answering before it is asked. Old crawlers with limps and routes and borrowed names. Springtails like spilled pepper on the weeping walls. A pair of snow finches at the north end. Once in a long while, a flash of crimson wings.

And now, on a ceramic shelf in a room nobody officially visits, an orange crust the size of a thumbprint on a lammergeier's bone. It is the beginning of the thing the whole city was built to hold off, and it is being kept alive by the people holding it off.

They are not confused about this. That is what took me longest to understand about them. They know what they are doing. They have given their lives to keeping a young mountain young, and they know they are losing, by the patient arithmetic of frost and seed and time, and they know the losing is the mountain's real life, and theirs is only a pause inside it. The bone is how they have said so to each other, without saying it. A small, deliberate breach in a city of seals. A way of telling whatever comes after that they saw it coming, and they did not hate it.

Pell visits it most days. She does not touch it. She sits on the floor of the empty room with her palm flat on the granodiorite and watches the orange, which has not visibly grown, and which she is sure has grown. Sometimes the fog pours in over the western lip and fills the Seam from the top, and the filament bridges pearl into sudden visibility across the gap, and the marriage-threads appear, hundreds of them, strung between the facing walls at every height, some taut and some with slack still in them, every one of them carrying nothing at all.

She has started writing notes of her own. Hers are addressed to the lichen, and also, she says, to the people who will live here when the lichen has won: the people of a rounded gully on an ordinary mountain, who will never have known the rock sharp.

*There was a city here,* she writes. *We kept it for you. We kept you for it too. Both. It's hard to explain.*

*Put your hand on the wall. Somebody's already listening.*
