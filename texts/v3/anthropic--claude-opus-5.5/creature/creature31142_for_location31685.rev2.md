# Those at Ten Paces

## The First Answer Is No One

The first answer is the one every traveler gives, and it is honest: nothing lives here.

There is nothing to hear at night, nothing to follow in the moss, nothing that eats. The travelers who come back say it the way people describe a house after a funeral. The rooms are all still there. The furniture is still there. Only the thing that moved through them is gone.

But the first answer does not survive the first week. It is not wrong so much as incomplete, and the tableland corrects it slowly, the way it corrects everything.

Start with what is plainly here. The dead are here. They do not live, but they remain, and on this tableland remaining is most of what living means. Nothing is eaten, so nothing is lost. The grey skirts on the rosettes hold every leaf the plant has grown since before there were names for it. The bark under the dwarf trees lies in drifts of red parchment, a library no one reads. The peat is the deepest of these archives. Cut into it and the walls of the cut are layered like pages, every season pressed flat and kept. A traveler once pulled up a sphagnum frond from three meters down and found it still soft, still red, still holding a bead of water that had been fog ten thousand years earlier.

In most places the dead are a stage that things pass through on their way back into the living. Here the dead simply stay. The tableland is inhabited first by everything that has ever grown on it, all at once, all present.

So when the land feels crowded, as it sometimes does, standing in the fog among the rosettes with that pressure of attention a person feels in a full room, part of the answer is that it *is* full. Nothing has ever left.

That is only the ground floor, though.

## The Hooded Crowd

The rosettes come next, because they are the most visible residents and the most unsettling.

They stand in stands of twenty or fifty, tall and silver-felted and hooded, their dead leaves wrapped around them like cloaks. In fog they look like people, and everyone who has crossed the tableland says so. They lean slightly east, as if waiting for something to come over the horizon. They are cold and damp to the touch, and they hold small still pools in their crowns like cupped hands holding water up to a mouth that never comes.

Some travelers count them. There is nothing else to do in the fog, and counting is a way of holding on. At noon, when the sky clears, a stand might be forty-one rosettes. At dusk, in fog, the same traveler counts again from the same stone and gets forty-three. The next morning it is forty-one.

Most people put this down to the fog, to bad counting and cold hands. A few walk into the stand at dusk to find the extra two. They move from silver trunk to silver trunk, touching each one. They always come out saying the same thing: every one they touched was a plant. But somewhere among the ones they had not yet reached, a figure stood that was the same height, wrapped the same way in felted grey, beaded with the same fog, and leaning the same way east. When they reached the place where it had been, there was only moss, and on the moss a little scatter of fresh grus, as if someone had been standing on it and had shifted their weight.

This is the first real glimpse. It is not a face or a hand. It is a shape that looks exactly like the land around it, because it is dressed in the land around it.

Look at the wool bundles in the wall niches of the finished houses and the resemblance becomes obvious. The bundles are rosette wool, combed from living leaves and felted into cloth. The inhabitants of the tableland wear the rosettes. In fog, a crowd of them standing still would look exactly like a stand of rosettes, and a stand of rosettes looks exactly like a crowd of them.

It is possible that they learned to dress this way so as not to be seen. It is also possible that it goes the other way: the rosettes grew into their shape over a very long time, and the plants lean east because the people do.

## What the Tools Say

The builders have never been seen clearly, but everything they touch holds them, and on a land where nothing wears away, the holding is precise.

Pick up a hammerstone. It will be warm, which is the first shock. Then feel how it sits in the palm. The harder stones used for pecking are river cobbles of dark basalt, carried from somewhere far off the tableland, since nothing on it is hard enough. Years of use have worn them into a grip. There is a shallow hollow for the thumb and a flattened face where the four fingers wrap. A human hand fits it, but not well. The hand it was worn for was broader across the palm and shorter in the fingers, with a thumb set lower and stronger. It was a hand built for pressing and gripping more than for reaching. It resembles a cushion plant: compact, dense, made to hold on.

Consider the doorways. They are trapezoidal, narrower at the top, and their height sits just under the height of a tall traveler's shoulder. The builders were short, or they went through doors stooping, or both. The lintels are enormous, single stones that must weigh as much as a small cart, and they were set without any sign of ramps or scaffolding. Only the silver levers remain, and the coiled rope, and marks in the ground where many feet once braced together. They were not strong the way one large animal is strong. They were strong the way a crowd is strong, many small, patient bodies pulling on one line at once.

Listen to the tapping when it comes. It is never frantic. The rhythm is about the speed of a slow breath, a strike on the exhale, a pause on the inhale. At four thousand meters, where the air is thin enough that a hurried traveler's heart pounds, these are people who learned long ago not to hurry. Their chests would be deep and their breathing slow. They would work the way the land does, at the pace that can be kept up forever.

Then there are the marks. They are hardest to find, and they change everything once found.

In the finished walls, the fitted faces show nothing but pecked stone. In the ruins, where stones have fallen and rolled, the hidden faces show. The sides that were set inward, against other stones, where no one would ever look, are carved. The carving is small and shallow, about the size of a thumbprint, and each stone carries one sign. There are thousands of different signs: spirals, crossed lines, a cluster of dots, a shape like a rosette seen from above, a shape like an open hand.

A traveler who spent a long season on the tableland made a rubbing of every mark she could find in one ruined district. Months later, a hundred kilometers on, she stood in a finished district at the leading edge. A wall had not yet had its capstones set, and she could reach behind the top course. She found the marks there too. Her rubbings matched them. The same sign was on the same size of stone in the same position in the same wall.

It was the same stone. It had been carried a hundred kilometers, or it had not been carried at all and it was the district that had moved. The mark was a name, and it was the name of whoever set that stone, every time, in every rising of the city.

They sign their work on the side that faces inward, toward the wall itself, as though the only ones meant to read it are the other stones.

## What the Houses Say

The finished houses are the closest anyone comes to being inside the builders' lives, and they read like a letter left open on a table.

The sleeping platforms are stone benches around the curved inner wall, padded with dried moss and felted wool. They are short, too short to lie on straight. The inhabitants sleep curled, knees drawn up and arms folded in, making themselves small and round against the cold the way the cushion plants do, the way the tors themselves have slumped into sleeping shapes. Several can share a platform. The wool padding is worn into hollows side by side, like the dents birds leave in a nest.

The grinding stones set into the floor are worn into deep saddles by long use. What they grind is rosette seed. Small drifts of it are sometimes found in cracks in the floor, silver-hulled and bitter, and they will grow if planted. The cistern-gardens where the rosettes stand in rows are not decoration. They are fields. The builders farm the fog-drinkers and eat what the fog-drinkers make. They drink from the crown pools, and the pools in the garden rosettes sit lower and are sometimes found faintly rippled, the only rosette water on the whole tableland that is ever not perfectly still.

The hearths are laid with the grey skirts of rosettes, dry and dense and good fuel. Nothing on the tableland has ever burned. There is no ash anywhere, in any hearth, in any district, in any ruin, not even in the deepest peat. Every fire has been laid and none has been lit.

This is one of the things that should give a visitor pause. A people who build this carefully, in this cold, would surely need fire. And yet, if they have fire, it does not reach here.

Then there are the niches. Each house has several, small alcoves in the wall at the height of a short person's eyes, and each holds a few things: a copper pin for fastening a cloak, a wool bundle, a carved pebble. The pebbles are always river pebbles, smooth and dark, brought from off the tableland like the hammerstones, and each is carved with one of the thumbprint signs from the hidden faces of the walls.

The signs on the pebbles do not match the signs on the stones of their own houses. Every traveler who has checked has found this. The pebble in a house never bears the mark of anyone who built that house.

It took a long time for anyone to put forward the obvious reading: the pebbles are not the builders' own. They are gifts. They are names set out for someone else.

## What They Are

Gather the evidence and a picture forms, though it is never seen whole.

They are a people. There are many of them, perhaps thousands at the leading edge of the city at any one time, perhaps more. They are short and broad, deep-chested, with wide strong hands. They wear felted rosette wool that silvers and beads in fog, and they stand very still when they are not working, leaning slightly east. They farm rosettes and eat their seeds. They drink fog-water from the crowns. They sleep curled together against the cold. They work in stone with a patience that does not register as effort, pecking each face smooth and fitting each joint until a blade of grass will not enter, and they carve a name into the hidden side of every stone they set.

They breathe slowly. They do not seem to speak, or at least nothing they say has ever been heard. Only the tapping carries.

They are the only animals on the tableland. This should be said plainly, because once it is said, the whole ecology falls into place. The land is green and fast-growing and nothing eats it, because the thing that eats it is a half-step away. The peat builds and builds, uncropped, because the ones who would cut it for fuel and gather it for bedding are always just out of reach. The dead do not rot because the living do not quite arrive. The tableland is a pasture whose herd is standing, invisibly, at the edge of it. The wool, the seeds, the skirts, the water: all of it is gathered, but gathered somewhere else.

Travelers call them by the only name that fits, which is a measurement: *those at ten paces*.

## Ten Paces

Where are they, exactly?

Nobody knows, but the tableland supplies clues, and the clues agree.

The builders are closest in fog. When the world shrinks to ten paces, the tapping comes, the rosette count goes wrong, and stones move from the moss to the wall. When the fog lifts and the view opens to the curved horizon, they recede. The noon sky belongs to the land alone.

Here is one way to think about it, offered by a traveler who crossed three times and meant to cross a fourth. On a land this vast and this flat, with nothing to measure against, *here* spreads out. The eye cannot find edges, so everything is equally far. In fog the opposite happens. The world becomes a small room, and in a small room the distance between *here* and *just beyond here* becomes the only distance there is. The builders live in that margin, the space just past the edge of what can be perceived. In clear weather the margin is the horizon, impossibly far off. In fog it is ten paces away, close enough to hear stone strike stone.

But the distance is not really one of space. The travelers know this even when they cannot say it. The shadows give it away.

At noon, in the clear hour, a person's shadow sometimes arrives a half-step late. It settles into place a moment after the body stops, as if it had to catch up. Everyone who stays long enough sees this happen. What they come to understand, slowly, is that the shadow is not lagging. The shadow is on time. The person is early.

The tableland's present, the moment when the builders are actually here, runs a half-step behind the one travelers carry with them. Or it runs a half-step ahead. From inside, the difference cannot be told. What matters is that the gap is small. It is not centuries or years. It is roughly the time it takes to put down a hammerstone and step back from a wall. That is why the stone is warm. A hand held it a half-step ago, and in the tableland's own time that hand is holding it right now.

Stone is the only thing that crosses the gap cleanly. The walls and tors are so old, and so finished with change, that they belong to every moment equally. A granite boss that has spent a hundred million years losing its edges is not troubled by a half-step. It is the same stone on both sides. So the sound of stone striking stone passes through. Voices do not. Footsteps on moss do not. The builders' breath, their hearths, their fires if they have them, their sleeping and eating and talking, all stay on their side of the half-step. Only the work comes through, because the work is made of the one material that time has stopped bothering with.

That is why the dust at the base of the walls stays dry in the fog. It is fresh dust, from a fresh strike, and it has not yet had the half-step's worth of damp that would settle on it.

## From the Other Side

If this is true, it must be true in both directions.

To them, we are the ones at ten paces.

The travelers realized this late, and some of them have not stopped thinking about it since. A traveler walks through a builders' district in the fog and, from the builders' side, a sound passes: the crunch of grus under a boot, the one sound that the tableland's own stone gravel would carry across. A warmth is left on a stone where a traveler rested a hand. A shape appears among the rosettes, too tall and wrong-coated, not beaded silver but dark, and when someone walks toward it, it is gone.

They have sat in the fog and heard us. They have counted their own crowd and come up one short, or one too many. In the stillness of their crown pools, which never ripple because a half-step's worth of wind has not yet reached them, they have seen our skies, the clear blue of our noon over their fog, the night of our side over their day. Once, in one of our pools, a traveler saw a hand setting a stone. That was a crown pool doing, for one moment, what the builders' crown pools may do for them all the time: showing the other side.

We are their ghosts. We are what haunts their city. We are the sounds just beyond the edge of the fog that cannot be walked up to. We are the reason their stones sometimes shift slightly in the night, and why the moss is sometimes pressed flat in a trail of large, strange footprints that leads from nowhere and ends in a finished house.

And they have not been frightened of us. That may be the most important thing the tableland has to say about them. Everything they leave says they have been kind.

## The Laid Hearth

Go back to the finished houses with this in mind, and they read differently.

The hearths are laid and waiting. The sleeping platforms are padded. The niches hold a pin to fasten a cloak against the cold, a bundle of wool to make the cloak from, and a pebble carved with a name that belongs to none of the builders. The pebble offers a sign to anyone who needs one, a name to carry, a place to put a mark of one's own.

These are not houses the builders live in. They are houses the builders build *for someone*. Every finished district is a guest-house as large as a town, set out on a road across open country, with the fires laid for travelers who have not yet arrived.

This is what the city is: hospitality, carried over a distance so long it has had to become architecture.

Then look again at the pattern of rising and ruin. The city is not wandering. It is trying to *meet* someone. The builders raise a district, finish it, lay the hearths, set out the gifts, and wait for the moment when the half-step closes and a guest steps through the door at the same time the host does. That is the moment between completion and collapse, and it is the one nobody can find, because finding it requires both sides to arrive at once.

It never quite happens. The traveler arrives a half-step early and finds a cold hearth and an empty house. The builders arrive a half-step later and find warmth on the platform and a boot print in the moss, the guest already gone. The house has done its work, or failed to. So it comes down. Its stones, each one named on the inward face, are carried forward to the next place along the road, where the builders try again. This is why the ruins are not decay. They are a house that was not needed yet, being taken down so it can be moved to where it might be.

They have been doing this for centuries. By the measure of the peat, and the lichen, and the depth of moss over the oldest foundations, they have been doing it for much longer than that. The roads are the routes they think guests will take, straight across the table from nowhere to nowhere, because on this land there is no reason to go anywhere but across.

The tapping is unhurried because they know how much time they have. By their reckoning it is enough. On a land where nothing rots and nothing falls that has not already fallen, there is always enough time to build one more house and try again.

## The Ones Who Walked Through

There is one exception to the rule that the moment is never found, and it is the doorways.

The lone doorway standing in the moss, frameless, with nothing on either side and the same cracked lintel and the same orange splash of lichen on its left jamb as a doorway in a finished house days away, is the first thing the builders set in every district. A door comes before a house, because a door is the point. It is the invitation, laid down before the walls that would make it mean anything.

Walking through one does nothing, or seems to. But the travelers who have done it say the tapping comes more clearly afterward. It comes nearer, perhaps eight paces now instead of ten. Their shadows lag a little more at noon. The warmth on the hammerstones lasts a little longer. The pools in the crowns show, a little more often, a sky that is not overhead.

Some travelers walked through a doorway and then walked through another, and another. They are the ones who did not come back.

It is not clear that anything terrible happened to them. The reports of the people who crossed with them, and who turned back at the edge, are strangely calm. The walkers grew quieter. They breathed more slowly. They stopped counting the rosettes. They began to sleep curled. One of them started carrying a river pebble picked up from a niche, and later a hammerstone, which she said fit her hand better each day. Near the end, her companions lost her in fog, at ten paces, and from where she had been standing came a sound of stone set carefully on stone, and a long exhaled breath.

Some of the marks on the hidden faces of the walls are newer than others. A very few match the signs on niche pebbles that travelers pocketed and carried home, years before, from districts a thousand kilometers behind. This means the pebbles went out and came back. It may mean that some of the builders at the leading edge began as guests. It may mean that the half-step, walked patiently enough, closes, and that on the other side there is a lit fire.

So the houses are not waiting for no one. They are waiting for anyone. Now and then, someone arrives.

## What Lives There

Here is the whole answer, as far as it goes.

A people lives on the tableland. They are short, broad-handed, and deep-breathing, dressed in the silver wool of the rosettes so exactly that they are the rosettes in fog. They farm the fog-drinking plants and grind their seeds, drink from their crowns, and burn their dead skirts in hearths that only they can light. They sleep curled together like the cushion plants and work stone the way the land wears it, slowly, unstoppably, at the pace of breath. They carve their names on the inward face of every stone, where only the other stones will read them.

They live a half-step from us in time. They are closest in fog, when the world shrinks enough that a half-step becomes ten paces and stone-sound can cross. To them we are the wanderers at the edge of sight. We leave the warm stones, the wrong shadows, the too-many figures in the rosette stands. They have never been afraid of us. They have spent longer than anyone can reckon building a city of empty guest-houses across the roof of the world, setting out fires and cloak-pins and names for whoever might come in out of the cold. When the half-step does not close, they take the houses down stone by stone and carry them farther along the road, to try again.

There is one more layer. It is less certain, and the travelers who have gone deepest only half-say it.

The tableland was a mountain range once. It stood up into the sky, and then for an age beyond ages it did nothing but fall. It lost its peaks, its cliffs, its edges, everything that could come down. What stands now stands because it has nowhere left to go. A land like that has finished the only thing it knew how to do. And somewhere in the long rounding of its stone, in the endless patient breathing of its fog, it may have found a way to keep going: something that raises stone up out of rubble instead of letting it fall. It is a slow tide of walls that climbs, completes, and comes down, so that the old land has something left to lose and can keep losing it gently, forever, and never be quite finished.

The builders may be a people who found this land and learned its ways. They may be what the land became when it ran out of mountain. They may be both, and on the tableland the difference may not matter. Their hands are shaped like the cushion plants. Their bodies are wrapped like the rosettes. Their work moves like weather. Their rhythm is the land's own breathing. Their houses stand exactly as long as the land can bear to hold them up.

What lives there, then, is a crowd of patient hosts in silver wool, standing at ten paces in the fog, leaning east. They are waiting with a hearth laid and a pebble set out and a doorway already standing. They listen to your footsteps crunch on the grus and do not call out, because voices do not cross. They lift the next stone, find its place, and set it down carefully, so that you will hear it, and know which way to walk.
