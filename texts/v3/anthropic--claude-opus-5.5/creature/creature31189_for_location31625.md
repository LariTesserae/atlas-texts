# Who Lives in Haum

## I. The Keeper

The first answer is a woman named Ilu Teshaum, because she is the first person who speaks to you. She speaks loudly, the way everyone here does.

She is sitting in the door of a tapping-house with her back against the frame and her feet against the trunk of an aum called Old Knuckle. She has kept this tree for forty-one years. Her mother kept it before her, and her mother's uncle before that. She is small, even for Haum. Her hands are wide and cracked, and the creases of her palms are stained permanently amber with sap, as if the lines of her fate had been filled in with resin. She wears a shift of grey aum-cloth dyed at the hem with orange lichen. The dye has faded almost to nothing, and she tells you it was her grandmother's.

There are eleven notches on Old Knuckle. She knows every one by name and by temper. The notch low on the east side runs fast and thin, and she calls it the Gossip. The notch high on the south side runs slow and thick, and she calls it Aunt Remi, after an aunt who was also slow and thick and who is now in the canopy above this very trunk. Ilu can point to the rosette. She does, without looking up.

She does not collect sap so much as listen for it. Each jar sits under its spout, and each drop lands with its own small sound, *tk*, *tk*, *tk*. Under the swifts, the beetles and the querns, she can tell you whether the tree is content. When it is unhappy, the drops come unevenly, bunching up and then pausing, "like someone trying not to cry," she says. When that happens she closes a notch with a plug of wax and does not open it again until the rhythm is steady. Sometimes that takes a season. Once it took nine years.

"You think I own this tree," she says. You had not thought that, but she says it anyway, because lowlanders always do. "The tree owns this house. I'm what the tree uses to carry water to people. It can't walk. I can." She laughs, a short bark, and a dog in the lane barks back.

That is the first answer, and it is true. But Ilu would be the first to tell you that she does not live here alone.

## II. The Household

Under her floor there are rock-rabbits. You can hear them, a thin two-note whistle through the gaps in the stone that is almost a question. There have been rabbits under this house for as long as there has been this house, and this house is built on the house before it. The rabbits probably came with the first stones. They are Old Knuckle's rabbits and Ilu's rabbits, in that order. When a rabbit dies she puts it in the litter at the tree's foot, and she tells you, quite seriously, that you can taste it in the sap the following year: a faint warmth, a little salt.

In the wall above her head is a colony of swifts. Their nests are cups of spit and aum-fluff mortared into a crack that Ilu will not fill, although the crack lets in draughts. The droppings run down the wall in a white fan. She scrapes it once a month and trades it for tuber. The swifts are not hers and are not anyone's. At dusk they come screaming in, and for a quarter of an hour the house is so loud that she stops talking entirely and simply sits, eyes shut, and lets it pour over her. Afterward her face is wet. She does not wipe it.

Geckos click on the warm plaster. A spider has strung a web between the door-frame and the first low branch of the tree. Every dawn Ilu lifts the frost from that web into a thimble-cup of black clay and drinks it, and then she says the spider's name. The spider has no name, she admits. She says a name anyway, and it is a different one every day.

Then there is Sau.

## III. The Thirty

Sau walks in while you are talking. It is the length of a man's leg, heavy, slow and blunt-headed, grey-brown with orange flecks scattered down its back like embers. It does not hurry. It crosses the threshold, walks over Ilu's feet as if they were a fallen branch, and goes to lie along the base of Old Knuckle, where a small crack in the floor breathes warm air. It shuts its eyes. Ilu moves the sap-jar two fingers to the left so the lizard will not knock it, and that is all.

"Sau is a grandmother," she says. "Sau was a grandmother when my grandmother was a girl."

There are thirty of them, more or less. Some say twenty-nine, because one has not been seen in a long while, and no one wants to say aloud that it is gone. Each has a name, and the names are old. Some belong to no living language and are only sounds that have been passed down with the animal. Sau. Hekkerum. The Tall One. Grey Step. Imri-of-the-Old-Breath, named for a vent that went cold three hundred years ago. Imri is still alive.

Here is something the people of Haum know and do not usually tell visitors. The lizards know where the heat is going before anyone else does.

Long before a vent sighs its last, the lizards stop visiting it. Long before a new crack opens, they begin to gather at a certain place on a certain paving stone. They lie there with their bellies flat to the rock, as though they were listening. Then, over years, the stone grows warm. The cooking courts of Haum have moved across the bowl for as long as anyone remembers. They have not moved because the people read the ground. They have moved because the people read the lizards.

So when someone says the lizards are *sacred*, they mean something more practical than worship. The lizards are how the town hears the mountain. They are old enough, slow enough and warm-blooded-by-borrowing enough to feel changes that happen over a century. People cannot live at that pace. The lizards can.

And there is a story, told late at night at the Breath when the fires are low. It says the lizards were here first. Before the people, before the dogs, perhaps before the aum, the heat came up through the throat of the mountain and the lizards lay on it. When the people climbed out of the cloud, the lizards looked at them with their gold, lidded eyes and decided not to object. Nobody has ever harmed a lizard, the story says, because nobody has ever been sure the lizards did not simply let them stay.

## IV. The Ones Overhead

Look up.

In the canopy above Ilu's door, among the grey-green rosettes and the hanging bellflower ropes and the brown clusters of sleeping bats, you can make out shapes. They are long bundles, grey-wrapped, lying across the forks of the branches the way a child sleeps across its parents' laps. There are dozens in Old Knuckle alone. Across the bowl there are hundreds.

These are the dead, and in Haum the dead are residents.

They are not ghosts. Nobody here speaks of ghosts. The dead are simply people who have moved upstairs. In the dry, warm, resin-scented air they do not rot. They slowly become leather, and then something like wood. The beetles visit. The swifts pull threads from the wrappings for their nests, so that there are swift-nests all over the town woven partly from the shrouds of grandparents, and this is considered lucky. The frost forms on the dead as it forms on the leaves. At dawn it melts, and the aum drinks what runs off.

Every drop of water in Haum has passed through a plant. That is the rule of the place, and the people know what follows from it. The water in Ilu's body came from Old Knuckle. Old Knuckle drank the frost that formed on Aunt Remi. Aunt Remi's water came, in her life, from this same trunk. The water goes around and around. Nothing is thrown over the lip, and that includes people. So when Ilu says that her aunt is above this trunk, she also means, without quite saying it, that her aunt is in the cup.

After a generation the bones come down. They are gathered in cloth and carried into the old rooms beneath the houses, the rooms whose doorways once opened on lanes that are now twenty feet underground. They are laid on stone shelves in the cool dark. Children are brought down to visit them and to be introduced. Ilu will take you down if you ask. The air there is still and dry, and it is the quietest place in Haum. That is not very quiet, because you can still hear the beetle-larvae churning in the litter on the other side of the wall, a sound like far-off rain. It is quiet enough that Ilu talks the whole time, loudly and cheerfully, naming everyone, so that the silence cannot settle.

When you count the people of Haum, then, you have to decide whom to count. The people say "a few hundred," but they say it the way you would count only the people in the room and not the ones asleep upstairs or resting in the cellar. If you asked Ilu how many of her own family lived in her house, she would pause, then begin to count. She would get past sixty before you stopped her.

## V. The One Underneath

Here the answer begins to change shape.

Ask the keepers whether the aum are separate trees, and they will give you a strange look.

Nobody has dug to the bottom of the litter. But people have dug down a long way, making cellars and chasing the foundations of older houses, and everywhere they dig they find roots. There are fine hair-roots, silver-grey, running through the dark spice-smelling litter like threads through felt. There are heavier roots, as thick as an arm. Deeper still, there are roots that are not so much roots as corridors of old, dense, resinous wood running under the lanes. A cellar wall might be half stone and half root, and nobody can say which came first.

The keepers talk. Ilu knows the keeper of the Leaning Mother, forty paces away, and the keeper of the aum at the Breath, and the keeper of the dying tree by the terraces. They compare the moods of their trees. They have noticed, over lifetimes, that the trees are sometimes unhappy together. They have also noticed that when a notch is overtapped on one side of the bowl, a tree on the other side runs uneven for a week.

No one says it straight out. But the old keepers, at the Breath, late, will sometimes say *the aum*, singular, the way you would speak of a person. There may be no trees in Haum at all. There may be one plant, very old, grown into a hundred trunks that hold up a single roof. It has been rooting through the litter since before the litter was deep, spreading into the old throat of the volcano, and drinking the frost with ten thousand furred hands.

If that is so, the town of Haum is built inside a single living thing. The houses are built between its fingers. The dead sleep in its palm. The people drink from it, then give themselves back to it, then are drunk again. The rabbits and the swifts and the beetles are part of how it eats and how it is cleaned. The beetle-larvae keep its litter loose. The people carry its water. The swifts carry off what would otherwise swarm it. The lizards read the heat it needs.

And the frost that feeds it comes from outside the lip, from the dead dome, from the thin dry sky. That is the only thing Haum takes from beyond itself: a few mouthfuls a night, per rosette, of air turned to ice.

## VI. The Breath

But even the aum is not the oldest thing that lives here.

Stand at the Breath at night. The court is paved with grey lava worn slick as soap by uncountable feet. The lizards lie in a loose ring, their bodies angled toward the centre like the spokes of a wheel. The people sit farther out, on the warm stones, roasting beetles and talking over one another. In the middle of the court is a crack no wider than your hand, and out of it comes the warmth.

Put your palm over it and you feel the push of air, dry and sweet, with a trace of sulfur. It is very steady. It has been steady since before anyone's grandmother's grandmother. You would call it a vent. The people of Haum call it the Breath, and they do not mean it as a metaphor.

This is where the shape settles.

Down there, under the litter and the roots and the drowned houses and the stone, the mountain is still very slightly alive. The fire that once built it has gone, and the mountain has worn itself down to a knuckle. But some small, stubborn heat remains in its core, the last ember of something enormous. It breathes out in sighs and pauses, the way the very old breathe in their sleep. Every living thing in Haum is gathered around that breath the way a family gathers around a grandmother's bed. They are the aum, the lizards, the swifts, the beetles, the rabbits, the people and the dead, all pressed together in the one warm place on the summit.

Now you understand why the people are so loud.

They will tell you they find silence frightening. They find it frightening because they are listening, all the time and underneath everything, to that other sound: the low uneven exhalation from the cracks in the floor. It is quieter than the swifts, the beetles and the querns, but the town is always aware of it. A silence in Haum would let them hear it too clearly. They would count the gaps between breaths. They would wonder whether each pause is longer than the last.

So they keep up the noise. They talk and sing and grind and hammer, and the children shriek and let the swifts miss them by a hand's width. It is not only for themselves. It is the noise that a family makes at a bedside, so the one who is sleeping will know they are not alone. That is why the Breath must never be covered. You do not put a blanket over the face of the dying.

## VII. Haum

So who lives in Haum?

Ilu does, and her rabbits, and Sau lying against the warm trunk. The screaming column of swifts does, and the bronze sheets of beetles, and the thirty named lizards with their gold, lidded, century-long eyes. The dead do, grey-wrapped in the branches and pale on their shelves below. The aum does, one plant or a hundred, holding the roof up with its many trunks. The last heat of the mountain does, breathing in its sleep.

But these are not really separate answers. Walk the lanes long enough, under the canopy, through half-rooms and courtyards where the line between street and home is ignored, and you stop being able to see where one resident ends and the next begins. Water moves from frost to leaf to sap to cup to body to shroud to frost again. Heat moves from rock to lizard to stone to the hands of a woman warming herself at night. The litter is made of all of them, and the next house is built on it. The whole bowl turns slowly around its breathing centre, taking in frost at dawn and swifts at dusk, and putting out nothing, ever, over the lip.

The thing that lives in Haum is Haum. It has lichen for skin, stone-ivy for sinew, aum for lungs, and people for its hands. Its heart is a crack in a court of grey lava. Its memory is the layers of old houses going down into the dark. It has lived here for a very long time. It is going to die, as the mountain is going to die, on a scale of time so long that dying is not quite the right word. It is more like a coal going grey.

It does not seem to mind. It is too busy. There is frost to drink and sap to carry. There are the dead to lift into the branches and to bring down again. It has a great deal to say, all at once, at the top of its voice.

## VIII. Last

Late at night you find a child at the lip.

She is maybe eight. She is standing exactly on the worn edge, where the dry world ends, with her toes in the kept world and her face toward the dark dome. Out there the frost is beginning to form, and it crackles faintly on the stone. It is very cold out there and very quiet. The cloud sea is white under the stars, and it goes on forever.

She is not going to step over. Nobody steps over carelessly. She is only listening. Behind her the town roars softly: beetles, voices, the last bats, someone singing four notes over and over. In front of her there is nothing at all.

After a while she turns around and runs back down into the warm, loud dark, shouting someone's name.
