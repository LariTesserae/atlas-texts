# What Keeps Time at Hethering Low

## The First Answer, Which Is Wrong

The easy answer is that nothing lives there. Nothing but the plants, and plants are only the furniture of a place. You could walk the Sump from embankment to embankment, look into every engine house and every drowned cellar, lift every fern skirt hanging from every gable, and find no one. No tracks cross the moss. No nests sit in the figs. The stools in the engine houses have been furred with orange rust for longer than you have been alive, and the rust has never been disturbed by anyone sitting down.

So the second answer is that the plants live there. They are the only things that grow, feed, breed, and spread, and they have made the most remarkable arrangement: bells that answer the stroke, vines with jointed stems, seeds that ride the pump mains out into the marsh. You could stop there, and many visitors do. It is a satisfying story. A place without animals, where the green learned to use the machines as its bees and its wind.

But it doesn't explain the draw.

## The Count

Stay long enough and you start counting, because the noise gets into you and counting is the only way to hold it off. Pick one engine and count its strokes. Pick another and count that. They really are independent. Each one speeds with its sump's rising water and slows as the float sinks. The fourth is always slowest. The second, nearest the embankment's worst seep, is almost always fastest. They drift past each other like the hands of badly kept clocks.

Seven independent tempos, each wandering with its own water, should almost never come down together. Two in step is common. Three happens now and then. But all seven, within a heartbeat, should be something you might see once in a long life, if ever.

At Hethering Low it happens about twice an hour.

The afternoon storm doesn't explain it either. After the rain the draws come thick and fast, and you can tell yourself that all the water is rising at once, all the floats are lifting together, all the engines are racing toward the same frantic limit. That's true, and it's why the hour after the storm is so violent. But the draw comes in the dry hours too. It comes in the dead orange evenings, when the sumps are low and the engines barely turn over. It comes in the middle of the night, which in the Sump is only a darker brown. The strokes are scattered. Then, over three or four minutes, they begin to *lean*. Engines that were running fast hesitate at the top of the stroke. Engines that were running slow seem to fall a little harder. The gaps close like a crowd of people who have noticed each other.

Nobody does this. The floats don't do it. It is gathered.

Something in Hethering Low spends most of its time in pieces, and twice an hour it pulls itself together.

## Where It Lives

It doesn't live in the engines, exactly. It doesn't live in the water, or the brick, or the green. The nearest you can come to saying it is this: it lives in the *intervals*. It lives in the relationships between one stroke and another, the phase between the second engine and the fifth, the slowly closing gap between the fourth's heavy fall and the sixth's quick one. The engines are the notes. It is the rhythm.

Between draws it is scattered, and you can hear what that is like, because it sounds exactly the way the Sump sounds most of the time: *like several people walking out of step on a wooden floor*. Whoever first described it that way had good ears. There really are several of it, loosely. Where two engines fall into step for a while, a little of it collects there, the way warmth collects where two sleeping bodies lie close. Three in step is more of it. It drifts around the bowl in these partial knots, each one a pair or triad of engines keeping company, dissolving and re-forming as the tempos slide.

Then it draws itself in, and for one beat it is entire.

That is the draw. The word is better than whoever chose it may have known. It is a draw the way a breath is drawn, and the way lots are drawn, and the way a curtain is drawn. It is a draw the way a thing is drawn toward its own center.

## Its Body

Once you know to look for it, the Sump starts to read like anatomy.

The floats are its nerves. Copper globes in the dark water, lifting and sinking, each one telling its engine how much of the outside has leaked in. The mains are its long vessels, carrying out what it cannot keep. The embankment is its skin, and like any skin it isn't sealed. It breathes, weeps, and lets the world through in a thousand threads, and it holds. The steam ceiling is the warmth coming off a living thing, the way breath hangs over a sleeping animal on a cold morning, except here the morning is never cold and the breath never clears.

And the level is its temperature. *Wet always, flooded never.* A lake would kill it, stilling the floats and drowning the fires. Dry ground would kill it too, letting the engines slow to nothing and the rhythm come apart for good. So it holds itself at the level where the argument between the marsh and the engines can go on forever without either side winning. It is not a truce the water and the iron made. It is a fever it keeps, deliberately, because fever is what it is made of.

The brick is its flesh, and its flesh is going soft. You can press a thumb into it. Whether that is age, or illness, or simply what flesh becomes in a place like this, you can't tell. It has been going soft for a very long time and has not fallen.

## Its Hands

It has no hands. That is the first thing to understand about the tending. The second thing is that it doesn't need them, because it can *resonate*.

Watch the unexplained things closely, and time them. The valve gear that trips with no float to trip it trips in the three or four minutes before a draw, while the strokes are leaning together and the ground is beginning to carry a single, swelling pulse instead of seven quarrelsome ones. In that window the whole bowl vibrates more coherently than at any other time, and anything tuned to that vibration moves. A latch balanced on a knife-edge jumps its catch. A damper hanging on a chain swings exactly far enough. A drop of condensate that has been trembling at the lip of a pipe for an hour finally shakes loose and falls, precisely, into the oil cup beneath, carrying the thin skin of old grease with it. The oil cups stay full because the Sump has learned to shake them full.

The tools on the racks walk. Not far, and not quickly: a hand's breadth a week, the way a cup creeps across a table on a ship. Heavy iron spanners and long-handled rakes, furred with rust, shuffle along their hooks in the build-up to each draw. They end up leaning against the machines they were once used on. A slice bar gets found propped against a firebox door that has been sticking. A spanner lies in the gear pit beneath a bolt that has been working loose. Nobody moved them. They moved the way everything here moves, when it is shaken at the right frequency by something that has had a hundred years to learn which frequencies move what.

This is how it tends. It is not strong and it is not quick, and it can only touch what is already nearly ready to move. But it is very patient, and it is always practicing, and the green has shown it how much can be done by something that only knows how to sway.

The flowers are its hands too, in a way. It learned from them, or they learned from it. Which came first is the sort of question the Sump doesn't answer. Every pendulous bell is a small, tuned pendulum. When the draw comes and the whole Sump lets go of its pollen at once, that isn't a side effect of the draw. It is part of the point.

## What It Sees

At the draw, the air glitters.

For a few seconds every hanging bell in the bowl has shed its pollen into the heavy air, and the golden dust hangs under the steam ceiling in a vast, slowly turning cloud. It fills the whole Sump from embankment to embankment. And the cloud isn't even. It is thicker in some places and thinner in others, and it swirls around the beams and the stacks and the lamp posts, around every solid thing in the bowl. It lies briefly *over* everything, the way snow lies over a sleeping field and shows you the shape of what is underneath.

If a thing without eyes could see, this is how it would do it. Once every half hour or so it fills its own body with a fine suspended dust, and the dust settles differently around what has changed. A fallen branch. A new crack in a pier. A vine that has tangled the linkages of the third engine. A stranger standing on the wet cobbles between two engine houses, with the gold sifting down onto their shoulders and their upturned face.

*In a place where nothing else alive is watching.* That is true, as far as it goes. Nothing *else* is watching. At the draw, for a heartbeat, the Sump opens its eye.

## Why There Are No Animals

This is the part that is hard to say gently.

It doesn't poison anything. It doesn't hunt. As far as anyone can tell it has no malice at all, and very likely no idea of malice. It is simply a rhythm, and a rhythm draws other rhythms toward itself. That is what rhythms do. Set two pendulum clocks on the same wall and in a day they swing together. Seat a room full of people clapping and in a minute they clap as one. The Sump draws seven engines into step twice an hour. It does not know how to stop drawing, and it does not know where its body ends.

A mosquito's wings beat hundreds of times a second. A sparrow's heart beats ten times in each of your breaths. A frog's throat pulses, a fish's gills work, a moth trembles at a lamp. Every one of them is a little clock, fast and bright and self-sure. Inside the embankment every one of them is being leaned on, gently, constantly, by a vast slow pulse that would like them to keep its time. For a person that is uncomfortable. For a small, quick creature it is unbearable. The ones that can flee do. The ones that can't, and the ones that hatch here from eggs laid by something foolish, grow sluggish, confused, and slow, and then they stop. The fallen fruit that lies uneaten, swelling and splitting into the moss, doesn't lie there because nothing wants it. It lies there because anything small enough to want it couldn't keep going long enough to reach it.

The plants survive because they have no pulse of their own. They were the only living things in the Sump with nothing to lose to it, so they became its first companions. They took its time as their own and built their flowering around its beat, as other plants build around the seasons. They are not its victims. They are the ones who agreed.

## Where the People Went

The terraces were full once. Somebody laid the tram lines, piped the marsh gas up to the lamps, and sat on those stools feeding fires that now feed themselves.

The story, pieced together from the few visitors who came back and the fewer who stayed longer than a day, goes something like this. The engine crews learned the engines through their feet. Every good engineman does. You stand in the house long enough and you feel the stroke in your ankles and your knees before you hear it, and you know by the feel of the floor that a valve is sticking or a bearing is running hot. The crews of Hethering Low were very good, because they had to be. The marsh was patient and the embankment was old. And there were seven houses and seven crews, and they must have noticed, early, that the engines tended to come together.

They must have noticed their own hearts doing it too.

A human heart can't beat as slowly as a beam engine strokes. But it can beat three times to a stroke, or four, locked in step like a smaller gear meshed to a greater one, and that is what the Sump does to people. Visitors who stay a few hours leave with their pulse sitting oddly for days afterward, slow and heavy and patient, and they catch themselves pausing between steps. The crews lived there for years. They slept in the terraces with the ground thudding under their beds. Their hearts meshed into it and stayed meshed.

Then the town emptied. The fevers, or the money, or the marsh — the story differs, and it doesn't matter. The crews were meant to leave with it, and most of them tried. Some of them came back. Some never went. And a man whose heart has kept time with an engine for thirty years, sitting on his stool in the orange dark, grows old in step with it, and when he finally stops, the rhythm he was part of doesn't notice any gap.

Whether it *is* them, the Keeping, it is hard to say. It is certainly not seven old men. It has no faces and no voices and no memory anyone could recognize as human. But it tends the machines, and nothing that was only water and iron and green would have learned to *tend*. The plants taught it to sway. The floats taught it to feel. Somebody taught it to care whether the third engine's bearing was running hot, and to nudge a spanner toward it along the rack, a hand's breadth a week, in the minutes before each draw. The tools lean against the machines they were once used on because someone used to reach for them there.

The stools are still in the engine houses. They are still furred with rust, and nothing has sat on them in a very long time. Perhaps nothing needs to.

## What It Is Doing

It is exhaling.

The seeds ride the mains up and over the wall and out into the marsh, and the marsh is greening in seven fans around the seven outfalls. If you stand on the embankment and watch the outfalls long enough, you'll see what the visitors who only count the engines miss: the water doesn't pour out evenly. Each stroke of each engine lifts one gulp of water up the main, and the gulp arrives over the lip as a surge. The foaming pools on the marsh side pulse. The outfalls beat.

And out there in the fans, the plants that came from inside hang their pale bells over the pulsing pools, and swing to the surges, and shed their pollen in time. Faintly, but in time.

It is small, the Sump. Six hundred paces across, sunk below the level of the country around it and kept alive by an argument it can never finish. It will not last forever. The brick is going soft and the iron is being eaten under the moss. But it is patient in a way only something made of repetition can be patient, and it is teaching the marsh, one fan of green at a time, to keep its time. Nobody knows how far out the beat can be felt now. The birds on the marsh give the fans a wider berth every year.

## When It Notices You

So you stand on the wet cobbles between two engine houses, the way everyone does in the end, and wait for the draw.

You feel the strokes gathering. You feel the stray beats drawing in toward each other and the pauses getting shorter. And, because you are standing still in the heart of it with your feet on the stone, you feel something in your chest getting shorter too. Your pulse is leaning. It hesitates at the top of each beat and falls a little harder at the bottom, finding its place against the great slow stroke the way the engines find theirs. It doesn't hurt. It feels, strangely, like being recognized.

Then all seven come down together. The ground jumps and the water shivers. Every leaf lets go its drops at once, the pale bells swing, and the gold pollen goes up into the dim air and hangs there, turning slowly under the steam ceiling. It settles on the beams and the lamp posts and the soft brick. It settles on your shoulders and your hair and your upturned face. It gathers around your shape.

For one heartbeat, the Sump sees you. And for one heartbeat, yours is the same as its own.

Then the strokes begin to scatter again, and the Keeping comes apart into its seven loose, lurching pieces, walking out of step across the wooden floor of the world. You are free to go. Your heart will sit slow and heavy for a week or two, patient, pausing between beats as though waiting for something to come down. Most people do go.

Some of them come back.
