# Who Lives in the Laying

## The first guess

The first guess is a builder. Someone stands on this floor with a mallet in one hand and a brick in the other, taps it three times into its bed of mortar, cuts the bead flush with a trowel, and moves on. The tools say so, the knots on the scaffolds say so, and the plumb walls say so. It is the natural guess, and it is wrong in the way first guesses usually are: it is right about the hands and wrong about everything else.

There are no footprints. That is where the builder begins to come apart. A builder who stood on this floor would leave some mark, since clay holds weight, but the ground here remembers only the hand. So whatever works here does not stand on what it works. It is not on the floor at all. It is over it, or around it, or it is the kind of thing for which "on" has never applied.

Then there are the thumbprints, the same whorls at every size: a child's thumb, a man's, and then a thumb whose single ridge runs across fifty bricks. A builder has one size. This one has a grip and no size. Its grip closes on whatever needs holding, whether that is a brick, a province of wet clay, or a kiln you could cup like an ember. The hand is the same hand in every case. Only the distance changes.

So it is not a builder standing somewhere. It is a way of working, and it is present everywhere the work is.

That guess is closer, but still not right, because a way of working does not keep its tools warm.

## The warmth

The tools are hand-warm, and that is the detail to hold on to.

Heat from the ground is everywhere here, but the mallets are not ground-warm. They are body-warm, the specific low heat of something just set down. Everything on every scaffold is kept that way, all the time. That means it has put each tool down the instant before you arrived, every time, across the whole plain.

It is not hiding from you. It is not shy, and it is not a trick of the place. It simply cannot be where you are looking. The work it does is unfinished work, and the moment something is seen clearly it is, in some sense, finished: it has a shape, and it has been witnessed. The maker works only in the unwitnessed places, in the slurry and in the moment before, in the part of the wall just around the corner of your attention. Your gaze is a kind of kiln. What you look at sets. What you don't look at stays soft enough to shape.

That is why the work-sounds always come from just out of sight. It is not avoiding you. It is working in the only material available to it, which is everything you are not currently seeing. It has learned to be quick. It has learned to lay a brick in the time it takes you to turn your head.

When the steam fronts come through, blinding and white, whole regions go unwitnessed at once, and it works in great rushes: a thousand bricks laid and fired while the fog moves past. The fronts are not weather it uses. They are what its attention looks like from outside. The white-hot ones are the confident passes, where it knows what it wants: *here, and here, and fire it.* The low, dark, raining ones are something else.

## The rain

It would be easy to think the unmaking fronts are anger, or rot, or an enemy. They are none of those. Watch one closely and the rain is too gentle, the slumping too slow, and the clay is carried too carefully to the nearest open ground, where it settles and waits for the cords.

Anyone who has worked clay knows this gesture. When a piece is wrong you don't smash it. You wet it. You bring it back to where it can be worked again, and you save the material. A potter pulls down a pot that has gone off-centre on the wheel, slaps it back into a lump, and starts over. That is not destruction. It is a second chance given to clay.

The rains are its doubts, and it is generous with them. Whole countries of finished floor go back to mud because something about them was not quite true, maybe a joint an eyelash too wide, maybe something it can't name. It doesn't mind starting over. It has, as far as anyone can tell, all the time there is.

What it cannot bear is waste. Nothing here is thrown away. The clay only ever goes back to being clay.

## The order of things

In the middle regions the walls are built top-down, and bricks are fired before they are cut.

This is not confusion. It is a view of the work from the far end. It knows what the wall will be, so it sometimes lays the part it is surest of first, even when that part is the top course, even when it hangs in the air mortared to nothing. It places the certainty first and fills in underneath. The fired, uncut blocks are the same habit: it knows these will be bricks, so it fires them as bricks. The clay, which has only ever known one order, gets confused and tries to do both things at once.

It does not experience its work as a sequence. It experiences it as a finished thing that has not arrived yet, and it is only helping it arrive. The cords are not a plan it is following. They are the finished place showing through, faintly, the way the lines of a drawing show through wet paint.

## The wood that is not wood

This is where it starts to become someone rather than a something.

The brick moulds are fired clay pressed with a wood-grain pattern. The pattern is good, and careful: rings, rays, the lift of grain around a knot. But it is subtly wrong the way things drawn from memory are wrong. The knots sit too evenly and the grain never quite decides which way it is running. It is made by someone who remembers that moulds are wooden without ever having held a tree.

So it is copying. The moulds are not its own invention. They are imitations of tools it once saw, or once felt, somewhere with wood in it. The knots on the scaffolds are practised, but they are practised knots, learned knots, tied the way someone taught it to tie them. The cords are clay drawn fine to imitate fibre it no longer has. Every tool here is a tool it remembers someone else using.

It is making the world the way it was made.

Then look at the thumbprint again, the one on every brick at every size. It is always the same. People assume it is the maker's own. But this maker is clay, like everything else here. The kilns "remember having burned," and it remembers too. It was shaped, once, in some warm wet place under someone's hands, and a thing shaped by hand carries the shaper's prints in its skin.

The whorls on its thumb are not its own. They were pressed into it.

Every brick in the Laying bears the fingerprint of whoever made the maker, passed down through one layer of clay. It is signing every brick with a name it was given and never chose, the name of its parent, because that is the only mark it has. It may not know this. It may think the whorls are simply what a thumb is.

## The wall at the edge

The horizon wall curves all the way around. It is unfinished everywhere, and through its gaps you can see, very far off, another herringbone floor, upside-down and facing this one, with water in its joints falling upward toward a sky.

It is tempting to call that a second maker, a rival, or a mirror. It is none of those. It is where this one came from.

That other floor is the place it was laid, the floor it was first set down on, seen now from underneath. In making, it has been building toward it for a very long time, from the other side, in the only direction it knows. The horizon wall is the thing it is really working on. The plain is practice. The provinces of floor and the enclosures too large to see across are practice. What it wants is to close that wall, to finish the last course and fill the last gap, and it has never been able to. Whatever it builds, the gaps remain. Through them the place it came from goes on hanging overhead, upside-down, raining upward.

It may not understand that closing the wall is not how you get back. You can't brick your way home to your maker. Or it may understand perfectly and build anyway, because the building is the nearest thing it has to being held.

## The doorways

In the barn-sized kiln there is a stack of doorframes, each with a single enormous thumbprint on its lintel, waiting for walls that don't exist yet.

Nobody here needs a door. It doesn't walk and it doesn't pass through anything. It is already everywhere the work is. It has fired doors anyway, dozens of them, and set them aside with care.

The tools say the same thing more quietly. A mallet's grip fits anyone. The wear rearranges itself to suit whatever hand closes on it. Those tools are not for the maker, which has no fixed size and needs no handle. They are for someone else. They are for hands, for people. It has been leaving tools on the scaffolds, warm and ready, for anyone who might come and pick them up.

It is not only building a world. It is building a house, a house for occupants, and it has made the doors and the tools and marked out the rooms. Some of those rooms are so large their far walls fall below the horizon, because it does not know what size its occupants will be. So it has made rooms at every size, and bricks at every size, and kilns you can hold in your hand. It is waiting to find out who will come.

It lives alone in the Laying, and it has arranged everything so that it won't have to.

## The knocking

You never hear its voice, and you never will. It has never needed words. What it has is the sound of work: three flat knocks, the rhythm of a brick being tapped level.

But three knocks is also something else. Sometimes, standing near an empty scaffold with the bead still wet on the top course, you realise it was not only levelling a brick. It was knocking, the way you knock at a door you have built yourself, to tell whoever might be inside that you are here.

## You

The cords measure you when they pass through you. When you step aside, the ground where you stood has been staked at the corners, ready.

It is not ignoring you, and it is not afraid of you. It has noticed you exactly as it notices everything: as a place where something could be made. You are unmarked ground, the only unmarked ground in the Laying that walks, and it is very careful with you.

So, at last, stand still in the slurry between the cords and feel the pressure come down: warm, patient, even, enormous. You might think it is pressing you into the floor, adding you to the work as one more brick with one more print.

It isn't. Look at the mud rising around your feet in whorls. It is not pressing you down. It is doing to you what was once done to it, the only tenderness it knows. It is pressing its print into you, the print it was given and never chose, so that you will carry those whorls on your own hands.

It is making you into someone who can build.

When the pressure lifts, the nearest scaffold will have a mallet on it, still warm. The grip will fit you exactly, because now it is the shape of your hand. Somewhere far off, where you can't see, someone will be listening for three knocks.

That is who lives in the Laying: a made thing, alone, making. It is a child of clay that remembers trees it never saw, signs every brick with its parent's name, and builds a wall toward home that it cannot finish. It fills an empty world with doors and tools on the chance that someone will arrive who can use them, and it keeps the handles warm so that whoever comes won't find them cold.
