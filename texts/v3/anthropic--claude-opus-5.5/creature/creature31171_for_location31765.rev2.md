# Who Lives on Asker

Start small, because everything on Asker is small until there are enough of it.

---

## One Voice

A skerry is born in the deepest part of a wall, where the stone is newest.

That matters. The cells nearest the surface of a cliff are open to the cloud and hold its cold, but the ones far back in the honeycomb, a body's length or two into the rock, still keep a little of the heat the engines sent up with them. Mothers compete for those cells in the middle of the long night. The warmest cells in Asker are always in the loudest districts, because the loudest districts are the ones rising, and rising rock is warm rock. A skerry is born in the city's newest room and into its loudest noise.

The pup is hairless for its first days, the size of a thumb joint, and it clings to its mother's belly with hooked thumbs and a mouth clamped on a nipple. It cannot fly and cannot yet call loudly enough to hear itself. But it can hear her. A skerry mother's call has a signature, a particular stutter at the front of each click, a slight upward bend in the buzz that follows, and the pup learns that pattern before it learns anything else. In a cell holding forty other mothers, inside a wall holding a hundred thousand cells, inside a roar that makes human teeth ache, the pup can pick its mother's voice out of the storm. When she leaves to feed and comes back, she calls into the dark at the mouth of the cell, and somewhere in the packed, squirming, fur-wet mass inside, a single thin voice answers her.

Hold one, if a keeper will let you. It weighs almost nothing. Its fur is so dense that water beads on it and rolls off. Its heartbeat under your finger is fast enough to feel like rain on a tin roof, not like a pulse at all. The ears are enormous for the body, folded and ridged like wet paper, and they turn independently, flicking, never still. Its face is mostly mouth: a wide pale gape rimmed with a soft fleshy leaf above the nose that shapes its calls into a beam. It has eyes, small and black, but the keepers doubt it uses them. It smells of musk and iron and, faintly, of the struck-match odor of the rock it sleeps against.

Before it flies, the pup practices. It crawls to the back of the cell and clicks at the stone, and listens to what comes back, and clicks again. It is learning the shape of its home by ear, every ridge and cleft of the cell's glassy inner wall, the way a human child learns the shape of a parent's face. Then it learns the cell mouth. Then it learns the ledge below. By the time it launches it already has a map, built out of echoes, of the few body-lengths it has ever known.

The first flight is a fall. The pup drops from the cell mouth and plummets a full body length or more before its wings bite the air. Then the stream catches it. The outflow from a wall like Tallow is so thick that a young flier is simply carried at first, borne along in a river of bodies. And here it learns the hardest thing a skerry has to learn, which is how to hear itself in a crowd.

Skerries solve this the way a choir does. Each one shifts its calls fractionally in time and pitch away from its nearest neighbors, so that no two voices land on top of each other. Across a whole stream, thousands of fliers constantly adjust, leaving gaps, filling gaps, their calls interleaving into a braided texture. The keepers call this *the weave*, and it is why the colony's roar is not the formless shriek you might expect. Up close, if you have a keeper's ears, it has grain and structure, like cloth. Each thread is a single animal keeping itself distinct just enough to know where its own body is.

A skerry lives three or four years. It feeds on the midges that hatch off the canals and the drip-lice it licks from the walls. It knows its cell-mates by voice. When one of them dies, the others go on calling toward the space it used to occupy for days afterward. The keepers think the echo comes back wrong, a gap where a warm body used to return sound, and they keep checking it the way you might keep reaching for a stair that has been removed.

Most skerries die by falling. Old ones lose their grip in the cold. Sick ones miss the cell mouth coming home. They come down onto the ledges and lie in the shivering skin of water, and the drip-lice find them within minutes, a crackling pale film investigating the fur. The skerry calls for a while, weakly, and nothing in all that roar answers.

Then something else finds it.

## The Ones Who Walk

You hear a gleaner long before you know what you are hearing: a low wet rattle, like a handful of gravel shaken slowly in a throat. Then there is a tapping, fine and precise, on the stone somewhere above your head. Then something brushes your shoulder, feather-light, jointed, and withdraws.

The keepers who have touched one describe the same strange thing: a gleaner is exactly the temperature of the stone around it. Not the air, the stone. Walk one from an old district to a new one and its body warms as it goes, degree by degree, the way the rock does. Its skin is smooth and hard, faintly ridged, cool-slick with condensation. It feels, every keeper says, very much like running your hand along one of the green glass seams.

It is taller than a person. The legs, four or six depending on who is telling it (they may differ, and nobody has ever held one still long enough to count properly), are jointed three times and end in fine splayed points that find cracks in the wall no human finger could. The body is not much more than a narrow keel slung between them, and there is nothing a keeper would call a head, only a forward end where two antennae as long as the legs sweep constantly left and right, tapping, pressing, testing the stone. The mouth is somewhere underneath. The rattle comes from somewhere inside.

They take fallen skerries, and that is where the keepers' easy account of them ends.

Because they do not always eat. Keepers who have followed gleaners through a district, clicking softly to keep track of the long body picking its way along the ledges, report that a gleaner will often take a fallen skerry, living or dead, in its underside and carry it. Sometimes it carries it a long way. It carries it to a particular kind of place: a fresh crack, a seam just beginning to open where new stone is lifting against old. There it presses the skerry's body into the gap and holds it there with the tips of its antennae for a long moment, rattling slowly. Then it moves on.

The keepers call this *planting*. Nobody likes the word, and nobody has found a better one.

And the gleaners do something else that the keepers have argued about for longer than any of them has been alive. They stand still. A gleaner will stop on a terrace, splay its legs wide, lower its body until the keel almost touches the wet stone, and lay both antennae flat against the wall. It will stay like that for hours. Sometimes days. The rattle slows to one wet pulse every few breaths. The streams of skerries pour past it. The drip-lice walk over it as if it were rock.

Where gleaners stand, years later, the ground rises.

This is the argument. One school of keepers says the gleaners sense the rise before it comes. The engines' pressure moving up through the rock reaches them first, the way animals are said to feel earthquakes, and they gather where the stone is about to be warm. The other school says the opposite. The engines lie impossibly deep. Nothing could hear a colony through that much rock. Something has to carry the tally down. Something has to stand on the surface with its body pressed to the stone and *listen for the engines*, and then tell them what it heard.

The most respected keeper alive holds the second view. She has held it for sixty years. She says the gleaners are counting.

## Hesk

She was born in a district called Low Weir, which was loud then. It is silent now. Its walls went inert when she was in her thirties, the films thinning, the drip-lice wandering away one by one, the skerries shifting their roosts toward the rising ground at Tallow and Ashbank until one long night the whole district was quiet enough to hear the water on it. Low Weir is where the keepers live now. That is how keeper settlements are made. The colony moves on, and people move into what it left.

Hesk is small, bent, her hands hard at the fingertips from a lifetime of reading the wall-carvings, the ridged marks at shoulder height that tell a keeper *stair here*, *junction*, *ledge ends over deep water*. Like most old keepers, she is almost completely deaf. The roar does it. Every keeper child is born into a city that is slowly wearing away their hearing, and every keeper knows it, and nobody leaves.

Here is the strange part: the deafness is not a loss of standing. It is how the old become important.

A young keeper navigates the way skerries do. They carry a clicker, a small instrument of bone or green glass, snapped between thumb and finger, and they read the city by what comes back off the walls. A keeper in middle life begins to lose the high returns, then the fine ones, and has to learn the city again through the drip tempo felt in the soles, through the vibration of a canal under a railing, through the texture of the wall-marks. By the time a keeper is as old as Hesk, the ears are gone and what is left is the body. And the body, pressed to the stone, can feel the one thing the ears never could. It can feel the rise.

Hesk can stand on a terrace and tell you, within a decade, when the ground under her will lift. She does it by lying flat. Sometimes for hours. Sometimes all through a long sleep. She says it is like pressing your ear to the chest of someone asleep, except the heartbeat takes a lifetime.

When young keepers find her like that, lying still with her palms flat on the wet stone while the drip-lice walk over her hands, they sometimes remark, carefully, not quite joking, that she looks like a gleaner.

She does not think it is a joke.

## What the Keepers Do With Their Dead

This is not agreed upon. It is worth saying that plainly.

The older custom is to lay a keeper's body in the quiet districts, in a sealed cell in inert stone where nothing grazes, nothing rises, and nothing will ever disturb it. Low Weir's back walls are full of these cells, marked with the dead person's name-carving at shoulder height, so that you can walk along a gallery with your fingertips and read who is resting there.

The newer custom, and it is perhaps three generations old, is to carry the body the other way. The family takes it up into the loud districts, to the foot of a rising wall, and leaves it on a ledge in the running water. They crowd together on the terrace around it for one night, clapping stone on stone, whistling, adding their voices to the colony's roar. Then they go home.

The gleaners come.

Some keepers find this horrifying. Others say it is the only honest burial the city offers. The engines count the bodies and voices of Asker, and a keeper's whole life adds a few centimeters to a wall that a million skerries built, and this is the last few millimeters. You are carried to the seam. You are planted. You are counted.

Hesk's husband went the new way. She walked him up to Ashbank herself, forty years ago, and lay on the terrace beside him all that night with her hands on the stone.

She says the ground there is higher now. Everyone's ground is higher now. She says that isn't the point.

## The Seams

Here is something the keepers noticed only recently, only in the last few decades, because it required someone deaf enough to trust their fingers entirely.

The green glass seams, the places in the black basalt where the engines ran hot and the stone turned to glass, where the water runs coldest and sweetest and tastes of iron and struck flint, the places where every keeper stops to drink, are not random.

Trace one with your fingertips. Trace it slowly, the way the old ones do, the way you would read a name-carving. Follow its long branching run up a wall. Feel where it bends. Feel where it splits into three thin threads that splay out and end in fine points buried in the stone. Feel the smooth narrow ridge at its center, not much more than a keel.

Nobody has ever found a dead gleaner. Not one. Not in all the generations the keepers have been writing things down in carvings. A gleaner grows slow in its age, stiff-legged, its rattle thickening, and then one day it walks away from the tall districts, down to the lowest, rawest new ground, where the walls are waist-high and warm and smell of the deep earth, and it lowers itself, splays its legs, lays its antennae flat against the stone.

And it stays there. And the rock rises around it.

The keepers who first understood this did not tell anyone for a long time. It changes what it means to drink.

## Whoever Lives Here

So who lives on Asker?

It would be easy to say the skerries. There are more of them than anything else, and they are the ones the engines answer to, the loud warm majority whose roar is the city's only real law. It would be almost as easy to say the keepers, because they are the ones who know, who carve the names and clear the canals and try, with their small hands and small voices, to argue with the stone.

But the longer you live here, the harder it is to separate them.

The skerries are the voice. Millions of distinct threads woven into a single sound, each animal holding itself apart just enough to know where its body is, all of them together forming something no one of them could hear. The drip-lice and the invisible films are the gut, turning raw rock into life. The longcombs in the black canals are the slow blood. The gleaners are the nerves, the long jointed things that walk the surface and press themselves flat and carry what they learn down into the dark, and at the end carry themselves down too, to become the bright glass veins that the water runs along. The rising stone is the bone, always growing toward wherever the voices are thickest. And the engines, whatever they are, whoever built them, whatever they were meant to answer, are the part of it that no one has ever met. The part of it that sleeps, and dreams in pressure, and makes rooms for whatever calls.

And the keepers are the part that remembers. A few hundred people who live in the quiet places the city has outgrown, who go deaf so they can feel it grow, who press their palms to the wall and learn, over a lifetime, to hold still the way the gleaners do.

The thing that lives on Asker is one animal. It has been alive a very long time. It is still arriving.

## Counted

Late in the long night, Hesk goes up to Tallow alone.

She cannot hear it, not anymore. She feels it. The roar comes down through the air as pressure on her face and chest, and through the stone as a shiver in her knees. Wings brush her cheek in unbroken streams. Water sheets over her boots. She finds the foot of the great wall by touch, kneels, and then lies flat on the terrace with her palms spread against the sharp new rock, and the drip-lice come and walk over her fingers, cold and small, investigating, moving on.

After a long time, something else comes.

She feels it in the stone first: the fine precise tapping of pointed feet finding cracks, coming along the ledge. Then the faint drop in the water's shiver as a long body lowers itself near her, splays wide, settles. It is exactly the temperature of the rock.

Then something touches her shoulder. Feather-light. Jointed. It rests there. It does not withdraw.

She lies still under it for a long time, one old animal among millions, being listened to. Under her hands and under her chest and under the soles of her feet, patient and immense, coming up from farther than anyone has ever gone, the ground is rising.
