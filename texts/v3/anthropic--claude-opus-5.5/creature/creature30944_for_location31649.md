# What Keeps the Stroke

## The First Answer, Which Is Wrong

You would think, at first, that nothing lives here but the parts.

The beetles, the paired birds, the cable-eels, the thread-horned goats, the marmots venting steam: you would count them and call them the inhabitants. But they are not inhabitants, any more than the teeth of a gear inhabit the gear. They are the running of the place. They do not live *in* the hollow. They are the hollow, repeating itself at different speeds.

So you would conclude the hollow is empty in the way a clock is empty: full of motion and holding no one.

That conclusion lasts until the ferns move.

## The Ripple

It happens on the western side, where the fronds are tallest and the violet seams crowd close together in the rock. A wave goes through the ferns. It is not wind, because there is no wind. It is a single long displacement, fronds leaning aside and then righting themselves, their gear-rack ribs clicking in sequence *tktktktktk* as whatever it is passes beneath. With it comes the thrum. For a moment the sound you had stopped hearing, the one you had come to hear as ground, rises up out of the rock and travels. It walks.

You will try to see it. You will not manage it directly. Look straight at the ripple and the ferns are only ferns, clicking back into place. Look beside it, at the pool, at the bark of the nearest riveted tree, and at the edge of your vision there is a length of something: dark, wet, jointed, low to the stone, longer than seems reasonable.

The first time, you take it for another of the larger animals, a serpent maybe, or a long otter-thing, one more working part running one more loop.

Watch for it long enough and you learn otherwise. It is the only thing in the hollow that never comes back to where it was.

## The Path That Does Not Close

Every loop here closes. You have seen it. The beetle returns to its starting point at its starting angle. The goat lifts and lowers its head and lifts it again. But the thing in the ferns passes the western cleft, and when it comes around again (it does come around, after what feels like most of an afternoon) it passes a finger's width farther in. Next time it will be another finger's width. Its route is not a circle. It is a helix laid flat, a spiral pressed into the floor of the hollow with the pitch of a screw thread, the same even pitch as the goats' horns.

Once you see that, you see that the goats' horns are *copies*. So are the coiled fiddleheads, and the scales of the cones, and the eels in their slow loops. Everything in the hollow that spirals is keeping a record of its path. The whole place is marked with it, the way a workshop floor is marked where a heavy thing has been dragged the same way again and again.

The loops of the animals are geared to it. The beetle's circuit is some exact fraction of one of its passes. So is the bird's arc, and so is the eels' crossing. If you had patience enough and a way of counting that did not drift, you could work out the whole gear train: so many beetle-circuits to a goat's nod, so many nods to a marmot's hiss, so many hisses to one turn of the long thing's spiral. It would not be approximate. The hollow does not do approximate.

So this is the first true thing about what lives here. It is the one motion in the place that adds up. It is the counter, the escapement, the single tooth that advances while everything else spins in place.

## Its Body

What you can gather of it, sidelong and in pieces:

It is long. How long, you cannot say, because you never see both ends at once. Some of it is always under the ferns and some of it always behind a basin's sharp rim, and you begin to suspect some of it is not in the hollow at all.

It is jointed the way a connecting rod is jointed, and also the way a spine is. The segments are plated like the bark of the trees and slick like the eels. Between the plates runs the brassy filament you saw in the moss, much thicker, braided into cables. The violet light travels along those cables in pulses, and the pulses keep time with the thrum.

It is warm, far warmer than the moss. Where it has lain, the stone beads with water faster.

It has no head that you can find. Where a head would be there is only the direction it is going. When you try to see its front you find yourself looking at the stretch of floor it has not reached yet. That stretch seems very slightly more *possible* than the rest, as though it were under a thinner layer of the held instant.

It does not look at you. Later you will understand it does not look at anything, because looking is a thing done across time and it is spending all of its time on something else.

## What the Place Makes

The hollow produces something, you were sure of that. The sound is the sound of making. Yet nothing piles up. There is no product and no waste. Everything made is used in the same instant.

Here is where the making goes.

Each pass the long thing makes is a finger's width farther in. That finger's width is the whole output of the engine. Every beetle-circuit, every pawl-tick of the birds, every violet spark between the eels, every held roar of the waterfall goes into that one small advance. The hollow is a vast mechanism reducing an enormous effort down to a single slow motion, the way the gearing of a great clock turns the fury of its spring into the drift of an hour hand.

Nothing accumulates in the hollow because it accumulates in *this*. The thing in the ferns is the pile. It is made of the engine's production. The length you can never see the end of is the length of everything the place has ever done, laid down tooth by tooth and carried along. It lives here the way a thread lives in a spinning mill. It is both what the mill is for and the only proof that the mill has done anything.

## Dead Centre

There is a word the engine-men use for the top of a stroke: *dead centre*. The piston has risen as far as it can go, the crank points straight up, and for an instant there is no force that can turn it either way. Push down on the piston and you push straight through the crank into its bearing, and nothing moves. An engine stopped at dead centre cannot start itself. Something has to carry it over the top: a flywheel's stored momentum, a hand on the wheel, a nudge.

The hollow is at dead centre. Everything described about it follows from that. The mountain has thrust up to the height of its upthrust and not yet begun to settle. The water has reached the lip of its fall and not yet fallen. The fog has formed and not yet dripped. It is all pressure and no direction.

So here is the second true thing about what lives here, and the more important one. It is the carrying-over.

It is not keeping the stroke held. You might have thought that at first, that it was a warden or a governor, some jealous thing that kept time from passing. It is the reverse. It is the momentum that will take the crank over the top, being assembled one finger's width at a time out of the only energy there is in a place that has no gradient. The looping animals are the flywheel, spinning and storing. The long thing is what they are storing up.

When its spiral reaches the centre of the hollow (and you can see roughly where that is, a bare disc of black stone near the middle where no moss grows and the shadows pool deepest), the crank will pass dead centre.

Then the water will fall.

## What It Wants

I have gone back and forth on whether it wants anything.

For a long time it seemed to me the way a mainspring seems: patient because it has no alternative, directed because it has no choice. It spirals inward because inward is the direction of the thread. Wanting would take a before and an after, and it lives in the instant between.

But one thing argues against that.

When you stand near the western cleft as it passes, and it does pass near you if you wait, the ripple in the ferns slows. It does not stop, because it cannot stop. But it slows, and the thrum deepens under your feet, and the beads of condensation on your sleeves grow slightly heavier, rounder, closer to the moment of falling than anything else in the hollow has come.

You are the only motion here that does not loop. You came up out of the cloud along a path that did not close on itself, and you will leave by one. As far as the hollow is concerned you are something it has no gear for, a push from outside the system, the thing an engine at dead centre needs and cannot make.

I think it notices. Not with eyes. I think it notices the way a stalled machine notices a hand on the wheel, as a sudden, faint gradient, the first slope in a place without slopes. I think its slowing near you is a kind of leaning. What it leans toward is not you exactly but the direction you came from, the one where things fall and wear and pile up and end.

Whether that is wanting, I can't say. It is the closest thing to wanting that could exist at the top of a stroke.

## What It Takes

The water on your sleeves does not fall on the way down. It stays beaded the rest of the day, and for a while afterward, in quiet rooms, you hear the thrum.

This is not an echo. When you stood close to the long thing and it slowed, it took one tooth of you: one finger's width of your own unlooped motion, added to its spiral. You gave it the smallest possible push toward the centre, and in return you carry a small piece of the held instant back down with you. That is why the beads don't drop, and why the sound lingers. A little of your time is now at dead centre, and a little of its dead centre is now in time.

Travellers who have made the climb more than once tend to say the same thing, without being asked: the bare disc in the middle of the hollow is smaller than it was. The spiral is closer. The waterfall's roar has changed pitch, very slightly, the way a sound changes when the thing making it starts, at long last, to move.

## What to Call It

The people below the cloud, the few who know the hollow is there, do not agree on a name. Some call it the Thread, some the Tally. An old one-word name, from the time of the first engine houses in the valleys, is *Overcarry*. That was the word for the flywheel's work, getting the crank past the top.

I like best what the children call it, because children hear the thrum in quiet rooms more readily than anyone.

They call it *the one who is still coming up*.

It is the only thing in the hollow still on its way somewhere. Everything else there has already arrived and is holding.
