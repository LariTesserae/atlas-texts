# Who Lives in the Gypsum Quarter

## I. The ones that can be counted

Start with what can be held in the hand.

**The cup-moths.** Each has four wings of something like mica, so thin that the veins seem drawn on the air rather than on a wing. They have no mouths in the usual sense, only a short hollow tongue that coils into a glassy knot when not in use. A moth drinks once a night, one bead from one flower, and that bead is the whole of its water. When it lands on a flower the cup bends, and for a moment the moth and the flower look like the same plant.

By day they sleep in the alcoves, pressed flat against the foam, and here is a small thing a visitor might not notice: the foam takes their print. A moth that sleeps in the same alcove for six days leaves a faint moth-shaped hollow, and on the seventh day it fits into its own shadow like a key into a lock. When the wall cracks and falls, the hollow goes with it into the dust. The moth has to find a new alcove and start over. Every moth in the Gypsum Quarter is always, gently, homeless.

**The salt-spiders.** They are almost nothing: eight legs of spun white, a body like a grain of rice. They live among the needle-crust in the dry plaza basins and are nearly impossible to see until they move. They move once, and then the moth is gone, and the spider is still again.

A spider drinks what the moth drank. The brine that came up through the mason-root, through the flower, through the moth, ends in the spider. When the spider dies, as it must, it dries where it sits, and within a week it is a few more needles in the crust. The crust in the plaza basins is not only salt. It is spiders, and the moths inside them, and the flowers inside those. Every depression is an ossuary the size of a dinner plate.

**The tall one.** Come back to it later. It does not like to be counted first.

## II. The one that visits every afternoon

The dust lives here more than anything that breathes.

It arrives from the plain each afternoon in slow flour-coloured sheets, and it does not behave like weather. It behaves like something that knows the streets. It goes up stairs. It pools on the sitting-ledges and slides off them at the exact moment the ledge is full. It enters upper rooms and stays for the length of an unhurried visit, then leaves by a door. Doors always face downhill, so it always leaves by the same kind of door.

The dust is the town's previous self: every wall that cracked at dusk, every room that sheared off and settled silently into the street. It is ground finer each time it passes through. Some of the grains have been walls a thousand times. The mason-root draws them up, lays them into new foam, and lets them fall again. From the dust's side of things, the town is not being built and torn down. The dust is simply moving from room to room, trying each one on, finding it not quite right, and moving on.

When it drains away through the gaps that were not there yesterday, it leaves a thin film on every floor. For an hour after, the whole town shows exactly where the dust lingered and where it hurried past. The mason-root seems to read this. The next day's walls lean toward the places the dust liked.

So the dust is a tenant. But it is not the tenant the rooms were measured for. Dust does not need a ledge at sitting height.

## III. The one inside the walls

Consider the silence properly.

A voice carries an arm's length and then thins into nothing, but nothing is not a place. The sound goes into the foam, into pores finer than the finest hair, and it stays there. Every sound ever made in the basin is still in it: the ticking of cooling walls, the click of a spider's first and only movement, the faint hiss of the dust tide, and the few voices of the few people who have come down from the rim. Each voice was taken whole, held in a lattice of gypsum like a breath held in a lung.

When a wall cracks, it does not release what it held. It keeps it. The fragments fall silently, crumble into dust, and the dust carries its cargo of held sound into the streets and eventually back up into new foam. Nothing is ever let go. The town has been accumulating every noise ever made inside it, compressed smaller and smaller with each turning of its walls.

If you could hear it, the town would be loud. It would be a crowd murmuring shoulder to shoulder: every footstep ever taken in it, every sigh, every muttered word a traveller said to themselves to prove they still could. It would sound like a market at noon, full of people who were never there together.

This is the second tenant: a population of sounds with no bodies. They have lived in the town far longer than anyone living, and they fill the rooms that look empty. The emptiness a visitor feels is not emptiness. It is the pressure of a very full room in which everyone is holding their breath.

But sounds do not sit on ledges either.

## IV. The shape the rooms are built around

At night the cold comes down from the rim, and cold air is heavy. It pours down the stairs, along the lanes, and through the doorways, which all face downhill so that it can. It pools.

Watch the selenite windows from the rim at midnight, the town a scatter of faint milky cells. In the brightest rooms, the newest ones, something happens that is easy to miss. The cold air settles into the room and fills it the way water fills a mould: over the lip of the ledge, into the alcove at shoulder height, up to the second tread of the stair and no further. The light through the selenite bends very slightly where the cold air is densest, and for a few minutes before the room reaches the temperature of its walls, there is a shimmer in it.

The shimmer has a shape. It is seated on the ledge. Its shoulder is in the alcove. One foot rests on the lowest step.

Then the air evens out and the shape is gone.

This is the thing the town is building. It is not a creature, and not a ghost exactly. It is the outline of a sitting person, drawn in cold air, visible only for the moment when a new room first fills. The walls are only its outline. The empty volume is the point. Every room in the Gypsum Quarter is a mould, and every night the town pours cold air into the moulds to see whether the figure comes out right.

It never quite does. The shoulder is a little high. The shin is a little long, which is why the stair risers are too tall. The body is a little narrow, which is why the treads are too shallow. The town looks at what it has cast (or does whatever a plant does instead of looking), finds it wrong, and by dawn the room is cracked, and by the next season it is dust.

Where did it get the measurements? Something must have given the town its first idea of a person.

## V. The thumbprint

Pressed with a thumb, the foam gives slightly and then holds the print.

People have come here. Not many: a handful across more years than the town has walls. A surveyor, lost. A salt-trader looking for brine and finding nothing but flowers. A woman who walked down from the rim because she had heard it was quiet, and stayed three nights, and left without ever saying a word aloud, because there was no point.

Each of them touched the walls. Everyone does. The foam is soft and strange, and a hand reaches out to it without asking. Each of them sat on a ledge because their legs were tired. Each of them climbed a stair, leaned in a doorway, slept curled in a room. The foam took the shape of all of it, and the mason-root runs along the base of every wall. It is continuous. The whole town is one plant. What one wall learned, every wall learned.

The town is not building for a person it has never met. It is building for everyone it has met, all at once. The surveyor's long legs. The trader's broad shoulders, set in an alcove. The woman's narrow frame, curled on a ledge. Averaged and blurred, a thumbprint laid over a thumbprint laid over a thumbprint, until the figure in the cold air is nobody in particular and a little of everyone. That is why the stairs are almost comfortable. They are comfortable for a person who does not exist: a composite, a remembered guest assembled out of fragments of real ones.

The town is waiting for that person to arrive. It has been waiting for them the way a coat waits on a hook, patient in a way that has nothing to do with hope.

## VI. The tall one, again

Now it is possible to look at the tall one properly.

It is thin, too thin, and taller than a doorway. Its head is small. It walks the lanes slowly and deliberately, like someone who long ago stopped being in a hurry. It stops at the crests of new walls and seems to taste them. Nobody has seen it drink, but the flowers open at dusk, and in the morning there are fewer beads than there were moths to take them.

Here is one possibility, which the town itself might not know is true.

Every so often a visitor does not leave. The silence gets into them. At first it is a pressure, and then it is a relief, and then it is simply the way the world is. They drink from the flowers, a bead at a time. They lick salt from the needle-frost in the mornings. They sleep in rooms that are almost their size, and the rooms, noticing, try to become exactly their size. The thumbprint deepens. The mould is corrected.

But a person who lives on brine and silence and gypsum does not stay the same shape. They dry. They lengthen. Their shoulders narrow, and their shins draw out the way the stair risers always suggested they might. Year by year they grow into the town's slightly wrong idea of them, then past it, until they no longer fit the doors at all. They walk the lanes stooping, then stop stooping and walk them in the open, too tall for any room, tasting the fresh crests because the young foam is soft and holds the most water.

It does not notice people because it has forgotten what they are for. And the town keeps building rooms at the old height, the height of the person it used to be. The tall one walks among them, pruning the walls, keeping the lanes almost straight, keeping the doors facing downhill, tending a house that was meant for it and that it has long outgrown.

Or the tall one is simply another animal, and none of this is true. It moves too quietly to ask.

## VII. Who lives there

So who lives in the Gypsum Quarter?

The moths, homeless every seventh day. The spiders, becoming salt. The dust, which was every wall and tries every room. The crowd of sounds held breathless in the foam. The figure of cold air that sits on a new ledge for a minute at midnight and then evens out into nothing. The composite guest, made of every stranger who ever put a hand to a wall. The tall one, who may once have been one of those strangers, and who is now something else.

And, not yet, whoever comes next.

Come down from the rim at dusk. Walk between the chalk walls and hear your own heartbeat, the click of your jaw, the blood in your ears. Sit on a ledge in an upper room while the selenite starts to give back the day. It will not quite fit you. The ledge will be a finger's width too high.

Rest your shoulder in the alcove anyway. Put your palm against the wall. Feel it give slightly and then hold.

In the morning, somewhere in the town, in a room that was a curved lip of foam at sunrise and closed over by evening, there will be a ledge that is a finger's width lower. Nobody will sit on it. By the end of the season it will be dust, and the dust will pour through the streets at walking speed, and some of what it carries will be you.

That is who lives there: everyone who has ever arrived, and no one who has ever stayed, and the person the town is still, silently, in the pale light of its own windows, trying to get right.
