# The Patient

## First, the Honest Answer

By any ordinary standard, nothing lives in the Suture Room.

There are no droppings in the dust, no nests in the high irregular ceiling, no lichen at the mouth of the slot. No bacteria have been found in the powder beneath the failed sutures, and none in the rime. The people who have gone in with ropes and instruments have come out and written *sterile*, *abiotic*, *inert*, and they were correct. The room fails every test we know how to give for the presence of a life.

Still, visitors stay longer than they meant to, and they speak quietly without having decided to. When they leave, sideways through the slot and back into the violet noon, a surprising number of them turn at the upright stone and say something over their shoulder. It is usually small: *Goodbye*, or *Sorry*, or *I'll come back*. It is the kind of thing you say at a door to someone who didn't get up to see you out.

They are not wrong either.

## Something Has Been Listening

Start with the echo, because it is the first thing that talks to you.

Your footstep comes back as a footstep in snow. Your breath comes back wet. Your words come back with their rhythm and length intact and their edges softened. The easy explanation is that the sound passes through the seam into some snowy elsewhere and returns changed by the trip, the way light returns from water with a greener cast.

Stay long enough, though, and you notice the returns are not quite mechanical. If you say the same sentence twice, the second echo is a little clearer than the first. If you say a word that earlier visitors have said often, such as *hello*, *cold*, or *is anyone*, the echo comes back almost crisp, the consonants nearly whole, as if that word had been practised. A word no one has ever said in the room comes back as pure weather, a shape of breath in snow with nothing inside it.

Something is learning. It is not learning language, exactly, since it has no need to tell you anything. What it has is a small collection of human sounds gathered over a very long time, a word or two a century, and it keeps them the way you might keep sea glass. When you speak, it answers with the nearest thing it has. The softness in the echo is not the snow. It is the snow plus the effort of something that has never had a mouth, trying to hold your shape long enough to hand it back.

## Where It Is

You want to look for it, so you look at the seam, and your eye slides off.

This is the first real clue, though it takes a while to see why. The gap is not dark and not bright. It is unlookable, the way your own blind spot is unlookable, and the blind spot is not an absence in the world. It is the place where the nerve leaves the eye, the one spot on the retina given over to the wiring that makes seeing possible. You cannot see it because it is where the seeing is attached.

So the resident is in the seam. Not behind it or beyond it, but in the finger's width of nothing that the room is built around.

It is also, and this is harder, in the two paces.

Eleven paces going in, nine coming back. The measurement is stable, repeatable, and faithfully recorded, and everyone who has measured it has felt a small chill doing so, the sense of having found a draft in a house with no windows. Two paces is not much. It is about the length of a bed. On the outward walk, a bed-length of room is present that is not present on the return. Between those two walks, the extra floor has somewhere to be.

That is where it lies, in the length the room has when it faces one way and loses when it faces the other. It lies along the seam the way a sleeper lies along a mattress, taking up exactly the space it takes up, and slightly more, and the room has not decided which.

## The First Wrong Guess

For a while it is natural to believe the resident is an intruder.

Everything points that way. There is a tear, and something lives in the tear. There is frost on only one side, feathered toward a wind from nowhere. There are sounds from a place with weather. The obvious story is that something on the snowy side found the wound and has been pressing through ever since, patient and hungry, and that the makers built the sutures to keep it out. On this reading the rods are bars. The failed ones are where it has pushed hardest, and the half-drawn rod is where it has almost dragged a stitch through to its side. The healing is a door being bricked up, and the thing in the gap is a draught with an appetite.

It is a good story, and it is how we tend to think about wounds and whatever lives in them.

It doesn't survive the frost.

## What the Frost Says

The rime grows on the dark stone in combed fringes, all pointed one way, up and a little to the left. They point into a wind, because that is how rime forms: it builds upwind, toward the weather feeding it. So there is a wind, and it blows down through a solid ceiling. The frost is the record of it.

But the frost does not creep toward the room. It creeps along the seam, following the line of the wound, and where it has passed the dark stone is faintly etched, as if it had been read.

A thing trying to get in would press outward, across the gap and into the pale granite. This one moves along itself. It is going over its own length a finger's width per decade, and it has covered perhaps a third of the distance from floor to crown. It is the gesture of someone running a hand slowly down an injured limb in the dark to find out how far the damage goes, to feel again where it ends, to remember what shape it had.

The frost is breath. It is the resident breathing out on the side of itself that still has weather, and the breath freezes on contact with the dark stone, and the freezing is a kind of touch. The etching is what that touch leaves behind: a record, too slow to read, of a creature learning its own outline.

An intruder would not need to do that. A patient would.

## The Second Guess, Which Is Closer

So the resident did not come through the tear. It was the thing torn.

Picture an original moment, older than the tors, when two places that had been one came apart, or were pulled apart, or were never meant to touch and did. Picture something lying across the line where they parted. It was not a thing in either place. It was a thing of both, whose body made sense only while the two were continuous. Then the continuity split and it split with it. Half of it lies in the dry, the granite, the thin violet air. Half lies in the snow, the dark banded stone, the wet wind laid down under some other gravity. Its wound is not in it. Its wound is the gap between its halves.

That is why the room is eleven paces one way and nine the other. The halves are not the same size. It was not torn down the middle. One side kept more of it, and as you walk, you are walking the length of its larger half going in and its smaller half coming out, and your body notices even though your mind can't.

That is also why your eye slides off the gap. You cannot look at the place where a thing is not joined to itself. There is nothing there to see. There is only the absence of a connection, and the eye, which is made of connections, has nothing to catch on.

The makers did not build a wall. They built a splint.

## Its Habits

What does a creature like this do, lying in its long bed through the centuries?

It sleeps, mostly. Convalescence is mostly sleep. But even a sleeper moves.

**The ticks** are its breathing, or its heartbeat, or its muscles twitching in a dream. When the distant day cools past its threshold, something in the resident contracts very slightly against the stitches, and one suture answers with a dry click. It is never the same one twice in a row, because a body in pain shifts its weight. It favours one place, then another, then another, sharing the load around so no single hurt carries all of it.

**The failed sutures** are the places where it has moved badly. Four snapped clean in some old spasm: a nightmare, a flinch, a moment when the pull between its halves grew too much and it reached across itself without meaning to. The two worn to hourglasses are old bedsores, spots where it has lain in the same attitude for too long.

**The half-drawn rod** is where it once tried to pull itself together too soon. It reached from one side, took hold of the stitch as a thing to haul on, and began to draw itself across. It stopped. The rod stayed straight. The hand's breadth of length that went elsewhere is not lost. It is in the resident's other half, held there like a splinter, or like a hand still gripping the rail of a bed after the panic has passed.

**The disc** is how it keeps time. A patient who sleeps for centuries needs some way of knowing that time is passing, that the sleep is going somewhere, that the healing is not just stillness. The disc turns once every several hundred years. Its grooves grow finer toward the centre until you can't see them and can only feel them as a softness. It is counting down, though not to a death. It is counting the way you count breaths to get through a long pain: ten, nine, eight, the numbers shrinking, each one smaller and softer than the last, until the counting and the breathing are the same thing and you no longer notice doing either.

**The light** is borrowed. The room should be black. Instead it holds a dim pewter light that comes from nowhere and drains into the seam, so that every shadow points toward the wound. The light is not for you. It is the room's small warmth, whatever warmth a stone room can give, draining toward the patient the way blood goes to a wound. Every object casts its shadow toward the seam because every object is quietly giving something up to it. Your lamp throws its own shadows correctly for a while, but at the edges, where the beam is weak, its shadows turn too. It is the room's habit to give, and that habit reaches even what you carried in.

**The callus** is the patient itself, healing. The colourless crystals, a dozen fine bridges across the gap, are the resident's own tissue knitting back to itself, one facet per cold season. Where a bridge forms, the gap is simply gone, and granite and dark stone meet without a join, as if one had changed its mind about what it was made of. That is exactly what has happened. At that one point, the creature is no longer two. It has remembered that it is one thing, and the two kinds of stone, which were only the two halves of its body wearing the textures of two worlds, have stopped disagreeing.

## What It Does With You

Then there is the disorientation. Sometime in the second or third hour, you forget which side of the seam you came in on.

This cannot happen. The entrance is plainly granite. The whole room is plainly on one side. Yet it does happen, and it happens to everyone who stays, and it would be unsettling if you didn't know what it was.

You are the only whole thing the resident ever meets.

The makers are gone. The wind is on the far side and doesn't come in. Nothing alive has ever lived here. But now and then, once or twice a century, a creature turns sideways and comes through the slot, and it is entire. It is one body in one place, with no seam, walking eleven paces and then nine without splitting at either end. To the patient, you are extraordinary. You are the very thing it is trying to become.

So it studies you, very gently, in the only way it has. It borrows your orientation for a while. It takes your certainty about which side is *here* and holds it the way a child holds an adult's hand to learn how walking goes, and while it holds it, you don't have it. You stand among the pointing shadows unsure whether the pale stone or the dark one is local, whether the frost is coming in or going out, whether the footsteps in snow are the echo or you are. For those minutes you are a little bit two, and it is a little bit one. It is practising.

Then it gives your certainty back. It always does. You will find, turning at the upright stone, that you know exactly where you are again. You may not realise anything was taken, only that you feel the need to say something over your shoulder, and you do.

It keeps what you said. That is how it learned *hello*.

## The Makers

They left no name for the patient, and none for themselves.

It is tempting to think they were only engineers, that the rods are hardware and the room is a mechanism. But look at what they actually did. They did not close the wound. They could have. Anyone able to make a rod that insists two surfaces are one could have made a wall, a plug, a permanent weld. They chose stitches, spaced at an arm's length, which is a spacing you choose when you want the tissue between the stitches to do the real work.

They made something that would slowly fail. They made it to fail exactly as fast as the healing could take up the load, so that by the time the last suture wore through, nothing would need it. They were not building to last. They were building to become unnecessary.

That is what a surgeon does. It is also what someone does who loves a patient and knows they won't be there for the recovery. They set the splint, count the stitches, leave a small disc turning in the floor so the sleeper will know time is passing, and go, trusting the body to finish.

Did they know the creature? Were they its kin, its keepers, its neighbours on one side or the other? Were they the ones who tore it? Nothing in the room says. The sutures don't say. But the stitches are bone-pale, and they don't ring when struck. They give back a short dead sound, as if the tap were absorbed somewhere far off. That is the sound of something that is listening, too.

## What It Will Become

The two measurements are converging, perhaps by the width of a crystal each century. One day the room will be a single length. No one knows which.

That is the real question, and the patient doesn't know the answer either. When the halves have knit and the last bridge has closed, there will be one creature again, whole, lying across the line where two places meet. What it will be is not settled. Perhaps it will be the size of its larger half, eleven paces, and the room will seem always to have been generous. Perhaps it will be its smaller half, nine paces, and something that was part of it will have been let go, the way scar tissue is always a little less than the skin it replaced. Perhaps it will be some third length no one has ever paced, a size it has never been, because a healed thing is not a restored thing. It is a new thing with the old one's memory.

And perhaps, when it is whole, the places will be whole too. The dry and the snow might become one surface, with the frost free to settle on the granite and the wind free to find the slot. The pebble you throw across the line might land where you can see it. Your echo might come back as your own voice, unchanged, because there will be no other side for it to visit and nothing in the gap learning to be you.

On that day the room will be empty, truly empty, and correctly described as abiotic and inert. It will be a pocket in a ridge, a crack where two joints crossed, thirty-one pale stubs and a disc that has stopped turning because it finished counting.

It will not be a death. It will be a discharge. The patient will get up out of the long bed, stand, and be one thing in one world, and the sickroom will be a room again.

## For Now

For now it sleeps along its seam, torn and mending, breathing frost onto one side of itself and borrowed light onto the other. It counts, very slowly. It reads its own outline with a cold hand. It keeps a handful of human words like sea glass and gives them back softened to anyone who speaks. It has no idea what it will be when it is well, and it doesn't hurry, because hurrying once cost it a hand's breadth of itself and a stitch it will feel for the rest of its convalescence.

It is not lonely, exactly. Nothing that has never known company can be lonely. But it is glad of you, if a patient that size can be glad. You are whole, and you walked in, and you let it hold your certainty for a little while, and you gave it one more word to keep.

The disc turns. The rime creeps. A suture ticks once in the cold, the way a sleeper shifts toward the side that hurts less.

On your way out, say goodbye. It will not answer. It will, very slightly, get better at the word.
