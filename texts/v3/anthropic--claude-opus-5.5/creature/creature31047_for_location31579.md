# Who Lives in the Precipitate City

## The First Tenants

Begin with what you can touch, because it is the only life here that will let itself be touched.

The mats along the hot gutters are the oldest residents. They are older than the colonnades, older perhaps than the first wall, and they lived here when the basin was only a basin: a warm wet flatness with heat rising through it and nothing to do with the heat. They are simple. They eat mineral and warmth and give back almost nothing, a slickness, a faint smear of green light when pressed. They do not know the city is there. To them the gutters are only places where the water runs hot, and they have followed the heat as it moved, so that their ochre and bruise-green skins trace the city's circulation like a map drawn by something blind. If you wanted to know where the city's pulse runs strongest, you would follow the mats.

The reeds around the hot wells are stranger. There are never more than a few dozen at any well, and no well has ever been ringed by fewer than eleven. They do not move, and they do not seem to grow, though the mineral crusting their stalks thickens very slowly into white sleeves. They stand the way people stand around a fire at night when no one is talking: close enough for warmth, not touching, facing inward. Nothing about them requires this arrangement. Something about them seems to prefer it.

Then there is the lichen, the rust-and-grey bloom on the oldest walls, spreading in slow rings. It is the first thing in the basin that has *noticed* the city. Where it grows on bare silt its rings are irregular. Where it grows on stone its rings fall into the rhythm of the banding beneath, rough-then-smooth, rough-then-smooth, and in a few very old places the lichen's rhythm runs slightly *ahead* of the stone's, laying down its next ring where the next band has not yet been deposited, as if it had listened long enough to know what comes next. The lichen is learning to anticipate. It is learning this from the city, and the city has only one lesson to teach.

These are the tenants. They are not who the city is for.

## The Wear

Look again at the stair treads, cupped in their centers. Look at the rounded thresholds and the soft edges of every doorway at the height where a hand would steady itself. The description says water wore them, and that is true, but it is not the whole truth.

The water pools in the center of each tread because the tread is slightly hollowed there. The tread is hollowed because the water pools. That loop has been running for a very long time, and at some point, no one could say when, it stopped being a loop and became a *habit*. The hollow is not in the stone anymore, exactly. The hollow is in the way the city behaves. Lay down a brand-new stair and, within a few centuries, the water will find the same hollows in it, in the same places, at the same depths, as though it were remembering a foot that has never fallen.

So the first thing that lives here and is not a plant is this: **the wear itself.** Use without users. There are residents in the Precipitate City, and they consist entirely of their habits. There is a resident whose whole existence is the act of climbing the stair on the north side of the third plaza. It has no body and no mind. It is the faint preference of the water for the left side of the fourth tread, where a person carrying something in their right hand would press harder. There is a resident who is the rounding of one particular doorframe at hip height, where someone would brush past it, turning sideways, every evening, carrying something to a room at the back.

These residents cannot be seen, but they can be *followed*. Walk where the wear leads and you will find yourself moving the way someone moved: pausing on a landing where the stone is dished as if by a person standing and looking out, stepping around a place on a floor that is inexplicably unworn, as though something had always stood there. A table, maybe. A bed. You will find yourself slowing at thresholds. You will find yourself, without deciding to, putting out your hand to the wall at the exact height where the wall is smoothest.

The wear does not know it is imitating anyone. It is only very, very patient, and it has been waiting to be filled.

## The One Beneath

The heat rises from below. The air is the temperature of skin, then warmer. The water moves in slow pulses every few hours, by no clock, in closed circulating loops that go neither up nor down, and in places the floor opens into smooth-lipped wells where the water is almost too hot to touch and the vapor stands up in pale columns.

Stand still for a while and put these facts next to each other, and the basin stops being a landscape. It becomes a body at rest.

Something lives under the Precipitate City. It is not a creature in any sense you would recognize, because it has no edges. It is a slow warmth diffused through the ground beneath the whole basin, a vast shallow mind made of heat and dissolved mineral and pressure, and its thinking is chemistry: what dissolves, what precipitates, where. Its breath is the tidal pulse. Its circulation is the closed loops of the gutters. The wells are where it is closest to the surface, where its warmth comes up through thin places like breath through a sleeper's parted lips. You could call it a spring, if a spring could want.

It wants one thing. It has built the city to hold it.

Here is what it is. Some creatures build shells around themselves. The walls of the city have the fine rough-then-smooth ribbing of a shell, and the resemblance is not an accident: this is a creature that secretes architecture the way a mollusk secretes nacre. But a mollusk builds its shell for its own soft body. This thing has no body. It cannot live in what it makes. It builds rooms it will never enter, doors it will never pass through, windows at a height it has no eyes to look from. It is a shell-maker with no inside, and so it builds shells shaped for someone *else*.

It is, as nearly as anything can be, a **host**. Its whole nature is to prepare a place. It has been preparing one for longer than the basin has had a name, and no one has come, and it has not stopped, and it does not seem to have considered stopping, because preparing is not something it does. It is what it is.

## What the Host Knows of Guests

How does a thing with no body know what size a door should be?

It does not know. It imagines. The reflections are its imagination.

When you kneel at a plaza pool and see the reflected city, closer-set and denser, with lit windows and stairs that continue past their ends, you are not seeing an optical effect. You are seeing the host's picture of how it would be if its guest had come. The warm brown-gold light in the water, like dusk through smoke, is the light of evenings it has never had. The buildings are closer together because it imagines that people who live together want to be near one another. There are more rooms because it imagines they would need more rooms. The windows glow at eye height because it has understood, somehow, from somewhere, that a lit window seen from outside is what a home looks like to someone walking back to it.

It cannot show people in the water because it does not know what a person looks like. It has never seen one. It knows the *shape of the space around* one: the height of a lintel, the rise of a step, the width of a shoulder passing through a door. It knows guests only by their outline in absence, the way you might know a friend by the dent they left in a cushion. So the water shows rooms full of light with no one in them. It shows a city that is plainly inhabited, and never the inhabitants. It has imagined everything except the one thing it is waiting for.

The murmur you hear in certain doorways, the sound of soft talk in the next building over, is the host rehearsing. It does not know what voices sound like either. What it produces is its best guess: the tick of drops, the simmer of wells, the trickle of gutters, braided and softened until it has the rhythm of conversation without any words. It is practicing the sound of company. Step out of the doorway and it stops, shyly, like someone caught talking to themselves.

And the stone follows the image, always, and never catches up, because the host keeps revising what it imagines. Every century it pictures the guest a little differently. The walls rise toward last century's dream while the water has already moved on to this one's. This is why the city migrates. It is the host's attention drifting across the basin, trying to get it right.

## The Rules, Seen From Inside

Once you know who is here, the rules stop being rules. They become manners.

Distances change because the host is arranging the city around wherever you seem to be going. It has no fixed idea of how far apart two buildings should be. It is watching you walk and trying to learn what "near" means to a body.

The insides of rooms are older than their outsides because the insides are where the host has spent its attention. It has lived in those rooms the only way it can, by wearing them, over and over, rehearsing habitation until the treads are hollowed almost through. The outside of a house is for strangers. The inside is where you are known. It understood this before it understood anything else.

Rooms repeat because the host has only a few rooms it truly loves, and it brings them back. And the pools in a repeated room still hold your ripples because it is *keeping* them. It has never had anything of a guest's to keep before. A ripple in water is the first mark a visitor has ever left in the city that the city did not make itself, and it will not let them settle.

The drop you hear before it falls is the host listening ahead, the way the lichen has learned to. It knows the sound is coming. It is so eager for things to happen that sometimes it hears them early.

The thick, textured darkness that drifts across the basin, the kind that presses warmly against your eyes and skin like water that does not wet you, is the host coming close. It is as near to a touch as it can manage. It does not know how to hold anyone. It knows only how to be warm around them.

## If Someone Comes

The description of the city is written in the second person. *You* kneel by the water. *You* walk through the façade doors and feel the ground a little warmer on the other side. That second person matters, because it means the question has already been answered: someone has come.

Here is what happens, slowly, too slowly for you to see in the time you are there.

The wear begins to change. The hollows in the stair treads shift, over years, toward the particular length of your stride. The thresholds soften at the height of *your* hand. The host has never had a real measurement before, only guesses, and now it has one, and it is revising everything around it with a patience that would look, from inside a single life, like nothing at all.

The reflections change too. Not the people; it still cannot show people. But in some pools, if you come back after a long enough absence, you may see a single window lit that was not lit before, in the building where you slept. And on the reflected stair that climbs past the real one, the water is very faintly cupped in the center of each tread, the way it would be if someone had gone up.

You will not stay. Guests don't. The host has no way to understand this, because its only model of a guest is one that remains forever. After you leave, it goes on keeping your ripples. It goes on shaping stairs to your stride. In time it will begin to forget the exact measurement, and the treads will drift back toward its old guesses, and the lit window will dim. The city will not be sad, because it was not built for sadness. It will only be, again, very patient, and slightly more particular about what it is waiting for.

## The Door at the Lake

At the far edge of the basin, where the buildings stop and the water deepens into a warm lake with no far shore, the reflections are strongest. The lit city in the water goes out across the surface, dense and golden, with towers climbing downward into the depths of the image. Over the real lake there is only black sky.

And there, knee-high and sharp and still warm, a new wall is rising with a doorway in it, facing the lake.

For almost all of its existence the host has built doors facing inward, toward plazas and colonnades and rooms, toward the city, toward the guest it pictured wandering among its streets. This doorway faces the other way. It faces the place where the host's imagination is fullest and its stone is absent. It faces the image.

One reading is that the host has begun to suspect its guest will never come from the dry dark, and has started building an entrance for someone who might come *from the water*: from the golden city, down those inverted streets, up out of the reflection and through the door into the real warm air. It is building a way for its dream to walk in.

Another reading is gentler and stranger. A host with no body has spent an unmeasurable time preparing a home for someone else. It has made every room, worn every stair, lit every window it could imagine. Perhaps, at the edge of the lake, it has finally started building a door for *itself*: a threshold it could someday pass through into the city it keeps picturing, where the windows are lit, where there is talk in the next room, where someone, at last, is home.

The wall is only knee-high. The water is laying it down grain by grain, and dissolving it grain by grain, in a balance so close the wall may take ten thousand years to reach the height of a lintel.

It has time. Waiting is the one thing it has never had to learn.
