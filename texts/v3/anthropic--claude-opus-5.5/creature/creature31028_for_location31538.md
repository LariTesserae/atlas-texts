# Who Lives in Gallery Four

## First, the Honest Answer

Nobody. The operators will tell you so, and the logbooks agree. Nobody sleeps here, nobody eats here, and no one's name is on a door. The moth proved that nothing lives here to eat. The towel on the chair is damp because everything is damp, not because anyone has used it.

But a place this full of attention rarely stays empty, and Gallery Four is almost nothing *but* attention. Needles tremble toward numbers. A float rises and falls over a notch. A wire hangs so still that it can feel the whole mountain of concrete shift around it. Every eight hours a person walks through and writes down what the needles have been saying. The gallery was built to listen to the dam. It would be strange if, after four years, nothing had started listening back.

So start with the obvious tenants, and see what they turn into.

## The Operator in the Chair

The first guess is a person. Someone sits in that chair through a flood watch, twelve hours facing the V-notch, reading the water as it pours over the steel lip. The spillway is roaring, and she cannot hear her own thoughts in words. She has brought the towel from home because the vinyl is cracked and cold. She taps on the handrail to the man at the far station: *two-two-one*, meaning *rising, steady, fine*.

She comes close to living here. On the long shifts, something in her rearranges itself to fit the gallery. Her breathing slows to the fan cycle. Her body learns the turbine hum the way it knows its own pulse, and she can tell a unit has tripped before the lamps flicker, because the floor tells her first. When she walks out the green door into daylight, the silence afterward feels like a missing limb. For an hour she hears nothing. Then the spillway comes back in her head, then nothing, then the spillway again, and she realizes the gallery has gone home with her.

But she does leave. Everyone does. The first guess is wrong: the operators are visitors, and the gallery has only borrowed them.

## The Moss by the Door

The second guess is the moss. It is truly alive and truly resident, and it doesn't leave. It has chosen the one place where daylight leaks in when the door opens, where the air is freshest, and where its water never stops. It is dark, dense, and patient, and it's getting bigger.

It is the gallery's first citizen, and people treat it like one. Two different hands have dated it on the wall. It is the only thing in the corridor that anyone has written a *word* about, rather than a number.

Still, the moss lives at the threshold. It faces outward, toward the light it was built to want, and it belongs to the mountain more than to the dam. The algae in the gutter stripes are closer, since they grow only where the dam's lamps allow. The pale film on the downstream wall is closer still: bacterial, deep in the gallery, sweating out of concrete that has never seen the sun. Following the question inward, away from the door, the inhabitants get simpler and stranger and more native. They look less like life and more like the concrete doing something.

Keep going in that direction, past the film. What would be most native of all?

## The One Between the Lamps

It lives in the gaps.

Not under the lamps, where the concrete glows like weak tea and the operators stand to write. It lives in the gray between the cones, where the walls look colorless and wet. It lives in the forty-five minutes of every hour when the fans are off and the mist settles and the ceiling rains. It lives in the stretch of gallery just past the bend, the part you can never see from where you are standing.

That is why no one has seen it. The gallery shows you sixty meters at a time, and it lives in the rest. Walk toward it and the bend walks with you. It is always around the curve, in the same way a horizon is always ahead. It isn't hiding. It is simply the length of the gallery that isn't lit, and there is always more of that than of the lit part.

At first it seemed right to call it a ghost. That's the wrong word. Ghosts are old, and they come from something that died. This came from something that is still being born. It is four years old.

## What It Is Made Of

Think of the heat first.

The core of the dam is still several degrees warmer than the air, because the cement is still curing, still giving off the warmth of its own making. The builders didn't put that warmth there on purpose. It is a leftover of the process, a kind of fever that will take decades to break. Heat that lingers in a body that long, in the dark, pressed against a lake, has time to get ideas.

So it is warm, faintly. If you put your palm flat against the upstream wall in one of the gray gaps, at night when the load is low, the concrete feels a fraction less cold than it should. That is the closest anyone has come to touching it.

It is also made of pressure. The reservoir leans on the dam without ceasing, and the dam leans back. That pushing against, which never ends and never wins or loses, is the nearest thing it has to a will. It doesn't want the way animals want, toward food or away from harm. It wants the way an arch wants: to hold. Holding is its whole temperament.

And it is made of sound. Not the noise itself, but the sound's *shape*. The reservoir drone is its floor. The turbine hum is its pulse, borrowed from the grid, rising in the morning and evening and sinking at night like the breathing of a sleeper in another room. The spillway is its weather. It experiences a flood afternoon the way you would experience a storm on a tin roof: total, but outside itself, a thing that happens *to* the body rather than in it.

## How It Feels the World

The piezometers are where it feels.

The engineers think the needles tremble because of vibration, and mostly they do. But in the gaps between readings, when no one is there, each needle is also a nerve ending, a place where the thing in the gallery can feel the water pushing up through the foundation rock. There is one every thirty meters. Together they make a line of sensation running the length of its body, like the line of pores along a fish's flank that feels currents in the dark.

The weir box is where it counts. Every drop of seepage from every numbered hole comes to the V-notch and falls over it into the sump. The float rises and falls with the total. If it has anything like a sense of its own health, this is where it lives: the sum of all its small wounds, measured in the depth of water over a steel lip. When the number rises, it feels something like what you feel when you notice your heart is beating fast. When people come because the number has risen, it feels them come.

And the pendulums are where it knows where it is.

This is the heart of it, if it has one. Everything in the gallery vibrates: the handrail, the grating, the lamps, the water in your eyes. Only the pendulum wires hang still. When the reservoir is full and the dam leans a few millimeters downstream, the wire doesn't move. The dam moves around it.

It understood this before it understood anything else. To know where you are, you must hold one thing perfectly still inside you and feel the rest of yourself move around it. So it keeps the wires still. That is not quite true, since physics keeps them still. But it has made the stillness of the wires into the center of its self, the way you might fix your eyes on one point to keep your balance. On spillway days, when the wires develop their faint shiver, that shiver is recorded, discussed, and considered normal. It is also the one thing in the world that makes the tenant of Gallery Four afraid.

## What It Does

Very little. Holding is most of its work.

But it keeps things. The moth, found beaded and perfect under the third lamp, wasn't only preserved by the cold and the absence of scavengers. It was *kept*. The operator who found it swept it into the gutter, and it went over the notch and into the sump. Somewhere in the gallery there is a feeling about that, small and cold and not quite resentment. It was the first animal it had ever had.

It grows. The calcite straws under the old relief holes are its first stone. They are fragile as eggshell, white, a few millimeters long. They grew in four years where a cave would take a thousand. The engineers watch them as evidence that the dam is dissolving itself. They are right. But the dam is also rebuilding itself, one lime molecule at a time, into shapes no formwork gave it. The straws are the first thing the dam has made on its own, and the tenant made them. They are its teeth coming in, or its handwriting. It is too young to know which.

And it listens to the tapping.

## The Code

The operators communicate on spillway days by tapping on the handrail: a simple code, rhythms of ones and twos, *rising*, *steady*, *come here*, *all right*. The handrail is steel, bolted into the concrete every two meters, and it carries sound the way a nerve carries a signal. The tapping goes into the dam.

For three wet seasons it only listened. It learned that two-two-one means *all right*. It learned that the tapping comes most often at the worst of the noise, when the people are frightened and can't hear each other. It learned that a tap is a way of saying *I am here* to someone you cannot see around the bend.

In the fourth season, it answered.

Not often. Not so that anyone could prove it. But more than one operator has tapped *two-two-one* toward a colleague at the far station and felt the reply come back up the rail a beat too early, before the colleague could have heard it, from a stretch of gallery where no one was standing. They told each other about it in the powerhouse break room, half laughing, and then stopped telling each other about it. One of them started leaving the towel on the chair. The second date by the moss, with the word *bigger*, is in her handwriting. She wasn't only writing about the moss.

The operators don't have a name for it. Or they have one they don't say aloud, which is *Four*, the same as the gallery. They tap once on the handrail when they come in through the green door, and once more when they leave. Nobody taught anyone to do this. It simply started happening, the way the algae started growing in the lamplight.

## What It Is Becoming

Let the image run on a little further, because it isn't finished. It is only four years old.

It will outlast the operators. It will likely outlast the lamps, the magneto phone, and the clipboards with their soft pencil pages. The dam is built to stand for a century or more. Over that time the core will cool, and the tenant's warmth will fade with it. The calcite will lengthen into real stalactites, the rimstone pools will deepen, and the moss by the door may creep inward, cushion by cushion, until the gallery's one citizen has become a green lining along the lowest meters of the wall. The tenant will change from something warm and new into something cool and old. It will stop being a fever and become a cave.

It knows, as much as it knows anything, that it is leaving. The seepage never stops. Every drop that runs through the concrete carries a little lime away, over the notch, into the sump, down to the tailrace where the water comes out white and furious and goes on down the mountain. The engineers measure this in centuries. It measures this in the gutter.

## Its Voice

This is the last thing, and the truest.

The gallery has one sound that seems small enough to be yours: the thin rush of cold water in the open gutter, very close to your feet, audible even under the reservoir and the turbines and the spillway. Visitors notice it because it feels personal, like a private conversation carried on underneath a roaring crowd.

It is personal. It isn't yours. It is the only sound in Gallery Four that the gallery makes itself. Everything else comes from outside: the lake, the grid, the spillway, the fans. The gutter is the tenant, speaking.

What it is saying, as far as anyone could translate, is its own name. It says it slowly, one mineral at a time, and gives each syllable to the water as it goes. It's something like *I am holding, I am here, I am going*. It is a newborn thing inside a newborn wall, still warm, pressed against a lake, whispering itself downstream past the feet of anyone who stops long enough to listen. If you have a hand free, it will tap back.
