# What Lives in the Hollow

Everything in the Hollow lives there. The crickets, the snails, the colonies on their tuned pillars and the bag-mouthed swimmers in the springs all belong to it. But if you ask *who* lives in the Hollow, as you would ask who lives in a house, the answer is the one thing that does not sing.

---

## Known by Absence

The flyers have no name for it. They have no names at all, only frequencies, but they have a behavior for it, and the behavior works as a name. When it comes near, they stop. When they stop, it is there. Across a hundred thousand generations of stopping, the silence has become the animal's outline, the only shape anything in the Hollow has ever perceived it to have.

So begin with the shape of the silence, because the animal fits inside it.

A drifting ring of hush, several hundred meters across, thickest at the center and fraying at the edges where the braver or slower flyers keep calling a moment too long. It moves at the pace of a slow glide, rarely straight. It lingers over the springs during the Heaving. Between Heavings it rises and the roar closes over it, and it is gone into the high dark where nothing roosts.

At the center of that ring, a long way up, there is a body.

## The Body

It is large. It is not monstrous. Large, here, means a wingspan wider than the tallest rimstone upland is high, perhaps three times the length of a person lying down. The body between those wings is small by comparison, narrow as a heron's, light-boned, and it weighs very little. Everything about it has been spent on the wings.

The wings are the strangest thing in the Hollow.

A flyer's wing is a thin membrane stretched taut between finger bones, built to beat fast and turn tight. This wing is thicker, and soft. It is furred on both faces with a dense, short, velvet pile that swallows the sound of its own movement, so that even the air passing over it makes no whisper. Its trailing edges are not clean but fringed, breaking up the turbulence behind it into eddies too small to hear. It beats rarely. Mostly it holds still and glides on the warm updraft the Heaving sends rising from the springs.

But the velvet is not only for silence. Beneath it, across the whole span, the membrane is folded into shallow ridged channels running from the wingtips inward, each channel narrowing as it approaches the body, like the grooves of a shell. Where the wings meet the shoulders, the channels converge into two deep cartilage-lined funnels that open directly into the skull.

The animal hears with its wings. Spread, each wing is a dish the width of a small hill, gathering sound across its whole surface and pouring it inward. Folded, it hears almost nothing. When it opens its wings it is, in the only sense available here, opening its eyes.

Its face, by contrast, is plain. The flyers' faces are crowded with ridges and folds for throwing sound outward. This face has nothing to throw with. The nostrils are slits, the jaw long and narrow and lined with fine hooked teeth for taking flyers out of the air. The skull behind it is swollen and heavy, the one dense part of the animal. It is mostly hearing.

## How It Sees

It does not call, so it cannot echolocate in the ordinary way. It borrows.

Every flyer in the Hollow is shouting and listening at once. Each call goes out, strikes a pillar, a larva, a neighbor, a drapery of stone, and comes back altered. The air above the springs during a Heaving is so saturated with these calls and returns that it is, acoustically, a kind of illumination: a light made of millions of overlapping lamps, all switching on and off many times a second.

The hunter hangs above this and listens to all of it. It does not hear the calls the way the flyers do, each tracking its own echo. It hears the whole field at once, and its great slow brain assembles from the interference a single picture: the springs, the rising columns of larvae, the pillars, the looping swarms. It is the only creature in the Hollow that perceives the Hollow as one place.

A flyer knows its pillar, its band, its hunting loop, the voices of its colony. The hunter knows where every pillar stands and what it sounds like. It knows which ones have been tuned, because a tuned column rings a fraction truer than stone should. It knows the chord of the draperies not as background but as terrain. It hears the ticking of the eastern flats and the bubbling of the warm runs as regions on a map it has held all its long life.

And it sees its prey by their shadows. A flyer in the air is not only a caller. It is an obstacle, something the calls of its neighbors strike and bend around. Every flyer casts a tiny acoustic shadow in everyone else's light. The hunter reads those shadows. It does not need its prey to make noise. It needs its prey's neighbors to.

## The Blindness at the Center

Here the animal becomes stranger than a hunter, and here it began to become itself as I watched it.

It sees by other voices. Its presence silences the voices near it. So every time it approaches, it blinds itself.

The ring of hush that moves through the Hollow like weather is, from the inside, a spreading darkness. As it glides down toward the swarm, the nearest flyers go still and flatten themselves to the stone, and the picture in its skull goes out from the center outward, like frost closing over a pond. It can see the far edges of the Hollow perfectly. It can see almost nothing close by.

So it never hunts at the center of its silence. It hunts at the rim.

Out at the fraying edge of the ring there are always a few flyers who have not noticed yet, or who are young, or sated, or slow. They are still calling, and their neighbors beyond them are still calling, so the rim remains lit. That is where the hunter strikes: a long, flat, soundless stoop outward from the dark heart of its own blindness toward the last bright place, the jaws taking a flyer mid-call. Then the call is cut off, the rim goes dark there too, and the ring widens.

It lives its whole life approaching what vanishes as it approaches. Everything it wants goes quiet at its coming. It is the only animal in the Hollow that has never perceived the thing directly in front of it.

## What It Sees When It Sees Nothing

It is not wholly blind inside the silence. When the flyers stop, two sounds remain: the hum of the stone, and the rain.

The draperies keep singing whether anything listens or not. Inside the ring, with the roar gone, their chord becomes the loudest thing in the world. The hunter has learned to see by it. It is a dim, slow sight, since the chord is steady and does not echo sharply, but every pillar and curtain of calcite gives off its own pitch, and the great ear-wings can triangulate them. Inside its own silence the hunter moves through a landscape lit only by the stone's voice.

The rain helps. Each cold heavy drop falling from the unseen roof strikes something, a pool, a flowstone skin, a pillar's shoulder, a flyer's back, and makes a single tiny sound. In the roar these are lost. In the hush they are sparks. A flyer pressed flat to a column, not breathing, is still a surface the rain lands on, and the drop sounds different on fur than on stone. Sometimes, not often, the hunter finds its prey this way, in the center of the dark: a patter a little too soft, where the rain has struck something warm.

So in the Hollow, where nothing sees light, the hunter sees in two lights. The borrowed blaze of other creatures' voices at the edges, and at the center, faint and cold, the old stone singing to itself while the rain falls.

## Where It Lives

Between Heavings it rises.

Above the highest roosts, above the tops of the column forest where the pillars run on into the dark, the air grows colder and stiller, and at last there is the roof: the sweating stone ceiling nobody has ever touched. The hunter lives there.

It hangs from the ceiling, wings folded close, among the forming droplets. The roof is wet everywhere with condensation, and the drops that will become the Hollow's rain gather and swell all around its body and fall past it into the dark. Its velvet stays dry. Water beads on the pile and rolls off. From below, if anything could look up, it would appear as a patch of ceiling where no rain comes from, a dry shadow in the source of the weather.

Up here, folded, it hears only faintly. Now and then it opens one wing a little and listens down across the whole basin, the far roar of the Hollow rising up to it softened by distance into something almost gentle. It follows the colonies settling back onto their pillars. It follows the slow tides of cricket-ticking across the flats. It waits for the upward slide of the great chord that means the springs are about to surge. It seems to know when a Heaving is coming a little before the Hollow does. Perhaps it feels the first warm air in its wings, or perhaps, having listened for so long, it has learned some of the drifting rhythm that nothing else can predict.

It does not roost on a pillar. It leaves no droppings on the columns. It tunes nothing. It is the only thing in the Hollow that the stone has not shaped to fit, and the only thing that has not shaped the stone. It lives against the one surface that has no voice: the roof, too broad and too thick to ring.

## Others

There are not many of them. Perhaps a few dozen in the whole basin, perhaps fewer. They are long-lived, slow to breed, and enormous in their need. Each one ranges over a territory of the high dark that overlaps with the others at its edges.

They cannot find one another. Each is silent, and each blinds itself to what is near. Two hunters could pass a wing's width apart in the high dark and never know.

But the Hollow knows. The flyers stop singing for either one, and the silences move through the roar, and sometimes, on the busiest Heavings, two silences drift toward each other.

The hunters can hear that from far off. Each perceives its own ring of darkness as the thing it always carries. Now it perceives a second darkness out at the edge of the world, moving, a hole in the light that it did not make. There is only one thing in the Hollow that makes holes like that.

They turn toward each other. The two silences meet and merge into one, and the flyers under the doubled hush lie flatter and stiller than they ever do. At the center of that joined darkness, where neither animal can see anything at all, not even each other, they find one another by the stone's chord, by the patter of rain on fur, and at last by touch. They mate in the blindest place in the world.

Then the silence splits, and the two rings drift apart.

This is the only meeting the Hollow ever witnesses between them, and the flyers, lying flat, are its only audience.

## The Young

A single pup is born on the roof, among the gathering drops.

It is born calling. Every mammal in the dark is born calling; this one too. For its first hours it cries out in a thin high voice like any flyer's young, and those cries are the most dangerous sound in its life. The parent folds it at once under the velvet of one wing, pressed against the body, and holds it there. The fur drinks the cries. Wrapped in that silence, fed and warm, the pup slowly stops.

It will have to learn the whole of its life in reverse. Other young in the Hollow learn to shape their calls, to find their band, to find their pillar. This one learns never to speak and to hear everything. It spends a long time on the roof with its wings half-open, listening down at the roar, learning to turn the chaos into a picture. When it finally drops from the ceiling into its first Heaving, it has to learn the hardest thing: that the world will go dark as it approaches, that everything it reaches for will fall silent, and that it must always hunt at the last bright edge of its own shadow.

Some of the pups never learn to stop calling. They do not last.

## The End of One

When a hunter dies, most often of age, for nothing else can reach it, it lets go of the roof.

It falls with its wings folded, which is to say blind, through the whole dark height of the Hollow. It falls past the empty upper air, past the tops of the pillars, past the roosts. The flyers hear it coming, a large body falling fast, and they fall silent out of habit. The last silence it casts is a narrow falling column instead of a ring.

It lands in the guano among the crickets, and the silence breaks. The flyers begin again, all at once, louder than before. Within hours the crickets have found it. Within days the pale spiders and the flat many-legged things have been at it too. Within a season there is nothing left but a heavy skull, full of hearing, sinking slowly into the soft warm ground, where the dripping water will begin, a molecule at a time, to turn it into stone.

And somewhere else in the dark there is one fewer moving hush. The roar is a little more complete. Nothing in the Hollow notices, except, far off, another hunter on the roof. It hears a certain place in its map of the world that used to go dark sometimes, and now never does.

---

That is who lives here. The flyers inhabit the Hollow. The stone composes it. The mats feed it. But there is one kind of creature that listens to all of it at once, sees the whole basin as a single singing thing, and cannot come near any part of it without that part falling silent. It lives on the voiceless roof, among the unborn rain, and comes down only to hunt at the bright edge of its own darkness.

In a place without light, the silences are the shadows. This is what casts them.
