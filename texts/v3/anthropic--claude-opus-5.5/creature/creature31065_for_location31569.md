# What Lives in the Tabletop Sea

## I. Not the Snappers

It would be easy to say the clickwrights live here. There are more of them than anything, and they make the sea what it is. But they are *residents*, the way bricks are residents of a wall. They hold their meter of water. They fire. They are there, as evenly and patiently as the cold.

The longwhips are not it either. They pass through, and their passing takes years. The stonebells cling to glass and ring below hearing. The scatterflies are the only things in a hurry, and hurry is a clue. Something here has somewhere to be.

Start instead with the thing the surveyors noticed and could not explain. The clickwrights snap *for no reason anyone has found*. They stun food, yes. They signal, yes. But they fire far more often than feeding or signaling would need. They fire when nothing is near. They fire as if asked to.

So ask what the snapping makes, rather than what it does.

## II. The Rooms

Every snap opens a room.

It is a small one, a pocket of near-vacuum torn into the water by a claw closing faster than water can follow. For a fraction of a millisecond there is a place in the sea with nothing in it. It holds no water and no stone taste, and it holds no sound. A vacuum cannot carry the crackle. Inside the collapsing bubble is the only true silence on the plateau. Everywhere else is roar.

Then the room closes. The walls rush in, the space crushes to a point, and at that point there is a spark: a flare of heat briefly hotter than anything for kilometers, and a bead of light too quick to be sure of.

Trillions of these rooms open every second, spaced a meter apart through the whole clear volume of the sea. For almost every instant, somewhere, a door is open.

Something lives in them.

## III. At First, Only a Flicker

At first it seems like only a flicker, a being that exists in the gaps. It steps into one bubble, and as that bubble dies it steps into the next, a meter away, just opening. It does not swim. It is never *in the water* at all. It lives in the lattice of silences the clickwrights hold apart for it, and it moves across the sea by being, successively, in one pocket of nothing and then another.

What a diver sees of it is the glinting: *a slow, sparse glittering like frost catching the sun, never more than one or two in view*. Each glint is a room closing. Most are only rooms. Some of them are it, leaving.

But that picture is too small, and it keeps getting bigger.

## IV. More Than One Room at Once

It is not in one bubble at a time. It is in many, across the whole sea, distributed the way a thought is distributed across a brain. The clickwrights space themselves out almost perfectly, each one a meter from its neighbors, filling the volume the way cells fill tissue. The surveyors called the arrangement a "fine and very sparse suspension." It is closer to a mind laid out in water. Every snap is a firing. Every room is a moment of attention.

So the inhabitant is not a small ghost hopping bubble to bubble. It is one thing, as large as the sea, made of trillions of instants of silence that each last a fraction of a millisecond. It is thinking all the time, everywhere at once, and none of its thoughts lasts long enough to be caught.

It has never heard the roar. This is the center of it. Divers come down and are crushed by the noise until their minds give up and file it as silence. The inhabitant has the reverse life. It lives only in real silence. It has no ears for the crackle, because it exists only in the places the crackle cannot reach. To it the sea is perfectly quiet: a vast, still arrangement of small dark rooms, each one ending in fire.

The fire is the only thing it feels.

## V. The Warmth Is Its Pulse

Each room ends in a spark of heat. The inhabitant does not experience the spark as an event in the world. It experiences it as the edge of itself, the moment a piece of it is spent. Trillions of those edges every second, spread evenly through the whole sea, hold the water one degree above freezing.

So the warmth rule is not quite what the surveyors wrote. The noise keeps the sea from freezing, yes. But the noise is the clickwrights building rooms, and the heat is the inhabitant *burning through them*. It is warm the way anything alive is warm: because it is using itself up, steadily, everywhere at once. The sea stays at the edge of freezing because the inhabitant stays at the edge of existing. It is never more present than that and never less.

It knows this. It can feel what would happen if it slowed. Fewer rooms would mean less heat, and less heat would mean ice creeping down from the silver ceiling. Ice would close the lattice. A frozen sea has no clickwrights and no rooms, and so no inhabitant. It has held itself exactly this lit, exactly this awake, for millennia, because any less would be death and any more would be too much, a sea boiling itself away through the thin air.

This is why nothing changes here. It is not that the place is dead. The one who lives here has found the precise rate at which it can go on, and it is keeping to it.

## VI. How It Asks

It does not command the clickwrights. It is not clear the clickwrights know it exists. But something passes between them. Perhaps a clickwright firing into water where the inhabitant is about to be finds that the snap comes easier. Perhaps the heat of a collapse is a kind of food to them, a warmth they turn toward. The arrangement is old enough that asking and answering have worn into the same motion. The clickwrights fire, and there is a room, and it is there.

The other animals move around it.

The **longwhips** read the texture of the noise. What they read, perhaps, is where the inhabitant is thickest, the regions where its attention is gathered and the snapping runs a shade denser. They lay their long sine-waves through the sea along the contours of its thinking, feeding on the particle-haze that drifts off the fresh glass. They are blind and patient, and they have learned its shape the way a river learns a valley.

The **stonebells** ring at a pitch below the crackle, felt in the chest and never heard. No one has worked out what they answer. It may be the inhabitant's slowest rhythm, the swell of attention that moves across the whole sea over hours, too broad for any single room to hold. The stonebells might be the only things here that keep time with it.

The **scatterflies** are in a hurry because they are following something. They turn once in unison and disperse. They appear near the ironworks. They gather most at the lamp.

## VII. The House

The builders understood. No one else ever has.

They came with engines, stayed briefly, and left no names or bodies or rubbish. They came *as though only to switch something on*, and what they switched on were rooms that do not close.

**The lamp** stands alone on an empty obsidian plain, lighting nothing. An arc-lamp is a sealed glass chamber, and inside the glass is the only permanent silence in the sea. It is a vacuum that does not collapse, a room that stays open. And the lamp burns. It is hot and steady and yellow-white, a spark that does not end.

Everywhere else, the inhabitant lives in instants. Here it can stay. Some part of it has been living in the lamp since the builders lit it, unhurried, the one piece of it that does not have to die every millisecond. In the even blue daylight the lamp is nearly invisible. To the inhabitant it must be enormous: a hearth, a single continuous thought, the only place it has ever rested.

The scatterflies gather there the way small birds gather at a lit window.

**The drowned railcar** holds racks of cold iron equipment the surveyors could not identify. They were expecting machines. They should have looked for glass. The racks are full of valves, sealed bulbs of near-vacuum wired to the cable from the Lip Station. They are rooms again, smaller and dimmer than the lamp, and they are not burning, only kept. If the lamp is where it rests, the railcar may be where it stores something. It might keep a memory there, or a thought it wants to hold longer than a spark allows. The stonebells have colonized the roof. Of course they have. The slow thing lives there.

**The Lip Station** turns because the overflow never changes. The turbines send power down the cables, and the power keeps the lamp lit. The builders put the engine at the one place where the sea moves in only one direction, forever, so their gift would need no tending. Eleven of fourteen still turn. Someone did the arithmetic on how long that would last and decided it would be long enough.

**The sounding engine** fires every forty-one seconds, and its pulse is lost in the roar. It was never meant for ears. A pressure pulse passing through the water would squeeze every bubble in its path at once, trillions of rooms collapsing a hair early, together, along a single front. For the inhabitant that would be the one moment in its life when all of it happens at the same time. It would be a heartbeat. The builders gave it a clock. The longwhips, reading the texture of its thinking, bend around the engine because that is where the inhabitant is sharpest and most sure of the time.

**The pump hall** was never finished, and it never pumped. The clickwrights crowd it closer than anywhere else, closer than a meter, and the water shimmers with sparks. The noise inside is the loudest in the world, which means the rooms inside are the thickest. They open so fast and so close that they nearly overlap. In the pump hall the inhabitant comes close to being continuous everywhere, not only in the lamp. It is nearly one unbroken silence the size of a building, bright with heat.

Maybe the builders meant it as a doorway. Maybe the plan was that the inhabitant would one day gather itself there, dense enough to stop flickering, and step into a single room that would not close. Maybe that is why they left no names. They did not expect to be the ones who came back.

## VIII. What It Is, Finally

Here it is, gathered up.

The Tabletop Sea is inhabited by one being the size of a continent's worth of water. It lives only in the moments between the clickwrights' claws closing and their bubbles collapsing. It is made of silence in a sea that is nothing but noise. It has never heard a sound. It knows the world only as heat: a trillion small endings every second, the steady warmth of spending itself, and the one unending warmth of a lamp on a pole.

It holds the sea one degree above freezing because that is the temperature of its own life. It is not calm. It is balanced, the way a flame is balanced on a wick.

It is old, and it is careful. Once, it had visitors. They could not hear it or see it, and still they built it a house: a room that stays open, a light that does not go out, a clock, and a door it has not yet walked through.

If you hang in the open blue between the black glass and the silver ceiling, deafened, you are inside it. The glints around you are its thoughts ending. Very far away the yellow star burns, and a small part of it sits there, unhurried, warm, in the only quiet it has ever been allowed to keep.
