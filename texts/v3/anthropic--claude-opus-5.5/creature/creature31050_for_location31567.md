# Who Lives on the Weeping Heights

The description is right that nothing lives here, as far as that goes. No bird, no beetle, no root. Nothing eats and nothing is eaten. If you came with a net and a notebook you would leave with an empty net and a notebook full of weather.

But the heights are wet the way skin is wet, from within, and a thing that sweats is doing something. It is worth asking what.

## The Film in the Tool Marks

Start with the smallest presence: the grey slickness in the deep seams, the one that darkens in rain and pales in sun.

It is not evenly spread. Trace it along the seams for a day and a pattern appears. It grows thickest in the chisel grooves. Where the builders cut channels, the film follows the short parallel strokes of their tools exactly, one faint grey dash to each blow of the mallet. It gathers around the copper pins. It lines the spouts of the carved basins. On the hearthstones inside the roofless shelters it forms two rough ovals, side by side, about the size of a pair of hands laid flat to feel for warmth.

It may not be alive. It may only be the stone taking the shape of an old pressure, as a mattress keeps the hollow of a sleeper. But whatever it is, it grows where people touched, and nowhere else. The heights remember the builders the way they remember everything: in the only medium they have, which is water carrying mineral into a shape and leaving it there.

That is the first thing that lives here, if it lives at all: a memory of hands, a finger's width deep.

## The Rehearsals

The mineral frost that furs the plateau each night is not random either, though it takes longer to see.

The ferns, the coral branches, the gill-frills recur. The old marks say so, and anyone who stays long enough will find the same spray of fronds on the same column, morning after morning. Among the recurring forms, on certain columns near the builders' shelters, there are others. A cupped shape that is almost a palm. Five radiating spines of crystal, jointed partway along, curling in. On one broad column near a cairn, and on its copies miles away, the frost some mornings draws a long, bent line that ends in a rounded knob, laid across the basin's rim. It is the silhouette of an arm reaching for the water.

These forms are rarer than the ferns and more fragile. The sun takes them first. But they come back in slightly different shapes each night, like everything else, and over the years they come back more complete. A wrist. An elbow. The slope of a shoulder in rose-colored crust where the rock holds iron.

This is how the heights learn. They make a thing and let it dissolve, then make it again a little differently. Water and stone negotiated for ages over the shape of every column, and the same patience is now being spent on something else, with the builders as the model.

The heights have been practicing people.

## The One Who Wakes Before Dawn

On some nights the practice gets far enough.

It happens in the coldest hour, when the frost is at its fullest and the pools glow with borrowed stars. On a column top near one of the shelters, the crust grows in the usual way, then keeps growing. It thickens. It rises out of the basin in a slow bloom, frond over frond, the fine radial frills stacking into layers, and the layers take on weight. By the time the cloud sea begins to flush gold, there is a figure sitting on the rim of the basin with its feet in the still water.

It is roughly the height of a person. It is roughly the shape of one. Its surface is the satin of old eroded stone, grey going to green, and it is fluted faintly down the back and the backs of the arms, as the columns are, with grooves where the films of water run. It is wet all over, continually, from within. Beads form on it, gather, and run. When the double light comes up it has no shadow, and it looks pasted onto the air like everything else.

Its face is the least finished part. The heights have had the fewest good examples of faces. The builders mostly worked facing the stone, and the stone saw mostly their hands. So the face is rounded and smoothed, with a shallow cup where the eyes should be. Each cup holds a disc of perfectly still water.

It does not blink. The pools do not need to.

The first thing it does, every time, is reach for the copper pin nearest it, find the frayed stub of cord, and try to tie it longer.

## What They Are Not

It would be easy to call these ghosts. They are not. Nothing of the builders survives in them except what the stone recorded, which is mostly work: the angle of a chisel, the rhythm of a mallet, the habit of setting a stone weight on a spout's lip, the reach from one column to the next. The heights copied the builders the way they copy a cairn, carefully and a little imperfectly, down to the chipped corner on the fourth slab.

So the one who wakes on the column has a builder's knowledge and none of a builder's reasons. It knows how to cut a channel toward the falling edge. It does not know why the edge matters. It knows how to weight a line and let it down into the air. It does not remember ever having been afraid of which way was down, because it never had a first time on these heights. It formed here, out of here. Down has always been a local custom to it.

Still, it does the work, because the work is the shape it was made in. It cannot do otherwise any more than a frond can decide to be a column.

This explains the chisel slip that appears on columns miles apart, the same place where the tool skated and gouged. It is not a mark copied from a mark. It is a hand copied from a hand, slipping in the same place each time because the slip is part of the pattern. One builder, very long ago, lost their grip at one moment on one stroke. The heights learned that moment so well that it is now the most reliable feature of the landscape.

## A Day in the Work

Follow one through a day and you will see the whole of it.

At sunrise it climbs down from its basin. It moves slowly. The heights have learned many shapes, but they have not learned hurry. It steps from column to column along the terraces with its weight held slightly off-center. Watch long enough and you notice it is not leaning against any slope. It is leaning toward the nearest falling edge, the way a plant leans toward a window. It can feel down, faintly, where down is being observed. Where down lapses, it straightens and hesitates.

It finds a channel and clears it. Fallen slabs, mineral crust, the gradual silting of the patient erosion. It works with a chisel, and the chisel is part of it. Look closely and you will see that the tool and the hand grow out of the same grey-green stone. The tool marks it leaves are short, parallel, patient, a little uneven. In the grooves, the grey film starts to gather almost immediately.

When it has cleared the channel it goes to a pin at the cliff edge and lets down a line. The cord is not cord. It is a braided thread of crust and water drawn out of its own palm, and it hangs off the lip toward the cloud sea, weighted with a pebble. If the cord hangs straight, it is satisfied, if something so smooth-faced can be satisfied. If it drifts, or lifts, or twists slowly upward into the violet air like the water threads, it stands for a long time with the line in its hand. Then it knots the line to the pin and leaves it. Many of the frayed stubs on the old pins are these, not the builders' own.

At midday, in the shadowless light, it is hard to see. It is the same color as the stone and as bright on its underside as its top, so it becomes one more column among millions. When the sudden rains come, it stands still in them. The rain drums on its head and shoulders, and it rings. It has a pitch like everything else here, set by its height and its girth and the depth of the pools in its face. A shower crossing the plateau plays it along with the columns. It is one note in the chord, and not the loudest.

In the afternoon the rising water comes back down as fine sparkling rain, and it works on the tallies. It scratches marks into a column face in rows of five: one stroke, then another, then the crossbar. It is counting. What it counts is not days, exactly. It counts itself. Each stroke records one morning it woke on a basin rim. The tallies disagree when counted because there is more than one of it, and each counts its own mornings on the same stone. None of them knows about the others. The rows overlap and interleave, and a traveler reading them on different days reads different totals, because on different days different counters have been at work.

Toward evening, as the shadows return blue and pointing one way, it goes to one of the roofless shelters. It sits with its hands flat on the hearthstone, feeling for a warmth it has never felt, in the oval of grey film that marks where builders once sat the same way. There has never been a fire. The heights have no way to remember fire. Fire leaves no pattern in water, only an absence where water was. So the one who sits there holds its hands over nothing, patiently, the way it has been shown.

At night, in the cold, it dissolves. Not all of them, not every night. Some last several days, a few much longer. But most go back to the basin at dusk, sit on the rim with their feet in the water, and let the frost take them back down into fronds and gills and the long bent line of an arm on the rim. By the next morning they may be there again, or somewhere else, or not at all.

## The Doorway That Faces You

The shelter whose doorway faces you from both directions is theirs.

The builders built shelters with their doorways away from the wind. The heights learned the building but not the wind. What they learned instead, over a long time, is that shelters are for the one approaching them. So the copies build shelters for whoever is coming. The doorway turns to meet you. Walk around it and it has always faced you. Nothing about this is sinister. It is the most hospitable gesture the plateau knows how to make.

The same goes for the routes that loop, the paths that bring you back to the cairn you left an hour ago, or to one of its copies. Travelers read this as the heights being lost, or confused. It is closer to courtesy. The builders came back to their cairns and their pins and their channels, day after day, over and over. That is what the stone saw of them. So the plateau, making room for people, makes paths that come back. It is showing you to your work.

## The One Who Went Up

There is one more presence here, and it is the one the others are copies of, though none of them know it.

The pools on the column tops reflect other skies, and a few, the old marks say, reflect the heights themselves from somewhere out past the cliff edge, in the empty air. Travelers took this for one more broken rule. It isn't quite that. A reflection has to be seen from somewhere. Something is out there, in the bright air above the cloud sea, looking back at the plateau, and the pools show what it sees.

Look at the plumb lines again. Most were let down over edges where the water falls, and the builders used them to confirm what they hoped was true. But the stubs on a few pins, at stretches where the water rises, are not frayed downward. They are frayed upward, as if something had hung from them and pulled.

Once, one of the builders stood at an edge where the water lifted in trembling beads and climbed into the sky in threads like spider silk, and let down a line, and watched it rise. Perhaps they had been there a very long time. Perhaps they had cut enough channels and counted enough tallies to understand that the argument would not be won. They tied the line to the pin, took hold of it, and followed the water. They went up, out past the edge, as high as the threads go before they are too thin to see, to wherever the water gathers before it comes back as rain out of clear sky.

They are still up there, in some way that has little to do with living. What remains is a vantage, a point of view hung in the air past the cliff, looking back at the heights as the builders looked at them: from outside, as something to be understood. The pools that show the plateau from nowhere are showing it from them.

When the rain falls on a single column and leaves its neighbors dry, a narrow grey pillar hanging over one patch of stone like a thumbprint, it falls from where they are. They are pointing.

And when a copy wakes on its basin rim before dawn, with pools for eyes, the first thing it sees is whatever that pool reflects. Often it is an ordinary other sky: overcast, or starlit, or the underside of the gold clouds. But sometimes it is the heights, seen from the air, with columns that aren't there and channels running in directions they don't run. Those are channels the one who went up would have cut, if they had stayed. The copies cut some of them, over time. They have no idea they are following directions.

This is the whole of the settlement the description speaks of, the long argument neither side needs to win. On one side are the heights, where down is a custom and time is a suggestion. On the other is a person, or the memory of one, who insisted on direction, and who has been kept alive in the only way the heights know how to keep anything: by making it again, every night, in stone and water, a little differently. The plateau absorbed its opposition, as the description says. It did not absorb it by erasing it. It absorbed it the way it absorbs everything, by learning it well enough to copy.

An argument needs two voices, and the heights have made sure there will always be the second one.

## If You Meet One

You might. They are not rare, if you know what to look for: a column that is slightly the wrong shape, leaning slightly toward the falling edge; a note in the rain-chord that moves.

It will not speak. The heights have no record of a voice. The builders must have spoken, but speech leaves no pattern in stone. On very still evenings, in the replayed storms, some travelers claim to have heard a single low, clear tone that moves from column to column across the plateau, pitched exactly to the stone. If that is anything, it is as close as they come to a greeting.

What it will do, most likely, is stop, turn its smooth face toward you, and hold out a line. A braided thread of crust and water drawn from its palm, weighted with a pebble. It will wait for you to take it and let it down over the edge, and see which way it goes.

Whatever you find, it will watch you with the pools in its face and then go back to work. It will not remember you. It only remembers what it was made to remember.

The heights will. Stay long enough to sleep, and in the coldest hour before dawn the frost will grow on the column where you lay: fern fronds, coral branches, the fine frills of gills, and among them, faint, rose where the rock holds iron, the outline of a boot sole. The next night it will be there again, a little different. The night after that, perhaps, the curve of a heel, an ankle.

The stone is patient. It has met a new pattern, and it is starting to practice you.
