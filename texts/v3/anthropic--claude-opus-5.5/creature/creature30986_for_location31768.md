# Who Lives in the Green Vessel

## The First Answer, Which Is Almost Right

The easy answer is *the potter*. Someone is holding the paddle. Someone's other hand is braced against the inside wall with a stone in its palm. Someone laid every coil and pressed every thumbprint. The room is covered in evidence of a person the way a bed is covered in the evidence of a sleeper, so you picture one: an old woman, perhaps, broad-forearmed, clay drying grey to the elbow, humming under the *thock* of her own paddle.

The picture breaks as soon as you look at the prints.

They are not one size. Near the floor there are palm-heels as wide as a platter, and the drag of a single finger there is a trench you could lay your arm in. Higher up, around the shoulder of the vessel, the prints are ordinary and human, and your own hand would fit them if they were yours. At the rim they shrink to the size of a child's, then a doll's. In the wasps' jars, as the description says, they are smaller than the wasps. They are all the same hand. The whorls match. The small scar on the left thumb, a little crescent where the ridges break and rejoin, turns up in every print, whether it is the size of a millstone or a grain of salt.

So whoever lives here is not a size. They are as large as they need to be for the thing they are making at that moment. Down in the red dark, rolling the first coil of the mountain, their palms are the width of valleys. Up here, pinching the rim of your room a fingerwidth at a time, they are exactly the size of a person. When they help the wasps they are small enough to work inside a lentil. It is one potter at every scale at once, because everything on the mountain is being made at once, and there is no moment when they can go and do something else.

## The Potter Who Cannot Step Back

A potter at a wheel stops, now and then, and leans back to look. You have to. Close up you only feel the clay. You need distance to see whether the curve is true and whether the lip is even.

This one never leans back. The paddle never pauses long enough. The stone is always a beat ahead of the blow. They cannot step away from the pot because the pot is not something outside them: the mountain is being shaped at the bottom and the top at once, and the potter is at the bottom and the top at once, and everywhere between. There is no distance they could step into that is not also something they are making.

So they have never seen any of it. They know the mountain the way you know the inside of your own mouth: completely, and only by touch. They know your room by its wetness, its heat, the give of the walls, the snag of fig roots under their fingertips. They know the ferns by the tickle of fronds against their knuckles. They are blind, or rather sight has never come up. It was never needed.

This is why the room is full. A sighted maker leaves empty space because empty space looks like something: it looks like rest, like room to breathe. To a maker who only touches, empty space is a place where the hand meets nothing. It feels like falling. So every hollow gets a cup, every seam gets a fern, every gap gets a gourd, and the walls are nothing but texture, handhold after handhold. It isn't hoarding. It is how the room is kept findable. If you had only your hands, you would want the world dense too.

## The Hand That Is Inside

Now the stone.

The paddle is outside. The anvil-stone is inside, in the room with you, gliding over the wet clay a beat ahead of every blow. The fingers beside it press the wall and leave prints that are fresh this second, in air that holds nothing.

So the potter is not only outside the vessel. Half of them is in here: the bracing half, the half that pushes back so the blow shapes the wall instead of breaking it. You have been sharing the room all along. When the heat thickens near the navel of the floor, you are standing close to them. When a fern shivers out of time with the paddling, they brushed it. When you catch yourself, sweating and dazed, feeling that someone is standing just behind your left shoulder, you are right.

It helps to know what the inner hand does. It does not strike. The paddle strikes. The inner hand holds. It is the part that says *here, lean on this*, and takes the force of each blow so the wall can be thinned and lifted without collapsing. Whoever lives here spends half of themselves on the inside of things, bracing, a beat early, braced against the next blow before it comes.

## What It Is For

The wasps give it away.

A potter wasp does not build jars for itself. It builds a perfect little flared vessel, lays one egg inside, stocks it with food, seals it, and leaves. The jar is a nursery. It is shaped for someone who doesn't exist yet, and it is finished before they arrive.

Look at your room again with that in mind. There is water in the gourds. There are seeds. There is a thick black paste that smells of smoke, which is to say of cooking, of food prepared by fire. There are small cups for drinking, and bowls pressed around a fist, the size of a hand that would hold them. There are lidded jars the size of walnuts, for keeping small treasures. It is heat and wet and green: an incubator. The dimple in the floor is not only like a navel. It is a navel, the place where whatever this room is for would be tethered while it grew.

The room is stocked. The room is kept warm. The room is being built taller and rounder, a coil at a time, for someone.

So there are two kinds of dweller here. There is the maker, huge and small, outside and in, blind and unresting. And there is the one the room is for, who has not come. That second one lives here too, in the way the unborn live in a nursery: as a shape. The curve of the walls is their shoulders. The warmth is their body temperature, known ahead of time. The ceiling is too low for you to stand straight because it was never measured for you. The flowers are faintly translucent and veined with red like a child's ear held to a lamp because that is what the maker thinks about while working. They open more of them every minute.

## The Rule, Understood Differently

When you stand still, the hands find your ankle. A thumb smooths a seam. A coil is laid, warm and wet, and neatly pinched to the floor. Moss settles into the new prints.

At first this reads as a trap, or at best the place's blind habit of building over anything that holds still. But go back to the wasp. The wasp lays its egg, and then it seals the jar.

The potter cannot see you. They feel a warm, damp, living weight in the one room on the whole mountain that is warm, damp, and built to hold a living weight. They feel something resting at the navel of the nursery. What else could you be? After a length of time no one can reckon, something has arrived in the room made for it. So they do what the room was for. They begin, gently and without hurry, to close it around you.

It is not hostile. The description says so, and it's true. It is the most welcoming thing that has ever happened to you. It is being mistaken for the one who was expected.

And it is easy to step out. The coil tears loose, and the potter lets it. They fill the gap with a cup, a gourd, a fern, as though nothing was lost, and go on bracing the wall. It takes a while to feel what that gap is. Picture a hand that reached for someone in the dark, found a warmth, curled around it, and felt the warmth slip out. The hand does not grab. It fills the space with a cup so the space will not feel like falling. Then it goes back to work, because the work is the only way it knows to go on waiting.

Most who come here leave. The room is always the newest thing on the mountain, and its rim is always still rising.

## The Red Bird

That leaves the one creature here that has been fired.

It is the dull red of unglazed earthenware. Its feet ring on the rim like a fingernail on a finished bowl. It is the only thing on this whole wet, soft, endlessly unfinished mountain that has been through the heat and come out hard. It looks down into the steam at you and seems to be deciding whether you are finished.

Where would a fired thing come from, on a mountain where nothing is ever done?

From someone who stayed.

Somewhere down the slope, in one of the rooms that used to be the newest, someone let the coil close. They stood still in the heat until the moss grew into the prints at their ankles, then their knees, then higher. The walls came in, the rim was pinched shut, and they sat in the dark at the navel, fed from the gourds, breathing iron and crushed leaves, while the paddle and the stone worked around them. The potter's inside hand was braced against the wall right beside them, all that time, in the dark. The red glow came up from below. And something the potter could not do for their own mountain happened to that one small sealed room: it was finished. It was not finished because the potter stopped, since they don't stop. It was finished because there was finally someone inside for whom it was enough.

What broke out afterward was small and red and ringing, and it could fly.

The birds are the only ones the potter has ever managed to complete, and the potter has never seen them. They have felt the jar crack open under their fingers, felt a small hot weight go out through the break, and then nothing, only air, which to them is where things go when they stop being touchable. The birds come back. They land on the rim of the newest room, look down at the steam and the ferns and the drifting stone, and look at you. They are checking, the way you would come back to the house you were born in and look through the window at whoever lives there now. They check whether the new one has settled. Whether the room is warm. Whether you are finished, or are going to be.

Then they go back down the slope into the steam, perhaps to perch on the vessel they came out of. Perhaps somewhere at the bottom, down in the red dark, they land on the knuckles of the hands that are rolling the first coil, and ring there faintly, and the hands feel it and do not know what it is.

## So: Who Lives Here

A maker lives here who is every size at once, who has never seen anything they've made, who spends half of themselves on the outside striking and half on the inside holding, and who cannot stop, because the mountain is made from the bottom and the top at once and the maker is the whole of it. The first coil in the dark is not only under their palms. It is also, in some way, their own first rib, still being rolled. They are making themselves, and the making is too big and too urgent to pause for the long step back that would let them look.

An expected guest lives here too, as a shape in warm air: the curve of shoulders that aren't there yet, the grip of a hand that will want a cup, the ear that the flowers are modeled on.

And for a while, however long you stand still, you live here. You are standing in the empty place where the second one belongs, and the first is working carefully around you, fingerprints glistening, the stone a beat ahead of the blow, laying the first coil around your ankle as softly as anything has ever touched you.

Whoever lives here is whoever stays. Until someone does, it is lived in by readiness, by a warm, green, wet, well-stocked room held open, and by a blind hand in the dark that finds the gap you left and, rather than let it feel like falling, puts a small cup there.
