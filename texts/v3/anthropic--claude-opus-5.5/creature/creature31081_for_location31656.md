# Those Who Live in the Galleries

## First, the Obvious Answer

The oilbirds live there. So do the bats, the swiftlets, the roaches that make the floors glitter, the pale crickets feeling their way forward on antennae longer than their lives seem to require. That answer is true, and it is the one the valley gives if you ask. *The mountain is full of birds. Don't go in. You'll come out deaf.*

But ask about the drains.

Some of the cisterns still hold water. The description says so plainly, and a visitor passes it by, because the builders cut good channels and good channels last. They do last, but not for this long. Silt settles, and guano drifts. A single fallen fig leaf lodged in a floor-slot will back up a gutter in a season, and the gutter will spill its drip onto the stone below instead of carrying it down. Over a few centuries every channel in the city should have choked. Most of them have. The dry cisterns and the black mud swamps are what a city of stone and water becomes when nothing tends it.

And yet some of the channels run clean. Put your fingers into a floor-slot in one of the long eastern stairways, three rooms back, where it is already full night. You'll find the stone worn smooth *inside* the slot, down past the reach of water's polish. It was worn there by fingertips.

## The Tapping

You will not hear them. Nobody hears anything here. You'll feel them first, the way you feel the drip on the back of your neck.

Stand in the dark with your palm flat on a wall. This is something you'll start doing without being told, because in the galleries the hand becomes the only reliable eye. The fitted stone is cool on the surface and warm underneath. Under the roar there is a stillness to it, because the builders set their blocks so tightly that the stone behaves almost as one body. Air carries the screaming. Stone carries almost none of it. Stone is the quietest thing in the city.

So you will feel it clearly when something knocks.

It comes as a small, distinct pressure under your palm, like a knuckle on a door three rooms away. You'll take it for settling, or a block shifting, or a root at work. Then it comes again, in a pattern: two, a breath, one. Then from somewhere else, fainter, through a different wall, comes an answer.

This is the first way you learn the people are there. It is also, for most visitors, the only way.

## Who They Are

They are people, or close enough that the difference is only in how they live.

They don't claim to be the builders' children. If you could ask them, and you mostly can't, they would neither deny it nor care. The builders left no tools, no hearths, no metal, no writing. These people keep none of those things either. That might be inheritance, or it might be that the city teaches the same lessons to anyone who stays long enough. Fire in the inner vaults would fill the halls with smoke and empty the colonies, and the colonies are the warmth. Metal rusts in the damp in a generation. Writing needs light. The city has been shaving its inhabitants down to the same few habits for a very long time.

There are not many of them, perhaps a few hundred spread across the whole face of the mountain. No single one of them has met all the others, and no one has ever gathered them in one place. They are thin in the way people are thin who eat mostly fruit and insects and the bitter hearts of palm seedlings. They go barefoot always. The soles of their feet are thick as bark and more sensitive than a stranger's hands. They can read a stair's pitch, a gutter's dampness, and the warmth of the stone that tells them how far inward they have come, all without breaking stride in total dark.

Their eyes are ordinary. It's their attention that is not. They don't look. They lean. A person of the galleries entering a room tilts slightly forward, palms open, face raised to feel the air on the skin. They take in the warmth that means bats, the wet heat that means oilbirds, the stir of air that means an opening. When you watch them in the green twilight of the outer terraces, they seem to be moving through a world slightly to the side of the one you see.

Most of them are deaf, or nearly, by the time they are grown. It doesn't much matter to them, and in any case they never counted it as a loss.

## How They Speak

They have two languages, and a third that comes only at night.

The first is the stone language, the knocking. It is slow and sparse, and it travels astonishingly far. A knock on a load-bearing block can be felt two galleries up and a dozen rooms inward. They use it for the things that need to cross distance: *here. come. a stone has fallen. the east cistern is low. someone has died.* It has no word for most things, because stone is not for most things. Children learn it before they walk, lying with their cheeks against the floor.

The second is the close language, spoken hand on body. It is how anything tender or complicated gets said. You place your palm flat on the other's sternum, where the roar is felt most. Against that background vibration, the pressure and movement of fingers, a press of the heel of the hand, a slow drawn line, become speech. It is intimate the way whispering is intimate, and it is the only speech that happens inside the colonies. Two people of the galleries talking in an oilbird hall look like two people standing very still, touching each other's chests, their faces inches apart in the dark. They might be discussing whether the figs on the fifth terrace are ripe. They might be quarreling.

The third language is their voices.

## The Hour When the City Is Theirs

At dusk the tenants leave. First the swiftlets go, then the bats in their smoking rivers, then the oilbirds, screaming as they go. The inner city goes into its quietest hours, which are not very quiet. There is the chirr of insects and the drip. There is the thin keening of oilbird chicks left on their ledges, fat and waiting.

It is enough, though. In those hours the halls are open enough for a voice to carry, and the people of the galleries speak aloud.

They don't do it much. Their spoken tongue is small, and it's used mostly for naming. The names of places are spoken, never knocked. So are the names of the dead, the names of the children, and the names of particular trees. Children learn to speak aloud only at night, and only in the emptied halls, and some of them grow up thinking of the voice as the thing you do in the dark when the animals are gone, a thing too loud and too personal for any other hour.

And they sing. Their songs are not complex. They are long held tones, mostly, sung into the vaults, because the vaults answer. The builders made rooms as high as grain silos, and a human voice in one of them, with the colony gone, rings back for a long breath after it stops. People will climb for hours through the dark to stand in a particular hall they know gives back a particular note. They sing to it, and it sings back, and before dawn the rushing starts at the openings and they go quiet and put their hands back on the walls.

No one who has lived in the valley below has ever heard them. The sound goes into the mountain, and the mountain keeps it.

## What They Do

They keep the drains.

It is almost their only work, and they do it as a matter of course, like breathing. Every day a person of the galleries walks a length of stair, feeling the gutters with their feet and the floor-slots with their fingers. They pull out the leaf, the lodged seed, the plug of guano. They follow the water inward to see where it goes. They keep some cisterns alive, not all of them and not even most. They keep the ones that feed the frogs in the dry months, and the ones they drink from, and the few that have been kept for so long that no one would think of letting them go. The city does not choose which of its cisterns survive. They do, in a quiet, generational way.

They take the pale seedlings too, sometimes. They'll gather the white lawn in the oilbird halls and eat the tender ones. Sometimes a person carries an armful of sprouts outward, three rooms, four, into the green twilight. There they press the sprouts into the soil at the edge of a terrace where a block has fallen and a gap has opened. Most die anyway. A few catch the light they were leaning toward and turn green. Some of the palms on the outer terraces were born in the dark, carried out by hand, and are now two hundred years old.

When a stone falls, and it happens maybe once in a lifetime, someone knocks the news through the walls. People come down the stairs to where it landed. They cannot lift it back. It is too heavy, and they have no tools. So they stand around it with their hands on it for a while. Then they clear the gutter it broke and route the water around the gap, and they leave the rest to the fig.

The description says nothing here is maintained. That is almost true. What they do is so light and so slow that from outside it is indistinguishable from nothing. Their work is the thumb resting lightly on the scale that keeps the balance "rough" instead of lost.

## What They Don't Do

They don't render the chicks for oil. Lamps would be useful, but every family carries some version of the same unspoken account. Other people came up the mountain once with nets and pots and fire. The halls went silent for a season, then two. In the silence the warmth drained out of the stone, the seedlings stopped, the roaches thinned, and the people of the galleries nearly froze in the dark. They learned what the city is: the animals are the heat, the heat is the life, and the life is the only light anyone needs. The chicks are left on their ledges, and the ones that fall are eaten, because a fallen chick is a gift and not a harvest.

They don't go out at dusk with the animals. Dusk is the city's breath going out, and they are the ones who stay behind to keep the house.

They don't leave. A few have, over the centuries. They went down to the valley and found it too quiet and too bright, full of people shouting at each other across open air. Some came back. The ones who came back are said to have lain down on the first stair of the lowest terrace and pressed both palms and one cheek to the stone, and stayed there for a long time.

## Where They Go

In the floors of the old halls, the guano lies deeper than a person is tall, and it keeps the record. Seeds are in it, and wings, beetle shells, the fine bones of animals that died and were not eaten.

Some of those bones are theirs.

When one of the people of the galleries dies, the others knock it through the stone, two, one, a long pause, two. It passes from wall to wall until it has crossed the whole face of the mountain. Then, that night, when the colonies have gone out, they carry the body inward to one of the oldest halls. They lay it on the floor under the roosts, among the pale seedlings, and they sing one tone into the vault. The hall gives it back.

By morning the colony has returned. By the end of the season the body is under the fresh layer, and the fresh layer is seething. In a few years it is part of the dark mineral stratum, almost soil, almost rock. The forest seeds brought in from the valleys sprout above it, lean toward nothing, fall, and become floor.

This is how the people of the galleries understand themselves, if they understand themselves as anything. They are not the city's owners, and not its builders, and not even its keepers exactly. They are one more thing it is made of. The birds brought spit and down, and the bats brought heat. The forest brought roots, and the valleys brought seeds, carried home in the guts of animals. They bring fingertips in the gutters, a knock on the stone, a voice in the emptied hall, and then their bodies, laid down in the dark and kept.

## The Last Thing

If you go in, you will not see them. You will not hear them. You might feel a knock under your palm, faint, from very far inward, and you won't know if it's for you.

Knock back. Two, a breath, one.

It is the only word of theirs a stranger can learn, and it is the one they use most. It is not really a greeting. It means something closer to *yes, still here, the water is still running.* Somewhere deep in the warm dark, a person with bark for soles and a palm pressed flat to the builders' stone will feel it arrive and understand that the city has, for the moment, one more tenant.
