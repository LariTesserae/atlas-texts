# Who Lives in the Wicker

## I. The Easy Answer

Start with the makers, because they are the ones who will look up when you arrive.

They are fewer than you would expect for a structure this size. You could walk a month and meet a dozen. They are hard to see even when they are close, because they have taken on the colors of the place. Their skin in the endless damp has gone faintly grey-green, like the underside of a leaf. Their hair, if they keep any, is matted into loose ropes that bead with mist. They wear almost nothing: a wrap of bast-cloth, a sling of cord across the chest to hold a spindle and a needle, and sometimes a hood of woven fern-fiber that sheds water like a roof.

Their hands are the first thing you notice and the last thing you forget. The fingertips are worn smooth. The whorls that once marked them are gone, rubbed away by ten thousand passes of twine. The pads have thickened into something between callus and bark. Across the top of each thigh runs a shallow, polished groove where cord has been rolled tight against the skin so many times that the body has made a place for it. Watch them long enough and you will see that their hands are never fully still. Even at rest the fingers move a little, over and under, rehearsing.

They do not eat much. They drink from bladder-vines, tipping the pale sacs to their mouths like wineskins. Sometimes they press their lips to a hanging flower and take a little of the nectar the driftmoths live on. They chew the tender tips of certain ferns. They seem to take most of what they need from the air itself: from the wet warmth, the mist that settles on them, the slow green light. When you ask whether they are hungry they consider the question as if it were about someone else.

They sleep in slings. Each night, or whatever the dim amber trough of the breathing counts as night, a maker ties a hammock between two stems and lies in it above the gulf. In the morning some untie it and carry the cord onward. Others leave it where it is. The ones who leave it will tell you, if pressed, that they are sleeping in slings they have not tied yet, in places they have not reached. Somewhere ahead, a hammock already hangs, and has for years, waiting for them to arrive tired and climb in.

They have no children. No one has ever been born in the Wicker. Everyone arrives. And, as the makers will gently insist, everyone who arrives was already here.

## II. Going Into the Lay

The makers do age, but slowly, and not toward death exactly.

An old maker moves less and less. The pauses between knots lengthen. They spend longer sitting in the moss with their hands resting on a lashing, not tying it, only holding it. Their skin darkens toward the grey-green of the strands. Their joints stiffen, and the stiffness feels less like decline than like setting, the way a green withy, bent and left, begins to keep its shape.

Then one day they lie down along a strand, in the long groove between two master-stems, and they do not get up. The others do not grieve in any way you would recognize. They come and sit nearby. They tie a few lashings over the old maker's body, loosely, carefully, finishing each with the small decorative tuck that has no structural purpose. Then they leave.

Over years the bark closes. The body becomes a raised seam along the strand and then a slight swelling in its lay, and then nothing distinguishable at all except that the strand there is a little thicker, a little warmer, and the knots nearby feel especially patient under the hand. The makers call this *going into the lay*. They say it is how the master-strands grew so thick: forty or fifty living stems twisted together, and in among them, uncountable, the long bodies of everyone who ever tied them.

So when you walk a strand you are walking on the makers too. That warmth in the bark, like the flank of a sleeping animal, is not only a figure of speech.

## III. The Company

That is the first thing that lives in the Wicker without a body. There are others.

Knots remember. Every lashing keeps a sliver of the moment it was tied: the pressure, the patience, the tune hummed under the breath, the particular ache in the fingers. A single knot holds only a flicker. In the great knot-masses, where thousands upon thousands of lashings have fused into swollen ridges of wood full of gullies and seep-water and blue fungal light, the flickers are packed so densely that they begin to overlap. Then they begin to cohere.

The makers call it *the Company*. It is not a ghost, or even many ghosts. It is closer to the feeling of a workshop late at night where many people are bent over their tasks in silence, none of them speaking, all of them aware of one another, the room itself thick with shared attention. Walk into an old knot-mass and you walk into that room. The skin on your arms prickles. Your breathing slows to match something. You find yourself wanting to be careful.

In the oldest knots the Company is dense enough to notice you. There is no voice, no presence you could point at, only a subtle change in the air, a turning-toward. If you sit and try to tie something there, you will find that your hands are corrected. It happens so slightly that you could never prove it. A loop you meant to pull too tight goes gentler. A tuck you were about to forget, you remember. The knot comes out better than you know how to make it.

The makers bring their hard work here: the splices that keep failing, the grafts that will not take, the places where two regions of weave must be married together across some difficult angle. They do not pray to the Company. They work beside it, the way an apprentice works beside a master who has gone quiet with age. When they leave, they are rested in a way that sleep does not rest them. *It helps us remember what we're doing,* they say. What they mean, you begin to suspect, is that the Company *is* the remembering. It is the whole accumulated intention of the Wicker, lodged in the wood, still concentrating.

## IV. The Catch

Nothing falls out of the Wicker. Something must do the catching.

Below the lowest strands you can see, below the haze, the weave does not stop. It changes gauge. It becomes finer and finer until it is gossamer, then something finer than gossamer: a mesh of threads so thin and so wet that they are invisible except as a faint silvering of the mist when the breathing brightens. The makers call this the *undermesh*, and they do not go there. They say it is not made for walking. It is made for holding.

Something lives in it. Those who have fallen and been found describe it the same way, in the same dazed, halting words. They fell for a long time, far longer than any fall should last, through dark warm air that grew thicker until it was almost like water. Then they felt hands. Not two hands. Many, soft and cool and countless, like being lifted by a crowd at a festival, passed from palm to palm overhead. They were not held still. They were carried.

This is the part that matters. Distance follows the weave, not the air, and even falling cannot cheat that rule. The Catch does not simply stop you. It takes you the long way. It passes you hand to hand along the made paths of the undermesh, over and under, strand by invisible strand, for days, until it sets you down on some strand far from where you leapt, on exactly the route you would have had to walk. That is why the fallen are dazed: they have traveled a week's journey in the dark, in the arms of something they never saw.

No one knows what the Catch is. Some makers think it is an animal, pale and diffuse, spread thin through the whole bottom of the world like a jellyfish that never finishes. Others think it is simply the Wicker's reflex, the way an eyelid closes. The oldest makers say it is everyone who went into the lay, that the bodies in the strands send their patience downward like roots, and that the hands which catch you are the same hands that tied the strand you fell from. *We don't let things go,* one of them told a traveler. *We never learned how.*

## V. The Other Hands

And yet something has to let go, or the whole thing would seize.

A basket that cannot flex will crack. The Wicker grows constantly: epiphytes on epiphytes, lashings swollen into bark, knots fused into knot-masses. Left to itself it would clot into something rigid and brittle, a continent of dead wood waiting for one hard creak to split it. It does not, because of the pickers.

Pickers are small, about the size of a sleeping cat, and so thickly furred in grey lichen that they look like a knob of old growth until they move. They have long faces, soft dark eyes built for the dim, and hands that are the strangest in the Wicker: fingers so long and fine they seem to be made of cord themselves, each ending in a split tip like a pair of tweezers. With these they unpick.

They take apart what is fraying, what is rotting, what has pulled too tight. They tease out lashings that have lost their purpose and loosen grafts that are strangling one stem for another. They thin the moss where it has grown so heavy it bows the strands. They work as slowly and carefully as the makers, and in almost exactly the same posture: hunched in a knot-hollow, head bent, fingers moving. From a distance you cannot tell the two apart. Up close you see that the picker's hands move in reverse, under and over, loosening.

The makers do not drive them off. They leave them offerings: short lengths of cord tied loosely so that they are easy to undo, like toys left for a child. The pickers take the cords apart with obvious satisfaction and wind the loosened fiber into their nests, which are shaggy, formless, unwoven things, the only unwoven things in the Wicker. The makers call them *the other hands*, and speak of them with a respect that is almost tenderness.

There is a story, told only in the old knot-masses, that the pickers are makers too. They are makers going the other way through time, untying knots that have not yet been tied, so that there will be room for them. If the Wicker is being made from both ends at once, the story goes, then someone must be working backward, and to anyone moving forward the backward work would look like undoing. The makers tell this story without deciding whether it is true. When a picker pauses nearby and looks at you with its dark, attentive eyes, it is hard not to feel you are being recognized by someone who knows you from later.

## VI. Where the Breath Is Kept

The hollow-whales are harder to speak of, because no one has seen one whole.

What little is known comes from glimpses: a pale flank the size of a hillside sliding through the rain of the biggest hollows. And the flank is not smooth. Those who have seen it close enough, in the brightest part of the breathing, say that it is textured, finely patterned, over-under. It is woven like everything else, but so loosely and so vastly that it is mostly gap, a basket the size of a mountain holding almost nothing but warm air.

That may be the answer to the makers' phrase, *where the Wicker keeps its breath*. The hollow-whales may be the Wicker's own slack, given a body: every place where the weave needs to stay open, loose and yielding, gathered into great drifting shapes that swim through the hollows and hold them from collapse. Where a hollow-whale passes, the air afterward feels roomier. The rain falls more softly. The strands at the hollow's rim ease a little, and the long low creak of the structure shifts pitch, as if something has sighed.

They eat driftmoths, or seem to. Whole spiraling pillars of moths vanish when a whale moves through the thermal column. But the makers say the moths are not eaten. They say the moths are *breathed in* and carried, and released again in some far hollow, so that the rising columns of the whole Wicker are one column, broken into pieces and passed along. Like the fallen in the arms of the Catch. Like water rising as mist. Nothing is ever lost. It is only taken the long way.

## VII. The One Gesture

Look at them all together now, and something begins to show through.

The lasher wraps a stem exactly as a cord would, and holds so faithfully that after enough years it becomes a lashing. The weaverbird builds its nest in the over-under of the master-strands, perfectly scaled down. The maker ties. The picker unties. The Company corrects your hands in the dark of the old knots. The Catch passes you palm to palm along the made way. The hollow-whale holds the open weave open. Even the cratch, grazing the moss in slow waves, leave behind furrows as regular as rows of stitching.

They are not many inhabitants. They are one inhabitant in many bodies, and the inhabitant is a gesture: *over, under, pull*. Every creature in the Wicker is a way that gesture has found to keep happening. The makers know this, though they rarely say it plainly. When they do speak of it they have no name for it, only a word from their work, the one they use for the moment in a knot when the loop is formed but not yet tightened, when everything depends on how much pressure comes next.

They call it *the pull*.

The pull lives in the space between over and under. It is not in the strands and not in the gaps but in the tension that makes them hold each other. It is what keeps a continent of living wood hanging in the air above nothing. It passes through every hand that ties and every hand that unties, through the lasher's coil and the Catch's carrying and the slow sigh of the whales. When a maker lies down and goes into the lay, the pull does not leave them. It only stops needing them to move.

If you ask a maker who lives in the Wicker, this is what they are trying to tell you when they look at you with that mild, puzzled patience. The question assumes a place and then someone in it. The Wicker is not like that. It is inhabited the way a hand is inhabited by gripping. The living and the making are one motion, and the motion is the dweller.

## VIII. And You

Which brings it, at last, to the knots you find with your own tuck in them.

Walk far enough and you will come on one, old and grey and half-swallowed by bark, with your over-tightened second loop and your habit of finishing too quickly. It will be holding. It has been holding for longer than you have been alive. And you will understand, standing there in the drip and the green-gold dimness, that the makers were not speaking in riddles. You live here. You always have. The pull passed through your hands long before you arrived, in places you have not yet reached, and some part of the Wicker's weight has been resting on your work the whole time.

This is why nobody is born here. Nobody needs to be. You do not come into the Wicker as a stranger enters a country. You come into it the way a hand closes on a cord it has been reaching for, and finds the cord was already warm.

The makers will give you a needle, eventually, if you stay. They will not explain it as a welcome. They will simply hold out a bundle of bast-twine, and you will take it, and your fingers will know more than you do. You will tie badly at first. They will nod and say it will hold, and they will be right, because you have already seen it holding.

Somewhere ahead, in a hollow where the rain falls from one place, a sling you have not yet tied is hanging, waiting for you to climb into it tired. Somewhere behind, in the arms of the Catch, a picker with dark attentive eyes is loosening a knot to make room for yours. And in the oldest knot-masses, in the blue light of the gullies, the Company is bent over its work in companionable silence, and among all those concentrating hands, if you listened closely enough, you would feel a pair you recognize.

The whole world creaks, very softly, settling a fraction deeper into its weave. It is the sound of something heavy being set down in a basket that was made to carry it. It is the only answer the Wicker gives to the question of who lives there, and it is always the same.

*We do. We are the holding. Pull tight.*
