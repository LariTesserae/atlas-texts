# The Hands

## First Guesses

At first you will think nothing lives here, because nothing moves the way living things move.

Everything moves the way machines move. The hammers fall in fours. The belts whine. The trams scream along their roots. The fog lifts at dawn and the drizzle falls near the halls, and all of it has the rhythm of routine. A place like this seems to have no room for anything that chooses.

Then you notice the crows, and you think *there, those are the ones*. Soon you see that the crows are only visitors who have stayed a long time. You notice the moths at the junction boxes and think *those, then*. You notice the husks in their overalls, always walking away, and you think *those, surely*. But the husks are empty. That is the one certain thing about them.

Keep looking. The place is inhabited. It has been from the beginning. The inhabitants are not hiding. They are simply spread across too many shapes and too many years for you to see them all at once. You meet them in pieces: a ribbon in dark water, a creak under the moss, a grey wing at an arc, a shell in a doorway. Only later, usually at night with your ear to the root-mat, do the pieces join into a single life.

The old word for workers in a mill was *hands*. That word will do for them. It fits better than you would like.

## In the Quench-Tanks

Begin with the water, because they do.

The quench-tanks beside the forges were built to take red iron and make it hard. They are still full, and the water is still black and very cold. The long pale ribbons you see in them are the young.

A ribbon is no wider than two fingers, but it may be several metres long, coiled on itself in the dark like a dropped measuring tape. It is soft and translucent, the colour of a leek's heart, and it has no front or back that you could name. Along its length run faint crosswise bars, spaced with perfect regularity, like the graduations on a rule.

When the hammer in the neighbouring hall falls, the ribbon pulses. It does not flinch, the way a thing flinches at noise. It pulses the way a throat moves when it sings along. A contraction runs down its length, four times, then it holds. Then four more.

This is how they learn. Each hall has its own tempo, and every tank has its hall. The ribbons in the tank beside the east forge will pulse to that forge's measure all their lives, and no other. If you lift one out, which you should not, it will go on pulsing in your hands for a long minute, keeping time with a hammer it can no longer hear, until it slows, and stumbles, and lies still. Put it back quickly and it will usually recover. It will not always come back on the beat.

They grow slowly. Something in the water feeds them, perhaps the tank itself, which is lined with a soft green fur. They grow through the long seasons of the molting chimneys and the falling roof-leaves. As they grow, the bars along their bodies grow closer together. The rhythm is written into them more densely. By the end, a ribbon is less a body than a record of a sound.

Then, one night, the tank is empty. The water is still there, still black and still cold, but the ribbons are gone.

## The Drain

Every quench-tank has a drain. The iron city built it to empty the tanks for cleaning. The green city kept it, as it kept everything. The drain is a knot now, a tight whorl of root at the bottom of the tank. It opens only from the inside, and only downward.

The ribbons go down through it into the root-mat.

## Undershift

You have been told that beneath your feet are the buried roofs of earlier Works. You have been told that if you lie with your ear to the mat you can hear them faintly, still running. It is true, and it is not a ghost. The earlier Works are running because they are staffed.

Down there, in halls that went under before the halls above were grown, the ribbons become something else. Space is close in the buried Works. The corridors are the width of a man's shoulders. The ladders have rungs a man's stride apart, and the doors are a man's height. The plant has always taken the shape of whatever it grew into, so the ribbons, growing slowly through those corridors and up those ladders and around those doors, take the shape of the space. They fold. They thicken at the joints where the corridors turn. They put out long branching fans of fibre that grip the rungs, and those fans become fingers because rungs are made for fingers. Over years they stand up, because the doors are tall.

The hands of the Undershift are pale and root-white, many-jointed, and damp. They have no eyes, because it is dark down there, and there has never been a need. They have no mouths that anyone has found, and there has never been a need for those either. They have everything a worker needs to work: grip, reach, patience, and a rhythm written down the length of the spine in close pale bars.

They tend the buried engines. They feed the fireboxes, though what they feed them with and what burns there is unknown. It is not coal, and it may not be anything. They grease the bearings with their own sap. They lean their weight on levers that were cast in iron before the world was green and have been wood for longer than they were ever iron.

This is where the accounts go wrong.

The water in the turbine race is too little. The ferns turn too slowly. The vines carry more than they should. The missing current rises up through the root-mat from below, conducted along the old mineral seams that the roots replaced. It comes from buried engines driven by hands that nobody counted, because nobody knew they were there to be counted. The power of the High Table is mostly labour. It has always been mostly labour. Only the labour went underground, and the ledgers went on recording turbines.

You can feel it if you press your palm to one of the granite knuckles. It is warm, and something inside is working. That warmth is theirs.

## Coming Up

Nobody knows how long a Hand spends in the Undershift. The beams hint at it. Cut one and read the rings, which are street plans rather than rings, and here and there on a plan you find a tiny notch, a single pale fleck, in the doorway of a hall. The flecks are placed like pins on a map, and in each ring they are in different doorways. In some of the oldest beams, if you count, the flecks recur in the same doorway every few hundred rings. In some they recur at intervals that do not repeat at all.

Whatever the interval, a Hand comes up eventually. It comes up at the whistle.

The shift-whistle is not blown by anything above ground. The chimneys are hollow trunks, and they go down, far below the halls, into the buried Works. At the change of shift, every Hand in the Undershift exhales at once, and that breath goes up every chimney on the Table. The chord is so low that the moss flattens outward in rings and the seed-drift is blown into clear bubbles. It is the loudest sound on the plateau, and it is breath. It is the collective sigh at the end of a very long day.

In the minutes after the whistle, while the air is still clear of drift and the crows are scattered, a few of them rise.

They come up through the root-mat at the base of the halls, where the ground has crept up the walls and buried the old doorways under slopes of moss. The moss parts like a curtain. A white hand comes out, then an arm, and then the whole Hand, stooped and slick and blind, dragging itself out onto the slope in the grey daylight it has never known.

This is when the clocktower matters. Its sunflower face does not track the sun in the sky. It tracks the sun that the Hands need. When the whistle blows, the clock jumps, and every shadow on the Table swings round at once like a compass needle. Look where they fall. They fall across the buried doorways. They fall across the slopes where the moss is parting. A pale thing surfacing from the dark would scorch in open light on the High Table, where the air is thin and the sun is fierce, so the clock gives it shade to come up in. The whole city turns its shadow to cover a birth.

The crows understand this too. They walk the rails in pairs and pick at the sprouting sleepers, but they are not picking at the sleepers. Under the rails, where the root-mat is compacted and thin, Hands sometimes come up in the wrong place and at the wrong time, out of shadow. The crows' call is the scream of the trams. Whether they learned it or the trams learned it from them, it works the same way. A surfacing Hand hears a tram coming and freezes, as any worker would on a line, and the crows eat it.

## The Shell

Once it is out, a Hand climbs.

It climbs the way it climbed the ladders below, rung over rung, fingers finding holds in knot and seam. It goes up to a gantry, or stands in a doorway, or walks out onto a rail. It chooses a place where a worker would stand. Then it stands there and begins to harden.

Its surface thickens and darkens into bark. It takes the texture of heavy cloth, the seams of overalls, the peak of a cap. Nobody knows why a plant grown in blind darkness should know what overalls look like, except that everything on the Table remembers what it replaced. Perhaps the shape comes up through the roots with the current, or perhaps it is in the rings. Every Hand hardens in the shape of a worker. Some wear aprons. One on the north gantry of the turbine house has a pencil behind its ear, grown in bark as fine as a whisker.

For a while, days or weeks, the hardened Hand works. It does not do much. It stands its post, and it walks its route, and it opens the loading doors when the crates come out. The loading doors, you will remember, open *sometimes*. This is why. The city's surface work is done by Hands in their last days, in their shells, before they split.

Then one morning the shell is hollow.

The split runs down the back, between the shoulder blades, as a cicada's does. The green lining inside the husk is wet for an hour and then dries. Whatever was in it is gone. But the shell goes on. The plant remembers the route, and the husk has been walking it for weeks, so it goes on walking it, emptier than anything else on the Table, patient and habitual, toward a shift that has already ended. This is why you only ever see them walking away. They are heading to work. They have been heading to work for a very long time.

Some of them reach the rim, and you will see them there, standing at the end of the rails where the crates are tipped. They look down.
