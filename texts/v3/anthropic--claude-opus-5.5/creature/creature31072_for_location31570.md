# The One Who Keeps the Condensary

## The First Answer, Which Is Wrong

The first answer is *no one*. You walk the terraces, count the moths, and find no doors, no bunks, no cups left on a ledge. No one lives here, you decide. It is a mechanism, and mechanisms don't need tenants.

The second answer comes on the second night, when you hear the trowel. So there *is* someone: a mason who works in the fog, who lays a course of brick above the rim each night and is gone by the time the white thins. You picture a figure, tall and patient, with grey hands, working by feel because it has never needed to see. You picture it coming up from somewhere below and going back down before dawn, an employee of the place, a keeper.

This answer is closer, but it is still wrong, and wrong in an interesting way. The mistake is imagining that the mason comes *to* the Condensary. It does not come to it. It never leaves.

---

## The Sound, Listened To Properly

Go back to the night sounds, and this time don't assume what they are.

The scrape is not quite a trowel. It is too long and too even, one continuous stroke that curves the whole way around the rim, like someone drawing a fingertip around the lip of a glass. Afterward there is a softer sound, a wet settling, the sound of something heavy and damp being pressed down and smoothed. The knock of iron on iron comes last, and it isn't the knock of a hammer. It is the click of something cooling into shape, the small report metal makes when it contracts.

None of these sounds moves. A mason would walk the rim, and you would hear footsteps, or the sound would travel. These sounds happen *everywhere along the rim at once*, faintly, the way a tide arrives along a whole beach at once.

Whatever lays the brick is as wide as the building.

## Down Through the Grates

By the fourth day you have spent a long time lying on your stomach on the central floor, face to an iron grate, the heat pressing on your eyelids. With patience, and with the eighty-second rhythm to wait through, you begin to see.

The red dimness isn't a furnace, or not only a furnace. It has texture. It is wet, and it glistens. In the glow there is a surface, and it is a skin, not a floor or a wall: dark, ridged, slick, fading into shadow in every direction. When the breath comes, the thud you feel in your sternum, that skin *lifts*. The whole visible surface rises by a hand's breadth, holds, and settles. The vapour comes up past your face in its soft exhalation. It smells of iron and wet stone and something faintly green, like crushed stems.

The thing you took for a piston is the rhythm of a body. The thing you took for a wheel, turning slowly in the dark, is a long muscular wave travelling across that skin. It rolls from one side to the other, over and over, the way a wave passes down the underside of a snail as it moves across glass.

It is under the whole building. It is the reason there is a building.

## The Shape It Takes

Here is the resident of the Condensary, as nearly as you can piece it together.

It is a single animal, and it is enormous by any standard you brought with you, though modest compared to what it is building. Its body sits coiled in the base of the drum, beneath the grates, and it fills the space entirely. The heat is its own. It is a warm creature, warmer than any snail has a right to be, and it burns something inside itself the way a hearth burns coal. That is what the spent coke in the manufactured earth is: its ash.

Beneath it, going down into the cloud, is the pier. You took the pier for a foundation, and in a sense it is. It is also the creature's foot: one column of muscle, grey and cold at the surface, descending further than anyone has followed it. The foot stands on whatever is down there, and it holds. That long rolling wave you saw through the grates is the foot at work, gripping, shifting, lifting the whole arrangement a fraction at a time.

The drum is its shell.

The shell doesn't coil the way you'd expect. The creature has turned its spiral outward and upward, like the mushroom-gill fins turned inside out, so that its shell became a tower, open at the top. Still, it is built the way every shell is built: *at the lip*. A snail lays down new shell only at the edge of its opening, a thin line of fresh material every day, so the oldest part of a shell is at the centre and the newest at the mouth. The Condensary works exactly this way. The newest course is at the rim, and the rim is the aperture.

At night, when the fog is thickest, the creature extends its mantle upward through the drum. The mantle is the thin, living edge of flesh that makes the shell. It rises past the terraces, pale as the cloud itself and nearly as thin, and rests against the rim. There it does what mantles do. It secretes. The wet settling sound is fresh mortar being laid down from its underside. The long curved scrape is its rasping tongue, the radula, drawn around the lip to smooth the new course flat. The brick is its bone. The cast iron is harder work, pressed out between two folds of mantle and left to cool. That is why every fin carries a casting seam: a thin fin of metal where the two halves of the mould met, because the two halves of the mould are two lips of the same flesh.

You can't see it because it is the colour of fog. On the nights you stayed up, you may have been looking straight at it.

## Why Nothing Is Old

This is the part that took you longest, and it is simple once you see it.

Some snails dissolve their own shells from the inside. They take the old inner whorls apart, the parts they no longer live in, and reuse the minerals to build at the lip. The keeper of the Condensary does this constantly. At the bottom, where the oldest plants finally crack a brick or two, the crumbled stuff falls through the grates, and the creature takes it in. It eats its old terraces. It eats the fallen fronds and the twisted trunks of the lowest trees, the manufactured earth that has gone slack, the moss that has lost its colour. It burns what will burn and grinds up the rest. By night it lays everything back down at the top, still wet, as new brick.

So no part of the building is ever older than the time it takes material to travel from the rim to the grates. The building is always fresh. The *animal* is not.

The fins keep that record. Each new fin is stamped at its base with the circle and the three lines, and with a number that climbs by one each night. The building has no memory, because the building is constantly eaten. The animal remembers. It counts its nights in iron and puts the count on its own growing edge. The top fin read 4,217 when you were there. That is more than eleven years of nights, in a structure where nothing is a year old.

The circle with three lines may be a signature. You spent a while deciding what it depicts, and your best guess is the creature itself as seen from above: a round shell with the rain falling in three lines down through it.

---

## The Garden

The keeper doesn't eat the garden so much as keep it, the way a person keeps a hearth or a well.

The seeds are in the mortar because the creature puts them there. It carries them somewhere inside itself and lays them into each new course, so that its shell is planted as it grows. This isn't decoration. At this height the air outside is cold and would dry a soft body to leather in an afternoon. The garden holds the wet. Every frond and cupped plate-leaf, every skin of moss on the ironwork, is a way of keeping moisture near. The ferns drip, the walls weep, and the creature below lives in that wetness the way you live in air.

The garden is also its larder. Growth is fast at the top and slow at the bottom, and the plants age as the terraces carry them downward, which isn't really downward. It is the rim rising away from them. By the time a tree has twisted its trunk into wrung cloth and begun to split brick, it has reached the grates, and it is ready to be eaten.

And the drum itself, the warm hollow you stood in with your coat off and your sweat refusing to dry, is not an outer room. Think of where a snail breathes: through a single opening, into a cavity under its mantle that serves as its lung. The keeper opens once every eighty seconds, and the warm breath rises through the terraces and meets the cloud, and the cloud falls back as rain.

You were standing in its lung. The garden grows in its breath. Without meaning to, you started breathing with it, because for four days you were part of its breathing.

## The Others, and the Ones Not Yet Here

The translucent snails in the warm channels near the bottom now make sense. They are the slowest things in the Condensary and possibly the oldest, older than any brick. They are small enough to sit on your fingertip, and you can see the pulse inside their shells. The pulse is fast and even, and if you count it you will find it keeps time with the breath of the building, eighty seconds exactly.

They are its young. They were born below, in the warmth, before any of the present brickwork existed, and they graze the algae on the walls and wait. They are too small yet to lay brick. But every one of them carries, somewhere in its thin body, the start of a mantle and a count of nights.

This changes how the empty spaces read. You felt that the Condensary was sized for animals that hadn't come: dry pockets unoccupied, ledges made for something to perch on, the long, patient readiness of a house nobody has moved into. You assumed guests. The moths are guests, and so perhaps is the bird. They came up through the cloud and found warmth and stayed, or didn't. The creature seems to tolerate them the way a tree tolerates lichen.

But the spaces aren't for guests. They are shaped, if you look again, like room for something to grow into. The dry pockets under the plate-leaves are the size of a snail that has doubled, then doubled again. The ledges along the lower terraces are wide enough for a young keeper to rest on while its shell is still soft. The house isn't empty. The family is small.

## What It Wants

You never find out why it is climbing. You can only put together what an animal like this might want.

It began somewhere below the cloud. Perhaps it began on something someone else had made. The stencils suggest it: *C-14*, *C-15*, *LOAD NOT TO EXCEED*, in clean white paint on every girder. A creature that lays its own iron has no need of load ratings. But a creature that once, very small, climbed the side of some built thing, a pylon or a tower or a pier, and grazed its surfaces for years, might keep what it had read there. Young birds learn songs from whatever sings near the nest. This one may have learned the look of human building the same way, and it goes on writing the old words onto every girder without knowing what they mean. They are part of what a wall is, to it.

Or perhaps the words are its own and only happen to look like ours. You can't rule it out.

Either way, it climbs. Snails climb when the ground is too wet, or too dry, or when something in them turns toward light, and the keeper has been climbing toward the top of the cloud for 4,217 nights. What happens when the rim finally clears the white, and the garden sees the sun directly for the first time, and the breath rises into dry blue air with no cloud to meet it? You don't know. It may seal its aperture, the way snails seal themselves behind a membrane to wait out a drought, and rest. It may open wider. It may be that this is when the young are carried up to the rim, set on the first dry brick, and left to begin their own count at one.

---

## The Last Look

When you leave, you look back once from the path down through the cloud. The Condensary is hidden already, but you can see where it is by the small patch of rain, falling in a circle, in the middle of the white.

This time you look longer. Far off, at the very edge of what the light allows, there is another circle. It is smaller and fainter, a faint greying in the whiteness where water is falling inward.

Beyond that, you might be imagining a third.
