# Who Lives in the Held Country

## I. The Ones Already Named

Begin with the grazers, since they are easiest to find.

Seen from a distance, they look like stones that have decided to move. They are low and pale, and they cross the green so slowly that you have to look away and back again to be sure they have moved at all. Up close they are older than they seem. The fur on their backs has faded to the color of the sky at the horizon, almost white, and it is matted into plates the way old felt mats. Their eyes are large, dark and wet. On the whole plateau, a grazer's eye is the only place you will see water uncovered, and even so they keep them half-lidded against the thin, drying air.

They live a very long time. A grazer might cross the same cushion a hundred times over its life and never finish grazing it, because it takes only what the cushion breathes out. Their droppings are small dry pellets, hard as seeds, and they leave them in the hollows between rosettes. Where a pellet falls, the cushion grows a little faster, about the width of a fingernail in a lifetime. The cushions feed the grazers with breath, and the grazers feed the cushions with everything the breath is not. Nothing passes through either of them without being kept by the other.

The hunting bird is harder to know. It lives in the air, and in the Held Country the air belongs to the afternoon. It sleeps at night on the tops of the stone domes, the only high points, with its wings folded around it like a dark cloak and its silver belly pressed against warm rock. It eats perhaps once a season. When it takes a grazer, it takes an old one, and it eats slowly, over days, on the spot. Afterward the cushion there grows faster for a while.

The beetles and moths are small and exact. The beetles are black and glossy, and they drink by standing in the gaps of the rosettes at noon with their backs raised to the mist, letting it bead on their shells and run down to their mouths. The moths come out only at dusk. They are pale and soft, and their wings are too big for their bodies. They can fly only in the saturated air of the inward breath, when the water is sinking back into the green and the air is briefly thick with it. They fly for an hour a day. For the rest of the day they cling, folded, to the undersides of stones.

That is the list the country gives you if you ask it plainly: grazers, the bird, beetles, moths.

But look again at the tracks.

## II. The Lines That Do Not Rise

A grazer's path sinks into the cushion surface and rises again over hours. By evening it is gone.

Some lines do not rise.

They are very faint, too faint to see from above and too narrow for grazers. They run between the cushions instead of over them, along the old dry riverbeds, the softened troughs of gravel that once carried water to a forgotten sea. In those troughs the gravel has been walked into a firmer path, a single footstep wide. Where a path crosses bare rock, the rock has been worn in a way the wind cannot account for. It is a soft, repeated dip in the stone, polished by something that stopped there and stood.

Then the stones. The wind has faceted them and set each one in its hollow like an egg in a nest. But in some places the stones have been turned. They are still in their own hollows, but rotated, so that at a certain hour every shadow in a field of them points the same way. The pattern lasts for a few minutes each day and is invisible the rest of the time. It is a clock, or a calendar, or a sentence. It was made by someone who knew how the light falls here, and who didn't want to move anything any further than it had to go.

Someone lives here, then. Whoever it is has chosen to leave marks only when it is noon.

## III. The People

They are hard to see even when they are near you, because they are made to look like the country.

They are small and slow, wrapped from crown to heel in cloth felted from grazer wool, pale and matted and dust-colored. Standing still on a cushion's shaded side, a group of them looks like a cluster of old stones. You find them the way you find the grazers: by looking away and looking back.

Under the cloth, their faces are covered in fine silver down, the way the cushion leaves are. It starts at the brow and runs thickly over the cheeks, the upper lip and the bridge of the nose. In the hard light it scatters the sun, so their faces shimmer faintly, as if lit from inside. In shadow the down lies flat and you can see how dark their skin is beneath it. Their eyes sit deep under heavy brows, and they keep their lids narrow. They do not sweat. Their lips are thin and pale, and they keep their mouths closed.

They drink as the grazers do, from the noon breath. At the height of the exhale, they lower the felted veils over their faces. The wool is dense and the silver down is fine, so both catch the mist, and the people breathe slowly through the veil and take the water in through their mouths and noses. It is quiet, careful and communal. A whole family will sit together on a great cushion at noon, veiled, breathing, and not speak.

They do not speak at noon because at noon they are drinking.

## IV. The Grammar of Breath

In the Held Country, every exhalation spends water. A spoken word carries a little moisture out of the body and into the dry air, and the land does not return it until dusk. So the people speak carefully.

Their language changes with the day.

In the morning, while the air is still dry and the cushions are only beginning to open, they barely speak at all. What they say is short, unvoiced and whispered through nearly closed lips. It spends almost no breath and carries almost no meaning, only the essentials: *here*, *careful*, *mine*, *yours*. Morning speech is poor and practical, like the morning country.

In the afternoon, once they have drunk and the mist is thick enough that a spent breath is quickly replaced, they talk. This is when they argue, bargain, tell stories and sing. Afternoon speech is extravagant. It has long vowels and many words for the same thing, and sentences that turn back on themselves for pleasure. They sing in the updraft, facing the center of the continent, and the day wind carries the songs inward. Some of them believe the songs arrive, eventually, at the exact center of the plateau and gather there, the way the clouds do.

At dusk they have a third way of speaking, which is breathing in.

The faint sigh a traveler might hear at dusk, so quiet it might be imagined, is them. They face outward, toward the invisible rim where the evening wind spills down, and draw breath together, slowly and audibly, as the mist sinks back into the cushions. It is a way of saying something. The closest translation is *we are keeping this*, or *nothing is lost*, or simply *yes*. A child learns to speak this way before it learns words. The dying do it last.

After dark they are silent, and silence is the most respectful thing that can be said.

## V. How They Keep House

They do not build. In a country where stone takes an age to wear and a cushion takes an age to grow, nothing is worth breaking to make something else.

They sleep in the lee of the great cushions, pressed against the shaded flank, where the green is darkest and gives off a faint warmth at night like a sleeping body. The cushions hold heat as they hold water, deep and slowly, and release it after dark. A family sleeps in a line along a cushion's curve, facing it, with their breath going into the rosettes.

They carry very little: felt, a few tools of polished stone, cord twisted from grazer wool, and small bags of the grazers' dry pellets, which they carry from cushion to cushion and set in the hollows as gifts. They carry no vessels. It is shameful to hold water outside a living body. A jar, a bowl or a skin would mean water that belongs to no one and is merely stored, and they consider that close to theft.

If water is ever spilled, it must be given back at once. On the hottest days, when the clouds grow too tall and a few drops fall through the virga and land on bare stone, the people run to them, which is the only time they run. They kneel over the drop, say one word, and push it with a fingertip toward the nearest rosette, which is already turning toward it. The word is the same one used when someone dies.

## VI. The Amber

A cushion cut open would be solid almost all the way down: packed stems, old leaves and amber resin in layers like sediment, with only a thin living skin on top. The plant keeps what matters most there.

The people know this.

When one of them is dying, and the living have learned to recognize the moment, they help the dying one out onto the broad back of an old cushion at dusk, during the inward breath. The dying person lies down on the green, face up, veil open, and breathes in with the land one last time.

The cushion takes a generation to cover them. The rosettes creep in so slowly that no one alive sees it happen. There is no rot, because rot needs loose water and the cushion lets none go loose. The body dries, settles and is gathered in. The silver hairs close over it, and over centuries it sinks into the amber and becomes one more layer of the past the cushion keeps beneath its skin.

Every great cushion holds people. The oldest hold thousands, stacked in their resin through all the ages of the plateau, the most recent near the surface and the first ones deep in the core, so old that the amber around them has gone dark as tea.

So when the cushions breathe out at noon and the people lower their veils and drink the mist, they know exactly what they are drinking. The water in that mist has passed through their grandmothers, through the grazers their grandmothers ate, and through the bird that ate the grazers. It has passed through everyone who ever lay down on the green. They drink it gently, the way you would take a hand.

The people have a name for the ticking of the resin in the morning, the scattered dry crackle across the land as the cushions warm. They call it *the old ones turning over*.

## VII. The Question Turns

By now the question of who lives here has grown strange.

The grazers live here, but they are made of the cushions' breath. The people live here, but they are made of the grazers and the breath and each other, and they end up as cushion. The bird is made of grazers. The beetles are made of noon and the moths of dusk. The clouds are made of all of it, and so is the wind.

One thing has been in every one of them.

The water came up out of the dying land long ago and never left. It rises every morning and sinks every night. At noon it is a veil, at dusk a sigh, and in the afternoon a tower of cloud over a hundred kilometers of green. It spends a few decades in a person, a century in a grazer, and ten thousand years in the amber heart of a cushion, and then it rises again. In all the ages of the plateau it has never spilled or scattered, and never become loose water on the ground belonging to no one.

The water lives here. It has lived here longer than anything else, and it is the only thing that has lived everywhere here. Everything else in the Held Country is a room it passes through.

The people understand this better than a visitor would. They call the country by the water's name, and they call themselves by the water's name too. In their language it is the same word as *held*. When they speak of the water, they do not call it *it*. They use the form of address reserved for someone old, present and listening. When they breathe in together at dusk, they are not just saying *we are keeping this*. They are speaking to it, and some of them say it answers, in the resin, in the morning.

## VIII. What the Water Remembers

There is one more thing, and the people say it only in afternoon speech, and only in songs.

The water remembers the sea.

The plateau was not always high. Long ago it bordered an ocean, and the ocean has been forgotten by the stone, the gravel troughs and the wind. The people believe the water has not forgotten it. On the hottest days, when the clouds climb too high and their tops turn to ice in the thin blue-black air, and grey veils of virga curve down from the cloud bases, the people say the water is trying to go home. They say it has pulled itself up as high as it can reach and is leaning toward the rim, toward the haze too far below to see, where the old ocean lay.

It never gets there. The virga dries before it touches the stone, and the few drops that land are taken back into the green.

The people do not know whether this is cruelty or kindness, whether the cushions are keeping the water or the water is choosing to stay. They argue about it every afternoon, extravagantly, at great length and with a great many words, and the argument has never been settled, and they would not want it to be. The songs about it are the longest they have.

## IX. One of Them, at Dusk

A woman is standing on the back of an old cushion, the one her mother lies inside, near the surface still. The day wind has just turned. It is starting to slide past her, outward, toward the rim, carrying a little resin-scent and a little warmth down into the lowlands no one has ever seen.

The mist is sinking. It drifts over the rosettes at the level of her knees, then her ankles. The moths are up, pale and clumsy in the thick air. Somewhere a grazer's tongue moves across the green, and somewhere a stone clicks as it cools.

She lifts her veil. The silver down on her face catches the last light and shimmers.

With everyone else, across the whole width of the continent, she breathes in, audibly, slowly, as the land does. *Yes. We are keeping this.*

Then she does the one wasteful thing the people allow themselves. It is permitted once in a life, and never spoken of in the morning. She lets one tear go, just one, out of the corner of her narrowed eye. It runs down through the down on her cheek, falls, and lands on the bare stone at the cushion's edge, loose, belonging to no one, for as long as a heartbeat.

Then the rosettes turn, the silver hairs stand up, and it is taken in.

Tonight, in the dark, that drop will sink into the amber beside her mother. In the morning it will warm and tick. At noon it will rise with everything else into the bright milky veil over the green, and someone will lower their face into it and drink.

It has never left. It is the oldest thing in the country and the youngest, and the one that has lived in all of them. The water lives here, and for a while it lives in her.
