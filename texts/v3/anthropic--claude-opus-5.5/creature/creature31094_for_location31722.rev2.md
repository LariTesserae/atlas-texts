# Who Lives in the Held Country

## I. The first answer is wrong

The first answer is *no one*.

You could cross a thousand miles of the Held Country and never see them. You would see grazers moving along the pale paths with their bristled backs full of fern. You would see walking birds lift one careful foot and hold it, and you would hear the eels tick under your ear when you lay down. You would find a ring of stones, and a bent house with grazers breathing in its doorway, and a pebble on a leaf at the rim of an eye. You would decide that the makers were gone, the way the mountains are gone, and that what remains is their shape held up by something else.

That is almost true, and it is the thing they would most like you to believe. It is not that they hide. It is that they are very good at leaving no residue. In a country where everything left behind is taken up, the people learned long ago that to leave a thing is to give it away. So they give away very little, and what they do leave, they leave deliberately.

## II. What they look like, at a distance

Suppose you did see one. It would be evening, with the fog coming down, and you would see it the way you see anything in that light: a column among the columns that is moving slightly wrong.

They are tall and narrow, built for turning sideways between clubmoss towers. Their limbs are long and their joints are soft. They walk with a slight forward lean that never quite resolves, because they are always about to take the next step.

Their skin is the color of the sward's darker shades, bronze going to olive going to near-black, and it is not entirely theirs. Like the grazers, they tolerate things. A fine green down grows along their shoulders and the backs of their arms. It is a moss so small it is almost a stain, and it lives on the salt of their skin. Filmy ferns root in the hair of the old. They do not tend these growths, and they do not remove them. The small plants on a person say how long that person has been walking, the way a stone ring's burial says how long it has been since anyone came.

Their eyes are large and dark and wet, made for a world with no shadows. They see the lichen-glow well. They see full daylight, if daylight can be called that, a little too well, and they squint through the bright middle hours with their lids half-down, like someone listening.

Their palms and the soles of their feet are pale, paler than anything else in the country except the stones and the flowers at the eyes. Those palms made the prints on the lintels.

## III. The one thing they carry

Each of them, from the time they can walk, holds a stone.

It is not carried in a pouch, because pouches rot. It is not worn on a cord, because cords rot. It is held in the hand, closed in the fist or moved from palm to palm. It is warm from them and smooth from them, and it is always one of the pale pebbles the weave has pushed up from the old mountains.

It is hard to overstate how strange this is. In the Held Country everything is alive or about to be taken up into something alive. The stone is the only thing there that is not becoming anything. Hold it and you are holding the one piece of the world that is not hungry, and that the world cannot quite eat. The mat will swallow a stone, slowly, but it cannot digest one. Stone passes through the body of the land unchanged, the way a thought passes through a person.

So the stone in the hand is a small still place carried through a life of constant motion. When they sleep, they sleep holding it. When they speak of someone's character they speak of their stone. They do not mean its color or shape. They mean whether the person holds it loosely or tightly, and whether they pass it hand to hand when they are anxious. A child's first stone is found, never given, and finding it is a long patient matter of walking with your eyes on the sward and waiting for a pale point to break the moss.

I thought at first the stone was a charm. It is closer to a vow.

## IV. How they sleep, and why the houses

The rule of the country is that the mat grows over whatever does not move, and the people are not exempt.

They sleep in the ways the animals sleep, and in one way the animals don't. Mostly they sleep walking. Two of them will link arms, and one drifts down into sleep while the other steers. Their steps shorten and their heads drop, and they move along the pale paths at the pace of the grazers, often among the grazers, who do not mind them. Halfway through the night they trade. By dawn both have slept and both have walked, and neither has been still long enough to be claimed.

This is how most of them live most of their lives: half-asleep in each other's keeping, moving.

The bent houses are the exception. Inside a living dome, on the compressed floor, the mat is slower. It is not stopped, only slower, the way it is slower on stone. The dryness does something to it, and so does the pressure, and so does the fact that generations of bodies have lain there and risen again. A person can lie down in a bent house and sleep for a whole night without being walked. In the morning they wake with a fine furring of root-hair along the side they lay on, threads as delicate as spider silk that have already started to push into the skin. They brush it off. It doesn't hurt. It leaves the skin faintly pink.

That is what the houses are for. They are not shelter from weather, since there is almost no weather. They are shelter from being held. A bent house takes three or four generations to make, and it is not a building. It is a *permission*, a place where the land has been asked, very slowly and very politely, to let someone rest.

They return to them rarely. Some houses are visited only once a generation. That is still often enough to pull the tie a little tighter, to press a young hand into young scale, and to lie down on the floor where your grandmother lay and feel the first root-hairs reaching for you and let them, for a few hours.

## V. How they speak

It took me a long time to understand that the pale paths are writing.

Not all of them. The grazers make most of them, along with the walking birds and the random traffic of heavy feet over thousands of years. But among those, crossing and recrossing, some paths were walked on purpose. They are too even, too regular in their curves, and they turn at angles no grazer would choose.

A person here writes by walking. Where they walk, the moss grows back paler, and the paleness lasts for centuries. So if you want to say something that will outlive you, you say it with your feet across the living country, one careful line at a time. A single mark might take a day to make, and a sentence might take a season.

To read one, you walk it. You feel the change of the moss underfoot, the way the pale grows back finer than the dark, and you follow. The meaning comes as a shape in the body rather than a picture seen from above. Nobody here has ever seen the Held Country from above, and nobody ever will, because of the lid. Their writing is not meant to be looked at. It is meant to be retraced.

So the long letters of the Held Country are read in the same way they were written, step by step, by someone following in someone else's feet. The oldest ones have buckled with the slow tide. The land has swelled and sunk under them, and their lines are stretched and wrinkled, so that reading them is like reading a hand that shakes. The people don't mind. They say the country has added its own voice.

Their spoken language is closer to the grazers' hum than to anything else. It is low and breathy and carried in the chest. It is meant for the evening fog, when you can't see the person you're speaking to, and it is felt as much as heard. There is another way of speaking, used over longer distances: a palm pressed flat to the sward, and a hum pushed down into it. The sponge carries it the way it carries everything, slowly and a little way. Two people on the same swell, a mile apart, can talk this way if they're patient. It sounds, to them, like the land clearing its throat.

When an eye collapses, and the *whumph* comes through the mat from a hundred miles off, they stop whatever they are doing and put their palms down. It is the one voice louder than theirs. They listen to it as news, though of what, I cannot tell you. A place has let go of its shape. Something has fallen inward toward the old ground. The moss will close. That is the whole of the message, and it seems to be enough.

## VI. The rings

The rings are not graves. I thought they were, and I was wrong.

Nor are they houses, or markers of territory, or calendars, though they are loosely all of those. The rings are where the people practice.

Every thirty-some years the country flowers, and turns violet, and fills with the smell of wet pepper and honey. When it does, they come from everywhere and gather. They don't all gather at one ring. They go to the nearest one, each group to its own crest, so that across the whole continent in those three weeks, on the highest swells, there are dozens or hundreds of small crowds standing in circles of stone.

And they stand still.

This is the only time in their lives they choose it. They stand in the ring, inside the stones, barefoot on the violet, holding their own stones in their hands, and they do not move. They stand through the day and into the fog and the lichen-light. The fog-moths rise around them and land on their shoulders. The root-hair begins to find their feet. By the second night it has threaded their ankles. It isn't painful. It is a coolness, then a warmth, then a faint, sure pull, like being gently taken by the wrist. The land is saying *yes, you, too, you are also mine*.

They let it. For as long as they can bear, they let it. It is a matter of honor and also of tenderness, and of learning, because every one of them will someday be held for good, and they want to know how it feels before it is final.

Then at some signal, a hum passed through the soles, the whole ring pulls free together. The threads tear softly. Everyone's ankles are ringed pink for weeks afterward. They step out of the circle and go back to walking. The flowers close and the seed falls. The stones of that ring sink a little deeper, a little further under the sward, because for a few days they were surrounded by bodies that invited the mat in.

That is why some rings are almost gone and some look fresh. The gone ones are where people gathered most, and longest, and most often. The fresh ones are new, or forgotten, or waiting.

An animal born just after a flowering might see one more. A person born just after one might see two, or three. They count their ages that way. They do not count years. They count how many times they have stood in the ring and been held and come back out.

## VII. The eyes, and what they give them

When one of them dies, the body goes to the mat, and the stone goes to an eye.

The body is not carried anywhere special. That would be the opposite of the point. When a person is old and has decided, they choose a place along a path they love and they lie down. Usually someone they have slept-walked beside for years sits with them for the first night, a hand on their chest, humming. Then that person stands and walks on, because staying would mean being held too. Within a month there is a green shape the size of a person. Within a year there is a slight swell in the sward, flowering a little brighter than the moss around it. The people know where those swells are. They route their walking writing to pass over them, so that the pale line of a sentence runs across the bright patch like a thread through a bead.

But the stone, the thing that was held through the whole life, the one piece of the world that was not hungry, cannot go into the mat. The mat would take it eventually, but slowly, and indifferently, and it would come back up somewhere in a thousand years with no memory.

So they carry it to an eye and give it downward.

Sometimes it is set on a flat leaf at the rim, to be found and taken down by the slow rise and fall of the water. Sometimes it is pressed into the root-wall just above the waterline, pushed into the soft living weave with a thumb. The flowers at the eyes climb in rings as the country rises, and in time they grow up past the stone and the stone is below them, below the tide line, going down.

I understand it now like this. The eyes are the only places that open toward what is under the country. Down there, presumably, is the old ground, whatever the mountains stood on. The stones came from the mountains. The weave spent millennia pushing them up to the surface, and a person spent a lifetime holding one, and at the end they give it back. Not to the living land, which has everything already. To the deep. To whatever was here before, which gave up its shape so the green could have it.

*You were lifted,* they are telling the stone. *Now go home.*

Or perhaps it is the other way, and they are telling the deep that the height still remembers it.

## VIII. The edge

They do go to the edge, once.

The grazers' paths bend away from the rim, and the walking birds keep far from it. The people's paths bend away too, mostly, and I think now that is grammatical. In their writing, the rim is the end of a sentence. You can walk toward it, but you cannot write past it.

Once in each life, though, a person goes alone to the rim and stands at the stopping place. They stand in the hiss of the falls becoming spray and look down into the white, and they hold their stone out over nothing.

They do not drop it. That is the thing. That is the whole of the rite, as far as I can see: to stand at the place where the country ends and hold the heaviest, stillest, most precious thing you own over the gap, and not let go. And then to bring it back.

I believe it is a way of saying *I choose to be up here.* The country holds itself up by root and water and the habit of height. The people hold themselves up the same way, by habit and choice and a hand that does not open.

## IX. What else, then

Having followed them this far, I no longer think the people are the answer to the question *who lives here*. They are part of the answer. The answer keeps getting larger.

The grazers garden without knowing it. The people garden knowing it, which isn't very different. Where people have walked their long sentences, the moss grows back richer, as it does where the grazers go. The stem-forest is held open along their paths. The bent houses are the stem-forest bending, and they would not exist without people, but they are also simply clubmoss that learned a new shape and kept it. The fern in an old person's hair is the same fern that roots in a grazer's bristle and on the eaves of the houses. The pink ring at a person's ankle after the flowering is the land's first word to them. The bright swell in the sward is its last.

The land takes things up sideways and downward and sends their minerals back into leaves. A person who lies down becomes brighter moss that a grazer eats, and the grazer's feet press the path that a child will walk to learn to read. The eels tick in the weave like a clock slightly out of tune. The people, I think, are the part of the clock that notices.

So maybe it goes like this. What lives in the Held Country is the Held Country, a single body or many bodies that forgot the difference, spread across a continent and lifted into the cloud. And somewhere in that body, over a very long time, something grew that could hold a stone. Something grew that could carry one still thing through all that motion and set it down on purpose, that could remember being mountains. The mat had no way to do that on its own, since it can only swallow. It grew hands.

## X. The handprint

On the lintel of one bent house there is a handprint that has been stretched by the growing scale into something long and strange, fingers drawn out like the shadow of fingers at evening, except there are no shadows here.

A child pressed it there, into soft young growth, the first time her grandmother brought her. She was maybe four, maybe five. The tie was fresh grass-rope. The dome was barely closed.

She came back once a generation, as they do. At the next flowering, she found the print a little larger than her hand. At the one after, larger still, and her hand larger too. By the time she was old, with ferns in her hair and moss down her arms and two ring-scars on each ankle, the print was far bigger than her hand had ever been, pulled into a shape no hand has, and higher on the lintel than she could reach.

She stood under it holding her stone. She had held it for seventy years, give or take, counting by flowerings. She didn't reach up. She knew how the land does things. You give it the small true shape of yourself, and it takes that shape and grows it, slowly, into something bigger and stranger, and it keeps it long after you are gone into the sward.

Then she walked on, because she had to, because stillness is permission and not a place to stay. That season she wrote one more long sentence across a swell she loved. It was a single pale curving line that crossed three bright patches in the moss, the places where her grandmother, her grandmother's walking-partner, and her own were lying.

She lay down at the end of it, and the one who sat with her that night hummed her into the ground. At first light he stood and carried her stone for eleven days to the nearest eye. He pressed it with his thumb into the soft root-wall, just above the water, and just below the lowest ring of pale flowers.

The flowers will climb past it. The stone will go down. The country will rise a little and sink a little, and the sentence she walked will buckle as it goes. Someone not yet born will retrace it step by step and feel it in their feet, and they will not know who wrote it. They will know it was written.

The fog comes down. The moths rise. A grazer hums, and a hundred miles away somebody with a stone in their fist hums back, palm on the moss, and the land carries it a little way.

That is who lives there.
