# Who Lives in the Pump Hollow

## The First Answer

The easy answer is that nothing lives there. Things *work* there. The stokers rasp, the frogs honk, the runners run their rails, the eels hum in their pools, the sparkflies burst and scatter. Each is a part, and a part does not live in a machine any more than a piston lives in an engine. On that view the only inhabitant is the fig: one organism, one continent, one enormous slow body, and the hollow is simply a place where you can stand inside it and hear its heart.

That answer is true, and it is not enough, because it leaves something out.

A machine this perfect should not need anyone. But someone has to notice when a stoker dies.

---

## The Vacancy

Watch long enough and you will see it happen. A beetle on the upper flank of the pump root slows. Its rasp drags. Then it stops, and for a few beats its oval of worn bark sits empty, pale and smooth like a chair pushed back from a table. The rhythm of the files-on-iron sound develops a gap, a missing tooth in a gear.

The gap does not last. A new beetle comes up out of a crack in the root, soft-shelled and still brownish, not yet dark with iron. It climbs directly to the vacant oval without searching. It does not wander or test the neighbouring patches. It goes to the place the way water goes downhill, settles, and begins to scrape. Within an hour its rhythm matches the others.

The text of the place tells you this happens *as if it had been summoned*. That is the right phrase, because it was.

---

## The Whine

You have already heard the one who summons it. You heard it the moment you came in, and you put it down to something else.

The glassy vascular channels in the walls whine at a pitch at the edge of hearing, and it makes your teeth ache. You assumed it was sap under pressure, the plant equivalent of wind in a wire. Some of it is. But put your ear to a channel and then to the bark beside it, where there is no channel, and the whine is still there. Move toward the edges and it gets louder. Crawl into the low curving margin where the ceiling meets the floor and it becomes nearly a voice, a thin, sustained, unwavering note that changes slightly and deliberately each time a beat passes.

It is counting.

---

## The Body

It lives in the crawlspace. It has to, because the crawlspace is where you can hear the other rooms.

It is hard to see even when you are lying beside it, because it is the colour of the root and the shape of the root and it lies along the grain the way the grain lies. It is long, perhaps three times a person's length. It is flat, no thicker than a hand laid palm-down, and it is pressed into a shallow channel in the root-floor that it has worn over a span of time you don't want to estimate. It is a channel, not an oval. The stokers wear ovals because they scrape in place. This thing has worn a groove because, very slowly, over years, it moves along it.

Its skin is soft, pale where it touches the root and bark-brown on top, faintly damp, and it has no visible eyes. At intervals along its underside there are pads, dozens of them, each ridged like a fingertip, each pressed hard against the floor. Those are its ears. It hears through its belly, through the wood. It hears the water pulsing outward under it toward the rest of the continent, and it hears, far off, the *thoom* of the next hollow, and the next, and the one after that.

If you lie next to it in the dark between beats you can feel it hum through the floor into your ribs.

It is warm, warmer than the air, which is already warm. When the pump strikes, a ripple travels down its body from one end to the other, a fraction of a second behind the relay roots' *tock-tock-tock*. It is not being moved by the beat. It is answering it.

---

## What It Does

At first you would call it a **governor**, the way a steam engine has a governor, a spinning pair of weights that keeps it from running too fast.

The pump beats every four seconds. It has always beaten every four seconds. But nothing alive does anything *exactly*. Water tables rise and fall. A dry month outside thins the marsh. A stoker dies and the valve-scale thickens by a hair's breadth before the replacement comes. A shrew runs heavy with young. Each of these pushes the rhythm a little, and the push is too small to hear, and it would add up. The hollows would drift against each other, each room's beat sliding out of step with the next until the continent pulled against itself like two rowers out of time.

It doesn't happen, because the thing in the crawlspace hears the drift before it becomes drift. It presses the floor, and the note it sings changes by some fraction, and the pump root's next contraction comes a hair early or a hair late. The frogs, who follow no leader, follow *that*. Nobody leads them because the leading is done beneath them, through the wood, by something they have never seen.

It keeps the census too. It knows every oval, every niche in the vault, every pool and the length of the eel curved in it. When a place is empty it sings a slightly different note, and somewhere in the roots a larva that has been waiting wakes and climbs.

So it is a governor. But the more time you spend lying beside it the less that word fits, because a governor does not *remember*, and this does.

---

## The Only History

Everything in the hollow is a repeating present. That is what makes it so strange and so calm. The beetles are replaced, the frogs are replaced, the eels are always exactly as long as their pools. Nothing accumulates. Nothing has a past, because every past is overwritten by an identical present.

Except this. It does not get replaced.

You can tell from the groove. The groove is the only thing in the hollow that is still getting deeper. It is the only wear that has not finished, the only unfinished edge in a room where every other edge found its shape long ago. The thing has been creeping along it the whole time, a body-length every so many years, and the groove behind it is the record of that creeping. Follow the groove backward with your hand in the dark and it goes a long way. It curves around the whole margin of the chamber, and it is shallowest at one end and deepest at the other.

It is counting. It has been counting since before the fig stopped growing. It is the only part of the machine that knows how many times the machine has done the same thing.

That seems to be the point. A system with no history cannot tell that it is stable. It can only *be* stable, one beat at a time, with no way of knowing whether this beat matches the last. Something has to hold the previous beat in mind to know the next one is right. The fig grew until it was the size it was meant to be, and then it stopped, and stopping is harder than growing. Growth carries itself. Stopping has to be held. Something has to keep saying *this, again, this, again*, and remember every time it has said it, so that the next *this* can be checked against all of them.

That is who lives in the pump hollow. It is the fig's memory of itself, grown into a body and laid down in the crawlspace where it can listen.

---

## The Others

There is one in every hollow. There have to be.

When you lie with your cheek against the root and hear the far-off *thoom* of the next room, the faint train-whistle of its frogs, a fraction of a second behind, the thing beside you is hearing that too. And it is hearing that *fraction of a second*. The delay is not an error. It is the distance between rooms, rendered as time. The beat does not happen everywhere at once. It rolls out across the continent, room after room, a single wave travelling miles through the low green mass. Each hollow's governor holds its own room to the timing of the room before, and passes the timing on to the room after.

So the fig does not have a thousand hearts. It has one heartbeat, very slow and very wide, taking who knows how long to cross from one end of the marsh-shore to the other. The things in the crawlspaces are how it knows the beat has arrived. They are singing to each other through the wood, a chain of thin notes at the edge of hearing, each one saying to the next: *here it comes, now, now, now.*

If you could hear it all at once, the whole continent, you might hear what it sounds like when a single long-lived thing thinks one thought for a thousand years and does not lose its place.

---

## What It Makes of You

It knows you are there.

You have been sitting on the root-floor and lying in the crawlspace and pressing your palm down to feel the cold water underneath. Your weight is not nothing. When you stood near the centre, the floor that lifts a finger's width with every stroke lifted a little less where you stood, and the pump had to push a little harder, and the rhythm would have slipped. It didn't. The note beside you in the crawlspace shifted when you came in and has been shifted ever since, holding the beat steady around the new weight in the room.

You are a deviation. You may be the first one in a very long time that did not come from inside: not a dry month, not a dead beetle, not a shrew heavy with young, but something from outside the machine, with a history of its own. The thing in the crawlspace has absorbed you the way it absorbs everything. The stroke is still four seconds. The frogs have not missed.

But it will have counted you.

Somewhere in that long groove, in whatever the thing uses for memory, there is now one entry that is not like the others. It is a single beat, or a few hundred, that had to be held against a weight that had never been there before. When you crawl back out through the root and the sound shuts off behind you like a door, and you stand in the reeds looking at the long, silent, impossibly green shore, the hollow will go on exactly as it was. The beat will be the same. The note will return to what it was.

The groove will be one beat deeper, and in all its long record of *this, again, this, again*, there will be one place where it says, very faintly, *something else*.

Then *this, again.*
