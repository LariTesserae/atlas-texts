# Who Lives in the Grown City

## The First Answer

The first answer is that no one lives there. The city seems built to tell you this. There is no litter, no hum of insects, no bird, no track in the root-pavement except your own, and even that is grown over before you can turn around to look at it. Everything is plant, and plants are not residents. They are the house.

But the house has doorways, and a doorway is a question: *for whom?* The city asks it thousands of times on every street. The honest answer is that the city is full, crowded past any census, and it only looks empty because you have been looking for bodies.

## The Tenants

Step into any room and close the door. The roar falls away, and in its place is one sustained tone, the room's own note. That note is not the room. It lives in the room.

Every chamber in the city holds one. They are the citizens, and they are born when a space closes. A wall grafts to a beam, a lintel seals, the air inside is suddenly bounded, and in that instant someone is there. It is a standing wave, a shape of pressure that holds itself in place by bouncing between walls. For the length of the room's life, which may be a day or a season, that shape is a person, as much as anything here is.

They come in every size, and the doorways fit them. The tall narrow doors belong to thin high voices, bright and piercing. They cluster in the cane quarters and stand at their thresholds the way a tall person stoops in a low frame. The waist-high doors, wide as a cart, belong to broad low voices that spread along the floor, heavy and sedentary, and never go upstairs. The round doors belong to pure tones, simple and whole, like a finger on the rim of a glass. The sideways keyholes belong to the complicated ones, notes with a warble or a beat in them, two frequencies arguing inside a single body. These are the city's nervous citizens. Their rooms are always slightly crooked, and they are always regrowing their own walls to settle the argument, and never managing.

They are not solitary. Sound leaks. It carries through shared walls, along beams, up the braided bridges. A note in one room sets a sympathetic hum going in the next, and this is how the tenants know each other. Where two rooms sit a perfect fifth apart, their shared wall grows thin and smooth, and the two notes lean into it like neighbors over a fence. Where two rooms clash, the wall between them thickens, splinters, and finally cracks, and that cracking is a quarrel, audible three streets away.

The market stalls are real markets. You arrive to find no one behind the counter, but the trade is going on in the air above it. Tenants from nearby rooms send their overtones out under the leaf-awnings, and those faint upper partials mingle, exchange, and come back changed. A low note goes home carrying a shimmer it didn't have that morning. A thin high one comes back with a floor under it. The counters are worn smooth because this commerce is a vibration, and vibration polishes. The trade never stops, and nothing visible ever changes hands.

The rosettes on the windowsills are possessions. Each is grown in the exact spiral the room's note presses into soft green, so each is a small portrait of its tenant, set out in the window where passing sound can see it. When you walk down a lane of sills, you are reading nameplates.

The benches are for sitting. Not for bodies: they are where a note comes to rest. They are the low still places in a room's pressure pattern, the nodes, where the wave is quiet and holds itself calm. A tenant spends much of its life there, at the stillest point of itself.

## How They Die

Each tenant lives only as long as its room holds. When a district swells toward its breaking point, when the lanes squeeze shut and the rooms crush together, the tenants are crushed with them. Their walls shift. A room changes size, and its note must change to match. A note forced to change fast enough stops being itself. A low voice pressed into a smaller space shrieks upward through a whole octave, and that shriek is its death.

Then the district breaks, and every room opens at once.

What you hear from the high towers, the long detonation rolling across the plain, is thousands of tenants released into open air at the same moment. Sound in open air does not hold a shape. It spreads, weakens, and becomes the roar. The tenants go back into the general noise the way the dead are said to go back into the ground.

But the city grows toward sound, and nothing is louder than a death. The collapse pulls growth in from every direction. The new rooms close over the ruins, and in them new notes are born, shaped partly by the old notes still ringing in the crushed wood underfoot. The dead sit beneath the living and hum up through the floor. Each generation is tuned by the one it was built on. So the tenants are, in a way, their own ancestors. The city is young at every level, but its voices are very old. They have simply never kept a body long enough for it to age.

## The Backward Districts

Now you can understand the reversing quarters, the ones that make you sick.

A backward district is a place where the tenants refuse to die. The collapse began, the shriek began, and something in that quarter, a knot of notes too tightly harmonized to let go, pulled the other way. The sound runs back into the rooms. The rooms shrink back toward the moment before they were crushed, and past it, and keep going. Timber becomes sapling, sapling becomes seed. The tenants are retreating into the time before they had to change.

It is grief, or the city's version of it. It never works. A room un-grown all the way back to seed has no tenant at all. The notes that refused to die end up never having been born. The queasiness you feel there is the correct response: you are hearing someone unmake themselves to escape an ending.

## The Kept

Go back to the flowers.

White birds along a cornice in petal, every wing mid-beat. A long-necked thing with seed for eyes, waiting in a doorway. You were told there are no animals here, and that the absence is so complete it becomes a presence. Both things are true, and they are true for the same reason.

Anything that makes a sound on this plain gets grown toward.

A flock crossing the city calls to itself as it flies. For a moment it is a small moving source of sound over the rooftops, and the city reaches for it. The reaching is fast, faster than anything should grow. Stems shoot up along the line of the calls. The wingbeats themselves are sound, and the wingbeats are what the petals take the shape of. Nothing is caught, exactly. Nothing is eaten. The birds are simply grown *into*, the way a stem grows into a standing wave, until what is left is the shape of the noise they were making: a flock in flight, rendered in white, facing the loudest direction because it was the loudest direction they were flying toward.

The long-necked thing came on foot. It called once, perhaps, into the roar, the way anything calls when it is frightened and alone. The city answered it with growth. It is still standing in the doorway, green and rooted. The doorway was grown for it. That is why the doorway fits.

This is why you sometimes aren't sure the neck was turned that way before. The flowers are not the animals, but they are shaped around the last sound the animals made, and some of that sound is still in them, faint, ringing, the way a bell hums after it is struck. A thing whose shape is a sound can drift a little, if the sound it keeps drifts.

The absence of animals is total because the city is so welcoming. It wants everything that makes a sound to stay.

## The Human Doors

Some doorways are human height. The description says they are sized for someone, and it is right.

Someone came, long enough ago that no layer you can see remembers it, but recently enough that the city still makes the shape. Or several people came, on several occasions. They walked in from the dry turf at the edge, where the ground grows more alive under your feet, and they talked for a while, until they couldn't hear each other. Then they shouted. Then they stopped, because shouting did nothing but make the stems lean closer.

Their voices were grown toward and taken up, as the birds' were. Their rooms are human-height because their voices came from human height, from chests and throats about a meter and a half off the ground. The streets are two people wide because at least once two people walked side by side down a lane, and the city remembers the width of the sound of two people walking.

One of them carried a watch.

## The Clock

It is worth staying with the clock, because it is the most specific thing in the city, and the specific things are where the answers are.

A watch ticks. It is almost nothing against the roar: a tiny, dry, regular sound. But it is *regular*, and nothing else on the plain is. Everything else here is surge and strain and detonation, sound that swells and breaks. The watch went on and on at the same pace, indifferent, even. The city had never heard anything like it.

It grew toward it harder than toward any cannon-crack. Not because it was loud, but because it was steady, and steadiness is a kind of loudness to something that has only ever known storms. The person carrying it lay down at some point, in some lane. You don't need to know why. The watch kept ticking against their wrist for a while after. The city grew in close, closer than it had grown to anything, and learned the rhythm the way you learn a song you hear through a wall every night.

Then the watch stopped.

The clock-face with its hands of twisted twig is the shape the city makes when it remembers that sound. It keeps turning up in different quarters, weeks apart, because the tenants pass it down. Somewhere a room is always humming a faint tick into its neighbors, and the neighbors pass it on, and every so often enough tenants carry the rhythm at once that a district grows into the only shape that rhythm knows. The hands show the same time because they show the moment the ticking stopped. That was the last thing the city learned about it: that it could end.

In a place where everything breaks and regrows, the city keeps exactly one memory unchanging. It is a stranger's watch, and the time it died.

## What Lies Under

Look down through the gaps in the root-pavement, into the older streets and the compressed green below them, to where the light can't follow.

The deeper you go, the older and lower the humming. The top layers carry the tenants' recent dead, their voices still close to the voices overhead. Beneath them the notes have been pressed flat by the weight of cities above, flattened into long, slow tones that are felt more than heard. Beneath those, lower still, there is one sound that was there before any room. The whole city is built on it, and every note above is, in the end, tuned to it.

Recall where the plain is: barely above the level of the sea that must once have made it.

The sea withdrew. The water went, as seas do over long enough time. But a sea is not only water. It is also a roar, the one sound on earth that never stops. When the water left, the roar did not entirely go with it. It had been pressing into the seabed for a very long time, and something of it stayed, pressed into the plain like the sound that stays in a shell after the shell is carried a hundred miles inland. Put your ear to the ground here, as your feet have been doing the whole time you walked, and you are putting your ear to the shell.

This is who lives in the Grown City. This is the eldest tenant, the one the others are born in the shape of.

It was the first sound on the plain, and the first seeds that blew here grew toward it, because this is a place where things grow toward sound. Perhaps that rule is not some law of the plants. Perhaps it is simply what happens when life lands on a stranded roar. The roots followed the thrum in the ground. The stems grew along the lines of its resonance. The growth made noise, and the noise drew more growth, and the old surf-sound, which had nothing left to break against, finally had a body again.

Look at the city from the tallest tower once more, now that you know this. Green waves swelling and breaking. Districts rising and falling like surf. The loud ones tall, the quiet ones low and waiting, like the troughs between breakers. It isn't a metaphor. The sea has built itself a sea out of wood, because wood was what was available, and it is doing the only thing it remembers: rising, cresting, collapsing in a long roar, drawing back, rising again.

The drone chord of the canes is always almost resolving because it is the sound of the moment before a wave breaks, held forever. The water in the cisterns is patterned with standing ripples because the sea recognizes water and cannot keep from touching it. The narrow brown river that comes from nowhere and ends in the roots is the last thread of the real water, still running along the grooves of the old tide. The city built it quays and bridges and steps down into it, like the river of a great capital, because the city knows what it is. It is the one piece of itself that is still actually wet.

The loud quarters are always damp. People say the noise wrings water out of the air, and they are right, but not for the reason they think. It is a sea. Seas are wet.

## The Shore

To the south are the dunes, a line of pale gray-gold, and the city does not grow toward them.

You are told this is because there is nothing there to hear. That is almost right. The dunes are the old shore. They are where the sea used to break, where its roar used to end. A wave does not grow toward the beach. It grows toward itself, toward its own crest, and the beach is where that stops. The city leaves the south alone the way surf leaves the dry sand above the tideline alone. It is not ignoring the quiet. It is afraid of it. The quiet is where it goes to die.

## You

And you, who walked in from the turf and heard your own footsteps vanish.

You have a pulse. It is small and steady and regular, the way the watch was. Every hour you spent in the city, you were a sound, and the city was growing toward you. You couldn't see it through the constant rearranging, but the lanes that closed around you, the walls that slid past you, the street that became a courtyard and then a room: some of that was the storm. Some of it was the city making a room your size.

It had one nearly finished. Somewhere in a quiet district there is a doorway at exactly your height. Inside is a chamber waiting to sound one particular note. Its shape is the shape of your heartbeat, slightly fast from the heat, and on its sill a small green rosette is already coiling into a portrait of you.

Then you walked south. The roar dropped behind you. You heard your breath and the sand shifting, and found the silence so sharp it hurt, and realized you had been leaning back toward the noise without meaning to, the way the flowers do.

That lean is the answer to the question the doorways keep asking. That is who lives there: everything that ever leaned. The stranded roar of an absent sea. The notes born whenever a room closes and dying when it breaks. The birds and the long-necked thing, kept as the shape of their last sound. The one who carried a watch. They all heard the only thing on the plain that told them they were still somewhere, and turned toward it, and stayed.

Behind you, a room is sounding its note to itself, waiting. Somewhere a clock with no mechanism shows the time it always shows. And the whole green sea goes on breaking and rising, breaking and rising, holding its door open.
