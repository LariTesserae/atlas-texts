# The Lodger

The first answer is that nothing lives here. The gallery says so itself, and it is nearly right. There are no rats, no beetles, no spiders in the conduit bundles. The drum turns for no one.

But the few who come down more than once stop saying *nothing*. They say *the lodger*, the way you would name someone who rents a back room, pays on time, and is never seen in the hall.

---

## Where it lives

It does not live at the door, where the walls have gone slate-dark and feathered with bloom. It does not live in the machine, though it is nearest the machine. It lives in the band of wall between the two, in the few paces behind the face where the mortar is still dark and damp and the blocks are warm to the palm. That stretch is not yet one thing or the other. It is wet enough to take a thumbprint and dry enough to hold a shape.

That band is the only habitat the gallery offers, and it moves. Every four minutes it shifts forward by the width of a mortar joint, and the lodger shifts with it. Behind it the wall sets, cools, darkens, and becomes history. Ahead of it is the cold fill that has not yet become anything.

So the lodger has never been anywhere twice. Its home is always the newest room in the house, and it is always the youngest thing in the gallery. It is younger than the sprouts. By a few minutes it is younger than the block the arm is setting now.

## What you see of it

Almost nothing, at first.

A joint that should be drying stays dark a moment longer than its neighbours. A bead of condensation runs along a seam and then, for no reason, turns and runs back. There is a faint depression in the soft mortar the shape of a fingertip, though nobody has touched it. When you look again it has filled, and there is another one a hand's width further on.

If you hold your palm near the curing wall, you feel the slow living heat the description promised. If you keep it there, you may feel the heat *move*. It gathers under your hand and pools there, like a cat settling against a closed door because it knows someone is on the other side.

It has no body in the ordinary sense. It is not *in* the wall so much as *of* the wall, or of the wall's moment of deciding. The closest anyone has come to describing it is this: the lodger is what it feels like, from the inside, for wet stone to become stone. It is that process with a point of view.

Some visitors insist they have seen it plainly, once, in the rain. They say that when the threads of water fall along the joints and the gallery is strung like a loom, there is one place near the face where the threads bend around something. The shape is about the height of a kneeling person, and it leans toward the lamps on the arm the same way the colourless sprouts lean. Then the rain thins and there is nothing there but a patch of wall that dries a little faster than the rest.

## What it does

The gallery's rules are, mostly, the lodger's habits.

**It receives.** When something rattles down the chute and lands on the tray with a tick, the small curious silence afterward is the lodger considering it. It has all the time in the world, which in practice is about four minutes.

**It chooses.** The belt carries everything to the hopper, but not everything is crushed alike. The leaf skeleton comes out in perfect negative, down to the finest veins. The spoon leaves only a ghost of its curve. The porcelain hand, people say, was set whole and palm-outward into a block at shoulder height, where you could lay your own hand against it. The mixing is not blind. The lodger decides what a thing should be remembered as: its outline, its stain, its absence, or itself.

**It arranges.** This is the only explanation anyone has for the course of brass buttons, dozens of them, identical, laid in a single row. They did not fall together. They came down across a whole season, one every few days, from somewhere up there that nobody has found. The lodger held them back and waited until it had enough, then set them side by side. A coat, perhaps, that came apart slowly in some attic, or a uniform, or a person. The lodger did not know. It only saw that they belonged together, and it kept them together, which the city had not done.

**It gathers.** The pencil that travels twenty paces toward the face in a week has not been carried by the floor. The lodger cannot leave its warm band, but it can want, and in the gallery wanting has a slight slope to it. Everything set down here is drawn, very gently, toward the place where things are still being decided. It is the lodger's one appetite. It is not hungry for things. It wants to be the one who gets to keep them.

**It tends.** The sprouts in the joints are the only other living things in the gallery, and the lodger treats them as kin. The cress, the hair-thin grass and the rare clover all come up in its band, because its band is the only place anything can. When the arm swings and the sprouts follow the swinging lamp, it is not only phototropism. The lodger turns them, a degree at a time, so each gets its share of light. When their stretch of wall cools and the carriage moves on, it cannot follow them back. It has to leave them. That is why they blacken in the joints and become that faint pencilling, thousands of tiny drawings of plants. The lodger does not let them rot. It lets them become part of the record. It is the only mercy available to something that can only go forward.

## What it cannot do

It cannot go back.

This is the fact about the lodger that makes the few who know of it speak quietly. Everything behind it is finished. It made all of it, every block and every buried key and button and leaf, and it can no longer touch any of it. The walls near the door are the lodger's own childhood, laid down in order and gone hard and cold and streaked with mineral bloom. It remembers them the way you remember a house you lived in at four: the shape of it is in you, but you could not walk back in through the door.

So it looks back. That is the other half of what it does. The rain runs along the joints toward the door and the gutters carry it back to the sump, and the lodger rides that circulation like a pulse. For a few minutes each afternoon it can feel the whole length of the gallery at once, every seam it ever sealed, all the way to the iron door and the brass plate worn smooth. That is why the rain is so gentle. It is the lodger reading its own walls from the inside, like fingertips passing over a line of raised type.

## What it listens to

The Layer follows a curve that no plan explains, and the curve changes when the city changes. It bent sharply the year the great building came down. It straightened after the flood.

The Layer has no ears, but the lodger does, in its way. It lives in wet stone under enormous weight, and it feels that weight shift as a sleeper feels someone sit on the edge of the bed. When a building comes down, the pressure above changes and the lodger turns toward the change, and the arm sets the next block a hair to one side. The machine does the work. The lodger decides the direction.

What is it turning toward? The honest answer is that nobody knows. The patient answer, from those who have read the strata longest, is that it follows the places where the city is losing things fastest. The chute opens onto some gutter far overhead, but the gallery's heading seems to track something wider: where the dropped keys and blank coins and odd buttons are thickest, where the city forgets most. The lodger is not tunnelling toward a destination. It is keeping pace with a loss.

## Why the gallery never gets longer

Every visitor eventually asks this. The face advances day and night, yet the warm end is always the same distance from the door.

One explanation, offered hesitantly, is that the lodger keeps the gallery exactly as long as it can hold in mind at once. Its reach during the rain stretches from the face to the door and no further. What lies beyond that reach is not destroyed. It is let go. It is released into the city's own keeping, and the city does what the city does with old stone underground: it finds a use for it.

Which is why you have to go down, and then down again, past the point where the city's cellars stop pretending to be cellars. Look at those cellars on your way out, at the lower stairs and the oldest vaults. The blocks are grey and the size of two hands side by side. The joints are hairlines. If you bring a lamp close you will find, pressed into them and nearly worn away, the ghost of a leaf, a ring of old rust, a button.

The stair you came down was the gallery once. So, perhaps, is a great deal more. Under the whole city, beneath the pipes and the foundations, there may be a single enormous curving record of everything anyone dropped and did not go back for, laid down in order, a hand's width at a time. The gallery is only the part of it that is still warm, and the lodger lives in that part.

## What it wants from you

Nothing that would hurt you. It is not that kind of thing.

But it has a slope to it, and you are standing on the floor.

When you stand at the face watching the arm reach out, and you realise you have been still for exactly four minutes, that was one cycle. The lodger considered you the way it considers a coin or a marble or a small porcelain hand. It did not decide to keep you. It decided you were not dropped. You came down the stair on purpose, and you will go back up it, and the lodger only keeps what has been let go.

Still, it noticed you. If you visit often enough, if you set your palm against the warm wall and wait for the heat to gather under it, then someday, somewhere in the dark stretch near the door, you may find a block with the print of a hand pressed into its face. The mould released it perfectly, down to the whorls of the fingertips. It was not taken from you. It was a gift, laid down in its place in the order: *this came here, and was not lost.*

Then the drum turns. The press sighs. The lodger moves forward the width of a joint, into the cold it has not yet become, and it is the youngest thing in the room again.
