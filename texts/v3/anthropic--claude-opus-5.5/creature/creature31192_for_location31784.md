# Who Lives in the Chorus Vault

## The First Answer, Which Is True

The obvious answer is the swifts, and it is correct.

A hollow-swift lives about four years if it lives at all. It hatches in the dark on a glass spine it cannot see, in a mat of bodies so dense that its first sensation is pressure: warm fur on every side and the heartbeats of strangers. It never builds a nest. It is born clinging. The mother holds the egg against the spine with her own body until it hatches, so the chick's first grip is on its mother, and its second is on the glass.

The first thing it learns, before it can fly, is its note.

Every spine rings at its own pitch. A chick hears that pitch constantly, hammered into the glass above it by the clicks of every adult in its colony. It hears it through its feet more than its ears, because the spine it clings to vibrates. Before it can see, it knows that one tone means *here*, *home*, *us*. When it begins to click, at around ten days, its calls are ragged and off-pitch and the spine does not answer. The adults around it click at it, correcting. By the time it fledges, it can make the glass sing, and so it has a name.

It flies out on the exhale. The first night is the most dangerous of its life. Most chicks that die, die then, swept out of the cleft in a river of wings into cold that thins the blood. The survivors hunt the high air above the cloud-floor, where moths and midges rise on the warmth of the valleys. The swifts feed by starlight over a white sea. Then, before dawn, they have to find their way back into a crack in a black mountain, in the dark, against the wind, on the first thread of the inhale.

They do it by listening for home. Even from outside, the vault leaks its chord. A swift that has lost its way circles until it hears its own spine, faint among the drone, and then drops toward it.

So the swifts live there. They are the obvious inhabitants and the loud ones, the architecture of fur on the ceiling. But the longer you stay, the more the answer starts to feel incomplete.

## The Second Answer, Which Is Also True

Consider what a spine *is* to a swift.

A colony does not hold a spine the way a family holds a house. It holds a note, the way a family holds a name. The individuals change constantly. They die, are born, and are lost on bad nights. A colony on one of the long bass spines near the vault's center might turn over every member a dozen times in fifty years, and the tone it sounds would never change. The clicking that keeps it ringing never fully stops. Some swifts always stay behind: the brooding, the injured, the old. As long as one mouth is working, the spine sings.

So there is something that persists on each spine that is not a swift. It is a *voice*. It has territory and it quarrels with its neighbors. It swells at dusk and thins before dawn. It remembers things. A colony whose spine rings slightly flat, cracked long ago by a tremor, keeps producing that flatness, and its chicks learn the note wrong on purpose because *wrong* is what *home* sounds like.

There are perhaps two hundred of these voices on the ceiling. They are born when a new spine cracks free of the rock's stress, or when a displaced colony claims a silent one. They die when their glass falls. They have lifespans measured in generations, and they fight, border to border, in frequencies. When two spines sit close in pitch, their tones beat against each other in a slow throb, *wah, wah, wah*, and the colonies on them are always in conflict, clicking harder and harder to drown each other out. When two spines sit at a clean interval, a fifth or an octave, the colonies seem to tolerate each other. Their mats grow toward one another across the gap until the boundary is only a line of slightly less crowded fur.

This is not metaphor. You can map the vault's politics with a tuning fork.

## The Third Answer, Which Takes Longer to Hear

Stay longer. Lie in the warm muck at the base of the wall, where the gleaners step over your legs and leave their blue prints fading on your clothes. Stop trying to separate the sounds.

After some hours, and it takes hours, the drone of the ceiling stops being many things. You notice that it *moves*. When the eastern colonies go out at dusk, the high tones drain from that side first. The chord tilts, darkens, and shifts its weight westward. When the birds on the walls scream, the ceiling answers, not with the random irritation of many animals but in a pattern: the nearest spines first, then a ripple outward, a wave of pitch spreading across the vault like a shiver across skin. When a tremor comes and a spine falls, the chord does not simply lose a note. For days afterward it *reaches* for it. The neighboring colonies drift in pitch, clicking at the edges of their range, as if trying to cover the gap. The whole ceiling keeps sounding a wound.

Nothing directs this. No swift knows the whole chord, just as no neuron knows a thought. Each colony only listens to its neighbors and answers. But the sum of all that listening and answering has a shape, and the shape holds. It has held since the mountain cooled.

That is what lives in the Chorus Vault. The swifts are its body, and the glass is its throat. Its breath is the mountain's breath, pulled in by day and pushed out by night, and it sings on the exhale like any singer. Its heat is their heat, and its sweat is the condensation that runs down the obsidian. Its memory is the tuning, the pattern of which notes are held, which are flat, which intervals are at war. It is several centuries old, and very young for what it is.

It does not think, probably. But it *persists*. It responds to injury. It changes and remains itself. It has a daily rhythm like waking and sleeping: the full roar of the returned colony through the day, the thinned drone of the night. It has seasons of health and seasons of grief. After every tremor there is a long, quarrelsome convalescence, and then the chord settles into a new configuration that is recognizably, somehow, *the same voice* in a slightly different key.

The people below the clouds, if they have a word for it, probably do not call it the swifts.

## The Thing That Lives in the Quiet

The chord is not alone in the vault. It has an opposite, and the opposite is patient.

Look at the silent spines, the ones hung with gray beards of stringy fungus that glisten and drip. The swifts avoid them. It would be easy to assume the swifts left first and the fungus came after, colonizing dead glass. But watch long enough and the order reverses.

The fungus grows on wet glass wherever the air is stillest. It starts as a faint gray fur at the root of a spine, where the obsidian meets the ceiling, and a spine furred at its root rings duller. The fur damps the vibration. The colony on that spine clicks harder to compensate, but the note fades slowly over years. It gets harder to find on the returning wind. Chicks learn it less well. Fewer swifts come home to it. The mat thins, and a thinner mat means less warmth, less movement of air, and more moisture beading and staying. The fungus grows faster. It creeps down the spine in beards.

Eventually the last clicks stop, and the note is gone. The colony's survivors scatter to other spines, where they are unwelcome and must fight.

The fungus feeds on stillness, and it makes more stillness. It is the vault's other inhabitant, a slow, soft, silent thing that has been eating the chord one note at a time since the chord began. It has claimed perhaps thirty spines. It hangs in the dark like gray hair on an old head. On some nights, if you listen at the edges of the ceiling where its beards are thickest, you can hear the chord's lowest voices bending *away* from those places, the way a body shies from a sore tooth.

The tremors keep it in check. When the mountain shivers, beards tear loose, fresh spines crack open clean and ringing, and the swifts rush to claim them. The chord is fed from below by the same restlessness that feeds the mountain. As long as the mountain is still rising, the song has the advantage.

Someday the mountain will stop rising. After that, the quiet will have nothing to hold it back.

## The Ones Who Count

And then there are the notches.

The people who come up from below the clouds are not residents. They stay a few hours at most, at the lip of the cleft or a little way inside, where the air is breathable and the passage is narrow. They come in some particular season, perhaps the one when the young fledge, the one night a year when the dusk exhale runs longest and loudest. They bring cord, blades, and a gourd. Most of what they take is probably guano, carried down in baskets to fields in the valleys, where it would grow crops of startling size.

But someone counts the notes.

The grooves in the obsidian are a score, or a record, or both. The oldest ones are frost-dulled and match spines that no longer ring, notes the ceiling lost generations ago. Newer ones match the chord as it sounds now. Someone has stood in that passage, year after year, generation after generation, listening to the voice in the dark and marking how it changes. The braided cord that runs a short way into the vault and stops may mark how far a person can go before the air turns and the ammonia burns the lungs. It may also mark how far someone went once and did not come back.

The notches hint that somewhere down in the valleys, under the white floor, there are people who know this chord. Their children may learn the intervals by rote, the way swift chicks learn their spines. They may sing it at harvest over the fields the guano fed. When a tremor strikes and the ceiling changes, the next person who climbs up may carry the new notes down. The valley song would drift the way the vault's song drifts, one interval at a time, following it across the centuries at a distance, the way a child's voice follows a parent's.

If that is true, then the thing that lives in the Chorus Vault does not live *only* there. It has put down roots below the clouds, in human throats, without knowing it. It is a voice that cannot think, echoed by people who can. The swifts carry it out every dusk into the high air, and the listeners carry it down into the valleys. When the glass finally dulls, when the cleft has widened and the light has come in and the gray beards hang from every spine, the chord may still be sung somewhere below, by people who have forgotten where it came from. Or perhaps they will remember, and still climb the mountain once a year to stand in a quiet crack in the stone and sing the old notes back at the dead glass, to see if anything answers.

## What Lives There

So: who lives in the Chorus Vault?

Ten million small furred bodies, clinging to each other in the dark and learning their names from the glass.

Two hundred voices, each older than any swift, fighting a border war in frequencies.

One larger voice made of all of them. It is centuries old, and it breathes with the mountain and flinches when it is hurt.

A gray, patient silence growing in the still places, eating the song note by note.

And, at the edge, a line of people who have been listening for longer than anyone remembers. They cut the song into stone so it would not be lost, and perhaps carry it down into the world, where it lives on in a form the vault will never hear.

At dusk the mountain exhales, and all of them are there in the breath at once: the wings, the chord, the quiet in its gaps, and somewhere far below, perhaps, a person hearing a faint thread of sound come down through the cloud and knowing what it is.
