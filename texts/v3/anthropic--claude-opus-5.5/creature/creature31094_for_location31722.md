# Who Lives in the Held Country

## I. The First Guess

The first guess is the obvious one. Someone made the rings, bent the houses, pressed the handprints into the living scale, and set pebbles at the edges of the eyes. Then they went away, or died out, or wandered off the edge of the known, and left their marks to be slowly swallowed.

That guess is wrong, though not by much. They are here. There are simply very few of them, and they are almost impossible to find, because they have spent their whole history learning to live in a country that eats whatever stops.

Call them the Unkept. It is not quite their word. Their word is a hum, and it doesn't translate. But it means something close to *not yet taken*, and they use it the way other peoples use "alive."

## II. What They Look Like, Seen Badly

If you saw one, you would see it in fog, at dusk, at a distance, walking. That is how anyone sees them.

They are tall and narrow. They are built for turning sideways between the clubmoss towers, and their shoulders fold forward in a way that looks like shyness and is really anatomy. Their skin is the grey-green of old lichen, finely haired over the back, the shoulders, and the backs of the arms. The hair is not for warmth, since the country is always warm. It holds moss. Spores catch in it within a day of any stillness. By the end of a long walk the upper back of an adult is faintly furred in green, and this is normal and nothing to be ashamed of, like dust on a traveler.

Their eyes are large and dark and take in what little light there is. Beneath each eye runs a pale streak of skin that catches lichen-glow the way the walking birds' wing-patches do. At night, in the blue-green, you would not see an Unkept. You would see two faint pale strokes moving at head height between the stems, and then not see them.

Their hands are the thing to notice. The fingers are long, very strong, and thickly padded at the tips. These are hands for bending living growth a little at a time over years, for braiding grass-rope, for gripping a shoulder in the dark. They have never made a tool. They've never had the material, since there is no stone to knap and no wood that is not also alive and growing. Everything they have ever made, they made by persuasion.

Their feet are broad like the grazers', and they walk the same way, on the pale paths, pressing the moss. Some of the silver-green tracks scribbled across the country are theirs. The land cannot tell the difference and doesn't try.

## III. Sleeping in a Line

The first thing an Unkept child learns is not walking. It is being walked.

A newborn is carried against the chest, facing inward, and rocked in the rhythm of an adult stride. It sleeps that way for its first year and never once sleeps lying down. Before it can walk awake, it learns to walk asleep. Held upright, its feet brush the moss and begin, on their own, to step. Every Unkept has a sleeping gait learned before memory: slow, loose-kneed, and utterly steady.

Adults sleep in pairs. One leads, awake, moving at the shuffle the grazers use. The other follows with a hand on the leader's shoulder, eyes closed, feet finding the path through the leader's body. Every few hours they trade. To sleep with someone, in their language, is to walk behind them. Two people who have slept a thousand nights in each other's shoulders can wake and trade places without a word, and they know each other's weight in the palm more exactly than they know each other's faces.

There is no word for bed. There is a word for the shallow, hand-sized hollow worn into a shoulder by years of someone's grip. It is a word of great tenderness, used almost only between old couples, and between parents and grown children who once slept behind them.

## IV. Grooming

When you stop, the moss starts. Every Unkept knows this, the way people elsewhere know that fire is hot.

So they groom each other. At the evening gathering, when the fog comes down and the moths rise, they sit in a close ring (sitting is permitted, for a while, if you are careful) and pick the green from one another's backs. They work it out of the fine hair with the padded fingertips, a few spores at a time. It is intimate and slow. Children fall asleep under it and have to be woken and stood up and walked.

To groom someone is to say *stay*. To be groomed is to be told you are wanted among the moving. There is a gentle insult for someone careless or aloof, and it means *green-shouldered*: nobody has cleaned them in a while.

There is also an older meaning, which the Unkept rarely say aloud. The moss you take from someone's back is the land touching them, beginning to ask for them. Grooming is refusing, on their behalf, for one more day. Every evening, all across the Held Country, in small scattered rings, the Unkept are politely telling the ground *not yet*.

## V. The Bent Houses Are for Stillness

The obvious guess is that the bent houses are shelters, somewhere to sleep out of the fog. They aren't, because the Unkept don't sleep still, and the fog is nothing to shelter from.

They are for the two things in a life that cannot be done walking.

The first is being born.

A mother cannot deliver on the move. For a day, sometimes two, she has to lie down. The mat does not care why she is still. So over generations the Unkept learned to make places where the mat is slower. The houses are domes of fused clubmoss drawn in overhead so the fog drips on the outside and runs away, with floors of moss pressed so hard, by so many generations of bodies, that it has gone dense and slow-growing, almost like felt. Inside a bent house the mat still comes, but it comes like evening rather than like weather. A mother can lie for two days and be groomed clean by her sisters at the end of it.

Every child born in a house has its hand pressed into the lintel on its first morning. The lintel is living scale, young and soft on its inner face, and the print takes. Then the house grows. Over a century the prints stretch upward and outward with the growth of the towers until the small hands are long and strange. The fingers become thin streaks, and the palm becomes a smear the width of an adult's chest. The Unkept read them as growth, not distortion. *Look how big you've become.* An old person can stand in the door of the house they were born in and put their full adult hand inside the print of their newborn one, and it fits.

A house takes three or four generations to make. A grandmother bends a ring of young towers a little, ties them with grass-rope, and walks away. Her daughter comes back in ten years and bends them further. The granddaughter ties the crown. The great-granddaughter is the first to be born beneath it. None of them live in it. Nobody lives in anything here. But each of them made it, and when the Unkept say *my house* they mean one they began or continued, one they will never lie down in themselves.

This is why their sense of a person is so strange. A single Unkept is a short thing: they see two violet flowerings, maybe three. But a house is a person too, in their grammar, made of four lives in a row. So is a path. So is a family's circuit of eyes. They do not think of themselves as ending where their skin does. They end, roughly, where their work ends, and their work is always unfinished and always being carried forward by someone walking behind them with a hand on their shoulder.

## VI. The Second Thing That Cannot Be Done Walking

The second thing is dying.

When an Unkept is very old, there comes a morning when they do not get up from the evening ring. It is not always illness. Often it is a decision, long discussed, like a traveler choosing where to stop.

They go to a bent house, usually the one they were born in, or one they helped bend. They lie down on the felted floor. Their family grooms them one last time, very thoroughly, every strand of green picked away, so that they begin clean. Then the family stops grooming.

It takes the slow floor of a house much longer than it takes the open sward. The old one has days, sometimes weeks, of lying still while the family sits and hums and walks in shifts around the dome. The moss comes up gently and furs them from the back and shoulders first, the way it always did, but this time nobody takes it away. There is an expression for this that means something like *letting the land finish grooming you*.

When they are gone, the family carries them out. By then they are light, and already green. They are laid on the open sward on the crest of a swell, and they are left.

Within a year there is a slight rise there, flowering a little brighter than the moss around it. That is exactly what the land does to a fallen grazer. The Unkept do not pretend otherwise. They do not bury, because nothing is buried here. Things are taken up, sideways and downward, into the body of the land.

## VII. Why Stone

So why the stones?

Stone is the one thing the land does not keep.

Everything else here is drawn in: feathers, bones, grazers, the Unkept themselves. But the pebbles come the other way. They are the last grains of the vanished mountains, and over thousands of years the weave pushes them upward through itself, slowly, like a body working out a splinter. The land takes everything and rejects only this.

The Unkept feel a deep, uneasy kinship with stone. They are not-yet-taken. Stone is never-taken. It is the only other thing in the country that moves against the grain of the mat, and it does it without walking, without grooming, without effort at all. Children are told that pebbles are the bones of the ground that was here before, the old country beneath the white, and that they are climbing home to the surface the way a sleeper rises to waking.

A pebble, found, is never kept. Keeping it would mean carrying it, and the Unkept carry very little. Instead it is placed.

**The rings** are placed on the crests of the long swells, the ghosts of ridgelines, the highest ground. The Unkept have no memory of mountains, but they have an instinct for where height used to be, and they put the refused thing on the high place. Each ring is a few paces across. It takes a family generations to fill one, a pebble every few years, whenever someone walking finds one showing pale through the moss.

Some rings are older than any living memory. Their stones are half-swallowed now and only just visible, because the mat grows over rings too, eventually. Stone does not move sideways, so the moss creeps across the tops of the pebbles and the land pulls them down again, very slowly, having pushed them up over thousands of years. The Unkept find this the saddest and most beautiful fact in the world: even the never-taken are taken, in the end, only more slowly. They do not stop placing stones because of it. That is the point.

**The offerings at the eyes** are different, and they are the most private thing the Unkept do.

The eyes are the only places where the land opens downward. Their walls go down braided and dark past where the light gives out, and a stone dropped into one never audibly lands. The eyes are how you go back.

When someone has died, and been carried out, and been left on the sward to be taken, their family keeps one pebble for them. It is the only time in a life anyone carries a stone. They walk, sometimes for months, to an eye that person loved. They don't drop the pebble in. Dropping would be deciding. They set it on a flat leaf at the waterline, or press it into the root-wall a finger's width above the tea-dark surface, and they leave it to the slow breathing of the eye.

Some of those pebbles sit for years. Some are taken within days when the water rises its inch. Some are overgrown by the flowers climbing the wall in tide-rings, one ring per wet decade, until the stone is far above the water and will never go under at all. The Unkept accept whatever happens. They set the refused thing at the edge of the way down and let the land decide whether to take it back.

The body goes into the mat, and the stone goes to the eye. Those are the two directions a person can leave in. The Unkept send each of their dead both ways at once, and do not claim to know which one is true.

## VIII. The Flowering

Once in a generation, the country turns violet.

For three weeks the creeping thing blooms, invisible the rest of the time, everywhere at once, and the air smells like wet pepper and honey. It is the only time the Unkept gather in numbers. For the scattered families across a region, it is the only time they are all in the same place.

They go to the rings, the old ones, whose stones are nearly under.

On one night of the flowering, not every night and never announced, they do the thing they do at no other time. They stand still.

Each adult steps onto a pebble of an old ring, or onto the moss where a pebble was, and stands on it barefoot. Then everyone else fills in the ring and the space around it, close, shoulder to shoulder. And for one whole night, from the fog's coming down to its lifting, nobody moves.

The moths rise around them. The lichen lights. The eels tick in the weave under their feet. The moss, sensing stillness, comes. It threads up between their toes and beads their ankles and furs the backs of their calves. By the small hours it has reached their knees, and every shoulder in the ring is faintly green. They let it. They stand in the violet dark and let the land begin to take all of them together, a whole people agreeing, for one night, to be still.

At dawn, when the fog lifts, they turn to each other and groom.

It takes all morning. Everyone is green to the thigh. They work in pairs and threes, sitting in the violet, picking one another clean, strand by strand, laughing a great deal. Marriages are made on this morning, by who grooms whom first. Children born just before a flowering are carried into the ring asleep, held against the chest, and are the only ones who do not come out green. It is said that this is the one night of their lives they will be protected from the land. It is said, too, that it is the one night they will not remember.

The grazers come to the rings in the evenings for the rest of the generation and gather around the stones and hum. Nobody taught them. Or the Unkept taught them so long ago, by gathering there, that it has worn into the herds' memory like a pale path. The grazers stand at the rings they did not place, in the fog, and do what the Unkept do, in a simpler form. The Unkept regard this as the land learning.

## IX. The Edge

The pale paths all bend away before the rim. The grazers never go there, and neither do the walking birds.

The Unkept do, rarely, and only alone.

Nobody is sent. Somebody goes, once in a long while, usually after a great grief or before a great change. They walk out past where the paths bend, into the warmth and the hiss. They stand where the braided root runs out over nothing and the water falls in threads and becomes air, and they look down into the white.

They are looking for the old ground: where the stones come from, the country beneath the country that the mountains stood on before they wore away.

No one has ever seen it. The falls turn to spray a thousand feet down, and the spray turns to cloud, and the cloud rises back up past the watcher's face to join the lid. The Held Country drinks back its own water and shows nothing. But the Unkept who go there say that if you stand long enough at the edge, which you can't, because the mat would take you, you might.

What they bring back is always the same. When they return to their family, they hand over one pebble. Nobody asks where it came from. There are no pebbles at the edge; the weave there is pure root. It is understood that you do not ask. The pebble goes into the ring on the nearest crest, and the family grooms the one who went, very gently, for a long time, and they walk on.

## X. What Else Lives There

The Unkept are the answer to the question as it was asked. They made the marks, and they are still making them, quietly, rarely, far apart.

But if you asked an old Unkept who lives in the Held Country, they would not start with themselves.

They would put their ear to the moss. In the silence you would hear the eels ticking, everywhere, a dry and constant clock slightly out of true. Very far away, felt more than heard, an eye's wall might give, *whumph*, and the old one would close their eyes the way you close them at the sound of someone you love breathing in the next room.

*That lives here*, they would say. *We are something it does.*

Most Unkept believe that the weave is one being, or many that have forgotten the difference, and that the land is not their enemy for taking them. It is only doing what it is: holding. It holds the height the mountains could not. It holds its water, drinking back the falls, and it holds every creature that stops. Their dead are in it. Their houses are grown from it. Their children's handprints stretch across its skin. The pale paths are the only writing they have ever had, and the land keeps that writing, all of it, for centuries, and forgets none of it.

They have a teaching. It is less a story than a thing said to children during grooming, while the moss comes away under the fingertips.
