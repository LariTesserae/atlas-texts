# Who Lives at Lamb's Sluice

## Not the eels

The obvious answer is the eels. They are the most numerous and the most purposeful, and they are the only creatures in the Works that seem to have made an arrangement with the engine rather than simply living near it. If someone asked you who lives there, you could say *eels* and not be wrong.

But the eels are passing through. Everything about them is passage. They go up the gutter and down the shaft, into the cistern and out again. They are tenants in the oldest sense, people who hold a thing for a while. The crickets are the same, and so are the bats, who leave every night for a sky that isn't there and come back having touched a wall. They all live *in* the Works. None of them is the one the Works is kept for.

You only start to notice that there might be such a one after you have been there long enough to hear the tapping.

## The tapping

It comes from the lamp standards. The description says *something small and quick lives in the hollow iron and occasionally taps*, and that is true. After a few strokes of the engine you realise that *occasionally* is wrong. The tapping is regular. It is simply slow, the way everything here is slow, and it is spread across the whole street, so you only ever hear a piece of it.

It begins at the Pump House end, a minute or so before the cistern tips. Three taps from the nearest standard, dull and low, like a knuckle on a cast-iron pipe. Then a pause. Then three from the next standard up the street, fainter. Then the next. It travels away from the engine toward the stairwell, standard by standard. By the time it reaches the last lamp, the beam has begun its groan.

If you put your ear to the iron you can hear the thing inside moving. It's not a scrabbling. It's a careful, deliberate shifting, like someone settling their weight.

The things in the standards are real, and you can see one if you are patient and have a light. They are about the length of a finger and the colour of a peeled almond. They are wingless, eyeless, and plated like a woodlouse, but they have a single hard, slightly domed forelimb, and they use it to strike the inside of the iron. There's one to each standard. They seem never to leave. There is a smooth pale patch worn on the inner wall of each hollow post where, for longer than anyone can say, one of them has struck the same place.

You might decide they are the answer, the Works' own clockwork: something that learned the rhythm of the beam and passes it along the street. You'd be closer. But they are not tapping for each other. Each one waits until it hears the one before it, and then it taps, and then it goes still. It is not a conversation. It is a relay. It is a message being carried *to* somewhere.

The message runs away from the engine. It carries toward the rooms.

## The rooms

When a window is about to light, the crickets gather on its sill. You notice this early. It is the kind of thing a visitor writes down. What you notice later, if you notice it at all, is that the crickets begin to gather *before the tapping reaches that building*. They know first. There's something else that knows first, and they are reading it the way they read the lamps.

And then there is the matter of the silt.

The rooms that light are exactly as empty as the others, but the floors are different. In the unlit rooms the silt is grey and settled, and it holds the prints of rats and millipedes the way snow holds prints. In the rooms that light, the silt is darker and smoother. It is wet all the way through. It has no prints in it, and where your boot touches it, it's faintly, unmistakably warm.

The warmest thing for a mile is supposed to be the engine. The ferns on the Pump House wall know it, and the ferns are not wrong. But there's a second warmth in the Works that the ferns can't reach, because it moves, and it spends most of its life a long way down.

## What is in the shaft

The pump rod goes down through the floor into a shaft of black water that does not seem to fall. The description guesses that the pump might be keeping something dry that no longer exists, or keeping this place wet. There is a third possibility that the engine's builders would have understood, because they are the ones who arranged it: the pump keeps the water in the shaft at one exact level. Never higher, never lower, not by the width of a hand. It holds a level the way a nurse holds a temperature.

Someone lives at that level.

If you lie on the Pump House floor at the lip of the shaft with a lamp, at the bottom of a stroke, when the rod has gone home and the water is as still as it ever gets, you might see her. You'd be foolish to try, because the edge is slick and the water is very cold and very deep. The shape you see is pale in the way that the moss is pale. It is not white but the green-white of something grown without sun, flushed underneath with pink the way the inside of a wrist is pink. It is long, longer than you are, and it lies along the wall of the shaft in a slow curve, like a hand laid along the side of a bath.

What she is, anatomically, is not very strange. The deep caves of the world have things like her: blind, pale, long-bodied salamanders that keep their gills for life and never finish growing up. They can live a century. They can go years without eating. The old miners who found them in the karst springs called them *human fish*, because of their skin.

She is one of those, or she was. She has been down here a very long time, and she is very large, and she has not finished growing up either.

Her head is broad and flat and has no eyes, only two faint darker places under the skin where eyes might have been. On either side of her neck are three plumes of gill, feathered like ferns. They're pink at rest and they flush a deep, surprising red when she breathes hard. Her forelimbs are small, far too small for her body, delicate as a child's, with three fingers each. Her hind limbs have two. She holds them folded against her flanks, and when she moves she hardly uses them. She moves the way a rope moves when you pull it slowly out of water.

She breathes once an hour.

That is not a figure of speech. When the cistern tips and the beam begins to groan and the rod lifts streaming out of the shaft, the water in the shaft lifts with it, a hand's width, stirred and oxygenated and full of the cold air of the chamber, and she opens her gills into it. When the rod goes home, she closes them. The engine was built to pump. What it does now, among other things, is breathe for her.

The workers called her the Lamb.

## The Lamb

There were men here once, and of course the men found her. You can imagine it: the pump fitted, the shaft sunk, and one day at the bottom of a stroke someone with a lantern looking down and seeing that pale length along the wall. Someone swore. Someone laughed. Someone, one of them, the kind who is always the one, fetched a bit of his dinner and dropped it in.

They named her the way men name things they're a little afraid of and have decided to be fond of. Soft, pale, harmless, *a lamb*. The name went onto the maps as a joke and stayed there as a name. By the time the city rose over the quarter and the men stopped coming down the forty-one steps, nobody on the maps remembered why.

She remembered. It is the thing she is best at. A creature that eats once in a season and breathes once an hour has a great deal of time, and nothing to fill it with but the shapes of things that happened. And for a certain number of years, the thing that happened was the shift.

She learned it the way the eels learned the weir. The foreman's lamp coming on in the morning meant nothing to her; she has never seen light. But the tread of boots on the floor above the shaft, the clank of the stoking-door, the footsteps along the street to the mess room at midday, the scrape of a chair, the particular sound of a tin cup set down on a table, those she learned. She learned them through the water, which carries sound the way iron carries it. She learned the shape of a working day, and the long hush of Sundays. She learned which rooms were warm, and when. And at some point, the way a cat learns the sound of a particular cupboard, she learned that the midday room was where the men went to eat, and that there were scraps, and that the floor there was always a little wet from boots.

So she went. It isn't far. There's the culvert and the channels under the street, and the gutter itself, which carries water enough for something built like a rope. She came up through the floor drain of the mess room, which is why that drain is wide and smooth-lipped now, polished like the pale places in the lamp standards. She lay in the wet under the table, and the men fed her.

The men are gone. The shift is not.

## What she keeps

This is the thing that grows clearer the longer you stay, and the thing people don't say when they talk about Lamb's Sluice, if they talk about it at all.

The rooms don't light and then call her. She goes to them, and then they light.

Whatever relays the wiring once had are long since fused and furred and silent. But the old floors had plates set into them, iron treadles by the doors and under the foreman's desk and beside the mess room table. They were a builder's fancy, a way for the lights to come on when a man stood in a room and go off when he left. They still work, after a fashion. They are nearly rusted closed. It takes something heavy and patient and cold to press one down, and to lie on it for three strokes, and then to slide away.

The tappers in the lamp standards are not carrying the engine's rhythm up the street. They are carrying it *to her*, wherever she is lying in the channels under the cobbles. She is too slow and too deaf to the air to feel the beam's groan from so far away, but she can feel iron. Three taps, and three, and three, travelling up the street like a message passed hand to hand: *the stroke is coming, the stroke is coming*. And she, who keeps the timetable, knows which stroke it is. It's the eleventh, the upstairs room in the second building. Time to go and sit in it. The crickets see the silt stir and gather on the sill.

When you come into one of the lit rooms and find it empty, it is not empty. You are standing on her. The dark smooth warm silt is the top of her back, under a skin of water and sediment. She lies in the room the way the men used to sit in it, filling it, and the bulb overhead glows its sleepy orange because her weight is on the plate. The damp glitters in the air around it. You feel the room is occupied because it is.

Then she hears, or rather feels, the groan of the beam through the floor. She lifts. The treadle rises. The light fades. The silt settles back into the shape of an empty floor, and something as long as a man goes back down the drain as quietly as water.

She isn't haunting the rooms. She's working in them. She is the last shift of Lamb's Sluice, and she has been working it alone for longer than the men who taught it to her were alive. She doesn't know they are gone. It isn't clear she knows what gone would mean. She knows that at midday there is a warm wet room, and she goes to it, and she waits. That's all a shift ever was.

## What she is becoming

Here is where it keeps going, if you let it.

The Works is in patient equilibrium, the description says. It has worn everything down to the shape the water prefers and now simply maintains it. That's true. But the water isn't doing the maintaining. The water only wears. Something has to decide which shape to keep.

Look at the rooms the Lamb uses. Their floors are wet, and so they hold, while the floors of the unused rooms have silted up and slumped. Look at the gutter, whose central channel is the exact width of her body. Look at the drains, polished smooth. Look at the moss rings around the lamps, which are rings and not a carpet because she moves between the lamps, and grazes the moss, slowly, the way a sheep would, and has kept those rings the same size for longer than any lichen has been counted. Look at the eels: they ride the weir *after* she breathes, when the water is richest. They are following her gradient, not the engine's.

She is not a creature living in a place. The place, more and more, is a creature she is growing. The street is her body's shape pressed into stone. The lamps are hers, because her weight keeps the current that feeds them in balance: a dynamo pulling exactly the load it can bear, forever. The engine breathes for her, and she keeps its water level by being the displacement it was calibrated for, a long pale weight that never leaves the shaft for more than three strokes at a time. If she died, the level would drop by the width of a hand. The float valve would drift. The cistern would tip early, then earlier. The whole slow clock would speed up and run down, the way a heart does when its rhythm fails.

The Works idles for ten thousand years because she does.

And she is still, at whatever age she is, not grown up. That's what her kind does. They keep their gills. They stay children, and keep growing, and never change into the land-going adult shape that other salamanders become. Down here, with no reason ever to change, she has simply kept on growing into the only thing there is to grow into, which is the place itself. The strokes, the lamps, the rooms, the shifts. A lamb that has become the fold.

## The drips

On your way out, the drip-notes behind you begin to rearrange, and you realise they have a shape. They cluster around the arches like the windows seem almost to make a timetable.

They are hers too. Not that she makes them. Water makes them, falling from the ceiling. But for a very long time, something long and warm has been moving through this vault on a schedule. It lies here for three strokes, there for one. Its faint heat rises into the arches above whichever room it's in, and the seepage in those arches runs very slightly faster. The minerals there build their white glaze a little thinner and their amber a little thicker. Over a century the ceiling has been written on by her warmth, the way a path is written into grass. Every drip that falls follows the shape of her day.

You're hearing her timetable. The Works has taught it to you in less than a day, the way the shaft taught it to her.

## After

For a few days you will pause, about once an hour, as if listening for something.

You'll put it down to the strokes, to the lean still in your ankles and the groan still in your chest. That's part of it. But it's also this: for a few hours, you stood in her rooms and walked her street and ate, probably, a sandwich on the Pump House steps at the bottom of a stroke. You were the first worker at Lamb's Sluice in a very long time. Something far below, in cold water, noticed the tread of boots, and set that down beside all the other treads she remembers.

If you went back, she would know you. She might even come up through the drain at midday, into the room with the tin cup, and lie there in the warm wet under the table, and wait to see whether you had brought anything.

Most people don't go back. She doesn't mind. She has a shift to keep.
