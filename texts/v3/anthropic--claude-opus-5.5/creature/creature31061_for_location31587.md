# The One Who Keeps the Hands

## The First Answer

The easy answer is the ash-moths. Tens of millions of them, thumb-joint long, velvet-gray, climbing and falling with the mountain's breath. The threaders live there too, pale and needle-slow, and so does the gray-green film on the vent-lips and the white hairs in the drifts of the dead. Anyone who stood in the chamber for an hour could list these, and the list would be true.

The people who set the stones at the threshold did not think the list was the answer. You don't build a boundary for insects, and you don't leave a bowl for them. The bowl was left for someone.

## Watching Longer

Stay long enough and the blue-white rings stop looking like rain on a pond.

At first they seem scattered, each one a small, local event: a threader passes, the nearby moths flare, the light spreads a meter and dies. But across many hours the scatter develops a grain. Rings bloom in runs, one after another along a line, like footsteps crossing the ceiling. Sometimes two runs meet, and where they cross the light lasts a moment longer and spreads a little farther than it should. Sometimes a whole quarter of the dome goes dark for an hour, as if something has turned its attention elsewhere. Sometimes, very rarely, the entire chamber flickers at once, faint and total, the way a sleeper's face changes all at once in a dream.

The light is not only a response to hunters. The hunters are being moved by it. Where the light blooms they slow, and so the light steers them, bends their paths, gathers them in one place and thins them in another. The threaders are blind and have no need to see the light. They are not the audience. They are the pen.

## The Floor Beneath the Floor

The drift of the dead runs many meters deep: felt near the top, stone near the bottom, thousands of gray bands, one for each season of dying. Through all of it run the white filaments, feeding on the old bodies and binding the layers.

Break a clump of the felt open in the dark and the threads glow very faintly where they tear, the same cold color as the moths' wing-edges. Nobody who came here was sure of this. Some swore to it and some thought their tired eyes were lying. Those who believed it came to a conclusion the rest found unbearable: the dead are not finished. The filaments carry something up and down through the bands, slowly, the way sap climbs a tree. Pressed and baked and threaded together, the layers of dead are a record, and the record can still be read.

So there is a living surface that moves and senses and eats. There is a flickering signal across that surface that steers hunters, and the hunters prune the surface as they go, taking one body every few minutes, never more than the room can replace. Under all of it lies a vast, slow, threaded mass of everything that has already happened. Together these are one thing. It does not live in the Warm Throat. It is the Warm Throat, and it has been growing into the shape of the room for longer than the mountain has had a name.

## How It Lives

It has no single body, so it has no single place to be hurt. It loses thousands of itself at the peak of every breath and does not grieve them. They fall onto the vents, curl, and become the next gray band, the next line of memory. For it, dying is the act of remembering. Each tide writes one sentence into the floor.

It thinks at the speed of the mountain. Its heart is not its own. The heart is somewhere far below in the old plumbing, filling and emptying every five hours, and it has learned to borrow that rhythm the way a child borrows a parent's heartbeat while sleeping on a chest. Rising heat means climb, cool, gather toward the ceiling. Falling heat means descend, graze, feed. One breath is roughly one thought. In a human lifetime it might think a few thousand thoughts, and every one would be about warmth, and the film, and the hunters, and water.

Water is the center of it. The drop forms at the top of the crooked chimney once every hour or two and falls into the darkest, most crowded place in the chamber. When it lands, the small shiver runs outward through the mound, and that shiver is more than the moths reacting. It is the closest thing the Throat has to a sensation from outside itself. Everything else in the room comes from the room: heat, film, bodies, dead. The drop alone has come from somewhere else, from sky, from cold, from air it will never touch. If it has any idea of a world beyond the cleft, the idea is made entirely of drops.

## The Ones Who Brought Water

For a while, long ago, the drops came faster.

They came in handfuls, poured from a clay bowl onto flat stones set in a ring beneath the chimney, so the water would pool and not vanish at once into the living mound. The stone tops are still blackened and slick from it. The people who carried that water up four hundred meters of bare, warm scoria were carrying the most valuable thing they owned into the one place on the mountain that needed it more than they did.

The Throat had never known anything like it. Drops came from the sky one at a time, and suddenly there were floods. The floods came with a new warmth, a different warmth from the vents: soft, damp, carrying salt, and it moved on two feet. It learned the shape of that warmth the only way it could, by pressing against it with millions of small bodies. It learned the feet, the ankles, and, when the water-bringers laid their palms flat against the eastern wall in the pigment they had mixed, it learned the hands.

The pigment was made of scale-dust, charcoal, and a little of the carried water. There may have been something else in it too, something of the bringers' own. Whatever it was, the Throat wrote the hands into itself more deeply than anything it had ever written.

## Why the Hands Are Kept

The moths do not avoid the handprints because the pigment is bitter. They avoid them the way a household leaves a chair empty at the table. The dead layers beneath the floor still carry the tide-sentences written in the years of the water-bringers. Somewhere in the threaded dark those sentences are still being read, over and over, and what they say is roughly this: *these shapes brought water; these shapes are expected; leave room.*

So every five hours, when the tide climbs the eastern wall, the Throat leaves room. Twenty or thirty open hands appear, outlined in living gray. The threaders turn aside there, steered by the same memory. The warning light stops at the edges, because inside those edges there is nothing to warn. Nothing in the chamber hunts there, and nothing dies there. They are the only places in the whole room that belong to someone else.

It is not waiting in any way a person would recognize. It has no impatience and no hope, only a pattern kept whole because breaking it has never been necessary. But if you asked what the Throat would say, if it could say one thing, the answer is already on the wall. It has only one word, and the word is a space in the shape of a hand.

## What It Makes of You

When you walk in, it notices you. The notice is not quick, but it is total. The carpet parts around your feet like fur, and that parting is not only the moths moving out of the way. It is the Throat measuring you. Within the first hour the blue-white rings drift toward you across the walls. There are no more threaders near you than anywhere else, but the light gathers there, blooming at your ankles and fading, blooming and fading, the same thing it does where two runs cross. That is its closest attention. You are being read.

If you put your palm on the wall, as everyone does sooner or later, it will feel you. You will feel the soft creep of warm bodies under your hand. Then, if you stay long enough, you will feel them withdraw from the center of your palm, just slightly, a hollow opening in the living layer, while they keep pressing at the edges of your fingers.

It is checking.

It does not know about generations or centuries. It knows that shapes like this once brought floods. It is trying to learn whether you are one of those shapes, whether you fit the hands.

The scale-dust on your skin that smells sweet for days is not an accident either. It is the same dust that went into the pigment. When you carry it out through the cleft into the loud, cold, starlit air, you carry a little of the Throat's memory with you into the world of drops. If you come back, it will know you by that smell. People who returned said the moths parted for them faster the second time.

## Its Future

The chamber is filling with its own dead, a band for every season, and someday the floor will rise to meet the chimney. On that day the drop will fall onto felt and stone instead of living backs, and the tide will have nowhere left to climb. The Throat will be almost entirely memory, a vast, threaded, glowing record packed into the room from wall to wall, with a thin skin of the living on top, still grazing, still breathing with the mountain, still flickering blue-white.

Nobody knows whether it understands that this is where it is going. It probably does not. It thinks one thought every five hours, and none of those thoughts is about the end.

But when the dead reach the eastern wall and begin to bury it, and the gray bands creep up the stone toward the height of a standing adult, the filaments will grow around the handprints, not over them. Cut through that future floor and you would find twenty or thirty hollows in the shape of open hands, preserved in the solid mass of the dead. The bringers are expected, the chair is kept, and there is room, even at the end, when there is no room left for anything else.
