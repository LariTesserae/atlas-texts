# Who Lives in the Accretion Highlands

## I. The first answer is the obvious one

People live here. Start with them, because they are easiest to see. It would be a mistake to stop with them, though, and they would be the first to tell you so.

Look at their hands. Everyone on the highland has the same hands: broad, dry, with a fine white map of old cuts across the palms and the pads of the fingers. The rock is too new to be gentle. It has no rounded edges anywhere, so every child who learns to walk here also learns to fall onto something that bites. Children's first scars come early, usually on the heels of the hands. Adults compare them the way people elsewhere might compare birthmarks. *That one was the step outside my grandmother's kitchen. That one was the rim, the year I was nine, when I shouldn't have been there.*

They wear felted boots of lichen-deer wool, thick-soled and replaced often, because the stone chews through anything. Their outer clothes are wool too, oiled against the mizzle, in the colors the dye-plants of the Core allow: grey, a smoky brown, a faint green from moss, and a rare deep yellow from a saxifrage root that is saved for weddings. From a distance a traveling group looks like a patch of the plateau that has decided to move. This is not an accident. Wolves notice bright things.

They are quiet people. It isn't shyness. They grew up in a place where sound doesn't go far and there is little of it, so a raised voice feels like a kind of violence. In the cities people talk at the volume of a courtyard, which is to say about the volume of a pool-lip lapping. An outsider, if one ever came, would think everyone was telling secrets. They laugh easily, but softly, and often with their eyes before their mouths.

## II. How they count

They are the only people I can imagine who measure a life in the ground beneath it.

The plateau rises about a finger's width a year at the center. So in the older cities, especially those near the Core, age is spoken of in fingers. A child is "six fingers." An old man is "seventy fingers and still climbing stairs." Everyone understands the phrase literally as well as figuratively. The ground you stand on has risen that much since you were born. You have been lifted.

At the rim the arithmetic changes. Out there the rock grows faster and in shelves rather than layers, so rim-folk count in *winters*: how many frosts the stone under their feet has taken. "One-winter stone" is ground you don't trust. "Ten-winter stone" will probably hold you. When a rim child is born, some families mark a small patch of unmarked new rock and bring the child back to it every clearing season to count the cracks. The crazing of that patch and the life of that child advance together, one fine polygonal net thickening year on year, until the child is grown and the stone is ordinary and grey with the first lichens. Adults who were raised this way sometimes go back and sit on their patch when they are troubled. It is hard to describe what this does for them. Something like visiting a sibling who never speaks.

## III. The asking

This is the center of their life, and it took me a while to understand how central it is.

The cities are not built and then finished. They are *held open*. Every street is a channel the lattice has been asked not to fill. The lattice does not forget, but neither does it stop. Water keeps seeping, mineral keeps settling, and the plateau keeps wanting to become one continuous body of stone. A street left alone for a generation grows shallower and narrower. A courtyard slowly lifts its floor. A doorway, over enough years, becomes a niche and then a slight irregularity in a wall.

So every city has its keepers, and the keeping is daily.

In a city I'll call Vesk, an old district near the Core, the street-keepers walk at dawn, before the mizzle has lifted. They are mostly older people. It is considered good work for the last part of a life, because it's slow and it has to be done with attention. Each keeper has a route of perhaps forty streets. They walk it with one hand trailing along the walls at hip height, palm flat. Every so often they stop, press harder, and hold still. Then they do something with their fingers, a pattern of pressure or a rhythm, that I can describe but they can't really teach. Apprentices learn it by laying their own hand over the keeper's for years.

What they are saying, as far as anyone can put it into words, is: *not here. Keep this open. Thank you for the wall; not the street.*

Some keepers say nothing aloud. Some murmur. In Vesk there is an old keeper named Sessa who hums. It's a tune with no words, very low, and the people on her route have heard it under their windows every morning of their lives. When she was ill one winter and her apprentice walked the route instead, people woke uneasy without knowing why. The streets stayed open. The lattice doesn't seem to care about humming. But the district noticed.

You might think this would feel like a burden, a whole civilization pushing constantly against a slow tide. It doesn't, or not in the way you'd expect. The people don't think of the lattice as a tide or an enemy. They think of the relationship as a conversation that happens to be very long. Asking is how you stay in it. A city that stops asking isn't destroyed. It is *finished*, in the sense a sentence is finished.

## IV. The finished cities

Out on the open plateau, days from any living town, there are hills.

This is strange in itself, because the highland has almost no relief. The accretion planes are too even. But here and there, rising out of the flats, are long low mounds of the same blue-black stone, smooth-shouldered and lichened, sometimes with a regular stepped quality along one side. Deer shelter against them. Falcons perch on them. Travelers following the sky-lines pass them and grow quiet.

These are cities that stopped asking. Nobody is sure how many there are. The records name some of them. Others are known only because, on certain slopes, in low evening light, the shadows fall in a way that suggests a street, a line of arcade, the ghost of a tower sunk to its shoulders. In one of them, called the Drowned Bell (though it didn't drown and there's no bell), the tip of a spire still stands a hand's height above the mound. It has been rounded by weather, which makes it the only rounded stone on the entire plateau. People make the journey just to touch it.

What happened to those cities is not usually dramatic. A sickness, a hard decade, a slow drift of families to other towns. The keepers grow fewer, their routes grow longer, and eventually a district is let go. Then another. The lattice is patient. It fills the cellars first, then the lower rooms, then the streets. The houses were grown from the same stone, so the city doesn't collapse. It becomes solid. Over a few centuries a town turns from a thing with an inside into a thing with only an outside.

The highlanders are not horrified by this. They are moved by it, which is different. The finished cities are the plateau's elders. Walking past one, you lower your voice.

## V. Their dead

It follows, I think, that they would give their dead the same way.

They don't burn them. There is nothing to burn with, and no one here would waste the heat. They don't bury them in earth, because there is no earth, only the thin carried soil of the gardens, and that is for the living. Instead, the family chooses a basin: a small low place out beyond the city's outer fields where two accretion planes meet and water gathers. The body is laid in the pool, wrapped in undyed wool. And the family asks.

Asking for the dead is the one kind of asking everyone learns. It doesn't take a keeper's training, and nobody would let a stranger do it for you. You kneel at the pool's edge with your palms flat on the wet rock and you ask the lattice to take them. Not quickly. It isn't quick. The water will still be there next spring, clear to the bottom, and you will be able to see the shape under it. The midges will hatch over it. The phalaropes will spin in it. By the time you are old, the pool will be shallower, and the shape will be a smoothness in the floor of it. Your grandchildren will find a slight rise in the flats that holds water a little differently than the stone around it.

They have a phrase for death that I find very beautiful: *gone under the water*, or sometimes just *gone low*. It is also, not coincidentally, what they call the careless builder's mistake. *Don't build low.* Low is where the land takes things back.

Here is what took me longest to see. Because the whole plateau rises together, the dead rise too. In a thousand years the woman you laid in the pool will be a finger's width of stone a thousand times over, and the pool that held her will be buried beneath new pools. She will not be lost. She will be *under*, part of the rising floor, carried upward with everything else. The highlanders don't think of the dead as departed. They think of them as foundation.

When someone in Vesk is being stubborn, their friends say, "You're asking to be a wall."

## VI. What the record-stones hold

Every city keeps an archive: long cool rooms deep in the oldest stone, lined with record-stones. Each is a smooth dark lozenge about the size of a loaf. Touched a certain way, it speaks with the voice of whoever filled it. Some are recipes. Some are legal decisions, arguments between cities, genealogies, songs. A great many are simply people talking about their lives to whoever might be listening later. The archivists call these the *hearth-stones*, and they are the most requested, especially in mist season, when the long wet months make people want company from any direction, including backward.

The archives go back a long way, farther than the oldest standing district of any living city. Their order is careful. Their keepers are proud, and slightly strange from spending so much time with voices.

At the very back of the oldest archive in Vesk there is a shelf of stones no one can read.

They aren't broken. When you touch them the right way, they answer. But what they hold isn't a language anyone living knows. The archivists argue about it. Some think it's the speech of whoever first came to the plateau. Others think it's older than that. There is a third, smaller faction, which I think is the most honest. They have spent long afternoons with those stones, and they say quietly that what they hold doesn't sound like speech at all. It sounds like water moving through stone. Seeping, pausing, dripping, seeping again. A rhythm with structure in it, the way a keeper's fingers have structure in them.

When asked what they think that means, this faction shrugs, or says, "It means someone was talking to it before we were." Or, rarely, very softly: "Or it was talking."

I'll come back to that.

## VII. The ones who go between

The cities are far apart, and the space between them is serious. Four days of open rock. Cold water, weather that crosses the flats like a dark hand, wolves in the Core, and in mist season a whiteness so total you can't see your own boots. And yet the cities marry each other, trade with each other, argue, visit, send children for apprenticeship. So there are people who travel.

They go in clearing season, in groups, following the sky-lines. The lines run at about twice a person's height, straight across the plateau, tethered every few hundred paces to posts that grew out of the rock. Walking under one is like walking under a single drawn breath. Travelers say that if you put your hand to a tether-post in the right way, you can feel the weather in the next city. Not see it or hear it. Feel it, as a kind of heaviness or lightness in the forearm, a pressure behind the eyes. Old line-walkers can tell you, with their palm on a post in the middle of nowhere, that it's raining in a city three days east. They're usually right.

The people who go between are a particular kind. They're rarely the ones who stay. They have more scars on their hands than most, because they sleep on open rock. Their laughter is a little louder, because they've grown used to open space. They carry things: salt, dyes, seeds, letters in record-stones wrapped in wool, and occasionally a child being taken to meet grandparents for the first time. In every city there's a word for them that means something like "threads." When a thread comes into town after a crossing, people come out of their courtyards to hear the news, and the thread stands in the square steaming slightly in the cold, telling it, in a voice pitched just a little too loud for the stone around them.

What the threads carry, more than anything, is the knowledge that the other cities are still asking. That the lights on the horizon on clear nights aren't finished yet.

## VIII. The others who live here, who were here first

The highlanders are very clear that they are not the main inhabitants of the plateau and never have been. They say it plainly, and it isn't false modesty. It's arithmetic.

A single spring bloom in a single pool holds more lives than a city. On a still evening in the Middle Reaches, the midge clouds rising off the flats are so dense they darken the air like smoke, and they whine faintly, a sound you feel in your teeth before you hear it. There are fairy shrimp by the billion, transparent and rowing on their backs. Water fleas pulse like tiny hearts. In any pool there are copepods, rotifers, and the green films of algae that come within a season of the basin forming. You can watch it happen at the rim. New stone, new pool, clear water, nothing. Then a faint greenness, as if the stone were blushing. Then movement.

The shorebirds come up from below the cloud line every year to breed. The highlanders greet them the way other people might greet the first warm day. There are plovers in their scrapes on bare stone, their eggs the exact speckled grey of lichen, invisible until you are about to step on them. There are phalaropes spinning in the pools like tops, stirring food up out of the water, the females bright and the males dull. The highlanders know this reversal and are fond of it. The stilts stand on legs too long for the world. And the larks sing at dawn, briefly, high over the flats: the only real music on most mornings. In Vesk they say a lark's song is the plateau clearing its throat.

Then the rim colonies. Tens of thousands of dark-backed, white-breasted seabirds nest packed on ledges that did not exist twenty years ago. This is something to sit with. Every generation of these birds raises its chicks on ground younger than the parents. Some of the old birds must remember when their ledge was open air, just cloud and fall. They come home low over the cloud sea in long silent lines, and they land on stone that is still weeping, still faintly warm from within. The din of the colony is the loudest living sound on the plateau. The highlanders who live at the rim say they can't sleep in the quiet of the inland cities. They miss the screaming.

The lichen-deer are the ones the people love most, I think. They are stocky, woolly, grey as the stone, and they cross the plateau in their slow migration like weather made of animals. They scrape lichen from the slabs with hard lower lips and lie down in the lee of pools when the wind rises. They come through the cities' outer fields, and nobody fences them. In storms they shelter against the city walls, and the stone there runs warm, because the people ask it to. It's a small gift, and the lattice gives it easily. Children in Vesk grow up falling asleep to the sound of deer breathing on the other side of their bedroom wall: a long, soft, steady huff through stone. They find other places' silence hard to bear.

The wolves follow the deer through the Core. Their tracks cross the frost-crazed slabs in winter, and on very quiet nights their howls come in from miles off, thin as a thread pulled through cloth. The highlanders don't love the wolves, but they don't hate them either. They say the wolves are the plateau's way of reminding the deer to keep moving. When the threads travel, they carry deer-horn whistles. The wolves generally leave a group of people alone, and people generally leave the wolves alone, and this has been true for as long as the archives remember.

The pikas whistle alarms from the oldest cracked rock. The stoats turn white in winter. The foxes raid the plovers' scrapes. The grey falcons sit on the highest points of city towers and stoop over the flats. The swallows nest in grown eaves, and the pigeons roost in stone that was grown for people and taken over by birds. None of these is a guest. The phrase the highlanders use, if you ask them what these animals are, translates roughly as *the older neighbors*.

## IX. And the first of all

Go to the rim at dawn, to the very newest shelf, where nothing nests yet, not even the seabirds, and stand in the thin steam rising off the stone.

Put your hand down. The rock is very slightly warm. It is damp, but not from rain; the dampness is coming from inside. Water is beading out of pores that haven't closed yet. There is no lichen. There is no frost-crazing, because this stone hasn't lived through a winter. It's as unmarked as anything in the world.

But it isn't empty.

If the highlanders' naturalists are right, and they have looked closely with thin-stone lenses, the weeping pores are already inhabited. Something is living in the film of water on new stone before the stone is even finished. It is too small to see, a slick and a faint sheen and a smell like struck flint and rainwater. It is the first colonist. It arrives before the algae, before the midges, before any bird. Where it comes from, nobody is sure. Maybe the wind, maybe the groundwater, maybe the lattice itself, carried up out of the deep like the minerals.

Rim-folk have a name for this film. They call it *the breath*. The steam you see on cold mornings, where the warm new stone meets the freezing air, is, they say, the plateau exhaling. They know perfectly well it's condensation. They say it anyway.

## X. So: who lives here?

This is where I stopped being sure, and I think the highlanders would respect that.

The obvious answer is the people, and they do live here, with their scarred hands and quiet voices, their keepers humming at dawn and their dead gone low. The better answer is the people and the older neighbors: the deer and the wolves and the billions in every pool, the seabirds on ledges younger than themselves, and the first film on the weeping rock. That answer is true too.

But there is one more candidate. The highlanders argue about it in the long evenings of mist season, the way people argue about questions they know they can't settle.

The engineers in Vesk speak of the lattice in careful, hedged language. They say it's a mechanism, very old, very large, built by someone for some purpose. It draws mineral from the groundwater and lays it down in planes. It responds to certain patterns of pressure. It runs warm where asked. They know a great deal about *how* it responds and almost nothing about *why*. They'll tell you that, if you ask plainly.

The keepers, who spend their lives with a hand on the walls, tend to say something different. Not that the lattice is alive exactly. They're cautious people. But that it *answers*, and that answering is not nothing. Sessa, who hums on her route, once said that forty years of asking had taught her the lattice had moods.
