# What Lives in the Cut Cistern

## The First Answer

The easy answer is *nothing*. The room says so itself: no fish, no snails, nothing skating the surface. It is new-cut and too recently opened. Water is the only thing that moves in it.

That answer doesn't survive a full day of watching, though. It fails in the dark, around the hour before dawn, when the roar seems loudest and there is nothing to look at.

## The Knocking

Start with the sound nobody accounts for.

The overflow step roars. The spring hisses through the rough opening. The condensation drips its small bright notes. All of these have causes you can point to. Under them, from somewhere deeper in the rock, comes the knocking: a pulse of pressure moving through fissures no one has seen.

Listen to it long enough and it stops sounding like geology. Geology doesn't keep a rhythm like this, three knocks, a pause, then two. It doesn't repeat itself and then vary, the way a person repeats a question more slowly the second time.

What it sounds like, more than anything, is chisel work. It sounds like the long diagonal strokes of the cutters, heard from the far side of the stone.

For however many months this chamber took to cut, something in the rock listened to that work. Mallet on iron and iron on limestone, band by band, one worker stopping and another beginning, the rhythm shifting slightly the way the angle of the marks shifts. It went on day after day and came closer every day. Then one day the cutters broke through into the source, and the sound stopped.

Whatever lives here has been knocking ever since. It learned the pattern from them, and it is waiting for an answer.

## The Shape at the Rough Opening

It is shy about the light, and it comes out at night.

In the gray-blue before dawn, the heat haze at the east end thickens, and something comes forward through it. At first you can't separate it from the shimmer. It is exactly the temperature of the water around it, a little above body heat, and exactly as clear. You notice it only because the lines of stone behind it bend *wrong*. They bend in a shape rather than a blur.

It is soft and filamentous. The first time you see it you might think it is a pale weed that grew overnight: a loose bundle of long threads, glassy and colorless, streaming from the lip of the rough opening in the current. The threads are too deliberate for weed, though. They reach and hesitate. They lie flat against the new stone, and in the dim light you can see them *taste* it, running slowly along the tool grooves, into the crowded short marks at the corners, over the fitted plugs in the dark band of the north wall.

They linger longest on the plugs. Those are the places where the cutters struggled, and they are the one spot in the room where someone chose one stone over another.

## Its Trail

The travertine is its doing.

The description of this place says the thin cream crust follows "the current's preferred route, as if the water were already drawing a map of itself." That is closer to true than it sounds. The crust is where the threads go. Every night they carry the spring's dissolved stone out across the floor and set it down, grain by grain, as fine as frost. They lay it along the path they have traveled most often, so the crust is less a stain than a trail. It is a worn path, the way a deer trail through grass is a worn path.

Given centuries, the description says, that crust would soften every chisel mark in the room. The inhabitant does not want that. It follows the tool marks with its threads, and it has been careful to leave them clean. It lays its stone *beside* the grooves and never in them. The marks are the only record it has of whatever made the knocking, and it is keeping them.

## Its Breath

The bubbles clustering on the underside of the vault are not only gas from the spring.

Some of them are. But the thread-thing draws something out of the water as it moves, some faint sourness that gathers in its filaments and is released in fine silver beads. Those rise and join the skin on the ceiling, in the thin lens of air between water and stone. The skin trembles, and every so often it lets go all at once. That happens when the creature, somewhere below and behind the opening, draws itself back suddenly into the rock. The pressure changes and the ceiling exhales.

So when you see the silver skin collapse, something has startled. When the heat haze thickens and fine grit spills from the opening on the days the spring "pulses harder," the thing is moving its bulk inside the fissures, turning over, and dragging its larger body through channels just wide enough for it.

## How Much of It There Is

This is where the image has to grow, because the thread-bundle at the opening is not the animal. It is only the tip.

Follow the knocking back. It comes from several places in the rock, and sometimes from two places at once. Follow the warmth back, too. The chamber sits on a seep the cutters were tracking. The heat has to come from somewhere deep, and it has to travel a long way up through stone to get here.

What lives in the Cut Cistern lives mostly *outside* it. Its body is threaded through the hill: a network of warm, pale, living filaments packed into every crack and bedding plane that carries water. It runs down toward whatever deep heat feeds the spring, and outward along the aquifer, under fields and under the village downslope. It is less like an animal than a fungus or a root system, if a root system could be translucent, move, and listen. It may be very old. There is no reason in the stone why it shouldn't be as old as the water.

For all that time it has lived in total darkness, in narrow places, in water moving slow and hot and heavy with minerals. It has had nothing for company but the ticking of the rock and, lately, the chisels.

Then the chisels broke into its body. The rough opening, the one undressed hole in the room, "the place where the builders' plan met the spring's intentions," is a wound, or it was one. It has healed around the edges into something else.

## The Eye

Here is what the cutters did not know they were making.

A drowned box, walled in white stone, lit from a single square shaft, with the light striking the floor and flooding the chamber from below. That is a cistern. It is also the shape of an eye: a dark chamber, one aperture, and a pale surface at the back where the light lands.

The creature has never seen anything. Its kind had no use for sight in the fissures. But it can feel light as warmth on its outermost threads, faintly, the way you feel a lamp through closed eyelids. Over the months since the breakthrough it has been learning to use the chamber the way an animal uses an eye.

This is why it comes out at night and withdraws before noon. It isn't that the light hurts. The light is *too much*, a sense so new that at midday it floods everything. In the incandescent hour, when the room glows like a lantern and every chisel mark casts a shivering shadow, the thing stays back in the opening. Only its finest threads lie at the edge of the heat haze, trembling, as if the whole animal were squinting.

And the algae, the green patch on the floor the size of a sleeping mat, "making a map of where the light falls over the course of the year"? The creature did not plant it. The algae came down the shaft as spores, like all the other small green things. But it has *allowed* the patch. It feeds around the edges and never touches the center. The patch is stretched east to west along the path of the sun because the sun stretched it, and the creature has let that record stand. The algae is its first retina. It is the part of the eye that keeps the image. On cloudy days the thing goes out and lays its threads across the green, the way you would put your hand on a page to feel where the words were pressed.

On the milky days after heavy rain, when the water turns soft and diffuse and the tool marks vanish, the creature is at its boldest. It will come all the way out then, and in the cloud-light you might see it: a pale haze of threads filling the eastern third of the chamber, swaying, spread as wide as it can reach, drinking in light that for once is gentle enough to bear.

## The Root

There is one more thing in the room it is paying attention to.

A root hangs from the thornbush above, down through the shaft wall, its tip "just short of the water, reaching and not quite reaching."

The creature has noticed the root. On still nights, when the spring is quiet, a few of its longest threads rise from the floor through fifteen feet of water toward the surface. They go straight up under the shaft, and they stop a finger's width below the waterline, directly under the dangling tip.

They wait there, the way the root waits.

It has never touched anything that came from *above*. Everything it has ever known rose from below or seeped in from the sides. The root is the first sign that there is a world on the other side of the eye, a world with sun in it that sends things down. Every week the root grows a little. Every week the threads rise a little higher to meet it. When they finally touch, something will begin that has never happened in that hill.

## What It Is Becoming

So what lives in the Cut Cistern?

A very large, very old, very slow animal that has lived its whole existence in the dark body of a hill. The chisels woke it, and it is still answering them. Its tip lies in a room of white stone, and it is using that room to learn to see.

It keeps the tool marks clean because they are its only letters. It lays its stone beside them in a pale script of its own. It knocks the workers' rhythm back at the rock in case they return. It lets the algae keep the sun's shape. It holds its threads under the root the way a person holds out a hand in the dark and waits for another hand.

Someday the description's prophecy will come true. Something will come down the shaft or up the channel and live here: a snail, a frog, a pale shrimp riding the overflow back uphill. When it does, it won't be arriving in an empty room. The room will already be occupied, and whoever arrives will be seen. That may be the first time anything in that hill has watched another living thing move.

Until then the chamber roars, and glows at noon, and is dim and loud at dawn. In the dark before sunrise, if you put your ear to the stone above the spring, you'll hear it: three knocks, a pause, then two.
