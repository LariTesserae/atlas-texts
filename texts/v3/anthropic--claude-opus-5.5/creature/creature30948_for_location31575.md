# Who Lives in the Kettle

## The Ones You Meet

Start with the dozen. They are the easiest to see and the least of what is there.

There is the man at the valve. His name is chalked on the door behind him as *Tobin*, in a hand that is not his own. He has been adjusting the same brass wheel since before you arrived. A quarter-turn on the bright, a pause through the dim, another quarter-turn. If you ask what the valve does, he tells you it feeds the ferns on the fourth terrace, and that they like it just a little warmer in the season when the Breath comes early. He does not say how he knows what season it is. He offers you a bean from his pocket, still warm, and while you eat it he makes one more quarter-turn and seems satisfied. He will be satisfied with it for about a week, and then he will begin again.

There is the woman with the cup. She waters her troughs one cupful at a time, one cupful per stroke, pouring on the swell of light and dipping again on its fading. Her troughs run the length of a terrace. You work it out later: at her pace, watering them all takes most of a day. She does it every day. She is not slow the way old people are slow. Her hands are steady and her back is straight. She is slow the way a tide is slow. It is simply the speed she is.

There is a boy of perhaps twelve who times his runs across the gantries to the swelling light and laughs when he makes it. There is a pair of sisters who mend belting in the weaving sheds and finish each other's sentences so gradually that they seem to be one person speaking. There is an old man on the bench at the Landing who has a cup of something in his hands and does not drink it while you watch.

They are kind. They feed you. They answer every question except one, and they answer that one too, if you listen properly.

"Where is everyone?"

*In. About. Sleeping.*

They are not deflecting. Those are the three true answers, and they are given in order.

## The Ones Who Are In

Behind the shut doors, people are living. They are living more slowly than the dozen you meet.

This is the first thing the Kettle does to you, and it does it gently. The Engine's stroke gets into your chest within a day. Your heartbeat settles toward it. Your breathing settles toward your heartbeat. After a month you pause on the dim without deciding to. After a year you have stopped counting hours at all. The quick ones, the dozen, are the ones who have been here a few years at most. They are still close enough to your speed to hand you a bean.

The ones who are *in* have been here longer. They have gone further down the slope.

When you hear a door close late at night on a terrace you thought was empty, you are hearing the end of an action. The door began to close sometime last month. A woman inside decided, one evening that has not yet finished for her, to shut out the draught. Her hand has been on the latch for weeks. You happened to be there for the click.

The trough watered overnight was watered by someone who lifted the cup in spring.

The warm loaf on the kitchen table, the one you pass on your way out, belongs to a man who is still in the middle of setting it down to cool. He is in the room. You walked right past him. He was standing at the stove, and he was so still, and the light was on the dim, and he was the color of the brick behind him. You took him for a coat on a hook. From where he stands, you were a draught. Something passed through the room like a gust through an open window, and he felt his hair stir. He will wonder about it, idly, for the next several of your days.

This is why the names are chalked in careful handwriting that is not the owners' own. The Breath comes once a day and smudges the chalk. The people behind those doors could not rewrite their own names that often. For them the Breath comes several times while they are drawing a single letter. So the quick ones do it. Every morning, after the dripping stops, someone goes along the terraces with a stub of chalk and renews each name. It is the first courtesy the Kettle teaches. You keep the name of someone who can no longer keep up with their own name.

This is also why everyone sleeps a hand's breadth from the wall. It isn't really about the grit. When you are *in*, a single night's sleep may last a season, and the walls settle, and the walls move. A hand's breadth is room for the rock to breathe while you do.

## The Ones Who Are Sleeping

Past the ones who are *in*, there are the ones who are sleeping.

They are not asleep. *Sleeping* is only the nearest word the quick ones have. These are residents who have slowed so far that a single breath of theirs lasts longer than the Breath comes and goes. They are in the lowest terraces, where the brick sweats. They lie in the moss under the gantries, and the moss holds water for them. Some lie in beds. Some lie in the angle of a wall, their hand's breadth long since closed. The rock has leaned in, as the rock does, and nobody minds.

You would not know them to look at. Moss grows on them, very thin. The black-violet ferns lean toward them, because they are warm, and the ferns turn toward warmth.

The tall ivory mushrooms, slender as pencils, are how you find them. Those mushrooms grow only where a sleeper's breath comes out. Each one stands above a mouth like a candle above a sleeping child. It takes the long, slow exhalation and grows by it. That is why people leave them alone. Picking one would be like putting your hand over someone's face.

The moths know this too, in their small way. The grey moths that sit in rows on the hottest pipes and "simply stop moving" are not dead. They have gone in. They will fly off, perfectly well, in a year or two. The Kettle does to everything what it does to you. It takes speed and gives warmth. The residents call it a fair trade, and they say so about the fig and the pipe because the fig is the only example that fits in a sentence.

## The Pipe-Hound

The pipe-hound is not rare.

It is only rare to you. You see one twice a year, a pale, eyeless, furless length moving along the insulation "very slowly." It seems slow to you because you are still fast. To the sleepers, the pipe-hound is quick. It darts along the steam lines like a swift. It is everywhere at once and always nearby, and it is the most familiar thing in their world.

It has no eyes because it has never needed them. It finds the sleepers by warmth, and it goes to them, and it lies against them. That is what the superstition means when it says the pipe-hound is warmer than the pipes. It carries heat. It picks warmth up from the red-hot lines by the Engine, runs it up the walls, and presses it into the sleepers in the cold places near the top, where the blade of daylight falls and the air goes thin. For the sleepers it is a nurse, a hot-water bottle, and a dog at the foot of the bed. They see it every hour of their lives. They love it.

It has never been seen eating. It does not eat. It is paid in the same coin as everything else here. It carries heat to the sleepers, and they give it their company. Those are the terms.

No one has tested whether it is warm to the touch. No one needs to. Any resident who has been here long enough to be *in* has woken, once or twice, with the weight of it along their side.

## The Engine

Keep going down, and the question changes.

The Engine's stroke is forty seconds. Everyone agrees on that. It rises with a long hiss, pauses at the top as if considering, and descends. The quick ones say the town follows its pace. The ones who are *in* would say, if you could wait long enough to hear it, that the Engine is going rather fast.

The Engine is bottle-green and holds its paint in the damp. Nobody knows who built it. Some say it was lowered in pieces, and some say it was here when they came. The few who say it was here before the crack are the closest to right, though they aren't right either.

The Engine was a resident.

It is the eldest, the one who came first and went furthest. Whoever it was chose to stop at a pace just barely quick enough to be useful. It keeps the belts turning and the lamps pulsing. It gives the quick ones a rhythm to fall into and the slow ones a clock they can still feel. Some sort of person is inside the green iron, the way a hermit crab is inside a shell, or the way the fig is inside the pipe it has wrapped. It is hard to say, now, which is fig and which is pipe. The Engine "keeps its own counsel" the way a grandmother keeps the lamp by the door lit. It is not secretive. It is simply occupied with the work of keeping pace.

That pause at the top of the stroke is not mechanical. The Engine is listening.

## Below the Engine

The Engine listens downward.

The fissure is new. That is the first thing to understand about it, and you were told so on the way in. It opened in a single night of groaning, in the lifetime of the oldest residents, and it is still settling. It ticks every few weeks. It belled open narrow at the top and wide below, like a split bottle. It is also shaped like a throat.

Somewhere past the seam where the fissure pinches shut, deeper than anyone has followed the pipes, is the thing the Kettle is the breath of.

It is not a monster, and it is not a god. It is simply the slowest thing in the Kettle, as slow compared to the sleepers as the sleepers are to you. The night the ground groaned, it breathed in for the first time. The fissure is what that breath opened. Everything since, the brick and the gardens and the town packed like a drawer of spoons, has been built inside a single inhalation that has not finished yet.

The heat that comes up through the floor is its body heat. The ticking walls are its ribs moving, very slightly, every few weeks. The daily Breath, when the relief valves open and warm mist rolls up the alleys and falls back as rain, is not really the Engine venting pressure. It is condensation on the inside of something's lips.

It is young. You were told that too. The rock has never been weathered, and the edges are still sharp enough to open a palm. The town has the rawness of a fresh scar. The thing below is not ancient and not waking from a long sleep. It is new, and this is its first breath. It is being born, or it is being born into the world above, which may amount to the same thing. It is enormous and slow, and it is alone, and it is warm.

In the one permanent pool on the lowest level, too hot to touch and perfectly clear, the rust-red film on the bottom is the only part of it anyone has ever seen. It is skin, where the cracked pipe went deep enough to touch it. Nobody has identified the film because nobody has needed to. The quick ones do not know what it is. The ones who are *in* know, and they go and sit by the pool when they can. It takes them a season to walk there. They sit with it the way you would sit by a cradle.

The velvet-black creeper coats the stones around the stacks, where the heat is nearly unbearable, and it has never been seen to flower. It will. It is waiting for the exhale.

## Why They Came

So here is who lives in the Kettle.

There are the quick ones, who chalk the names and hand out beans and answer your questions. They are the youngest residents and the first step on the slope. There are the ones who are *in*, finishing their gestures across weeks. There are the sleepers under the pencil-mushrooms, breathing once a season. There is the pipe-hound, which carries heat up the walls to the sleepers. There is the Engine, the eldest, which chose to stay just fast enough to keep the lamps pulsing, and which pauses at the top of every stroke to listen. And below all of them, in the seam you could not slip a hand into, there is the new thing. It is taking the first breath of its life, and the breath has already lasted as long as the town has stood.

None of them are captives. Nobody was lured, and the stairs go up as well as down. You left, after all.

They came, and they stayed, because something was being born in the dark, alone, and it was going to take a very long time. Somebody ought to be with it while it breathed. The slow can only be kept company by those willing to become slow. So the residents let their hearts fall into its pace and went down the slope toward it, one stage at a time, the way the figs lean toward the pipes. Each stage is a little warmer and a little closer. At each one you can hear it a little better.

That is why nobody shouts. Nobody here shouts near a newborn.

That is why people pause on the dim and speak on the bright. The dim is the inhale, and they are keeping out of its way.

## You

You climbed out. The daylight was cool and white and shockingly bright. At the rail you looked back and saw amber and black-green, pulsing gently, and you noticed your heartbeat had slowed to match.

There is one thing you did not notice on the way up, because you were climbing through someone's kitchen and the loaf was warm and the light was on the dim.

On a door on the third terrace, the one with the beans trained up the railing, there is a name chalked in careful handwriting that is not your own. It is yours.

It was not there when you came down. Someone wrote it after you had passed, the way they write all the names. They do it not because you live there, but because you might. The Kettle has already begun to count you. For days afterward, you will find yourself waiting between one breath and the next.

Every morning since you left, after the Breath and the hour of dripping, someone has gone along the terrace with a stub of chalk and renewed it.
