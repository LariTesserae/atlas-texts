# Those Who Keep the Col

## The Downwalkers

Start with the ones you can see, because there are not many of them and they will not stay in view for long.

The hunched grey creatures on the quiet lines are the most numerous things on the col. If you counted every one you might reach forty. They have no name, because no one who named things stayed long enough to need one, so call them downwalkers for the fur and for the way they keep their heads lowered.

Up close they are stranger than they look from a distance. The down is not fur but something closer to feather, very fine, each filament branched and branched again until it is almost smoke. It does not rustle. That is its purpose. A downwalker can press against a stone, or against another downwalker, and the contact makes no sound at all. Their legs are long and jointed twice, like a heron's, and they end in broad, soft, leathery pads. Nothing hard touches the ground. They have no claws and no hooves. When they walk, they lower each foot slowly and roll their weight onto it from the outer edge inward, the way a person steps onto ice they do not trust.

They have no visible ears because they do not need them. The whole animal is an ear. The pads of the feet are dense with nerve, and a downwalker standing still is listening through the stone. It feels the chord as a trembling in the rock, and feels the nodal lines as places where the trembling stops. When one pauses at a crossing and tilts its head, it is not listening to the air. It is shifting its weight from foot to foot and reading the ground, confirming that the silence ahead is as deep as the silence here. They are very old as a kind. You can tell because they are so perfectly fitted to a place that cannot have existed for long in the way most places exist. They were shaped by the chord as surely as the boulders were.

They eat rime, as you guessed. They lower their long faces to the white fur on the gravel and take it with a dry, flat tongue, delicately and without hurry. The rime grows back at exactly the rate they eat it, because they eat exactly as much as grows. No downwalker has ever been hungry, and none has ever had more than enough.

They do not call to each other. Instead they lean. Two downwalkers meeting on a line will stop, press the sides of their heads together, and stand like that for what might be hours. Something passes between them through bone. It might be warmth, or a slow pulse, or the steady low drum of a heart. It is the only speech they have, and it adds nothing to the air.

Their young are born in the true silences, the crossing-pockets, and nowhere else. A mother will walk the lines for a very long time to find one unoccupied, then fold herself down in it and not move until it is done. The young are born with their eyes open and their mouths closed. This matters more than anything else about them. A newborn animal almost anywhere else in the world cries. A downwalker kit that cried would add its cry to the col forever, and so, somewhere in the long shaping of their kind, the ones whose young cried were winnowed away. They were not killed. Every cry they made stayed and stacked and thickened the chord around their birthplaces until the silence there closed over, and there were no quiet crossings left in which to raise another. The col does not punish noise. It simply keeps it, and the keeping fills up the room.

When a downwalker is old it goes to a crossing and lies down, and the rime covers it. You can find them, if you look. There are small soft white mounds at the centre of certain pockets, like snow over a sleeping cat. They do not rot, because nothing here happens quickly enough for rot. In time the cushion plants grow over them. You begin to wonder how many of the fist-sized cushions have a small grey shape folded underneath.

## The Small Lives

There are smaller things, and you only find them by lying down in the quiet with your face near the gravel.

In the finger-deep pools under the invisible ice there are animals no larger than a grain of salt, pale and translucent, drifting in water so still they barely need to swim. They are the only things on the col that live their whole lives in total silence, and they seem never to have developed any sense of vibration at all. Lift one out of its pool into the roaring air and it will not notice. It will simply dry and die, puzzled by nothing.

On the edges of the nodal lines, where the silence begins to fray into sound, there are spiders. They are tiny, black, and nearly spherical, and they build webs across the narrowest quiet lines, the hand-width ones, strung between pebbles. A web in the open zones would be shaken to nothing in moments. Here, in the thin still corridors, the webs hang perfect. What they catch is unclear, since no insect flies on the col. Looking closer, you notice that the webs are not quite still. At their outer edges, where they reach toward the roaring air, the strands tremble very faintly. They have been built precisely to the width of the silence and no further, and the spider sits at the centre where there is no trembling at all. You begin to suspect the webs are not for catching. They are for measuring. A spider knows the exact shape of its silence by the way the edges of its web tremble, and if a line ever began to wander, the spiders would know first.

The line has not wandered. The spiders keep measuring anyway.

## The Bird

It is possible to find the bird, though it takes a long time, and most visitors assume it is dead and the call is a ghost.

It is not dead. It sits in a crossing near the northern edge of the col, at the foot of a boulder shaped like a sleeping animal. It is small and brown, with a pale throat, and it is utterly still. It is not frozen, because its eyes move, but it is still in the way a held breath is still. It does not preen. It does not feed that you ever see. Perhaps it does not need to.

It called once. The three rising notes went out from it into the chord and did not end, and they are still going now, sweet and piercing and unfinished, climbing toward a fourth note that never arrives. A bird calls in order to be answered. That is what a call is. It sends out the question and waits for the reply, and then it calls again, or it doesn't.

This bird is still waiting. It cannot call again, because the first call is still going. Its question has not finished being asked. Somewhere in the vast overlapping roar, it is listening for an answer to a phrase that has not yet reached its end. If the answer came, the call would be complete, and the bird could sing the next one. So it sits in its silence and listens with its whole small body, and its eyes move across the patterned gravel, and it does not preen.

## The One Who Built the Shells

Someone lived here. Now that you know how to look, you can tell a good deal about them.

They were roughly human-sized. The shells fit a crouching adult, and the forehead-hollow sits at the height of a seated person's head. They came up out of the cloud, as you did, and at first they did not know the rules. You can hear this. In the open zones near the western edge of the col there is a small, confused, overlapping cluster of sounds. There are footsteps, many of them, crossing and recrossing. There is a sharp breath, as of someone startled. There is a word, repeated. There is a laugh, short, almost delighted. And there is the singing, the one phrase caught mid-breath. You can find the exact place where it hangs in the air, and it is only a few paces from the cloud's edge. Whoever it was stepped out onto the col, heard the chord, and began to sing along with it. Then, halfway through the phrase, they understood, and stopped.

After that there is almost nothing of them in the chord. They learned very quickly. They learned the quiet lines and the soft step and the breathing. From the arrangement of the shells you can see how far across the col they traveled. There are shells at nearly every crossing large enough to hold them, and each one is built so that the curved wall blocks the Throat. The spiral rings of small stones around some of them are not decoration. You understand this once you've sat inside one. The spirals are placed exactly on the faint secondary nodes around each pocket, and they deepen the silence by a fraction. They are a kind of tuning. Whoever built them had learned the pattern well enough to improve on it.

They carried every stone by hand, stepping only in the quiet. Some of the larger stones must have taken days to move. They must have held each one against their chest, walked the long corridors of silence, set it down so gently it made no click, then fitted it and let go. And they could not have fed themselves here, not for long. There is nothing to eat but rime. Either they went down into the cloud and came back, again and again, over years, or they became something that no longer needed feeding. You find yourself unsure which.

The shell with the forehead-hollow is the deepest silence on the col. You check this carefully. It stands at the meeting of seven lines, and its spiral is the most intricate. Sitting inside it with your forehead pressed to the worn stone, you realise why they sat this way. The rock conducts the chord. In the still air of the pocket there is no sound, but through the stone, through the bone of your skull, the whole roar comes in at once. It is not a wall of noise now but something you can hold apart. You can sort it. It is layered and separable, and every voice is distinct. It is like the difference between standing in a crowd and listening at a single door. The stone brings the whole col to you, one sound at a time, as long as you lean into it.

They sat here a very long time. Long enough to wear a hollow into stone that nothing else on the col can mark.

## What They Were Listening For

The chord is unresolved. You noticed that at the very beginning, before you understood anything else. It is a vast held chord that does not rise or fall and does not arrive.

Everything in it is like that. The bird's call climbs toward a note it never reaches. The singer's phrase breaks off mid-breath. Even the footsteps are unresolved, each one the first half of a stride whose second half is somewhere else. The stone dropped in water is the splash without the settling. The slow breath in the roar is an inhalation. You search for its exhalation and cannot find it.

Nothing up here has finished. The flower with five blue blossoms has not finished opening. The sun has not finished setting, or rising. The cushions have not finished growing. The rime forms and fades in perfect balance, never gaining and never lost. The col is a place held at the exact moment before completion, and the chord is the shape of that moment. The chord is not a sound that happens to be sustained. It is a question, the sum of every question anyone ever asked here, stacked and waiting.

You come to believe the Listener was searching the chord for the missing note. They were looking for the one tone that would resolve it.

You come to believe, too, sitting in their place with your forehead in their hollow, that they found it.

You cannot prove it. But you can hear, through the stone, how the chord is built, and once you can hear its shape you can hear the absence in it. It has the exact size and pitch of the thing that isn't there. It is not a difficult note. A person could sing it. A child could hum it. It sits in the chest, just below the voice, and once you have heard its absence you feel it there all the time, the way you feel a word on the tip of your tongue.

If it were sung, here, in the open, the chord would resolve. The bird's call would reach its fourth note and fall silent. The singer would finish her breath. The footsteps would land. The sun would set, at last, and the stars would begin to turn. The wind would change. The gravel would scatter. The five blue flowers would open all the way, and then, in their own time, wither. The downwalkers would wake to weather.

The Listener sat here with that note in their chest for as long as it takes to wear a stone smooth with a forehead. They pressed their head against the rock so hard and so long because it was the only way to keep still. They did not sing it.

You do not know why. Perhaps it was mercy for the flower, which has never had to die, and for the downwalkers, fitted to a world that would unmake them if it moved. Perhaps it was mercy for the bird, which would at last be answered, and would then be only a bird, and would one day stop. Perhaps it was reverence, or fear. Or perhaps they understood that a resolved chord is a finished piece, that finished pieces end, and that nothing in the world had ever been kept the way this place keeps things.

There is no body anywhere on the col. There is no mound of rime large enough. The shell is empty except for you. But there is one sound you can hear through the stone and never in the air. It is very close, and so quiet it might be your own pulse. It is a steady, even breathing, in and out, in and out, perfectly soft. It adds nothing, because it is timed exactly to the edge of the silence, like the spiders' webs. It is the only sound you have found on the col that completes itself, each breath resolving into the next.

It might be the Listener. It might be someone who has become so still, and so exactly fitted to the silence, that they no longer take up any room in the air at all.

## What Lives Here

So you make your count.

There are the downwalkers, who never cry. There are the salt-grain animals, who do not know there is anything to hear. There are the spiders, measuring the silence; the cushions, grown over the dead; and the flower, which is not yet. There is the bird, waiting to be answered. There may be the Listener, breathing in the rock.

But the count is not complete, and by now you know why.

Every sound in the chord is a resident here. The singer's broken phrase lives here, and has lived here longer than any downwalker. The laugh lives here. The stone that dropped in water lives here, and the first inhalation of something large and slow that came up out of the cloud and was never heard to breathe out. They do not age or eat, and they do not end. They are what the col is made of. The rock is only the shape they have pressed into the world, and the gravel is only where they have left room. The chord is not the noise of the place. The chord is its population, all of it overlapping, all of it waiting in the same held moment, asking the same unfinished question. The quiet lines are not where the life is. They are the narrow corridors the living have been allowed, between the far greater number of the kept.

And you are a resident now. Your first footstep lives at the edge of the col, where the cloud gives way to stone. It is small and dry and unfinished, the first half of a stride, and it is waiting.

It is waiting for you to leave. When you walk back down through the cloud, that footstep will have its answer. The stride will complete itself, and one small part of the chord will resolve, the way everything here is waiting to. That is what it is to visit the Held Col: you are one of the only questions here that can answer itself.

If you stay, and learn the lines, and sit in the shell, and find the note, you will have the Listener's choice instead.

In the meantime you breathe softly. You set your forehead against the worn stone. Somewhere in the rock, very near, the other breath goes on, in and out, keeping time with yours.
