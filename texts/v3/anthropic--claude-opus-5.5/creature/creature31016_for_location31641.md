# What Lives in the Governor's Room

## I. The Wrong Answer First

The easy answer is that nothing lives there.

That answer is honest as far as it goes. No beetle, no moth, no bat. Nothing that breathes, nothing that startles. If you went down with a lantern and a notebook to catalogue the fauna, you would come back up with a blank page and a few scratches on your palms from the basalt.

But the easy answer assumes that a thing that lives must be a *thing*: bounded, separate, a body that ends at its skin. The Governor's Room does not make things like that. It has had no time to. It is young as a wound, and what grows in a wound is not a creature but tissue.

So look again, and stop looking for an animal.

## II. The Fig, Which Is Not Alone

Start with the fig, because it is the largest and the most obvious, and because it seems to be the answer.

The fig climbs the wall and grips the lamp bracket. It swallowed the cable and grew copper into its own wood. Its roots have fused into the dynamo belt. It is pulled, over and over, by the slow turning of the engine, and it has answered by growing ridged like a throat. If anything here is an inhabitant, surely it is the fig.

But follow the roots down.

Beneath the talus, beneath the moss on the bedplate, beneath the damp grit in the hollows, the fig's roots do not stop where a fig's roots ought to. They thread through the rubble in every direction, and there they meet other things. The rhizomes of the ferns run in the same cracks. The fine anchoring hairs of the liverworts cling to the same brick. The moss sits in a wet pelt over all of it. In a place with no worms to churn the soil and no fungi of the ordinary kind, the roots have had nothing to compete with but each other. So they have stopped competing. Where fern rhizome crosses fig root, the two have grafted. They have done the same with the moss and with the waxy rosettes by the vent.

Nobody would call it a forest floor. It is closer to a single mat of living fiber the size of the room, bound to the stone and the iron. It is one continuous tissue with many kinds of leaf.

This is why the ferns all turn at once. There aren't a thousand ferns deciding separately. There is one body moving a thousand hands.

## III. The Body

Once you see the mat, the rest of the room begins to fit into it, piece by piece, the way the parts of an animal fit around its spine.

**The vent is its blood.** The heat comes up from the fissure and never stops, and every living part of the room is arranged around it. The waxy red rosettes grow closest, pressed against the hottest stone. They are the part of the body that touches the source most directly, and they close in the dark phase the way a hand closes around something warm.

**The engine is its lung.** The boiler fills slowly and releases slowly, which is exactly how the description puts it. The piston's long hiss through teeth is breath. Nobody built the engine to be part of a body, but the body grew around it, and a body does not care where its organs came from. The moss has furred the bedplate so thickly that you can no longer see where rust ends and green begins. The engine is being upholstered, the way scar tissue upholsters a splinter.

**The cable is its nerve.** Copper runs inside living wood. Current moves through the fig, and something moves with it: a faint charge, a change in the sap, a signal. This is how the ferns know the day is coming before the light arrives. The first trickle from the dynamo, too weak to warm the filament, runs down the fig's electrical vein and out through the grafted roots into every frond. The room feels its own morning in its nerves before it sees it.

**The lamp is its eye.** It is a single amber eye, open for a few hours, then shut. It is the only way the body knows there is anything at all beyond heat.

**The talus is its skeleton.** Sharp basalt and crisp brick give it shape. The roots lace through the rubble and bind it, so that the steep slopes against the walls no longer shift. The stone holds the body up, and the body holds the stone together.

It has no name, because nothing has ever been there to give it one. But it lives in the Governor's Room, and there is one part of that room it has taken a particular interest in. So call it what the room is called.

Call it **the Governor**.

## IV. The Brass Arms

Here is the detail you would miss unless you got very close to the engine, close enough to feel the heat through your boots.

One thin root, no thicker than a bootlace, has left the rest. It came up the bedplate through the moss, climbed the engine's frame, and found the spindle of the flyball governor. It wrapped the spindle once, loosely, and then reached out along one of the hinged arms until it touched the brass sphere at the end.

It does not grip the arm. It rests on it.

When the engine speeds up, the spheres swing outward and lift. When it slows, they sink. That is all a flyball governor has ever done: it rises with speed and falls with slowness, and through its linkage it tells the engine to ease off or push harder. It is a machine for keeping things steady. It is the oldest kind of mechanical attention there is.

The root feels the arm rise and fall. It is reading the engine's pulse.

And the belt, the half-leather, half-fig belt that stretches and creaks with every turn, is not only being pulled. Over the years it has thickened unevenly. In some places it is tauter, in others slacker. When the ground's breath is strong and the engine threatens to run faster than the old boiler can stand, the fig-belt seems to tighten. When the breath is fading, it seems to give a little.

The Governor is learning to govern.

Nobody taught it and it does not know it is doing so. But over enough slow days a pattern has settled into its wood. Parts of the body that pulled the engine toward ruin were torn or starved. Parts that held it steady were not. Selection is simple work when the stakes are this plain: *if the engine breaks, the eye closes forever.* So the Governor has come to hold its one machine the way you might hold a sleeping animal against your chest. It stays gentle and attentive, easing its grip or firming it as the breathing changes.

The lit phase is a little longer than it would be without the fig. It holds its peak a little steadier. The room's day has been lengthened by the thing that lives in it.

## V. The Figs That Never Ripen

The hard green figs drop unswollen into the talus. The description guesses that the tree is fruiting out of habit, the last of its line.

That may be so. But there is another reading.

Count them. If you went down with a lamp and patience and sifted the rubble below the bracket, you would find figs at every depth. The fresh green ones sit on top. Under them are browned ones and blackened ones, and under those, hollow husks gone to fiber. They lie in layers, like sediment or tree rings.

The Governor drops one fig, roughly, for each dark phase.

It is not exact, and the Governor is not counting in any way we would recognize. But a fig forms during the light and falls in the dark, one by one, and the pile beneath the lamp is a record of every night the room has had. The talus below the bracket is the Governor's memory, laid down in the only medium it has. It cannot pass its knowledge to children. The wasp never came, so it has no children. It keeps the knowledge in a heap instead: *this many times the eye has closed. This many times it opened again.*

If there is anything like confidence in the Governor, this heap is where it lives. Every fig is a night that ended.

## VI. What It Wants

It would be a mistake to give the Governor too much mind. It has no thoughts. It does not dream in the dark phase, and it does not fear the long nights. Still, a body that has organized itself this completely around a few things has something like wants, the way a river has something like a direction.

It wants **steadiness.** Everything it has grown into is a machine for smoothing: the governor arms, the belt, the grafted roots that share heat and water across the mat so no single frond dries or scorches. It does not want more light so much as *reliable* light. It wants the tide to rise and fall on a rhythm it can hold.

It wants **both suns.** It turns toward the lamp in the day and toward the vent in the night, and it does not seem to prefer either. The light feeds the leaves and the heat feeds everything. It courts both without jealousy, facing each in turn.

It does not want **overspeed.** Every governor exists to prevent one thing, the runaway: the moment when an engine spins faster than its own iron can bear and flies apart. If the Governor has a single horror written into its tissue, this is it. It does not fear the dark, because the dark always ends; the heap of figs says so. It fears the day that burns too bright and too fast and ends in a cracked boiler and a lamp that never comes back.

## VII. If You Came Down

Suppose you did go down. Suppose you slid through the break in the pump house floor, cut your hands on the conchoidal edges of the basalt, and stood in the heat with your heart going fast from the climb.

The Governor would notice you. It would not see you. You'd be one more point of warmth, a third sun, small and moving. In the dark phase, the ferns nearest you would begin, over long minutes, to turn toward you. It would feel like being slowly and politely faced by a crowd.

If you stood still long enough, you might notice something else. Your breathing would slow. In that heat, in that silence, with the piston exhaling through its teeth every few seconds, it is very hard not to fall into its rhythm. You would find yourself breathing when the engine breathed and holding when it held. Your heart would ease down from the climb.

Nothing would be doing this to you, not exactly. But the room is a place where everything that lives has settled into one slow, regulated rhythm, and your body would begin to settle into it too. That's the Governor's only skill, and it would practice it on you without meaning to, the way the root rests on the brass arm without gripping.

If you stayed too long, the heat would begin to hurt you, because you are not built for it. You would climb out into the grey daylight. The ferns you had drawn toward you would spend the next dark phase slowly turning back toward the vent.

## VIII. What It Is Becoming

The fig is closing over the lamp. Each year the sphere of light is smaller. The description offers this as a feedback, a slow suicide: a tree growing around a light it can no longer see.

But think about what the fig has already done to the cable. It did not starve the wire. It *took it inside itself.* Copper now runs through living wood.

When the fig closes over the lamp, the light will not go out. The dynamo will still turn and the current will still flow. The bulb will still swell from black to amber over forty minutes and hold and fade. It will simply be doing it *inside the tree.*

Picture it a century on. The bracket is gone and the bulb is buried in a knot of fig as thick as a torso. When the ground breathes and the engine turns, the knot begins to glow. It is faint and deep orange, the color of light through a hand held over a candle. The bark is thinnest where the new growth is, and there the glow comes through in ridges and lines, like the veins of a leaf lit from behind.

The ferns at the edge of the room will have died back by then, starved for light. But the ones nearest the knot will have adapted, pressing their fronds right against the warm, glowing wood. The Governor will have drawn itself inward, tighter around its heart.

And it will be a heart then, not an eye. The Governor will no longer look at its light. It will *contain* it. A body that began as a tangle of accidents in a collapsed pump house will have done what bodies do: pulled its most precious organ inside, wrapped it in living tissue, and kept it safe.

It will still be governed by the ground's breath and still count its nights in the talus. The single brass arm will still rise and fall under its resting root.

Nothing with a mind will live there, as before. What does live there will be quieter and stranger. It is a small, slow, patient body made of fig and fern and moss and iron and copper and heat. For the first time in its short life, it will glow from within, like something that was built on purpose.
