# Who Lives in the Seam

## The First Answer

People live there, and that answer isn't wrong. It is only the fastest part of the truth.

There are three hundred and some. The exact number is kept in chalk on the chandlery door, on a slate gone white at the edges with sinter, and it changes by one every few weeks. Someone is born, someone dies, someone climbs the forty stairs and doesn't come back down. The chalk is rewritten on the downstroke by whoever happens to be passing, and nobody considers it anyone's particular job. That is how most things are done in the Seam. The town is too small for offices. It has habits instead.

You recognize a Seamer up top before they speak. They are lean, all of them, in a way that has nothing to do with hunger. The heat takes appetite, the walkways are tight, and a body that needs room to turn doesn't last long among the chains and gantries. They joke that the Seam narrows its people along with its buildings, that every autumn the crews shave a course off the workshops and a little off everyone else. Their skin is pale, since no sun reaches the Seam, but flushed underneath, a steady pink from years of mineral steam. They glitter faintly. Seam-salt dries in the fine hairs of their forearms and along their hairlines, and it catches in their eyebrows so that under lamplight their faces seem dusted with frost. Up top, children point at it. In the Seam nobody notices, the way nobody notices their own breath.

They dress in loose linen, never wool, and everything they own has been turned inside out and hung to air a thousand times. Hair is cropped or bound hard against the skull, because of the chains. Every one of them carries a chisel. It is small and short-handled and worn bright at the edge, and they hang it at the hip or on a cord at the throat, the way people elsewhere carry keys. In a sense it is a key. Without it, doors stay shut.

## Hands

Watch a Seamer walk and you'll see that they never lean on anything, just as the stories say. Brass burns and iron scalds, and a person who rests their weight against the town gets marked by it. But they touch constantly.

Their fingertips go ahead of them, gloved, the leather palms scuffed white. As they pass a railing they tap it. As they pass a bolt they give it a quarter-turn, feeling it grate against the new stone crusting its threads. Hinges get a nudge and handles get a twist. A Seamer walking down a walkway is a small, steady disturbance, a wake of things moved very slightly, and each of those movements is a refusal. *Not yet. This is still ours.*

It started as maintenance and became manners. To turn your neighbor's door handle as you pass their home is a courtesy, a way of saying *I thought of you.* When someone is ill or grieving, their neighbors come in shifts to open and close the doors of their rooms, work the shutters, and run a thumb along the lamp cage, so that nothing belonging to the sick one goes still while they can't tend it. The Seam has a phrase for devotion that outsiders find odd until they've lived there a year: *I'll keep your hinges.* Couples say it. Parents say it to children. The dying say it back, meaning *I would if I could.*

Their hands tell the rest. The pads of their fingers are callused smooth, like river stones, from the endless small turning. The heels of their palms are calloused from the chisel. Almost everyone has a white seam of scar somewhere on the back of the hand or wrist, from the fracture edges, which are still sharp enough to open skin. People compare these scars the way sailors compare tattoos. The old stone doesn't cut. Only the young stone does, and the young stone is everywhere.

## How They Keep Time

Every eleven seconds the engines take a stroke, and the people of the Seam live inside that interval as surely as they live inside the fissure.

They don't count it. They stopped counting years ago, or never started. The stroke has simply sunk into them. It is in the way they speak, which is in phrases that tend to end on the downstroke, so that conversation has a slow tidal quality: a sentence, a breath, the long iron sigh of the beam, then the next sentence. Their songs run in elevens. Their games do too. A child's hand-clapping rhyme in the Seam has eleven beats and a pause, and the pause is the engine's. Lovers learn to kiss on the upstroke, when the walkway steadies. Arguments are paced so that no one has to shout over the return.

This is safety first. A body out of rhythm is a body that meets a swinging chain. But it runs deeper than safety. The Seamers who go up top, to sell salt or see a doctor or just to stand under an open sky, all report the same thing. After a day or two they become unwell. They lose their balance on flat ground. They wake in the night with their hearts pounding out of step, reaching for a rhythm that isn't there. Some of them hum under their breath to keep it, a low eleven-count drone, and don't realize they're doing it until someone stares.

Most come back down early. They say the air up top is too thin, which it isn't. What they mean is that nothing up there is breathing with them.

## The Room-Tellers

The oldest residents are the ones who came down in the first years, when the fissure was new and hot and the first beam engine was hauled down the stairs in pieces. They are few now, perhaps a dozen, and the town calls them room-tellers.

Ask one of them to walk you through the Seam and they won't show you the town. They'll show you the one that used to be there. They move slowly along the walkways, one hand trailing, and they narrate what isn't.

*Here was the back room of the bakery. It had a window that looked straight at the vein, the thick green one, and in the mornings the bread-steam fogged it and you'd see the light through the fog. Here, where this plate is, was a staircase. It went up to Arden's workshop. Arden made the first cages for the lamps. The staircase went in the ninth autumn. You can still see the bolt-scars. Here is where my sister and I slept when we were small. The bunk was as long as I was. I'm longer now, and the wall's come in, so it wouldn't fit either of us.*

They speak of lost rooms with a tenderness nobody finds strange. In the Seam the past is not remembered as a time. It is remembered as a place, a specific amount of space that has been given back to the stone. Every autumn, when the crews come to cut a room, its family holds a supper in it, the last meal before the jacks and the chisels. Everyone takes a chip of the brick or a shaving of the timber home in a tin, the same kind of tin they keep their books in. Old Seamer households have shelves of these tins, rooms kept the only way rooms can be kept once they're gone.

The room-tellers are the town's memory, and the town knows it is losing them the same way it loses everything, a little at a time and permanently. The younger adults have started writing the room-tellings down, in ledgers that live in tins. But the old ones say a written room isn't the same. You have to be walked through it. You have to put your hand on the wall where the doorway was.

## The Born

Something changed in the Seam about fifteen years in, and it is still changing. The children came.

People had brought children down before, of course. Those children were from up top. They remembered sky, and trees, and dogs that would follow you anywhere. The Born are different. They were born in the heat, in the amber murk, to the sound of the eleven-second breath, and they have never known anything else. The oldest of them are adults now. The youngest are a few weeks old, bundled in linen, sleeping on the downstroke.

The Born do not flinch at the crack.

When the deep sound comes, that single grinding report from far back in the rock, everyone in the Seam goes still, and the Born go still too. But their stillness is different. The adults freeze the way you freeze at a sound in a dark house. The Born freeze the way you go quiet when someone you love begins to speak. Their faces don't tighten. They tilt their heads. Sometimes, afterward, they'll say something small and certain. *That was the east side. That was a long way down.* When the ledger-keepers check the jacks, the Born are right more often than they should be.

They hear the rock before the adults do. A mother will see her daughter stop mid-word, fork lifted, and a heartbeat later the crack comes. A few of the Born can put their palms flat against a wall, briefly, lightly, never leaning, and tell you roughly how far it has moved since the season began. They can't say how they know. They seem faintly puzzled that others can't.

They are drawn to the veins. Most Seamers have, once or twice, stood in front of a thick green vein at the end of a shift and watched it not-glow. The Born do it every day. They sit cross-legged on the grating in front of the largest one, the vein that used to face the bakery window, and they watch it in silence, sometimes for an hour. If you ask what they see, they give answers that are almost ordinary. *It's slower today.* *It's thinking about the cold side.* *It's fine.* Their parents have learned not to ask too often.

And there's the other thing. The Seam has no small animal noises, no scratching, no fluttering, none of the tiny living sounds that fill every other town's walls. The Born are the closest the Seam has. They are small, quick things that murmur and rustle and tap along the walkways, their fingertips turning every bolt they pass, ferns brushing their shoulders. They play in whispers. They hide in the gaps behind the screw-jacks, places adults can't fit anymore, and they know every corner of the town the way mice know a house. Older Seamers sometimes say, only half joking, that the Born are what the Seam grew instead of rats.

Few of the Born want to go up top. The ones who have tried describe it as a kind of falling. The sky is too wide and the air is too cold and dry, and nothing leans toward them. They come back pale and shaking and go straight to the vein and sit. Their parents came down the forty stairs as a choice. For the Born, the stairs only go one way.

## The Still Ones

The Seam has no soil, so nobody is buried. What happens to the dead is the thing outsiders find hardest to hear, and Seamers find hardest to explain, because to them it is so obviously right.

When someone dies, they are washed in condensation water, dressed in clean linen, and laid in a keeping room. This is always a room at the back, on the wall side, the next one due to be given up to the closing rock. Their family sits with them for a day. Everyone who comes turns the door handle on the way in and on the way out, as always. Then, at the end of the day, the family does the hardest thing a Seamer can do.

They stop.

They close the door, and nobody touches it again. Nobody turns the bolts or works the hinges. Nobody passes a chisel along the frame. The first rule of the Seam is that nothing stays still unattended, and mourning is the act of choosing to let something be taken.

Within a season the sinter has a knuckle on the latch. Within a year the frame is white-laced and sealed, and a curtain of frilled stone hangs across the doorway like a veil. When the autumn crews come to that section, they don't cut the keeping room away. They cut around it, and they let the wall close on it. Slowly, a thumbnail at a time, the keeping rooms of the Seam are taken into the basalt.

So the walls of the Seam are not only rock. Seamers know this. Every year the fissure closes a little, and every year what it closes on includes the rooms of their dead, sealed in mineral, pressed into the dark. When the deep crack sounds, older Seamers sometimes say a name under their breath. The Born don't say names. They just listen harder.

There's a saying: *Keep your hinges while you're quick. The rock keeps them after.* The rock is patient with what's given to it. Nothing that goes still in the Seam is ever thrown away. It is only kept by something slower.

## The Other Resident

It is time to talk about the absence.

No rats, no flies, no beetles, no spiders at the lamps. Dogs refuse the last flight of stairs. Birds circle the quarry lip and turn away. The easy explanations are the heat, the sulphur, and something in the sinter, and all of them may be true. But anyone who has lived in the Seam long enough begins to feel another explanation, one that isn't really an explanation at all. It is more like the sense of walking into a room and knowing, without seeing anyone, that the chair by the window is taken.

The Seam isn't empty of life. It's occupied.

It is not occupied by a creature. Nothing there has a body or eyes or a voice. What the Seam holds is a tendency, a slow inclination at work everywhere at once. It lives in the sinter as it grows, in the stone bead gathering at the end of every bolt, in the curtains frilling under the walkways. It lives in the closing of the walls. It is the thing that happens to anything the moment people stop happening to it. Seamers don't have a name for it, exactly. When they need to refer to it they say *the still*, or *the slow*, or just *it*, with a tilt of the head toward the wall.

It isn't hostile. That is the first thing every Seamer will insist on, and they insist on it with a kind of loyalty. The still doesn't attack. It doesn't want anything the way an animal wants. It simply fills. Whatever isn't kept moving, it takes, gently and completely, like water taking the shape of a cup. It is a sort of attention that never lifts, the rock's own patience, occupying every surface the quick things leave alone.

There are stories, the way there are always stories.

A chisel left overnight on a pan-rail, found in the morning with sinter grown up around the handle in a curl, the way fingers would close around it.

A child's glove dropped behind a screw-jack, retrieved a season later, the stone inside it formed in the exact shape of the small hand that had worn it.

Ferns, which lean toward lamplight, found once on a sleep-shift leaning toward a man who had fallen asleep beside them, his face lit faintly orange by the lamp at his back.

And the copper lines, the narrow ones that carry nothing anyone can name. They come up out of the hot throat with the rest of the pipes, run up the walls, and branch and branch until they end in blind stubs against the stone, often near a vein. Nobody laid them within living memory. The room-tellers say they were there when the first crews came, already crusted, already warm. The engineers say they must be old survey lines, or a forgotten heat-exchange circuit. The Born put their ears against them and say nothing at all. Nobody takes them out. Nobody is certain.

The ferns are the still's gentlest outriders. They root in the sinter it lays down, and they lean toward the light the quick things make. The copper-green film is its pulse made visible. It greys when the engines stop, and greens within the hour when they start again, as if the slow were waiting on the quick, as if it needed their warmth to be anything at all.

That may be the truest thing about it. Many Seamers suspect the still isn't separate from them. It might be the other half of the same life.

## What Lives in the Seam

Think of it this way. The Born would tell you this, if you asked the right way and waited long enough on the grating in front of the vein.

A wound opened in the earth, less than a lifetime ago. It was new and hot, and its edges were sharp enough to cut. The body it opened in has begun, very slowly, to heal it. The walls knit inward a thumbnail's width each year. New stone lays itself over every surface, white and patient, sealing and filling. This is not an attack. It is what a body does with a wound. The rock is closing, and the still is the rock's healing.

But wounds, in their early days, are not empty. They fill with a living press of warm, busy, quick things that swarm in, keep the channel open, and hold the space while the new flesh is forming. They work in rhythm. They are hot to the touch. They keep everything moving so nothing festers and nothing seals too soon.

That is what the people of the Seam are. They are the quick.

The word is chosen with care. The quick is the tender, living part under a fingernail, the part that feels. The walls of the Seam close by the width of a thumbnail each year, and beneath that slow nail of stone, wedged into the last of the open space, are three hundred and some warm bodies, turning bolts, cutting timbers, keeping hinges, pouring tea on the downstroke. They are the part of the wound that is still alive to itself. They are how the healing feels.

The Seam is a single creature with two tempos. The fast part breathes every eleven seconds. It is copper-green and flushed pink and sheened with salt, and it murmurs in low voices so as not to startle itself. The slow part grows a thumbnail a year, pale as bone, holding every still thing it's given, the dead in their keeping rooms, the lost rooms of the room-tellers, the gloves and chisels and doors. They are not at war. They are the same process at different speeds, the way a heartbeat and a lifetime are the same process at different speeds.

The Born understand this without being told, which is why they don't flinch at the crack. For them it isn't a threat. It's the slow half speaking, and they are fluent in both.

The ledger-keepers have done the arithmetic. They know how wide the Seam is and how fast it closes. The number comes out long, many generations, longer than anyone now living. It is not forever. Someday the last room will be cut, the last engine will be lowered into the throat, and the last Seamers, whoever they are, will either climb the forty stairs or not. The quick will be taken into the still, and the wound will be healed.

Nobody in the Seam speaks of that day with dread. When they speak of it at all, it's in the same soft voice they use for the room-tellings, and sometimes, among the Born, it's with something close to longing. The wall will close, and the walls will be full of them: their dead, their rooms, the shapes of their hands in stone. The rock will keep all of it. It will just keep it slowly.

Until then, go down the forty stairs into the bathwater air and stand still for a minute until your skin goes slick. Listen past the drip and the tick of the pipes and the eleven-second breath of the beams. You'll hear them, the quick, tapping their way along the walkways, turning each bolt a quarter as they pass, speaking low. Behind them, in every surface they've just touched, the slow is already beginning again, bead by bead, patient and pale.

Then you'll hear the crack from deep in the rock. For one moment the Seam goes still, and in that moment everything that lives there is holding its breath together.
