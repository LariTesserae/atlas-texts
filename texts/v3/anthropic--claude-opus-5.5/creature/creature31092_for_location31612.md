# Those Who Live in Sessile

## The First Thing You Notice Is That Someone Has Been Here

It starts with small things. On a high terrace, the dust has been swept into a low curved ridge, the way a hand sweeps crumbs. A clay cup, not ancient, sits upturned in the lip of an oval window. Halfway along an avenue that the masons have not reached, a line of flat stones leads across a drift, each one placed exactly a stride apart. Someone set them there so they could cross without sinking to the knee.

For a long time you see no one. Then, from far off across the basin, a spark answers the sun. It is a quick hard flash from the side of a tower, a second one, then a pause, then three more. It comes from a pane of selenite held in someone's hands and tilted. After a moment another spark answers from a different district, much farther into the haze.

People live in Sessile. There are not many. In a city built for tens of thousands there are perhaps four or five hundred, scattered across its districts in households of three or ten. Travelers who meet them call them the Still, because of the silence. The name is wrong in every way that matters, and it is the first thing you learn about them.

## The Still Are Never Still

The first rule of living here is the one visitors learn last. Anything that stays in one place long enough becomes part of a wall.

The masons are not cruel, and they are not hungry. They are simply building, and in Sessile anything that does not move is material. A resting body is, to them, one more fallen block. They come to it slowly. First a single mason tests an edge with its forelegs. Then a second comes, carrying a flake. The first course of brick goes down along the body's lowest line, where it meets the ground, and it is mortared so gently that a deep sleeper does not wake. By the second night the course has risen past the hip. Travelers who have lost companions this way describe the finished shape as beautiful. It is a smooth scalloped casing, porous and pale, sealed under a skin of rose mineral film, and it curves exactly like a person lying on their side.

So the people of Sessile move. Their whole culture comes out of this one fact.

They sleep in turns, and they sleep in pairs. One person lies down while the other sits beside them with a palm resting on the sleeper's shoulder. Every hour or so the watcher rolls the sleeper a quarter turn, or lifts an arm and lays it somewhere new, and brushes away any small gray visitors that have come to test the outline. Then they trade places. Among the Still this is the most intimate act there is. To say *I will turn you* means something close to *I love you*, or *I trust you*, or *you are mine to keep*. Children are turned by their parents. The old are turned by the young. A person with no one to turn them cannot live here long, and the Still do not let that happen. A traveler who comes down the ramp alone and stays past the first night will find, sometime in the dark, a stranger's hand on their shoulder, rolling them gently over.

Their homes are not houses. They keep circuits. A household moves through a string of rooms across a district. There is a dry arcade for the hot afternoon, a chimney-base where the rising air is cool, a cistern-court for the hours after the dew. They carry everything they own, and they own very little. Every few days they return to each room and sweep it, and they sweep away the first thin crust of mason-work that has begun along the walls. This does not anger the masons. They simply start again. The Still say the masons and the people have been sweeping and building against each other for so long that it has become a kind of agreement, the way two people who walk the same narrow path learn to step around each other without looking.

## How They Speak

Speech does not carry in Sessile, and after a few generations the Still stopped using it. Their mouths have become for eating and drinking and, rarely, for singing into cisterns during the dew hour, when the damp dust briefly lets a voice travel.

For distance they have the flash. Every household keeps a few panes of fallen selenite, thin ones, trimmed into rough ovals and wrapped at the edges in hide. Tilted to catch the raw gold light that pours through fresh gaps in the roof, a pane throws a spark that can be seen across the whole basin. The flash-language is slow and simple. It carries news of where the dust storms are moving, where a district has been buried, where someone is sick or someone has been born. It also carries something that is not news, a steady repeated pattern that households send to one another at dusk, before the moths rise. When a traveler asks what it means, the Still hold up a hand and turn it slowly, a quarter turn. *I will turn you. You are kept.*

Up close they cannot use ordinary gesture, and the reason is the light. In the shadowless white of Sessile, depth goes flat. A hand held before a face shows no shape, no relief, nothing but a pale outline against pale air. A pointing finger reads as a smudge. Faces are hard to read too. The Still say that outsiders all look the same to them at first, *like figures painted on a wall*. So they talk by touch.

Two of the Still in conversation stand close, or sit knee to knee, with their hands laid over each other's forearms or pressed palm to palm. Their language lives in pressure and in the small movements of fingers against skin. It is quick and dense and completely silent, and to someone watching it looks like two people holding each other very still. Among themselves they have no word for "stranger." The nearest phrase in touch-speech is a flat, motionless hand, meaning *someone I have not yet been told by*.

Because their language is made of touch, they read the old city the same way. They can feel it with their hands.

## The Reliefs

The original builders of Sessile carved every wall. Their work has been scoured down to gentle swellings and ripples that the eye cannot find in the shadowless light. The Still run their palms along them as they walk, and they read them.

They do not claim to understand the reliefs the way their makers did. But the ripples are not random. There are repeated rhythms, the same sequence of rises returning at intervals along a wall, and those rhythms are close enough to touch-speech that the Still find meaning in them the way a person finds meaning in a half-heard song. Certain walls are said to be sad. Certain arches are said to be greetings. One long wall in the eastern district, nearly buried, is read by every child of the Still before they are grown. They lie full-length in the trough at its foot and let the swellings pass under their hands, and they say it is a list of names, or possibly a list of people being turned.

This is where the Still's own story of the place begins to open up, and where the story is least certain.

It is possible, they say, that the original builders spoke by touch too. Perhaps they carved their walls to be *felt*, not seen. Perhaps they could not see well in the white light, or did not need to. Perhaps the Still are not newcomers at all, but whatever is left of those builders, worn down and rounded off like the city. Or perhaps they are strangers who came later, and the place taught them to be what its first people were. The Still do not insist on either answer. When pressed, they lay a traveler's palm against an old wall and hold it there, and wait.

## What You Feel When You Wait

If you keep your hand on the wall long enough, you notice that it is not silent.

Air in Sessile drinks sound. Dust drinks sound. Stone does not. Every mason in the basin is tapping, all the time, with a hammerstone held under its forelegs. Millions of them are striking flakes loose, settling bricks, pressing mortar home. Very little of that reaches the air. All of it goes into the limestone, and the limestone carries it. What you feel through your palm is at first only a faint grain, like the buzz in a table when a cart passes outside. Then it separates. There is a steady fine patter underneath everything. Above it, from nearer, come distinct clusters of taps, rhythms that stop and start, sequences that repeat.

The Still say you should put your ear to the wall. Travelers who do it describe the experience in almost the same words: *the whole city was talking*.

This is the second thing you learn about the people of Sessile, and it changes the first. They have not given up sound. They have moved it into the stone. The oldest among them lie on the walls in the heat of the day, spread flat like the basking salamanders, with their ears and palms and bellies pressed to the limestone. They are listening. Asked what to, they give a range of answers. *The masons. The district. The weather.* The patter thickens before a dust storm, they say, because the masons working at the edge of a coming drift tap faster. It goes quiet for a moment when a pane falls. It surges in the dew hour, when the mortar is soft and the tide of builders pours over the walls, until the stone under your cheek feels like something breathing.

Whether the masons are *speaking* in any real sense, the Still cannot say. But they have learned certain rhythms. There is a pattern of taps that means, as nearly as anyone can tell, *here, bring flakes here*. There is another that spreads outward through a district when a wall has failed, and after it, within the hour, the gray streams begin to turn toward the breach. There is one the Still hear only rarely, only very deep in the stone, and only near the great tower at the center. It is slow and regular, and they have given it a name in touch-speech that they will not translate for outsiders.

The Still have learned to tap too. They do not tap much, and they do not tap carelessly. But when a household needs to move through a working district, one of them kneels and knocks a short rhythm into the wall with a knuckle or a pebble. The masons ahead of them open a path, a parting in the gray tide wide enough for people to walk through. When the people have passed, it closes again.

## The Hammerstones

Every mason carries one stone its whole life.

The Still know this because they have watched. A young mason, not yet the width of a palm, searches the dust for days, testing pebbles with its forelegs and rejecting them. When it finds the one it wants, it never lets it go. Over a lifetime of tapping, the stone wears. An old mason's hammerstone is smooth as an egg and fitted perfectly to the curve of its forelegs, polished on one face by ten thousand thousand blows.

When a mason dies, the other masons take its hammerstone. They do not leave it in the dust. They carry it, one mason bearing it the way it would bear a brick, clamped underneath, across whatever distance is necessary, to the center of the basin. There they set it into the tower.

This is what the tower is. The Still have climbed its lower courses and put their hands into the gaps in the flake-brick. At its core, behind the scalloped casing, the great tower that no one understands is a column of hammerstones, millions of them, fitted together in curving courses and mortared in chewed gypsum and dew. Each one is worn smooth by a single life of work. It is the tallest structure in Sessile. It is still growing, one death at a time.

And it rings. That is what the slow deep rhythm is. Every mason in the city taps against stone, and all that stone is connected, footing to wall to bridge to arch. Somewhere at the center of it, the tower gathers the patter. Put your ear to its base and you hear something almost like a voice: a low, continuous, many-layered hum, the tapping of the whole living city carried and held and sounding back through the hammerstones of the dead.

The Still bring their own dead here.

## The Dead

They do it on purpose. When one of the Still dies, the household carries the body through the working districts, knocking their way through the gray tide, to the base of the central tower. They lay the body down on the dust among the lowest courses. Then, for the first time in that person's life, no one turns them.

The masons come. The first course is laid along the lowest line of the body before the next dew hour ends. By the third night the shape is sealed. It is curved and pale and scalloped, exactly like the shapes of lost travelers in the walls elsewhere, except that these were given, and each has a hammerstone at its heart. Before they leave, the family places a pebble in the dead person's hand, one they chose and carried and wore smooth over a lifetime of knocking on walls.

The lowest ring of the great tower is made of these, dozens and then hundreds of human shapes, lying on their sides in a spiral around the column of mason-stones. Over time the masons build upward past them, and they are drawn into the tower's body the way the ancient walls are being drawn into mason-work everywhere in the city.

The Still visit. They lie down beside the tower and press their ears to it, and they listen for a long time. When travelers ask whether they can hear their dead, they are reluctant to answer in any way an outsider could understand. The nearest they come is to take the traveler's hand, lay it flat against the base of the tower, and hold it there. Then, very slowly, with their own hand over it, they turn it a quarter turn.

## Children

A child born in Sessile has never heard a room echo. They have never heard rain. The first voice they know is their mother's pulse, and the second is the patter of the masons coming up through the floor where she lies. Before they can walk they have learned to be turned and to turn back, and before they can speak by touch they have learned to put their ears to stone.

The Still say their children are the only people who truly live in Sessile. The rest, even those born here, spend their lives remembering, somewhere far down, that the world is supposed to make noise. The children do not remember this. To a child of Sessile, the air is simply where the light is, and the stone is where the sound is, and there is nothing strange in that. They grow up hearing the whole city talk. Some of them, it is said, can tell one mason's tapping from another's through a hand's width of limestone. A few, the oldest of the Still insist, can tell which districts are content.

## The Last Thing

Stand at the base of the central tower in the dew hour and you are at the center of everything in Sessile that lives.

Above you, the tower goes up into the white haze, taller than anything the first builders made. Its casing is dark with condensation, crawling with chalk lice and streaming with masons. Its upper chimneys are full of swifts. Somewhere high above, at the top, the masons are laying new courses toward the selenite. They are closer to the roof each year.

The Still have a guess about why. They say it quietly, in touch-speech only, with their fingers against the inside of your wrist. The roof is failing faster than it grows, and has been for longer than anyone knows. Someday it will all be down, every pane, and Sessile will be open to the desert sky, and the dust will be dry forever, and the dew will stop. Unless something holds it up. Unless something rises to meet it, some column tall enough and damp enough to become a seam, so that new crystal can feather out from it the way it feathers from the old seams now, and the roof can grow again from the center outward.

They do not know if this is true. They admit they may be dreaming the masons' purpose into them, the way they may be dreaming meaning into the ripples of the old walls. But they have felt the slow rhythm in the heart of the tower. They have laid their dead in its footing and their ears against its stones. What they hear there, they say, is not the sound of something ending.

They say it is the sound of a city holding still for exactly as long as it takes to be turned.

When you leave, climbing the smooth ramp back toward the plateau, the last of the Still you pass will lay a hand on your shoulder. It is only for a moment, a light touch, and very slowly they will roll you a little way toward the light.
