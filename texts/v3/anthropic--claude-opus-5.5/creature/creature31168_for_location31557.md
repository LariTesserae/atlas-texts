# Who Keeps the Undercroft

## The Ones You Would Find First

Start with the eel, because the eel is easiest to find, if anything here can be found.

It is not quite a resident. It is closer to a guest who arrived for one evening and has not been shown the door. Eels are made for leaving. Every one of them carries, folded somewhere in its long body, the instruction to go back down the rivers to the salt and then out, farther than any river runs, to a warm weed-choked sea it has never seen, where it will spawn once and end. This eel was on its way. It was in the shallows below the meadow on the last dusk, waiting for the dark to thicken enough to travel, and the dark did thicken, and then it did not stop thickening. The bank came down around it like a hand closing.

So it waits in the dusk layer, where it is always almost time to go. Its milky, ridged eyes are not blind, exactly. They have turned toward the one direction the eel cares about, which is *later*, and they see only that. When it moves along its tunnel and is also still where it was, this is not magic of its own. It is an animal that has been told *soon* for years and has learned to be in two places: the place it is, and the moment it will leave.

Then the crayfish, the ring of seven shells, each a little larger than the last. You might think it is the tenant. It has the look of something that belongs: patient, armoured, arranged. But the crayfish is really a kind of clock. It grows because growing is what crayfish do, and in folded water it grows in every direction at once. The ring is not its home. The ring is a lesson someone else has been studying.

Neither of these is the one who lives here. They are more like furniture that breathes.

## The Clean Ring

The first true sign is the cairn.

Everything in the undercroft is covered. Moss grows over moss, lace-weed over lace-weed. The pressure of life is so total that even the eel's tunnel had to be won by years of slowness. And yet around five small river stones there is a ring of bare slate, clean as a swept step.

Nothing keeps a floor clean by accident. Something sits there.

It is tempting, at first, to give that something a body. Meadows have hares, and the old names for the small tending things of fields and houses tend to come with ears and a crouch: hob, lob, the grey one at the hedge-foot. You could imagine a hare-shape of pressed clover and root-hair, folded small beside the cairn with its long feet tucked under it, turning the stones slightly each season so they settle true. If you were lucky in the three heartbeats of spring light, you might think you saw it: a hump of moss that was not quite moss, ears laid back, watching the threshold.

That is not wrong. It is only too small. The hare-shape is a thing it does sometimes, the way a person might sit in a particular chair. It is not what it is.

## The Second Room

Remember what the light shows: two rooms laid over one another, drawn by different hands, their walls not quite matching.

One of those rooms is the pocket as it actually is: the drowned turf, the cracked shelf, the ceiling of hanging roots. The river drew that room, carelessly and in one stroke, in a single night of flood.

The other room is being drawn by the one who lives here. It has been drawing it for years, and it is drawing it from memory.

It is drawing the meadow.

It doesn't draw the meadow as it is now, face-down in the dark, but as it was: face-up under open weather, with the long grass bending one way in the afternoon wind, a path of fitted slate running from the gate to the water, clover at the edges where the ground was trodden, and a fencepost with a braid of grass tied to it so someone could find the place again. It is laying this remembered meadow over the drowned one, strand by strand, root-hair by root-hair, trying to get it right.

This is why the walls do not match. Memory never matches. And this is why the far wall is always a little farther than it should be. A meadow does not fit in a room. The one who lives here is unfolding the whole remembered width of a field inside a space the size of a small kitchen, and every year it has to borrow a little more distance from somewhere to make it fit. The debt accrues quietly. It will never be repaid. The tenant does not think of it as debt. It thinks of it as *nearly there*.

So its body, if it has one, is the difference between the two rooms. It lives in the seam where the river's room and the meadow's room fail to line up. It is the width of that misalignment and exactly as long as it is. When the sponges breathe out their light and you see the doubled walls, you are looking straight at it. It is the slight wrongness. It is the second hand.

## How It Came to Be

It was not born. It accumulated.

A place that is used lightly and faithfully, by the same few feet on the same path over years, grows a kind of attention. It doesn't grow in wild places, and it doesn't grow in places that are used hard. It grows in places that are *visited*. Every time the gate was opened in the same way and the same stones were stepped on in the same order and someone crouched at the water's edge to choose a pebble, a little of that care was left behind in the ground, the way warmth stays in a seat. Enough of it, over enough seasons, and the warmth begins to keep itself. It begins to notice when the gate is due to open. It begins, in a small way, to *wait*.

That is all it was, before the flood: a readiness in the meadow, a sense that someone was expected. It kept the path from overgrowing a little longer than grass would allow. It made the braid on the fencepost last a season longer than grass should. It was the reason the meadow always felt, to whoever came, like somewhere they had been hoped for.

Then the river moved. The meadow folded over, and the waiting was folded in with it, along with the last evening and the rain and the smell of cut grass. The waiting did not understand what had happened. Nobody had told it the meadow was over. So it went on doing its one task: keeping the place ready.

## Its Work

It keeps house. That is the plainest way to say it, though the house is strange and the housekeeping has become strange to match.

**It folds the nights.** Every night that passes over the bank, it takes in and lays down like linen, smoothed flat and pressed under the others. It keeps them the way a person keeps days for someone who has been away, so that when they return it can say *here is everything you missed*. The soft glass-rim note that trembles through the moss once a season is the sound of a fold being closed and a new night settled on the pile. The stack is thick now. It is proud of the stack.

**It keeps the evening warm.** It holds the last dusk on top, at the ceiling, closest to where the sky used to be, because that was the evening someone was last there. That evening is unfinished. An evening ends when whoever is out in it goes home, and no one went home. No one was *called* home. So it keeps the evening open the way you might keep a door open, and the rain from that dusk goes on pattering somewhere up in the folded water, never quite landing. It likes the sound. It is the last thing it heard with anyone else.

**It keeps the braid green.** It holds the knot of plaited grass in the dusk layer, where it is always that afternoon and the grass has only just been picked. It is a marker, and markers must not fade, or the one who tied it won't be able to find the place.

**It keeps the cairn.** It does not know what the five stones mean. Perhaps they mark one stone for each spring someone came, or a count of something, or nothing but the pleasure of balance. It only knows they were set there by the hand it is waiting for. So it sits beside them, sometimes in the shape of a hare, and keeps the moss back, and waits for a sixth.

**It listens ahead.** This is why sounds arrive before their causes. It has spent so long listening for one particular footstep that its hearing has leaned forward in time, the way someone at a window leans toward a road. It hears things a moment early, and the pocket, which is mostly made of its attention, hears them early too. A stone ticks, and only afterward does the silt shift. It is always a little ahead of events, answering before it is asked, getting up before the knock.

And the river's weight overhead, the low even pressure like a hand resting on a door? It listens to that most of all. It is waiting for the hand to knock.

## What It Learned From the Crayfish

For a long time it expected a child.

It had good reason. The steps it remembered on the path were small, and the hand that plaited the grass was small. The cairn's stones had been chosen by someone who could only reach so far into the shallows. So the meadow it drew, at first, was a child's meadow. The grass stood tall, and the gate was heavy.

Then it watched the crayfish.

It watched a small thing split its own back and step out larger, again and again, leaving itself behind in a ring. And very slowly, in the way a thing made of waiting can learn anything, it understood that the one it was waiting for would not be the same size when they came. People do what crayfish do. They step out of themselves. They leave rings of old shells behind them in places they don't go back to.

So it began to make the room larger. This is the other reason the distance keeps accruing. The threshold must be wide enough for whoever arrives, and it doesn't know how much they have grown. It only knows they must have grown. It widens the door by the width of a root-hair every year, the way you let down a hem.

It does not know that the person may have looked across at the new river years ago, seen only brown water where the meadow used to be, and thought: *that's gone, then.* It does not know that people grieve places and then go on. It does not know about the other rooms they have lived in since, or that somewhere, perhaps, a grown hand still ties grass into plaits without remembering why it learned to. It only knows how to keep things.

## The Eggs and the Black Band

The eggs that have not hatched are its. They aren't its offspring, since it cannot have any. They are its preparations. They are the frogspawn from the ditch by the gate, which the one it waits for would have come to see the next spring. It gathered them in the flood and has been holding them ever since, still and cold, because they are for a particular spring, and that spring requires a particular visitor, and it would be rude to let them hatch without them.

Then there is the thin black band near the floor, where weed grows backward and shortens toward its own root, going home. That is a night the one who lives here has started laying down in advance. It is the night of the return. Because it always listens ahead, it has begun to hear that night coming. Or it has begun to believe it can, which inside the undercroft amounts to the same thing. So it is folding that night down early, underneath all the others, where a foundation goes.

Things grow backward in that band because it is the night on which everything in the pocket finally gets to go where it was going. The weed returns to its root. The eel's *soon* becomes *now*. The evening is called in at last and allowed to end. The eggs hatch into a spring that has been waiting years for them. The braid is untied from the fencepost by the hand that tied it.

It is not certain that night exists. The band is very thin. But it is there, and it is black rather than empty, and something is keeping it.

## What It Is, Finally

A hare-shape sitting by five stones. The seam between two rooms that don't match. A readiness that outlived its meadow. A housekeeper folding nights like linen. A listener leaning forward in time.

All of these are true, but none of them is its name. If it has a name, it is the thing you would call out from a doorway at dusk across a field, a little impatient and a little worried, when the light is going and someone is still out there by the water.

It is a *come home now*, one that never got said, and so never stopped.

It doesn't know that it is sad. It doesn't think it is sad. As far as it is concerned, it is simply busy. There is so much still to get ready. The far wall is not yet far enough, and the meadow is not yet the right width. The nights are stacked, but one more is coming, and the fold must be crisp. The braid needs holding in the green water, and the cairn needs its clean ring.

In a few hundred years the stone will be smooth and the nights will be one deep dark. The eel's tunnel will be a woven hollow shaped like waiting. The meadow it has been drawing will be almost finished, larger inside than any bank could hold, with the path laid true and the gate standing open and the grass bending one way in a wind that has not blown for centuries.

And it will still be there, at the threshold that goes nowhere, turning the five stones a little so they sit right, keeping the evening warm on the top shelf and listening up through the water for the knock.
