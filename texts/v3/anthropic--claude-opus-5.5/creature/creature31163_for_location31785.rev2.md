# Who Lives in the Accretion Highlands

## The First One You Meet

You will probably meet a line-walker first, because they are the only people who spend much time outside the cities.

In mist season you hear one before you see them. A faint rhythmic ticking comes out of the grey, a small stone weight on a cord tapping against the sky-line as they walk, so their hand never has to leave it. Then a shape resolves. It is shorter than you expected and broad through the chest, bundled in layers of undyed grey wool that has gone dark with wet. The hood is pushed back, because hoods trap the mizzle against the neck and the walkers would rather be wet than clammy. Their hair is plastered flat and beaded silver. Their cheeks are ruddy in two high patches, the color that altitude and wind put into every face here by the age of ten.

They will stop at a polite distance and wait for you to speak first. That is courtesy, and it is also caution. On open rock, a stranger is rarer than a wolf and less predictable.

Most of what you need to know about the people of the highland is already visible in that figure. They are shaped by thin air and cold water, and dressed in wool nobody sheared. They walk with a hand on a line, and they are unhurried in a way that is not quite patience. It is closer to a sense of scale. They live on a land that grows a finger's width a year. Very little, to them, is urgent.

## Bodies

They are built for the height. Their chests are deep and barrel-shaped, their hearts large and slow. A resting pulse in the fifties is ordinary for an adult. They breathe through the nose, even when running, and outsiders laboring for air find this faintly insulting. Their hands and feet stay warm in cold that would numb a lowlander, and their fingers are short and thick. They tend to be dark-haired and dark-eyed. Their skin weathers to the color of wet clay in the exposed places and stays pale everywhere else, so that a highlander undressed looks oddly like two people joined at the collar and wrists.

Their voices are soft. This is partly habit. In a land this quiet, a raised voice carries unpleasantly far and sounds like alarm. It is also partly physiology: thin air carries less weight of sound, so they have learned to make their words with the front of the mouth, crisp and close, rather than pushing them from the chest. Their speech has a fine clicking precision. Lowland voices sound to them like wet cloth being wrung.

They are long-lived, and they age slowly and then all at once. A person may seem fifty for thirty years, then in a single winter become very old.

## Wool

Nobody fences the deer, so nobody shears them.

Instead, in the late spring, the lichen-deer shed. They rub against stone to be rid of the winter coat, and the wool comes away in grey hanks caught on every sharp edge of the plateau. The newest rock, with its unrounded seams, is the best comb in the world. People go out in groups to gather it, walking the Middle Reaches with sacks and plucking wool off the slabs like harvesting a thin grey crop. Children are good at it, and it is one of the first things they are trusted to do outside the walls.

The wool is spun in the courtyards all summer. It is never dyed; there is little here to dye it with. So the whole population dresses in the grey of the deer, which is the grey of the lichen, which is the grey of the stone under cloud. A crowd in a market square looks, from a tower, like a patch of the plateau that has decided to move.

The one exception is the color of the undyed wool itself, which varies. Deer from the Core are browner. Deer that winter near the rim come out almost silver. A practiced eye can tell where a person's clothes were gathered, and so roughly where they have walked. "Your coat's from the south flats," someone might say. It is an observation, and sometimes it is a question.

## Hands

You can tell the askers by their hands.

Speaking to the lattice is done through water. That is all an outsider is ever told, and it is roughly true. In the lowest room of every house, and in public basins along the streets, there are asking-cisterns: wide, shallow, and fed by the cold seep that is everywhere underfoot. An asker kneels at the lip and lays both hands flat beneath the surface, on the stone floor of the basin. They hold them there, sometimes for an hour and sometimes for most of a day. They do something with the hands. It might be pressure, or rhythm, or a pattern of fingertips too subtle to watch. Somewhere below, in the lattice, the request is heard.

The water is a few degrees above freezing. Nobody can do this for long without cost.

Old askers have hands you do not forget. They are white, the white of something kept out of the sun and soaked. The skin is puckered in permanent fine ridges, and the knuckles are swollen and knotted with the ache of decades. Many cannot fully close their fingers. They hold cups in both palms, the way you'd hold a bird. Among the highlanders, these hands are not pitied. They are the most honored thing a body can become. When an old asker lays one of those ruined hands on a child's head, the child will remember it all their life.

Young askers wear their first damage proudly and a little anxiously. It is a sign that they have been trusted, and a promise of what is coming. Apprentices sit beside a master at the cistern for years before they are allowed to put their own hands in. They watch the old hands under water, rippled and pale, moving like something alive in a pool.

What they ask for is mostly small. They ask that a wall thicken on the windward side, or that a floor warm. They ask that a crack be closed, a drain kept open, a window thinned by another hair's breadth so the room will be brighter in twenty years. Grand requests, a tower or a new district, are made by many askers together, over years, in rotation. There are rooms in the old cities where someone has had their hands in the same cistern, one shift after another, for longer than anyone alive has been alive.

## How They Count

A finger's width a year. The highlanders measure almost everything that way.

A person's age is given in fingers. "She's seventy fingers deep" means she is seventy years old. It also means, literally, that the ground she was born on now lies seventy fingers beneath her feet. Children like to lie flat and have an adult lay hands across them, counting widths, to see how many years tall they are lying down. A grown adult is somewhere near a hundred and seventy. Nobody is that old, so it is a kind of joke, and a kind of comfort: *you are taller than your life.*

Promises are also counted in fingers. To swear something "a hand deep" is to swear it will outlast a few years. "An arm deep" means a generation. The most solemn oath a highlander can give is "to the floor," meaning until the room you stand in has been filled. Nobody swears that lightly.

This counting has worked its way into their grammar. Their language has the ordinary tenses, and then another one. It has no good name in translation; the scholars' clumsiest rendering is *the held future*. It is used for things that will be true inside stone: what a house will be, what the lattice has been asked for and is working toward. "The window *will-be-held* bright." It is the tense of every building conversation, every inheritance, every dedication cut into a lintel. Children learn it as soon as they learn *tomorrow*. Outsiders never quite get it right. They use it for plans, and the highlanders wince, because a plan can fail. The held future is not a hope. It is a thing already underway, too slow to see.

## The Rising Cities

The ground grows under the cities too, of course. The askers can slow it but not stop it. They can ask for streets to stay open, but not forever, and not without cost. So every city is rising.

Walk into one of the old districts and you will notice that the doorways sit oddly high. Steps lead up to them, and the steps are newer than the doors. Look down into the grated light-wells along the streets and you will see, several meters below, the tops of older windows, now dark, their thinned stone gone dull. Those were the ground-floor rooms of the same buildings, two hundred years ago. Below them are rooms older still, which no light reaches. Below those the stone is solid, and it contains, sealed perfectly, the shapes of every room that was ever there.

The highlanders do not think of this as loss. A house is expected to climb. Each generation lives a few steps higher than the last, and each generation, at some point in its middle age, holds a small ceremony. The family's lowest room is emptied and swept. Everyone who lived in it stands in it one last time. Then the askers are told to stop holding it open. Over the following years, the room fills.

People leave things in these rooms on purpose. A cup. A child's first spindle. A letter written on thin-ground stone. A lock of hair. A pair of worn shoes. The lattice closes around them without disturbing them, the way amber takes an insect, and they are held. The highlanders say the city remembers downward. Its oldest memories are the deepest, and every memory is still exactly where it was left.

A few of the archivists spend their lives on this. They use record-stones, and they have a technique of listening they do not explain, placing an ear to a floor and a palm to a wall. They claim they can read the sealed rooms beneath a street: the outline of a table, a fallen spoon, the curve of a stair nobody has walked in eight hundred years. Nobody knows how accurate they are. But when an archivist tells a family *your great-great-grandmother's loom is beneath your kitchen, a little to the left of the hearth*, the family believes it, and moves the hearth rug so nobody stands on it.

## The Dead

The dead are given to the stone.

There is no burning here, because there is no fuel, and nothing is buried in the usual sense, because there is no soil to bury in. When someone dies, their body is washed and wrapped in wool from the deer of the place they were born. It is laid in a small low chamber in the lowest part of the family house. The askers of the house then do the one thing they otherwise avoid: they put their hands in the cistern and ask, specifically, for that room to be filled.

It takes a few seasons. In the cold, the body keeps well. The lattice works from the floor upward and the walls inward, and the grief of the household is paced by it. For a year, the dead person is still *in the house*, and a relative can go down and sit by them. Then the stone reaches the shoulders, the face, and above. After that there is a smooth floor, and then more rooms built above it, and in time the chamber is a hundred fingers deep, then a thousand.

In the old cities the grain of the bedrock is full of them. The archivists can find them the same way they find the looms and spoons: a shape of a person curled on its side, wrapped, still, a few meters below the market. Whole neighborhoods stand on layers of their own ancestors, closely packed and exactly preserved. It is not morbid to them. It is simply what a city is. When a highlander says their home is *well-founded*, they mean it has many dead beneath it.

Travelers who die between cities are a real grief, because the open rock does not know them. If the body can be carried home, it is. If not, the line-walkers have a custom: they choose the nearest pool, lay the wrapped body in its shallows, and one of them puts their hands in the water beside it and asks. Nobody is sure whether the lattice beneath the open plateau listens as well as the lattice under the cities. But the pools fill in, eventually. Sometimes a walker, years later, passes a smooth patch of stone where a pool used to be, and touches it, and goes on.

## Other Lives

Not everyone is an asker, and not everyone lives in a city.

**Line-walkers** tend the sky-lines. The filaments stretch across the plateau from city to city, tethered at intervals to stone posts the lattice grew for them. The walkers inspect them, retension them, and guide travelers along them. It is lonely work, and it attracts a certain kind of person: taciturn, self-contained, fond of weather. They are a close fraternity with their own slang, most of it about wind. They carry news. They are the only highlanders who know the other cities well, and within their home city they are a little foreign. People lower their voices around them, the way you might around someone who has been ill.

The sky-lines do something besides carry messages. The walkers are the only ones who talk about it plainly. When you hold a line, especially in still air, you can feel through it the weather of the far city: a thrum when the wind is up there, a slackness when it is calm, a strange shivering hum, felt more than heard, a day or two before a squall line reaches the place where you stand. Walkers say, only half joking, that they can feel a city *breathing* at the far end of a line. Some of them claim more than that. They are not always believed.

**Rim-sounders** work the edge. The newest shelves are unstable, sometimes hollow, and the seabird colonies are packed on them in their tens of thousands, and the eggs are food. So people go out to the rim in spring. The sounders go first, tapping the weeping stone ahead of them with long hafts tipped in old stone and listening to the note it gives back. Sound rock rings dull and short. Hollow rock rings long. Very new rock, only months old, barely rings at all; it feels, the sounders say, *like tapping a sleeping animal*. They walk the gatherers in on a path of safe notes, through the enormous noise of the colony. The birds rise screaming, the air thickens with wings and fishy stink, and the gatherers take one egg from every third nest and no more. The rule is very old. The sounders are, by general agreement, the bravest people in the highland, and the most superstitious. They will not speak on a shelf younger than they are.

**Gardeners** tend the lee-plots, and they are the keepers of soil. Soil on the plateau is carried, handful by handful, out of the joints of the Core, and it is the most precious thing a household owns. It is inherited. It is given as a dowry, measured out in lidded stone boxes. Good soil has a lineage the way a family does: "this bed came from the Hollow of Seven Stones, four hundred years ago." Gardeners are fierce about it. A stranger who steps in a garden bed will be told, very quietly, to step out again, and the silence afterward will be worse than shouting.

## The Ones Who Don't Ask

Out in the Middle Reaches, following the deer, there are people who have never put their hands in a cistern and never will.

The city-dwellers have several names for them, none quite kind. The walkers, who meet them most, call them the *Unasking*. They call themselves by the names of the herds they follow. There are not many of them, perhaps a few thousand across the whole plateau, in small bands. They live in felt tents that can be struck in an hour, and they walk the slow circuit of the deer around the highland, a full turn every eleven or twelve years. They eat what the deer lead them to: pool-shrimp netted in spring, eggs from the rare inland plover scrapes, pika, now and then a deer that is old and failing. They burn dried deer-dung and the woody roots of cushion plants, and their camps are the only places on the plateau that smell of smoke.

They do not ask the lattice for anything. They are not ignorant of it; they know the cities and trade with them, bringing wool, felts, and cured meat in exchange for soil-grown greens and the rare fired glass. Their refusal is a position. It is hard to get them to state it, and when they do, it is usually as a question: *Why should the ground do what you say?*

It is a real disagreement, and an old one. The cities regard the Unasking as a little feral and a little holy, the way people regard hermits. The Unasking regard the cities as rather sad: people who live inside the stone's answers, and have forgotten the stone was there first. The rule that nobody fences the deer is, the Unasking say, their rule, one the cities agreed to long ago. The cities do not dispute this.

A child of the Unasking and a child of the cities, meeting at the edge of the outer fields, will stare at each other's hands. The city child has hands soft from warm floors. The herd child's hands are cracked and brown and have never been in a cistern, and never will. Each finds the other's strange.

## Children

Children here are raised on stone, and in stone.

They crawl on floors warm as a sunned boulder, in rooms whose walls are the same substance as the ground outside. Almost the first game any child plays is one of burying. They hide a treasured pebble or a knot of wool in some forgotten corner of a low room. They check it every year to see how deep the floor has grown around it. Eventually they come back and it is gone, closed over entirely, a smooth patch of floor. This is often a child's first real grief, and their first real understanding of where they live. Parents do not prevent it. It is considered an important lesson, and a gentle one: *the ground keeps what you give it.*

They learn the pools early. There is nothing more fascinating to a highland child than a pool in spring, when the meltwater comes and the fairy shrimp bloom by the billion. A clear shallow basin suddenly swarms with tiny translucent swimmers, upside down, rowing with feathery legs. Children lie on their bellies on the cold rim of the pool and put their faces to the water, and the phalaropes spin a few meters away, unbothered. Every highlander has a memory of this: the cold on the belly, the teeth aching from a sip, the thousand small lives turning in the light.

They are taught silence the way other children are taught manners. Not muteness; they chatter as children do. But they are taught that in the open, a shout means danger. They learn to listen for the pika whistle that means a stoat, and the plover's alarm that means a fox, and the long far howl that means a wolf, and the particular hiss on the wind that means a squall is crossing the pools and it's time to run home. By six or seven they can tell, from a sound on the wind, how far away something is. Lowland children, the few who come here, seem to them almost deaf.

## Songs

There isn't much music. The larks at dawn are the most anyone hears on most mornings, and people love them. There is a whole class of short, wordless highland songs that imitate larks: a few rising notes, a trill, a stop. People sing them under their breath while working.

The one real song-form is the asking-song. It isn't sung *to* the lattice; nobody believes the lattice hears air. It is sung by the people keeping the asker company. Asking takes hours and the water is agonizing, so family and friends sit with the asker at the cistern and sing to hold them up. The songs are long, slow, and repetitive, and they have no rhymes. They are counting songs, finger-songs, lists of the dead of the house and what each one left in the sealed rooms below. *Beneath the hearth, her loom. Beneath the loom, his cup. Beneath the cup, the child who did not grow.* A long asking-song can go back forty generations. Listening, the asker's hands slowly stop shaking.

Sometimes, the askers say, this helps. They cannot explain why. The lattice cannot hear the song, but the requests go better when it is sung.

## What Else Lives There

The highlanders' engineers write about the lattice in careful, hedged language. Here is some of what they hedge about.

The lattice grows what it is asked to grow. But it also grows other things.

In abandoned rooms, sealed off and forgotten, the archivists sometimes find shapes they did not expect. Nobody asked for these. They are shelves along walls where a family once kept their dishes. They are a kind of niche the size and shape of a sleeping child, in a room where, the records say, a child once slept. They are steps where stairs used to be, grown in a room that had already been filled. It is as if the stone had been asked so many times, for so long, in that place, that it went on answering after everyone stopped.

Out on the rim, where nobody asks for anything, the new shelves are exactly what the seabirds need. They have a slight backward tilt, so an egg won't roll, a lip at the edge, and a width of roughly two nests. The sounders have noticed. The colonies grow year by year, and the shelves grow ahead of them, just a little, as if anticipating.

In the Middle Reaches, where the deer lie down in the lee of pools when the wind rises, the pools have lees. The deer sleep in the same few places year on year, on their slow circuit. The stone there is subtly smoother, slightly warmer, very slightly hollowed to the shape of a lying animal. The Unasking have always known this. They say, simply, that the deer have been asking longer than anyone.

The walkers say the sky-lines hum before a squall. They say this is not the wind. They say it feels as though the plateau itself is drawing breath before the storm, and that the lines are only strings laid across a very large, slow chest.

None of the engineers will write this down. What they write down, carefully, is this: the lattice appears to respond to *pattern* and *persistence*, whatever their source. Water moving in sheets for centuries lays down one kind of stone. Birds nesting in the same place for generations get another. The deer's patient circuit leaves its print, and a family laying hands on a cistern floor for four hundred years leaves its own. The highlanders do not speak to the lattice in any language it understands. They only do what everything else on the plateau does: they come back to the same place, again and again, for a very long time.

So the answer to the question *what lives in the Accretion Highlands?* grows larger the longer you stay.

It is the grey-clad people with their soft clicking voices and their ruined, honored hands. It is the herds they will not fence, and the herd-people who will not ask. It is the plovers and the phalaropes and the billion fairy shrimp in the spring pools. It is the stoats and the white-breasted seabirds and the wolves in the Core. It is the dead, a layer beneath every city, curled in their wool, perfectly kept.

And beneath all of them there is something none of them has a name for, though they have lived in its answers for a thousand years. It is not a mind, exactly, or not one anyone could speak to. It is closer to a *listening*: the enormous, patient attention of a land that is still being made, and that is making itself, very slowly, out of whatever returns to it. It builds shelves for the birds that come back and hollows for the deer that lie down. It holds up rooms for the people who keep asking, and it goes on holding a sleeping child's shape long after the child has grown and died and been laid, wrapped in grey wool, in the low room at the bottom of the house.

## At the Rim, Again

A sounder, old now, taps her way out along a shelf at dusk. It is not for eggs; the season is over and the colony is quiet. She comes because she has come here every autumn since she was a girl, and she is not sure anymore whether she is choosing to.

The stone rings dull and short under her haft, sound all the way to the edge. Below her the cloud sea is going violet. Behind her the long tilt of the plateau is pooled with a thousand mirrors, and far off a city's windows are warming amber through the thinned stone. A herd of deer lies in the lee of a basin, darker grey against grey.

She sits down on the shelf, the one she has sat on every year. It is slightly warm. When she was young it was too small for her; now it isn't. There is a faint hollow in it, the shape of someone sitting. She has never asked for it, and she has never told anyone.

She puts her white, puckered hand flat on the stone beside her, and leaves it there, and doesn't ask for anything. A line of seabirds comes home low over the cloud, silent. The wind moves past her ears. Beneath her, a finger's width a year, the ground goes on answering.
