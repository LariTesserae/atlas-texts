# Those Who Live in the Held Sea

## The Ammareti

The first thing to know about the people of Ammaret is that they are not in a hurry. Everything else follows from that.

They are tall, taller than lowlanders, though you rarely see them at full height. In the water they fold and drift, and in the bells they sit hunched on the ledges with their knees drawn up and their feet in the pool. Their chests are deep and rounded, like the bells themselves. Their breath is slow: a lowlander breathes four or five times in the span of one Ammareti inhalation. If you sit beside one in the close air of a bell, you begin to breathe with them without meaning to. Then you feel the drowsiness come on, and you understand that this is how they want you to breathe.

Their skin is pale, the pale of things that have never been in direct sun: the underside of a frond, the inside of a shell. Beneath the pallor is a faint bronze cast, as if the iron in the water has worked its way in. On the old it deepens to something like tarnished copper. When they climb into a bell and the water sheets off them, their skin holds the lampmoss light for a moment, a blue sheen that fades as they dry.

Their eyes are large and dark, the pupils so wide there is almost no iris. In the glow of the weed forests they see perfectly. On the rim, in the pewter daylight, they are nearly blind. The few who have climbed out describe the upper world as a white roar, a pain behind the eyes. They go back down quickly and are quiet for days afterward.

They never cut their hair. In water it is never in the way. It lifts and spreads around them like the smoke the description spoke of, and an Ammareti swimming through tallstem trails a dark cloud behind them that tangles with the fronds and comes free again. They bind it loosely with weed-cord for work, and the bindings mean things: a mason's knot, a carrier's knot, the double loop of someone in mourning. A child's hair is left loose until the child has laid down their first full hand of stone.

Their hands are the most human thing about them, and the most worked. The masons' palms are thick and gray with callus, and the pads of their fingers have been polished smooth by years of pressing warm rock into shape. Masons have almost no fingerprints. Their children say you can tell a mason's bell by the walls, which are covered in the faint, overlapping ovals of a hand that pressed and pressed and did not leave a mark.

## How They Speak

Voices do not carry underwater, so in the forests and passages the Ammareti speak with their hands.

The hand-speech is slow, like everything else. Each shape is held a long moment before it melts into the next, and a sentence underwater can take as long to say as a meal takes to eat. There is no hand-shape for *hurry*. There is one for *soon*: a cupped palm slowly opening, like a bellflower before it lets go. It is used for anything from an hour to a lifetime, and context is expected to supply the difference.

They also write, and their writing is the lampmoss.

A fingertip dragged across the moss leaves a line of brightness that fades over the course of an hour. The Ammareti have built a script from this. It is spare and looping, made of strokes that can be drawn in one continuous movement. Messages are left on the terraces: *gone to the eastern vents*, *the old eel Hessa passed this way*, *come to my bell when the moss is dark*. By the time the moss has dimmed, the message has either been read or no longer matters.

Nothing in Ammaret is written to last. They have no records except the stone itself. When a lowlander asked an elder how they could bear to have no books, the elder took a long time to answer and then said: *The moss remembers for an hour. The stone remembers forever. What else is there worth remembering for?* She seemed to think this settled the matter.

In the bells, where the air holds sound, they talk aloud, softly. Their language is full of long vowels and open, unhurried consonants. It has an odd grammar that lowlanders struggle with. There is a present tense and there is a *not-yet* tense, and nothing else. The past is not a tense but a place: you point downward, toward the older stone, when you speak of it. *My grandmother* is said with a small gesture toward the floor.

## Names

A child is named for the vent it was born nearest, and for the layer.

The masons keep a count of flows the way lowlanders keep a count of years, and every Ammareti carries the name of the stratum laid down in the season of their birth: *Oresh-of-the-Quick-Flow*, *Tai-of-the-Pale-Seam*, *Menn-of-the-Stillness* (born during one of the rare decades when the vents quieted). These layer-names are real places. A person can swim down to the buried terraces, where the oldest living bells give way to sealed rock, and put their hand on the band of stone that is their own. Many do this when they are troubled.

As they age, the Ammareti rise through the city. The new stone climbs, the old bells are buried, and families move upward with the living band, so a person dies many feet higher than they were born. They say you spend your life climbing out of your own name. Toward the end, people swim down to visit their birth layer less often, because it has become hard to reach through the sealed rock. Then one day it is simply gone, beneath the floor, and this is understood as a sign that the person is nearly finished.

## Sleep

They sleep a great deal. The vent-gas makes certain of that. But the Ammareti do not think of sleep as rest. They think of it as listening.

The creaking and ticking of the cooling stone never stops, and they fall asleep to it from infancy. Their dreams, they say, come up through it. Dreams in Ammaret are shared property. On waking, people murmur them to whoever is in the bell, and the dreams are compared. When three or four people in different bells dream the same thing on the same night (a wide pale plain, a red light moving under a crust, the sensation of being very large and very slow), it is spoken of in the passages for weeks.

They do not say *I dreamed*. They say *it dreamed through me*, and they do not always agree on what *it* is.

The breath-carriers have the strangest dreams, because they breathe the freshest gas, straight from the fissures. Some of the old carriers claim to know which vent a dream came from. The gas from the northern cracks, they say, brings dreams of pressure and holding. The gas from the deep central vent brings dreams of something turning over. One carrier, very old and badly burned across the shoulders, told me she had once dreamed she was a single grain of rock, being slowly pressed into place among millions of others, and that she had never felt so welcome anywhere.

## The Old Grow Heavy

Here is something the Ammareti do not often say to visitors.

The water of the Held Sea is rich in minerals, and over a lifetime those minerals settle into the people who live in it. It happens in their bones first. A child of Ammaret floats easily and must work to sink, which is why children are always kicking upward and bobbing into bells uninvited. An adult is balanced, neither rising nor falling. But the old begin to sink.

Their bones thicken and grow dense. Their skin takes on a faint gloss, the texture of the newest basalt, smooth and cool and ever so slightly glassy. Their joints grow stiff. Their movements, already slow, slow further, until an old Ammareti crossing a terrace is like a pillow of lava moving, a finger's width at a time. Eventually they can no longer swim up to the high bells where their families live, so they move down to the low bells near the vents, into the warmer water, where the red glow comes up through the moss.

The low bells are very quiet. The old sit on the ledges for days at a time, barely moving, humming single held notes that pass through the stone. People in the upper city put their palms to the walls to feel their grandparents singing.

When the dead are laid in the path of the lava, the Ammareti say it is not so much a burial as a completion. The body is already most of the way to stone. The lava only finishes the work. Mourners watch the slow red edge close over the glossy, bronze-dark skin, and sometimes they swear they cannot tell where the person stops and the rock begins.

This is why they say, without sorrow, that the city is built on its ancestors. They are not speaking about memory or honor. They are speaking about masonry.

## The Rope-Keepers

Not everyone lives beneath the water.

Someone tied the weighted rope. Someone twists the weed-fiber, replaces the stone at the bottom when it wears, and hauls lowlander visitors up again when they are finished dreaming. On the rim, in the steam, in cramped shelters of stacked glass-rock, live a handful of people who belong to neither world. The Ammareti call them *the ones who looked*.

They are Ammareti born, mostly. A few in every generation go up to the rim as children, the way all children are dared to, and see the open sky, and cannot unsee it. They come back down, but they are restless in the bells. The drowsiness does not take them. They breathe too fast. Eventually they go up for good.

They live on the warm black rock and slowly go half-blind in the daylight, and their skin burns and peels and burns again. They tend the rope. They trade weed-cord and snail shells with the goat-herders from the scrub pines below. They sit at the edge of the water with their feet in it, the way their families sit on the ledges of the bells, and they look out at the mist.

I asked one of them about the small stones in the burst bubbles of the glass. She said that when one of the ones-who-looked dies, the others take a pebble from the deep floor, brought up by a carrier, and set it in a hollow in the rim, facing the sea. Then she said she was not sure that was the reason. The stones were there before the rope-keepers remember, she said. Her grandmother, also a rope-keeper, had not known either. Maybe the custom came after the stones. Maybe they are only continuing what someone else was doing, the way the masons continue the bells.

She said it was possible the mountain put them there. When I asked what she meant, she only said, *It is making itself. It might want to see out.*

## The Not-Yet Creatures

There are no fish in the Held Sea, and the Ammareti have an answer for this, though they do not usually offer it to lowlanders. They say the fish have not been made yet.

The sea is young. The stone is young. Everything in it is at the very beginning: weed that clings with no soil, snails that graze a film on glass, a few hundred eels who remember more than the people do. The Ammareti believe the Held Sea is an early sea, the kind of sea the whole world must once have been, and that in it life is only now getting started.

In the upper forests, just below the mist, there are things. Almost no one has seen them clearly. Carriers returning from the surface catch them at the edge of vision: faint translucent flickers in the tallstem, a wobble of light that moves against the drift, a shape that is there and then is only water again. They have no bones that anyone has found and no eyes that anyone has seen. They may be the size of a finger or of a hand. When the lampmoss is disturbed near the upper terraces, it sometimes shows trails that no Ammareti made: thin, wandering, and faintly glowing, like writing in a language that has not decided on its letters yet.

The Ammareti call them *the not-yet*. They believe these are what fish will be, one day, after long enough. Or what something will be.

It is the not-yet that the diving bird hunts. The bird is black and silent and comes from the finished world outside, and it plunges into the upper forest and takes something the people cannot see. Then it rises again into the mist. This is why they disagree so much about the omen. Some say it is a warning: the finished world is already reaching in and eating the beginnings. Some say it is a blessing: the not-yet are real enough to be eaten, and so they are almost ready. One elder told me the bird is the mountain's mother, come to check on it. Another told me that was nonsense, and then was quiet for a while and did not argue further.

## The Eels

The lamp-eels are known individually, and here is why, though visitors are not always told.

They are very old. The oldest living eel, called Hessa, was an adult when the grandmothers of the current elders were children. She is the color of wet slate, forty feet long, and moves through the tallstem so slowly that the fronds do not bend around her. They part and close again as if she were a current rather than a body. Her eyes are clouded and pale.

The Ammareti say the eels are the only creatures in the sea who remember the old floor, the stone that is now buried. They say Hessa sometimes descends to the lowest living terraces and lays herself along the rock where the sealed bells are, and stays there for days. The carriers say she is visiting. They do not say whom.

When an eel dies, its skin becomes breath-bladders, and the air of the city passes through it. People say that the air carried in an old eel's skin brings the deepest sleep and the oldest dreams, dreams of a floor that is lower, of bells that are gone, of faces no one living has seen. Carriers are careful about which bladders they bring to which bells. The old eel-skins go to the grieving.

## What Lives Here

I came to the Held Sea thinking I would describe a people, and I have. But the longer I stayed, the harder it became to say where the people ended.

The Ammareti breathe the mountain's breath, drawn from its vents in the skins of its oldest creatures. They sleep to the sound of its stone settling and wake with its dreams in their mouths. They are made, slowly, of its minerals; they grow heavier every year until they are almost rock, and then they are laid down and become its floor. The masons press the new stone into hollows with their smoothed palms, eleven years to a single bell, guiding the growth the way a hand might steady the head of something newborn. The children are named for its layers. The old sing into its walls.

There is a phrase the Ammareti use for themselves, rarely, and usually only among each other. The nearest translation is *the ones it is using to think*.

The mountain is still being made. That is what they believe, and they believe it the way you believe the floor is under you. It is being made in the water, the way a child is made in water. It is warm as blood because it is warm with its own becoming. The pillows of lava that stack and slump like loaves in an oven are its body forming. The ticking of the cooling stone, which the children fall asleep to, is the sound of it growing. And somewhere in all that stone, in the ancestors pressed layer on layer, in the dense bones of the old and the drowsy slowness of everyone, it is beginning to be someone.

It does not yet know it is there. That is what the dreams are, the Ammareti say: the first stirrings. The sensation of being very large. The red light moving under a crust. A single grain, pressed into place and made welcome.

When the mountain is finished, the sea will drain, and the new world will stand in the open air, dry and complete. The Ammareti will be gone by then, or rather they will be everywhere: in every layer, in the foundation, in the long buried line of the city climbing upward toward the rim. They will be what the mountain is made of. They will be how it remembers. Perhaps the not-yet will be something by then. Perhaps the stones in the rim, facing the sea, will be how it first looks out.

The Ammareti do not fear this, and after enough time breathing their air, I found I could not fear it either. It is very far off. The rock grows a finger's width a day. There is time for the moss to glow and fade a hundred thousand times, for the bellflowers to swell and let go, for Hessa to lay herself along the buried floor and visit whoever is down there.

In a low bell near the central vent, where the water glows red and the air is thin and sweet, an old woman sits on a ledge with her glossy, copper-dark feet in the pool. She has not moved in three days. She is humming a single long note. It passes through the wall, and through the next wall, and through the stone of the whole city, faint and warm, and in the high bells people put their palms to the rock to feel it.

It sounds, if you listen long enough, less like a song than like something very large beginning, slowly, to breathe.
