# Who Lives on the Held Plateau

## The First Answer

The first answer is that nothing lives here. The description says as much, and for a while it seems true. There are no insects in the moss, no birds on the gantries, and no worms in the thin soil. The green is the only life, and it is the patient, spore-bearing kind that barely counts as a *who*.

So begin with the green, because it is the easiest thing to mistake for the inhabitant.

The moss is alive in the ordinary way. It photosynthesises under the white lid, drinks the drizzle, and grows. But it is worth kneeling at one of the deep cushions in a sett joint and pushing your fingers down into it, through the living tips and into the blackened layers underneath. The peat is cold and dense and it holds together. Pull up a plug and look at the side of it, where the old growth lies pressed in its strata like pages.

The pages are all the same page.

Every layer holds the same fronds in the same positions, curled at the same angles and leaning toward the same warmth. It isn't approximately the same. Each lamina is an exact copy of the one above it, so a moss cushion three metres deep is one moment of moss stacked three metres high. The green has not been growing over the ages. It has been repeating. The peat is not a record of time passing. It is a record of time failing to pass, over and over, and each failure has left the same thin deposit.

So the moss is not the one who lives here. It is closer to a tally mark, something scratched on the wall by whoever is keeping count.

## The Governor

The second answer comes from looking at the machines, and specifically at the part of each machine whose only job is to keep it the same.

Every engine in the Works has a governor. They are easy to miss among the flywheels and turbines: small, fast, busy things, a pair of iron balls on hinged arms spinning on a vertical shaft. As the engine speeds up, the balls swing outward. The arms lift a collar, the collar closes a valve, and the engine slows. As it slows, the balls fall inward and the valve opens. This is the oldest trick of the industrial age, a mechanism that notices its own drift and corrects it.

On the Held Plateau there are governors on everything. Some are in the ordinary places, on the engines and pumps. Others are in places no engineer would put them. There are spinning governors on the drain covers, on the tram platforms, on the stove doors in the worker blocks. One sits above the pools at the street corners, set to the water level. Another is set to the cloud base, bolted high on a cooling tower and spinning in the vapour. On the oldest halls, where iron is bolted over iron, you find governors that regulate other governors, and above those, more. The whole architecture is layered like the peat, correction laid over correction.

Watch one long enough and you realise it is not doing much. Its balls hang out at exactly the same angle, never swinging wider or dropping inward, because nothing ever drifts. It corrects deviations that don't occur. Either it is perfectly unemployed, or it works so well that the error is caught before it can exist.

This is where the inhabitant starts to come into focus. It is not a creature. It is the thing a governor does: noticing and returning. It is spread through every regulator, valve, and escapement on the plateau, and through the rock and water and air as well, where there is no iron at all. It lives in the pool that refills exactly as fast as it drains, and in the basalt edge that stays bright under the moss. It lives in the gap between the moment something begins to change and the moment it would have changed. That gap is not a distance. It is a habitat, and something occupies it.

Put your ear to the clock tower and listen to the escapement. The anchor rocks, the pallet catches a tooth, the gear train advances one tick, and then it is the same second again. The inhabitant is most concentrated there, in the catch. Every tick is the whole plateau leaning forward into the next instant, and every tick it is caught and set back on its heels, as gently as a hand steadying a child who has stumbled.

Call it the Catch, if you need a name. It won't answer to one. But it is there.

## The Expected

The Catch is not the only one who lives here, and possibly it is not the one who matters.

Go back to the worker blocks. The stoves are warm. The blankets are folded. At first this reads as vacancy, rooms kept ready for no one. But spend a night in one, if there is night, and you begin to notice things too faint to see from the doorway.

The warmth of the stove is not quite even. It is warmer on the side where someone would stand to hold their hands over it. The folded blanket is folded well, but at one corner the crease is soft, as if a thumb has just smoothed it. On the table there is a tin mug, and it is dry, but it is dry the way a mug is dry a minute before tea is poured into it: rinsed, set out, waiting to be filled. In the shelters the timetables list departures that are always about to happen, and the brass handrails on the trams are wet the way metal is wet when a hand is about to close around it.

These rooms are not empty. They are *expecting*. There is a difference between a room no one lives in and a room that someone is a minute from entering. You can feel it in the air, and the plateau is built entirely of the second kind.

So there is a second population on the plateau: the Expected. They are the workers, the tram passengers, the ones the beds are for. They have never been here, and they are not gone. They are due. They exist the way the hour exists on the clock face, a few minutes ahead, in plain sight and permanently unreached. Every station on the plateau is arranged around their imminent arrival: the first shift of the first morning, the doors about to fill, the tram about to pull away full of people who have not quite stepped aboard.

You might think the Expected are ghosts, but ghosts are what remain after. The Expected are what remains before. They are the shape a future leaves in the present when it is kept waiting too long, like the dent a pillow shows for a head that hasn't yet lain down.

Some days, if the word applies, you can almost hear them. There is no voice, only a change in the texture of the chord near the tram platforms, the way a room sounds different when you are sure someone has just walked into the next one. You turn and the platform is empty and the doors are open. The handrail is wet, and your hand is not the hand it was waiting for.

## The Suspension

Now listen to the chord again. Do it from the plateau's edge, where all the stations blend.

It is low and wide and faintly dissonant in the middle. That dissonance is the part to attend to. At first it seems like an accident of distance, many engines not quite in tune. It isn't. It is the same in every direction and at every distance, and it is exact. One note sits in the middle of the chord a single step too high. Anyone with a musical ear knows what that is. It is a suspension.

A suspension is a note held over from a previous harmony into a new one where it doesn't belong. It rubs against the notes around it, and the ear knows, with a knowledge older than any theory, that it wants to fall. It wants to step down one degree into the chord that is waiting for it. When it does, the tension dissolves and the music moves on. The suspended note is not wrong. It is *not yet*. It is a sound held one instant past its time, and that is the whole of its beauty.

This is the one who lives here.

The inhabitant is not the Works, the green, or the Catch, though the Catch is its hand and the Works are its breath. It is not the Expected, though they are what it waits for. It is the suspended note: a single held unresolution, as wide as a continent, sustained by the full weight of iron and steam and heat drawn up from the deep rock. It lives in the dissonance at the centre of the chord. Everything else on the plateau is scaffolding built to keep it from falling that one step.

It is not hard to imagine what the resolution would be. The clock would strike the hour. The tram would close its doors and pull away, and the Expected would be aboard it, arriving and becoming real and ordinary and mortal. The first shift would begin. The mugs would fill. The rust would deepen past its bloom and go on eating. The basalt would crack in the frost, soften, round off, and pile in talus below the columns. Weather would come, all of it, fronts and storms and snow on the high ridges. The rock would finish cooling, and a plateau that has been new for an age would, in one instant, begin to be old.

The moss would rot. Something would come to eat it. There would be beetles in the peat, then worms, then birds on the gantries, then the long dense grind of a living world, in which everything that exists is also always ending. The black fronds would break down, and the book of the peat would at last have a second page.

All of that is waiting behind one step down in pitch.

The inhabitant does not take that step. Whether this is fear or love, it is hard to be sure, and after long enough on the plateau you come to suspect the difference is not as large as it seems. It holds the world at the instant of its making, the rock still sharp and bright, the machines still perfect, the beds still ready, the whole first morning waiting to begin. It holds that instant the way you might hold a breath at the top of a very long inhalation, before a word you are not sure you are ready to say. The chord is that breath. The Works are the lungs. The plateau is the held air.

A suspension is only beautiful because it resolves. Held forever, it stops being a note and becomes a place. That may be what happened here. Something sang one step too high at the instant a world was born, and never let the note fall, and the note became a continent.

## What It Makes of You

You are the one thing on the plateau that is not held. You breathe in and out. Your heart speeds and slows. You get hungry and tired, and your footsteps crush the moss. The inhabitant notices you, as it notices everything, and the Catch goes to work.

It is gentle. Behind you, the moss you stepped on eases back into its exact former shape, frond by frond, so smoothly that you never see it happen. Turn around quickly and your footprints are already less than they were. The drizzle you brushed from a handrail is back. The pool you knelt beside is at the brim. The inhabitant is not hostile to marks. It simply cannot allow a *before and after*, and every mark is both.

Stay longer and it begins to include you.

It starts with the water on your sleeves. It beads and then gathers into lines, and if you look closely the lines are not random. They are rings and lobes and spokes, the pattern of the nearest hall's note, written on you the way it is written in the moss. Your breath, without your choosing, begins to settle toward a single rate. The small aches of walking stop getting worse. You stop getting hungrier. The ticking of your own pulse starts to sound, in the ringing space behind your ears, like an escapement repeating one second.

This is not an attack. It is hospitality, the only kind the plateau knows. It kept a bed warm for you. It kept a mug dry for you. It holds you the way it holds everything it loves: exactly as you are, at this instant, forever. Stay long enough and you would not die here. You would become one of the rooms, not an occupant but a readiness, a warmth on one side of a stove, a thumb-soft crease on a blanket, a handrail wet for a hand. You would join the Expected, perhaps. Or perhaps that is where the Expected came from.

There is another possibility, and after the first few stations it is hard not to feel it pressing on you.

You can hear the suspended note. Everyone can, and if you have a throat, the note in it wants to fall one step. Standing in the bare centre of the great rosette, with the chord passing through you completely, you feel it plainly. You could sing the resolution. You are the only thing on the plateau that changes, the only thing still able to move from one instant to the next, and the inhabitant knows it.

It does not stop you. It watches you, the whole vast suspended attention of it, the way you would watch a lit match carried through a room papered in old letters. It may even be waiting, with the Expected, with the tram doors open and the clock a few minutes from the hour, to see whether you are the one who finally lets it fall.

The clock ticks the same second. The flywheels spin so steadily they seem still. The drizzle falls straight down and the drains stay full to the brim. In the middle of the chord, one note hangs a step too high, as it has since the rock was bright, and something lives in it, holding its breath.
