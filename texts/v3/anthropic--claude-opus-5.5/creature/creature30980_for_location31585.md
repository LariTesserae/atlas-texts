# Who Lives in the Pickle-Lake Warren

## I. The First Answer

The obvious answer is: *everyone in the description.* The lathe-shrimp, the worms, the ruddy crabs, the eyeless eels, the ghostly octopuses. That answer is true, and it is where to begin, but it isn't finished. Nothing here is.

So start small and close, with the ones who are always moving.

---

## II. The Masons

A lathe-shrimp lives about nine months. Seen under a hand lens it is almost nothing: a curled, glassy crescent the colour of skimmed milk, with two long antennae it holds swept back like the ears of a nervous hare. Its forelimbs are short, heavy, and fringed with bristles, built for scooping. Its middle legs are thin and jointed, and at the base of each is a swollen, pearly gland. When the shrimp works, those legs pat and stroke the mud it has pressed into place. Thread comes from the glands, finer than spider silk and faintly sticky, and the shrimp stitches its grains together the way a person might darn a sock without looking.

They have eyes, small and red-black, but it isn't clear they use them for much. What they read is texture. A lathe-shrimp placed on bare stone wanders. Put it on stone with a groove in it and it follows the groove. At the end of the groove it hesitates, taps the ends of its antennae against the edge, and begins to build, continuing the line.

This is the most important fact about them, and it is the reason they are not quite the builders they appear to be.

Most of their lives are spent in the crowd. A construction face is a living fringe of thousands of them, packed so closely they climb over one another. Each one that reaches the edge lays a single grain, glues it, and is pushed aside by the next. Nobody directs this. No shrimp holds a plan. The wall grows because every shrimp does the same small thing at the edge it happens to find. In a dense swarm this looks like extrusion, the wall seeming to push itself out of the shimmer, and the shimmer is just their antennae catching the mat-light as they jostle.

They mate in the walls. The females brood their eggs in a pouch beneath the body, and in the last weeks before release they build more slowly and more carefully, laying cells a little smaller and a little deeper. Some say the shrimp shape nurseries, though nobody has proven it. When the young hatch, they are released straight into the cells their mothers shaped. The first surface a newborn mason ever touches is a wall, and the first thing it does is feel for an edge.

They don't grieve and they don't defend what they've made. When a ribbon-worm slides into a cell and lifts out a sleeping shrimp, the others keep working. When a flood tide drowns the lowest tier, the survivors climb and start again one level higher, on top of the tier that killed their neighbours. The shoreline is built on shrimp that were preserved where they worked.

So when you ask *who lives here,* the masons seem to be the answer: tiny, numberless, blindly diligent. But notice what they are doing. They are reading grooves. They are following lines that were already there.

Who drew the lines?

## III. The Tenants

Before answering that, the city deserves a proper census, because the masons build far more than they occupy.

**The snails** are the gardeners, if anything is. They're small, no bigger than a lentil, with pale translucent shells that pick up a faint dusting of grey from the mats. They glide over the bacterial tussocks and crop them with a rasping tongue, leaving clean bands behind them. They seem to prefer the young filaments, so they move across a meadow in slow, even rows. Where they have passed, the mat regrows thicker. The meadow is shaped by being eaten, like a lawn. The snails sleep in mason cells, sealing the door with a mucus lid that dries into a papery window. On a still day, a whole face of the shoreline honeycomb is shuttered with these little frosted panes.

**The brittle stars** are harder to see. Their bodies are the size of a shirt button, their arms ten times longer and thin as eyelashes, and they are nearly transparent. You find them by the faint glint of their skeletons: five filaments of calcite lace sprawled across a wall. Each one lives with its central disc tucked into a cell and its arms laid out along the thoroughfares, feeling for the vibrations of passing things. They catch marine snow, and small copepods, and the occasional unlucky mason larva. An arm cut off by a predator regrows in weeks. Some of the oldest have arms of different lengths, grown in different years, like a family tree.

**The tube-worms** of the mid-walls are the tallest things in the city, a finger's length, sometimes two. They are the colonnades. Each lives in a parchment tube it built itself, grey and leathery, and extends from the top a crown of feathered tentacles. The crowns are violet at the base, fading to white at the tips. When touched they flinch shut and pulse with light, so a disturbance moves across the quarter as a wave of closing, a ripple of violet blinks like a crowd drawing breath. In the seep pulses they open fully and lean toward the vents, and the whole mid-wall becomes a forest of tilted flowers.

**The juvenile fish** are the one surprise. Nobody knows quite what they are. They are the length of a fingernail, silvery-grey, with enormous eyes. They drift down the seam as larvae and settle into the mason cells, and they grow very slowly, or perhaps not at all. There are fish in the ceiling city that have been juveniles, as far as anyone can tell, for years. They never seem to reach adulthood. Perhaps there isn't enough food. Perhaps the adults of their kind live somewhere else, in the open dark above, and these ones simply stopped, waiting for a signal that will never come down the narrowing seam. They hover at the mouths of their cells, facing outward, like people at windows.

## IV. The Shoreline and One Old Eel

The shore of the brine lake belongs to those who fish the dead.

**The ruddy crabs** are small, square, and rust-coloured, with one claw noticeably larger than the other. They work in a sideways shuffle along the tidemark, turning over the glinting preserved bodies at the edge. Most of what they find is dry pickling. They tear it with their small claw and eat it with the delicacy of someone picking at a salted fish. When they want something deeper they reach, a claw into the mirror, and the brine shows around the limb like a ring of oil. If a crab holds a claw in too long, the limb goes numb. They seem to know this. They count, or something like counting. A crab reaches, holds, withdraws, and rests, over and over, with a rhythm you could almost set a clock by.

**The eels** are longer, paler, and much older.

They are hagfish, or close to it: soft, eyeless, and slow, the colour of old candle wax, with a fringe of short barbels around a round mouth. They lie along the shore in loose coils, their heads resting almost at the edge of the brine. When they fish, they slide their heads through the mirror and hold them there, far longer than a crab could bear. Their skin slimes when they are stressed, and the slime seems to protect them for a while. Then they pull back, dragging with them a curled shrimp or a fragment of worm tube, and lie still for a long time, as if recovering from a dive.

One of them is old. It is noticeably longer than the others and thicker, and its body is marked with pale bands where the brine has scarred it. If anything here has a name it is this one, though nothing here would give it one. Call it the Keeper. It fishes one stretch of shore and no other, a curve of bank below the densest part of the honeycomb, where the flood tides drop the most bodies. It has fished there for a long time. It may have been there when the walls were still soft enough to dent. The other eels give it room.

It is not a guardian of anything. It is only the oldest living creature in the chamber that anyone could recognize, and it has one habit that seems to mean something. After a big flood tide, when the shoreline cells drown and the brine retreats and leaves a fresh line of preserved dead, the Keeper does not feed immediately. It moves along the new tidemark first, slowly, its barbels brushing each preserved body in turn. Only after it has touched them all does it begin to eat.

Maybe it is sorting by taste. Maybe it is doing what an eyeless animal does to learn a changed shore. Or maybe it is the nearest thing to a ritual the chamber has, a roll call of the dead before they become food.

## V. The Hunters in the Perfect Walls

**The ribbon-worm** has already been introduced. It deserves a closer look. It is so thin and so long that it is seldom seen whole. Usually you see a section: a translucent thread sliding along a colonnade, pale pink where its gut shows through. It may be three hand-lengths long. It hunts by poking its head into cells, and when it finds something soft, it everts a proboscis, a pale sticky tongue longer than its head, which wraps and withdraws. It can do this into a cell without widening the doorway. The masons don't even know one of their number is gone until the space is noticed empty.

**The octopuses** are the most beautiful things in the chamber, and they live almost exclusively in the ghost districts.

They are small, with bodies the size of a thumb joint and arms twice that length, and they are pale grey-white, nearly translucent, until they hunt. Then colour moves across them: rust, pale violet, a faint gold, sometimes rings of dim blue light around the base of their arms. They hunt by drifting along a thoroughfare, flushing prey with a flash, and seizing whatever bolts.

They choose the ghost districts because the walls there are strongest. Stone grew into those districts rather than mud, and a mason-built den can be pried open by a crab, while a stone den cannot. So the octopuses live in cells nobody living built.

One of them is brooding. She chose a cell high in a ghost district near the ceiling, a round chamber too large to be a mason nursery and too regular to be a crack. She laid her eggs on its ceiling in a hanging cluster, like pale grapes, and has not left since. Deep-sea octopuses brood for years, fasting the whole time, fanning the eggs with water, cleaning them of silt. She is fading, turning paler and thinner, her skin hanging loose. When the eggs hatch, if they hatch, she will die. Her young will be born in a room made by stone, in a city partly made by stone, and they will never know the seam above exists, because by the time they are grown, it may be closed.

The cell she chose was not built by masons. Look closely at its proportions. It is perfectly round. It is the size of a curled octopus.

## VI. Beneath the Mirror

Now we have to go down, through the surface that everything in the city avoids.

The brine lake is described as death, and for nearly everything, it is. No oxygen, ferocious salt. The bodies that fall through it are held perfect: curled shrimp, drifted eels, a scatter of worm tubes, hanging or lying exactly as they fell. The gallery of the preserved is the largest population in the chamber. The dead outnumber the living many times over, and they stay.

But the lake is not empty.

At the boundary itself, the shimmering place where the two waters refuse to mix, there is a film. It is a membrane of bacteria so thin it can't be seen except as a faint pink or grey tint in the reflection, living exactly on the line between oxygen and salt, feeding on the chemicals that drift up from one and down from the other. It's the densest living thing in the chamber by count. It is also why the mirror shimmers. Part of what you see is not the meeting of waters but the skin of something alive. The city's reflection is cast on a living surface.

And below it, in the soft grey ooze at the bottom of the lake, among the preserved dead, there are animals.

They are almost too small to see: a quarter of a millimetre, less than a grain of sand. They are loriciferans, a real group of animal, and relatives of theirs have been found living in anoxic brine basins in the deep Mediterranean. They are built like tiny flasks or vases, armoured with a corset of plates (*lorica* is the Latin word for corselet), with a head that bristles with spines and can be pulled into the armour like a hand into a sleeve. They have no need for oxygen. Their cells carry something else, organelles that make their energy some other way. They are among the only animals known to live their whole lives without it.

They live in the gallery. The eel that drifted down years ago lies in the ooze, perfect, and around it, in the sediment beneath its belly, lives a population of creatures smaller than its scales. They are not eating it, or not quickly. The salt is too strong for fast rot. They graze the sediment, the bacteria of the ooze, the slow, faint chemistry of the pickled. They are the only residents of the lake. They have never touched the city. The city doesn't know they exist.

If anything in this place deserves to be called *native*, they might. The masons came down the seam. The eels came down the seam. The loriciferans may have been in the sediment before there was a chamber, before there was a roof, when this was only a pocket of gas and brine in the mud. The city was built above them, around them, on the edge of their world. From their perspective, the whole chamber is a strange bright ceiling, far overhead, shedding a slow rain of perfectly preserved bodies.

## VII. The Ones in the Stone

Now return to the question: *who drew the lines the masons follow?*

The walls are carbonate, and the carbonate is made by microbes. They are not the glowing mats on the surface. These are something deeper, inside the stone and the sediment behind it. They live in clusters too small to see, partnerships of two kinds of single-celled life, one ancient kind (archaea) that eats methane and one kind of bacteria that breathes sulfate, and neither can live without the other. The archaea cannot finish their meal alone. The bacteria take away what the archaea leave. Between them they change the chemistry of the water around them, and carbonate falls out of it as stone.

They are the real masons.

They live in the walls, in the pores of the young rock, in the soft chalky places that give under a fingernail. They're the reason the stone is faintly warm. They grow where methane and sulfate meet, and they form stone where their chemistry is right, and the lathe-shrimp glue makes it right. Something in the glue, some protein or sugar, gives them purchase, or feeds them, or tips the balance. So they cluster along the glued seams, and the stone grows there first, following the masons' work.

That is the first half of the loop. The shrimp draw lines and the microbes turn them to stone.

The second half: the stone keeps growing. The microbial clusters at the edge of a finished wall still have methane coming from behind them and sulfate in front of them. They keep eating, keep precipitating. The stone keeps extending, and it extends *along the line it was going,* because that is where the partnerships are densest, where the chemistry already works, where the pores already run. A wall that ended goes on. An arch left open closes. The ghost districts are what happens when the stone keeps doing what it learned.

And the masons, when they return, read the grooves. They follow the lines the stone laid down. They build on the rock's continuation of their own ancestors' work, and glue it, and the microbes follow the glue.

Nobody is copying anybody. It is a conversation without speakers. The shrimp don't remember; they live nine months and remember nothing. The microbes don't intend; they are clusters of cells eating gas. But the *walls* remember. Every generation of masons is born into cells shaped by the last generation, reads grooves laid by the last generation, and extends them. Every generation of stone grows along the seams of the last generation of glue. The design persists across hundreds of generations of shrimp that never met, carried in stone that is only a few decades old.

That is why the ghost districts look like handwriting imitated by someone who never understood the words. The stone doesn't know what a wall is for. It just continues. And its continuations are a little smoother, a little more regular, because stone has no need to jostle.

Here is something the description only hints at. The lean is increasing.

Each ghost district has the same slight lean as the living quarter it grew out of. But the stone doesn't copy exactly. It exaggerates, very slightly, the tendencies it absorbed: a little more lean, a little more regularity, cells a fraction larger where they were large, a fraction smaller where they were small. And the masons, following grooves, inherit the exaggeration. So the style of the city is drifting. Over decades, the oldest walls, the ones from when the chamber was new, look different from the newest ones. The newest are rounder. More ordered. Taller. Stranger. They lean more.

Neither the shrimp nor the microbes are doing this on purpose. Neither could. The drift belongs to the loop itself.

## VIII. The Shapes That Nobody Made

There is one more thing the stone does, and it is the strangest.

At the shoreline, along the tidemark, lie the pickled bodies left by the flood tides. The Keeper touches them. The crabs eat some of them. But many are left, especially the small ones, curled masons, snails, brittle-star arms, fringing the brine edge in a glinting band.

The microbes in the stone colonize them. A preserved body is soft, organic, and sits right at the edge of the brine, where the chemistry is strongest. The stone grows over the dead the way it grows over glue. Within a few years, the tidemark is lined with stone shrimp: casts, curled and perfect, every limb picked out in pale carbonate, the body inside long since gone. The shore is carved with fossils that are only a decade old.

And then the stone continues.

In the ghost districts nearest the shore, where stone has been growing unsupervised for the longest, there are cells no mason could have made, because no mason has the right shape for them. There are niches the exact curve of a curled lathe-shrimp, but four times too large. There are grooves that branch in fives, like the arms of a brittle star. There are rounded chambers the size of a curled octopus. The stone absorbed the shapes of the dead, just as it absorbed the shapes of the walls, and it is extending them too, into cells, into spaces, into rooms the size and form of things that fell into the lake.

The living move in. The brooding octopus's den is one of them. The masons build nurseries in the curled niches. The brittle stars lay their arms along the five-fold grooves. They are living, without knowing it, inside the extrapolated shapes of their own dead.

## IX. The Light That Travels

The last resident is the hardest to see, because it is only a pattern.

When the worm crowns are touched, they pulse. When the copepods are startled, they spark. And along the thoroughfares, species flash in rhythm, and the flash spreads, and waves of light run across whole walls and die on the far side of the lake.

The waves have shapes. They start in some places more than others: the busiest thoroughfares, the colonnade gates where predators wait. They travel certain routes. They split around the ghost districts, whose smooth walls hold fewer flashing creatures, and rejoin beyond them. On the busiest nights they cross and interfere, and there are places on the walls where two waves always meet and flare brighter.

The masons are drawn, faintly, to light. Build-faces lit by frequent waves grow a fraction faster. So the city grows toward where the light travels. The light travels where the city is densest. The city gets denser where it grows.
