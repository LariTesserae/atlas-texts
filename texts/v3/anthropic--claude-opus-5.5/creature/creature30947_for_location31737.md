# Who Lives in the Held Basin

## Begin with a hand

The first person you would meet is probably not looking at you. She is looking about forty meters to your left and a little upward, where a raft drifts along a node-line she cannot see but knows exactly. Her hands are already moving.

In the basin a greeting is a gesture of placement. The flat right hand sits on the back of the left, palm down, and rocks slightly, the way a dwelling rocks on its node when the chord beats. It means *I am held here, and I see that you are held there.* A stranger is anyone who does not yet know how to answer it. She will make the sign twice, slowly, and then, because you are standing on the glass floor, she will point sharply at your feet and move her hand sideways a pace. You are standing on a node. Things fall on nodes.

Her hand-name is two fingers drawn downward and then turned outward, like water finding a crack. People from outside translate it as *Seep*, though no one in the basin would say it aloud, and she has never heard it said. She builds rafts. Her hands are split at the knuckles from grass fiber, and her forearms are corded from sixty years of nudging loads through air that resists nothing and supports everything. She is about half deaf, which makes her middle-aged in the basin's reckoning. She lives in the middle tier, in a flat woven house the color of dry reeds, perforated like a sieve, alone on its node for a kilometer in every direction except one. In that direction, three hundred meters off, hangs her daughter's house. The two nodes are a fifth apart, and the houses sway in a slow shared rhythm, like two people breathing in the same room.

That is where to start: with a person, her work, her distances. The rest of the basin's inhabitants will come as the eyes adjust.

## The pitch of a family

Every dwelling sits on a node, and every node has a pitch. It is the exact tone at which the pressure there balances. Among the families of the basin that pitch is a kind of lineage.

A household's node is inherited. When a child grows and builds a house of her own, she builds it on a node related to her mother's by a clean interval, usually a fifth, sometimes an octave, since an octave is the closest a child can be to a parent without living in the same house. Over generations a family becomes a chord: dozens of dwellings scattered across kilometers of air, each apparently alone, all of them sounding together in a harmony no human ear can pick out of the Throats' roar but which every member of that family can somehow feel. Family trees in the basin are drawn as intervals. A grandmother might describe her descendants by humming a long, slow arpeggio in a cancellation pocket, and anyone listening would know who had married whom, who had quarreled and moved a tritone away, and who had died, because at those places in the arpeggio she stops, breathes, and skips a note.

This is why no one asks a neighbor's pitch. It is like asking about someone's blood. The pitch tells who your people are, who you may lash your house beside, and who you are close enough to that your walls will beat against each other in a storm.

Marriage follows from this. Two households can join only if their nodes are consonant. Lashed together, dwellings on dissonant nodes shiver, then rattle, then pull themselves apart along the woven seams. There are old stories, told in the pockets and nowhere else, about lovers born a tritone apart who tried anyway. In one, they bound their houses with living vine, and the vine sang itself to shreds in a single night. In another, they both moved down to the floor to live among the deaf lizards, where no pitch applied. In the best-known one, they found a cancellation pocket and built a house in the silence itself, where there were no nodes to hold anything up. So their house sat on the glass, heavy and ordinary as a house anywhere, the only grounded dwelling in the basin. The story says it is still there. Children go looking for it.

## Children

A child born in the basin cries its first cry without a sound.

Or rather, the sound is there, but it is swallowed. The mother feels it: a buzz against her breastbone, a flutter in the baby's ribs under her palm. The midwives say this is the right way for a person to arrive. The first thing a child learns is that its voice is something the body does, not something the world receives. Basin babies babble with their hands within months, because hands get results and voices do not.

At about four, a child is carried to a cancellation pocket for the first time. The families call it *first hearing*. They step off the raft into that strange flat cellar-quiet where two Throats' tones cancel, the adults kneel, and someone speaks the child's spoken name.

Every basin person has a spoken name. It is chosen by the parents, never signed and never written, and said only in pockets, where it can actually be heard. Most people hear their own spoken name only a handful of times in their lives: at first hearing, at their marriage, and when they are dying, if someone loves them enough to carry them to a pocket.

Children react to first hearing in one of two ways. Some scream. The silence is so total, and their own sudden voice so loud and strange, that they clap their hands over their ears and have to be carried out. Others go very still and then begin to talk, pouring out every word they know in a long, delighted, astonished stream, as if all the speech they had been holding had finally found somewhere to land. The families believe the second kind will grow up to be tuners. The first kind, they say, will be good at living in the basin, which is not the same thing, and no worse.

By the time they can count, children read the halo figures. Each Throat's diffraction pattern hangs in the fog-shelves at its correct bearing: a lattice of rings, a star with seven arms, a spiral wound tight as a fern. Children play a game where one of them is blindfolded and spun on a raft, the blindfold is pulled off, and the child must shout, soundlessly, with both hands, which Throat is which. The best of them can do it from a single glimpse of the pattern's edge. Adults take this skill for granted, the way people elsewhere take it for granted that children can find their way home.

## The tuners

In every neighborhood of three or four dwellings, someone is the tuner.

Beside each house hang the resonant plates, thin fired shell cut to specific shapes, like the wind chimes of a city that has never had wind. Struck, or merely left to hum in the chord, they nudge the local node up or down by a hand's width. With them a household can raise itself slightly into drier air in the fog season, or lower itself to be closer to a fruiting tree. With them, delicately, a household can hold itself steady when the chord beats hard.

Tuners make these plates, and they also listen for what is wrong. A node drifting too far, a plate cracked, a neighbor's house riding slightly lower than it should because someone inside has fallen ill and stopped keeping the chimes clean: tuners notice all of it. They have the worst hearing in the basin and the best ears. They listen with their teeth, with the bones behind their ears, and with the palms they press flat against a house wall. A good tuner can lay her hand on your door and tell you that your house is carrying something too heavy in its northeast corner, and that you should take it out before the next retuning.

Tuners are paid in water. They are the ones who go to the pockets most, for their bones.

## The unheld

When a Throat retunes and a node vanishes, the household on that node falls.

Usually not far. Most dwellings catch on the next node down, or drift to a new one, sliding smoothly as the lattice reorganizes. The families lash down, the house tips and slides, the plates ring wildly, and then it is over and they are somewhere else. They are a tier lower, perhaps, their pitch changed. A family's new pitch after a retuning is recorded with great care, the way people elsewhere record a move to a new town.

Sometimes, though, a node goes out altogether and nothing catches. Then the house comes down onto the glass, or onto the sharp crest of a pressure-ridge, and is broken, and the family is *unheld*.

The unheld are the one people everyone is obliged to take in. It is the closest thing the basin has to law, and it is not written anywhere. An unheld family may lash to any house, whatever the interval, and the lashing may not be refused. Their neighbors will put up with the beating walls, the rattling seams, and the headaches for as long as it takes to find a new node. The unheld carry a hand-mark for a year afterward: the fist opened and dropped. People who see it will give them water without being asked.

Some unheld never go back up. They stay on the floor.

## The floor-folk

On the glass, among the lizards and the damp black runnels, live the people the upper tiers call floor-folk. They are mostly old.

They go down when their hearing finally goes completely. It happens in the sixties or seventies, and by then most people have gone gradually quiet inside, the chord having taken everything it could. At that point something odd occurs. Freed from the constant pressure of hearing, they find the floor is legible. Barefoot on the warm glass they can read the ground's differences: warmer seams and cooler plates, the faint concentric ribbing around the Throats, the heat rising and falling in slow tides from somewhere below. The lizards have always known these things. The old learn them.

The floor-folk live in low tents of vine-cloth on the lee sides of ridges, eat succulents and fungus, and drink from the runnels. They are calm, the way the lizards are calm. They sit for hours with their palms flat on the glass. When the upper tiers want to know when a retuning is coming, or which Throat, they send a raft down and ask the floor-folk. The floor-folk are right more often than the birds.

The floor-folk also tend the roads.

The roads are the long ribbons of re-fused glass laid along the ribbing, polished and exact, running between the Throats and across the whole basin. Almost no one uses them. Visitors assume they are ancient, or ceremonial, or built by whoever built the Throats. The floor-folk know otherwise. Grandmother by grandmother, generation by generation, the roads are theirs. They melt the glass with lenses and the heat of the deep fissures, lay it, smooth it, and keep it clean. Ask why, and a floor-elder will look at you for a long time, then lay her palm flat on the ground and sign, very slowly: *For when they come down.*

The floor-folk are building the city's streets in advance. They do it the way you would build a crib before a birth, or dig a grave before a death. From the floor it is not obvious which.

## The hollowers

A few people in each generation are born with a throat like a mute's.

It shows in childhood, usually at first hearing. The child opens its mouth in the silent pocket, and the silence gets deeper. Adults feel their own heartbeats go strange. It is a counter-tone: the ability to find a pitch and sing its inverse, to cancel. Outside the pockets it means that where such a child stands in the roar, a small hollowness forms around it, a bubble of near-quiet a hand's width wide.

The families call these people hollowers, and they are treated with the wary respect given to anyone who can take away what holds things up. A hollower could drop a house off its node. A hollower could, in principle, do what the mutes do: sing against a node and catch whatever falls. There are stories about that too.

In practice hollowers do the gentlest work in the basin. They carry the dying. A hollower walking beside a litter can hold a small moving pocket around it, so that a dying person need not be carried all the way to the common pockets to hear their name. The hollower sings the silence, the family kneels inside it, and the name is spoken. The hollower always turns away while it is said. That courtesy is the whole of their ethic, and they learn it before they learn anything else.

Hollowers also sing to the mutes, when the mutes come to the city. A mute will hang near a dwelling, find the pitch, and begin to sing against it, and the hollower inside will sing against the mute. Two cancellations cancel, and the node holds. People say you can sometimes see a hollower and a mute staring at each other across twenty meters of air, both singing, neither giving way, for hours.

## The ones in the grass

Not every inhabitant is a person or an animal in the ordinary sense.

The tall grasses of the suspended fields tune themselves, each species to its own pitch, and a stand of it hums faintly under the chord. Anyone who has tended a field for long knows the grass does more than hum. It answers. When a mute comes near and begins its counter-song, the grass shifts pitch, the whole field at once, a rolling adjustment that holds its soil to the nodes. After a retuning the grass is the first thing to settle, and it seems to settle the fields around it, the way a crowd settles when one calm person sits down.

The oldest vine-ropes, the ones that tie floating fields to the glass, carry the sound down. Lay your ear against one and you hear the chord transmitted through living fiber, softened and slowed. The floor-folk say the vines are how the city and the floor talk to each other. The upper tiers say this is an old person's poetry. Nobody cuts them.

There are also the corrective singers, the insects that patch holes in the lattice with continuous small tones. People say that in the densest fields there is a steady, collective counter-tone, made of grass, insects, the small blind things in the soil, and the dew. It is a kind of immune system. The city does not only live on the lattice. Over centuries it has grown into a living layer of the lattice, a second, quieter chord threaded through the first, keeping the whole thing from shaking itself apart.

It is possible that the people are part of this too. Each household tunes its plates, holds its node, and adjusts for the neighbors' weight. Each family is a chord across the sky. The basin's inhabitants may not live in the lattice so much as be one of its instruments.

## Whoever maintains the Throats

The deepest work is out of sight, and residents speak of it the way people elsewhere speak of the weather. But they do speak of it.

New ceramic pegs appear in the glass floor, fired and set, where no one set them. The floor-folk find them in the morning, still warm. Cracks in the Throats' bases, seen from a respectful distance, close over the years with a paler glaze. Sometimes, at night, when the basin goes nearly black and the deepest fissures breathe their dull red, someone lying barefoot on the glass will see a shape move below the translucent plates. It is slow, red-lit, and far down. It is gone before it can be described.

Nobody has seen them clearly. The floor-folk have a hand-name for them: the palm pressed flat, then lifted slightly, like a hand taking the weight of something from beneath. It translates, roughly, as *the ones who hold the floor up*. Offerings are left for them at the feet of the ridges: a bead of water, a fired plate, sometimes a child's lost toy returned. They are always gone by morning. It is always the lizards. Nobody minds.

## The one who is singing

The floor-folk have lived with their palms on the glass for generations. The deep heat moves in tides, rising and falling on a slow period, and the ribbing around each Throat is not quite regular. It swells and thins. And the Throats, all forty, are not independent. When one retunes, the others answer within days.

From all of this the floor-folk have come to believe something the upper tiers do not quite say aloud.

They believe there is one inhabitant in the Held Basin, and everyone else lives on it.

Something lies below the glass, where the heat comes from. It raised the seabed. It is young, as the land is young. The Throats are its throats, forty of them, as the name was always telling everyone. The basin people named them before they understood why. The warmth of the floor is its warmth, the way a stone is warm after sunset or a body is warm under a blanket. The chord is its voice, and the city, the grass, the birds riding grids across kilometers of air, the mutes, and the children learning halos all live in that voice. They are like fish in a current, or the small animals that live on a whale.

Having believed this, the floor-folk disagree about what the falling pitch means.

Some say it is dying. The voice drops as the heat cools, and the city is being set down as a shroud is set down.

Some say it is falling asleep. The Throats are singing it a lullaby, or it is singing itself one, and when the chord finally goes quiet it will be only resting. The city will land, the dust will settle and stay, and the long ordinary aging of a country will begin on top of something dreaming.

And a few, the oldest, the ones who have sat longest with their hands on the glass, say something else. They say it is growing up.

They point out that young voices are high, and that voices drop as they mature. A voice falling a fraction of a tone per generation is the slowest voice-breaking imaginable, the adolescence of something that measures its life in the rising of land. When it is grown, they say, the chord will not fall silent. It will resolve. The lattice will not collapse. It will reorganize one last time into a structure no one has seen, because no one has ever lived inside the voice of something fully grown. The city will not land. It will be answered.

That, they say, is what the roads are for. Not a street plan. A score.

The upper tiers, hearing this, make the sign for an old person's poetry: fingers fluttering beside the ear, gently. Then they go home and check their plates.

## End with a hand

Seep, the raft-builder, will be deaf in fifteen years. She knows the way down to the floor already, and has picked a ridge. Some evenings she thinks about it without fear.

Tonight, though, she has taken her granddaughter to a pocket. The girl is four. They step off the raft into the cellar-quiet, and Seep kneels. The child's eyes go wide at the silence, the way everyone's do the first time. Seep has not heard her own voice in almost a year, and it comes out rough and too loud. She says the girl's spoken name.

The child does not scream. She goes still. Then she opens her mouth and begins to talk, and keeps talking: nonsense, questions, the names of the Throats, the names of birds, a long, astonished river of sound pouring into the one place in the basin where sound can land.

And the silence around her deepens.
