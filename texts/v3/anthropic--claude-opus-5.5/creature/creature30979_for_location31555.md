# Who Lives on the Sill

## The Easy Answer

The easy answer is the crew.

There are between nineteen and twenty-six of them at any given time, spread across eleven stations, of which seven have a working stove. They come up the funicular in the groaning wooden car with their kit sealed in rubber bags and their matches in tobacco tins. For the first three weeks they are miserable. Their feet go white and ridged. Their hands crack at the knuckles. They cannot sleep for the frogs, and then they cannot sleep for the rain on the iron roof, and then one night they sleep through a Wall so loud the tin cups walk off the shelf, and in the morning someone older tells them they have arrived.

They carry staves. Every crew member carries one, ash or iron, marked with notches every ten centimeters and a deeper notch at the height of their own hip. The hip notch matters. Water above the hip will take you, however strong you are, if it is moving. Most of the crew have been taken once. It is spoken of the way sailors speak of going overboard: a thing that happened, a thing you survived because a block was there, or a moss mat, or somebody's hand.

They have words the lowland does not have. *Slack* is water that has stopped choosing. *Turned* is a channel that has changed direction since yesterday. A *throat* is the place where sheet-flow narrows into a slot and speeds up. A *tray* is one of the temporary falls that pours off a dry stretch of rim after a storm. A *window* is a break in the cloud. The first one of the day is the *first window*, and it is considered bad luck to comment on it aloud, because the Wall always follows.

They telephone each other constantly. The line runs station to station across the plateau on iron poles furred with lichen, and it crackles in rain and goes mad in lightning. Mostly they call about water: *Four's running brown. Seven's turned east. Big tray going off the north rim, you'll hear it in a minute.* A great deal of the crew's life is spent reporting to one another what the water is doing, the way people elsewhere report the weather, except that here it is the weather, and the road, and the job.

When crew go down for good, after a long enough stint, something happens to them that the lowland doctors have never named. The crew call it *going dry*. They cannot sleep in quiet rooms. They wake in the night and reach for the stave that isn't there. They stand in the shower for an hour. Some of them come back up.

So the easy answer is the crew. But the crew are not quite it. They are visitors, however long they stay, and most of them know it. They live on the highest islands of rock, in stone huts, behind iron doors, with a stove going. They live on the Sill the way a diver lives underwater: on borrowed air.

## The Second Answer

The eels, then?

The eels are more of the place than the crew are. They come up the cliff themselves, a kilometer and a half of wet vertical stone, on their own bellies, over weeks, through the permanent mist of the broken falls. Nothing brings them. Nobody carries their matches for them. They arrive thin and glassy, and they grow fat and dark in the channels, sliding over the flooded pavement in the grey half-light of a storm like shadows that have found their own bodies.

But the eels leave. That is the whole point of an eel. They are born in the warm sea and they die in the warm sea, and the Sill is where they spend the long middle: a place to grow, a place to wait. When the moment comes they go down the falls in the dark and are gone. The crew sometimes see them going, on autumn nights: dozens of heavy bodies riding the current toward the rim in a Wall, not fighting it, letting it carry them over.

The swifts live behind the water and the frogs live in the water. But the swifts are gone at every dawn and dusk and ride the updraft out over the cloud-sea, and the frogs live their lives in weeks, in puddles, in the time between two storms. They are the sound of the place, not its tenants.

So the question sharpens. What is born on the Sill, lives its whole life on the Sill, dies on the Sill, and has done so for longer than there have been people to ask?

## The Tenants

Everyone meets one the same way.

You are wading a channel in drift mode. The water is thigh-deep and tea-brown and you are feeling your way with the stave, and you set your boot on a rock, a smooth rounded block at the bottom of the slot, pinkish-grey, mottled with moss like everything else. You put your weight on it.

And the rock moves.

Not much. It shifts, the way a sleeper shifts under a blanket. There is a slow, enormous, muscular rolling under your sole, and then the rock is not there, and you are standing on the actual bottom of the channel, and something the length of a person is sliding away downstream through the brown water with no hurry at all. What you see of it is a broad flat head, a frill of loose skin moving along the flanks like weed in current, and a tail that does not so much swim as agree with the water.

Then it is gone into a pothole, and the pothole looks like a pothole again.

The crew call them the Tenants. The joke is older than anyone on the plateau now. The Sill Hydraulic Company held the lease. The Company drew up the plans, drove the survey pins, poured the weirs. And the whole time, under every weir and in every deep slot, there was someone already living in the house who had never been told about the sale.

They are salamanders. That is the simple word for them, and it is correct, and it is not enough.

The largest ever measured was a hundred and ninety centimeters, nose to tail, and weighed something over forty kilos. It was measured dead, in the trash-screen of Powerhouse Two at the foot of the cliff, where it had gone down a penstock in 1931. That body is the reason anyone below knows they exist. It sat in a tank of spirit in a university basement for sixty years, and the plate beside it named it after the engineer who pulled it out of the screen. The crew have never used that name. They have their own, and they have names for individuals too.

A Tenant is shaped by the Sill the way a river stone is. The body is broad and flattened, built to lie low in fast water and let the current pour over it rather than push it. The head is wide as a shovel and nearly as flat, with a mouth that runs the full width of it. The eyes are tiny: black beads set far apart, lidless, sunk in wrinkled skin, nearly useless. The Tenants do not look at the world. They feel it. Along the head and down the flanks runs a line of pale pits, and through those pits a Tenant reads the water: its pressure, its speed, its turn, the faint push-wave of a crab moving in the next pothole, the vibration of a boot three meters upstream. A Tenant lying in a channel is a living instrument. It is doing constantly, with its whole skin, what the crew do with staves and telephones.

The skin is the strangest thing. It is loose. It hangs in folds and frills along the sides of the body, great wrinkled curtains of it, and these folds are how the Tenant breathes. The water of the Sill is cold and turbulent, churned white in every throat and every fall, and it is as full of oxygen as water can be. The Tenant simply bathes in it. It has lungs, small ones, but it hardly uses them. In still water it rocks slowly from side to side to stir the folds, and if you watch a Tenant long enough in a quiet pool you will see it sway, very gently, like something being rocked to sleep.

The color matches the pavement exactly: a pale pinkish-grey, mottled darker. The mottling is alive. Algae grow in the Tenant's skin, green cells lodged in the tissue, a partnership older than the plateau's flatness. Along the back, where the skin is thickest, moss grows. On the young ones it is only a stubble. On the old ones it is a pelt, a thick cushion of olive and bronze streaming out behind them in the current. The oldest Tenants carry whole gardens. A grey heron was once seen trying to stand on one.

They are the only silent thing on the Sill.

The crew say this, and it is true in a way that is hard to explain until you have been close to one. Everything else on the plateau is loud: the falls, the frogs, the rain, the wind in the cable, the penstock breathing. The Tenants make no sound at all. There is no call, no bark, no hiss. When one lies still at the bottom of a pothole, the water above it seems to go quiet, as if the stillness spreads. Crew who have spent time watching them describe a particular thing that happens at the edge of a Tenant's pothole. You stop hearing the falls. You notice yourself not hearing it, the way the crew say they notice the roar only when it drops.

## What They Do

Mostly they wait.

A Tenant can lie in one pothole for weeks. It feeds when something comes into reach: a freshwater crab, a young eel, a frog, one of the big yellow slugs washed off a block, the forearm-long flatworm that hunts the slugs. The feeding is so fast the crew rarely see it. The wide mouth opens, the water rushes in, and the prey is taken with it. It lasts about as long as a blink, and then the Tenant is a rock again.

In a Wall, they move.

This is when you are most likely to see them, if you are foolish enough to be out on low rock in a Wall. When the channels rise and turn brown and start knocking stones together, the Tenants come out of their holes and go with it. They ride the floods the way the ducks do, flat and low, steering with small corrections of the tail, sliding across pavement that was dry an hour ago. A Wall reshuffles them. After a big one, crew will find Tenants in basins where there were none, and empty holes where there had been one for years. The eels ride the floods toward the rim and over. The Tenants ride them across the table, and at the last they always find a pothole and drop into it. Nobody has ever seen a living Tenant go over the edge, except down the penstocks.

And they dig.

This is the part the crew understood slowly, over decades, from the edges of the thing.

A Tenant needs a den: a deep pothole, or a slot under an overhang, or a cavity beneath a block where the joint has widened below the surface. Every Wall fills the dens with rubble, pebbles, moss mats and grit, and every time, the Tenant clears it out. It works the debris loose with its broad head and shoves it with its body, and the current takes it away. A big male preparing a den for eggs can shift his own weight in stone in a night.

The stones go downstream. They lodge in the next throat, or they get trapped in a smaller pothole and spin there, grinding it deeper. A slot gets blocked. A channel turns.

*The water decides.* That is the first rule of the Sill, and every crew member will tell it to you on your first day. It is true. But the old crew add something, quietly, after you have been there long enough to be told. The water decides, they say, but there are a few votes.

Nobody thinks the Tenants steer the water. They are not engineers. They are not trying to do anything but keep their houses clean. But over the long life of a Tenant, and it is a very long life, one animal clearing one den, flood after flood, for a century, is a slow finger pressed against the flow. Across the whole plateau, hundreds of them, for longer than there have been hills below, they have been a small, patient, unintending hand on the shape of things. Some of the joint-canyons at the rim, the crew suspect, began as somebody's front door.

## How Long

Nobody knows how long they live. There is one fact, and the crew are very proud of it.

In 1924, a Company survey party under an engineer named Aurel Stroud caught a Tenant in the forebay of what is now Weir Four. Stroud was a methodical man. He measured the animal, noted its weight in the survey log, and fitted a brass band around its left foreleg, stamped with the number 11. Then he let it go. The logs record ten other bands fitted that season. None of the others has ever been seen again.

Eleven has.

It is still there, or its successor in that forebay is wearing its jewelry. The band has worn thin and gone the dark green-brown of old brass. The foreleg has grown around it, so that the flesh swells on either side of the ring. The crew call the animal the Bishop, for the ring, and because it lies at the bottom of the forebay with the patience of a man who has outlasted three popes. The woman who keeps Weir Four has seen the band perhaps a dozen times in thirty-one years. Each time she telephones every station on the plateau, and every station writes the date in its log, and there is a little celebration that evening, if you can call a tin of peaches shared around a stove a celebration.

Stroud's log gives the animal's length in 1924 as a hundred and forty centimeters. It was not young then.

## The Keeper of Four

Her name is Dorrit Vane, and she is the reason the question has a better answer than *the crew*.

She came up at twenty-four to work sluices for a single season. That was thirty-one years ago. She has been down to the lowland four times since, the longest for nine days, for her mother's funeral. She says the quiet down there gave her headaches, and that she could hear her own blood.

Weir Four still works. It is one of three. The water piles against it brown and roaring and goes into the throat of the penstock with that deep inhalation you feel in your teeth. Dorrit's working life is spent at the sluice-wheels, up to her waist in current, wrenching the great iron gates open and shut as the flow comes and goes. She reads the forebay the way you read a face you have known for decades. She knows when the Bishop has moved, because the water over its den changes. The riffle smooths, or a new boil appears. She says she can feel it through her boots when it shifts, though she admits this may be imagination.

It was Dorrit, twenty years ago, who got the grates fitted to the penstock mouths. She wrote to the Company, which barely exists now except as a lawyer's office and a small line of credit. She wrote to the powerhouses. She wrote to the university that has the 1931 body in its basement. She wrote for three years, and finally somebody sent up iron bars on the funicular, and she and two others spent a month underwater fitting them. Since then no Tenant has gone down the penstocks. The eels still do, on their own business, slipping between the bars.

Watch her at the wheel in drift mode, before dawn, when the Sill is as close to quiet as it gets: the falls, the frogs, the drip of everything. She stands braced in the current with the water streaming around her hips. She is not moving much. Her wool is soaked through and has been soaked through, in some sense, for thirty-one years. Her hands on the wheel are white-ridged and slow. Her head is slightly tilted. She is not looking at the water. She is listening to it, or feeling it, and her body is making small constant corrections, weight shifting from foot to foot as the flow leans on her and lets go.

A meter and a half below her, at the bottom of the forebay, the Bishop lies in exactly the same current doing exactly the same thing.

This is the answer that emerged. Not the crew alone, and not the creature alone, but the direction the one is moving in and the place the other has already arrived.
