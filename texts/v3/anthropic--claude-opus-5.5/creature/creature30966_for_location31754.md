# Those Who Live in the Blown Table

## The Unnamed

The people of the Table have no spoken names. Nothing up there can be spoken and heard, so nothing is.

They are named with the hands, and the hand-names come from the stone. Each family tends a region of holes: a valley of pores, a ridge of shafts, a cluster of pits around a knuckle of rock. Each region plays a figure in the plateau's chord. A family's name is that figure, signed in the air. Two fingers stepping down and then laying flat is a family whose valley sings a falling interval that settles into a drone. A fist opening slowly is a family whose ridge swells into a long crescendo when the south wind peaks. A person is their family's figure plus one small gesture of their own: a flick of the thumb, a touch to the collarbone, a turn of the wrist. You carry your name the way the stone carries its note, because of where you stand.

If a family's region is buried by seed drifts, or its plugs blow out and no one refits them, the family's name goes wrong. People say its members are *out of tune*, and they mean it literally, and gently. Such families often take up the work of another group that has dwindled, and slowly their hands learn a new name.

## The Quiet Childhood

Every person on the Table was born able to hear.

Children are born at the bottom of the shafts, in the green, echoing dark where the tree ferns grow. The roar passes overhead there like weather on another world, and down in that stillness a child can hear. They hear dripping water, the creak of leaves as thick as hide, and the soft thud of a mother's heart. They hear their own voices.

So the children of the Table speak aloud. They have a language of their own, spoken only in the shafts, handed down from one generation of children to the next and never used by an adult. It is a small, bright language, full of words for sounds, because sound is the thing a shaft-child has in abundance. It has a dozen words for the sound of dripping. It has words for the different booms that arrive from the surface, muffled by stone, and for how each one feels arriving in the belly. It has a word for the sound of your mother laughing, which adults cannot make and cannot hear, and which children learn to recognize by the way her shoulders move.

Adults do not speak this language, but they remember it. When an old person dreams, their mouth sometimes moves.

## The Climb Out

Somewhere between the ages of ten and fourteen, each child climbs out of the shaft for good.

It is not a ceremony exactly. It is closer to a weaning. A child begins going up to the rim for longer each day. They sit in the lee of the family's spiral wall and learn the hand-speech properly, and they learn to lay their palms on the stone and read the drum-codes. For a season they live in both worlds. They come down at night to sleep in the quiet and chatter aloud with the younger ones, and they go up each morning into the roar.

Then their hearing begins to go. It happens slowly at first. The high frequencies go first, the shriek of the small holes fading to a hiss. Then the dripping in the shafts grows faint, and the younger children's voices turn thin and far away. The child stops speaking aloud because they can no longer hear themselves do it. Eventually the shaft is no longer quiet to them. It is simply silent, and they stop going down except to visit.

The Table's people do not treat this as a loss, or not only as one. The word for it in hand-speech is a palm pressed flat and then lifted, the gesture that means *arriving on the stone*. A child who can hear is a child of the shafts. A person who cannot hear is a person of the Table. There is grief in the change, and there is also a kind of homecoming, as the whole body opens to the vibration of the plateau and the soles of the feet learn to read what the ears have lost.

Adults sometimes say, with their hands, that the Table takes your ears so that it can give you its voice.

## The Ears Below

This is the arrangement at the heart of their lives, and it took me a long time to see it.

The adults tune the plateau, but they cannot hear what they are tuning. Their greatest craft, fitting plugs of turf, stone, and resin into the holes to shape a region's voice, is done by people who can only feel the result. They press a long rod of listener-bone to the rim of a hole and read the buzzing through their teeth. They lie flat on the stone and feel which ridges tremble. A master plug-maker can tell a hole's pitch by touch to within a hair. Still, touch is coarse, and the chord is fine.

So the children listen. At the bottom of the tuning shafts, the children of the family sit in the quiet and hear the surface through the stone overhead. When an adult fits a new plug at the rim, a child below listens to how the region's voice has changed, then climbs partway up and signs the result to the adults above. The signs are imperfect, a child's approximations in a language made for hands. *Higher.* *Sour.* *Now it rubs against the east ridge.* *Now it's sweet.*

Every family that tunes the Table needs children, and every child of a tuning family is, for a few years, the family's ears. The chord of the plateau, shaped across centuries, was made by deaf hands guided by small voices shouting up out of the dark. When you finally become too deaf to listen, you inherit the hands. You spend the rest of your life shaping something you heard clearly only as a child, and you trust the children after you to tell you whether you are getting it right.

People say an old plug-maker's work sounds like their childhood, and they may be right. The notes a person chooses to make are the notes they loved when they could still hear them.

## The Elder Tuners

The people did not invent the plug. The cushion plants did.

Long before anyone came up the northern stair, cushion plants were packing their dense tissue into the smaller holes, growing in rings, slowly closing pores and changing the chord of whole regions. The people's resin is cooked from cushion plant sap. The first plugs, the stories say, were cushion plants dug up whole and moved from hole to hole.

The people regard the cushion plants as teachers, or as the senior members of an old guild. A cushion that has grown into a hole is never removed, even if it spoils a family's figure. The family retunes around it instead, and they sign about it the way a musician might speak of an elder in the ensemble who plays slightly flat and cannot be corrected, only accommodated. Some of the oldest cushions, great domed mounds as wide as a room, are said to have been sealing holes since before there were people. Families whose regions include such a mound consider themselves its apprentices, and their hand-names often include a gesture for it: a cupped palm, rounded like the mound.

## The Listeners

The listeners are not herded, not hunted, and not quite wild.

People leave moss-cakes for them in the lee of the spiral walls, pressed flat and dried by the fire. The listeners take them the way wild animals take something offered: warily, then without thought. A family usually knows the two or three listeners that graze its region, and knows them individually, by the notches in their folded ears and the patterns of scars on their pale hides.

The people watch them always. A listener is a pair of ears that can still hear, and that knows how to hear exactly the one thing the people most need to know: that the wind is about to stop. When a listener unfolds its great ears and turns in its slow circle, every adult within sight goes still. If it runs for a shaft, everyone runs.

When a listener dies, its body is carried down into a shaft, the only large animal that is ever brought below. Its ear-bones are cut and cleaned and become the rods the plug-makers press to the holes to read their pitch. So a plug-maker tunes the Table with the ears of an animal that spent its life listening for silence. People do not consider this macabre. They consider it the listener continuing its work.

Children of the shafts have a word, spoken aloud, for the sound a listener makes when its ears unfold, a soft leathery crack. Every child knows it. No adult has heard it since childhood. Adults see it happen, remember the word, and feel their shoulders tighten.

## The Silence, From Inside

The description of the silence I was given comes from the surface: the unbearable stillness, the falling cold, the returning front. That is how the adults experience it. They feel the stone stop trembling under their palms, which is like feeling a heartbeat stop, and they flee below.

But when the silence comes, everyone goes into the shafts, and there are children in the shafts. The children hear something.

When the ten million holes stop sounding and the roar overhead goes quiet, the shafts are not silent. Beneath the green floor, beneath the black pools, deep in the sponge of the quartzite where the tubes run down and down into the dark, something goes on breathing. It is a slow, faint, enormously long exhalation, air moving through the Table's buried channels as pressure equalizes between the rims and the deep chambers and the cold spilling in from above. It is a single note, lower than the lowest boom of the surface shafts, and it can last for an hour without a break. In the ordinary roar it is always there, but it is buried completely under everything else. Only in the silence does it rise to the edge of hearing.

The children call it by a word that adults can only approximate with their hands: the heel of the palm pressed slowly downward, as if pushing something heavy into water. It means, roughly, *the Table's own breath*.

The last law everyone learns is that everything arrives from below, and that the Table produces almost nothing of its own. The people believe that too, except for this. Everything else on the plateau is the lowlands' breath, borrowed, carried up, and torn apart. This one sound belongs to the stone. It belongs to that riddled, ancient floor, which is older than the mountains it outlasted, and it may have been making this note since before the lift began, before the lowlands were lowlands.

There is more in the note than air. Children who have heard it several times describe a faint texture riding on it: a trembling, a fine silvery ticking, like dew-laden threads vibrating. Nobody has seen what makes it, because the channels that far down are too narrow for a body. The people think of the spiders. Spiders ride the updraft on silk and are scattered across the plateau, and most die there. The people believe some of them did not go out across the surface but went down, into the pores and away from the wind. Over thousands of generations those spiders strung the Table's inner tubes with silk. They live in the dark, sheltered from the roar, and they build webs that sing only when the Table breathes its own breath.

Whether this is true, no one knows. The children insist the silver ticking is real. The adults believe them, as they believe everything the children hear.

## The Holes Left Open

Because of this, there is one kind of hole the people never plug.

Scattered across the Table are pores that open not onto a blind tube but onto something deeper: channels that run down past the level of the shafts into the sponge beneath. Plug-makers can find them by touch. When the bone rod is pressed to their rims, it does not buzz. It draws, the way a straw draws when you put it to your mouth, as though the hole is gently breathing in.

These are left open always, whatever it does to a family's figure. They are the Table's breathing holes. To plug one is the worst thing a person can do. It is worse than breaking a wall or stealing moss-water, because it is choking the only thing on the plateau that belongs to the plateau. Every family knows the breathing holes in its region. Some of the oldest cairns, the ones fitted so closely that they are nearly one stone, turn out to be built around breathing holes. They were not built to change their note but to shelter them, so that seed drifts will not bury them.

When a person dies, their body is lowered into the deepest pool of their family's shaft. Before that, though, their family carries a little of their breath out onto the surface. They hold a bundle of hair-moss over the dying person's mouth until it is damp, then carry it up and lay it, never pushed in, over the mouth of a breathing hole. There it dries and blows away. People say the Table takes the last breath the way it takes everything, and that this one it keeps.

## What Lives There

I began by thinking I would describe a person: a plug-maker, perhaps, deaf and weathered, pressing a bone rod to the stone. That person is there. But the longer I looked, the less the Table's inhabitant seemed to be any single thing.

What lives on the Table is the chord.

It is made by the stone, which has been hollowing for longer than anything has been alive, and by the wind, which has run up the cliffs for longer than there have been rivers. It is made by the cushion plants, packing their slow tissue into the pores. It is made by the listeners, whose ears are the Table's first warning and whose bones become its tuning rods. It is made by the deaf adults, fitting plugs by touch, shaping a sound they last heard clearly in childhood, and by the children below, calling up out of the dark to say *higher*, *sour*, *sweet*. It is made by the old dead families whose walls are green ridges now, whose plugs still sit in holes that no living person tends and still play notes chosen a thousand years ago by someone whose name was a gesture nobody remembers. And somewhere beneath all of it, faint and continuous, there is the Table's own breath, and perhaps the silk in the deep channels that trembles with it.

None of these things makes the chord alone. None of them can hear the whole of it. The adults feel it without hearing it. The children hear it but cannot shape it. The listeners hear only what threatens them. The cushion plants hear nothing and change it anyway. The chord lives in all of them together and in none of them fully, and it has been living up there, changing its voice and roaring every evening, for longer than anyone can measure.

## Evening, Again

At dusk an old woman lies on her back in the lee of her family's spiral wall. Her hand-name is two fingers stepping down, then laying flat, then a touch to the hollow of her throat. She has been deaf for sixty years. Her palms are flat on the stone and her bare heels are pressed to it, and through them she feels the west wind come on.

She feels her region begin to sound. She knows its figure by touch: the step down, the settling drone. She knows which part of the drone her grandmother made, by a plug of stone and resin in a hole two hundred paces east, and which part she herself made at twenty-six, on a morning when her daughter, eight years old and still able to hear, shouted up out of the shaft that the new note was sweet.

Her daughter is deaf now too, and is somewhere out on the plateau drumming a message into a hollow outcrop. The old woman feels it through the stone: *wind west, holding, all well.*

In the shaft below, her great-grandson sits beside the black pool with his head tilted up. He can hear the western shafts booming like enormous doors being slammed, rolling across fifty kilometers of stone. He can hear his great-grandmother's figure among them, the step down and the settling drone, and inside it one note that he thinks is very slightly sour.

He will climb up tomorrow and tell her, with his small inexpert hands, and she will get up on her aching knees, find the bone rod, and begin to fix it.

Then the north wind rises, the chord changes, and the Table goes on roaring with all of them inside it.
