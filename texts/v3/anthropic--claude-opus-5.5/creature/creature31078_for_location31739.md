# What Lives in the Gullet

## I. The Knocking

Start with the drummers, because they are the easiest to hear and the hardest to see.

Put your ear to the trunk of an old spiral, one of the deep ones that groans below the pitch of speech, and you will feel the knocking before you properly hear it. It comes through the resin-wood into the bone of your jaw. *Tak. Tak-tak. Tak.* Then a pause long enough that you think it has stopped, and then it starts again, a little differently.

The gatherers say drummers are grubs, and they are half right. If you split a fallen spiral, which the gatherers will not do and will not watch you do, you find them in the hollow. They are pale as boiled bone and about the length of a finger. They have too many legs, fine as eyelashes, and they cling to the inner walls of the bore in loose spiral bands that follow the twist of the trunk. They have no eyes. Their heads are one hard, blunt, amber-coloured knob, polished from use. They are warm to hold, like everything here, and they smell faintly of struck flint.

They are not eating the spiral. That is the first strange thing. There are no galleries, no frass, no hollowed pith. What they do is strike. They swing that amber knob against the inner wall, and each strike leaves a tiny dimple no larger than a pinprick. Over the life of a spiral, the inside of its bore becomes textured with millions of these pits, arranged in patterns so fine you need a lens to see them properly. Under a lens they are not random. They look like the grooves on a fingertip, or like the ripples that wind leaves in sand, except these ripples cross and interfere and resolve into something closer to script.

A spiral's note is set by its length and the width of its bore. That is what the gatherers say, and it is true of the young ones. The old ones are different. The drummers have been at them. Their pitted inner walls catch the wind and roughen it, and they add overtones, faint secondary voices riding on the fundamental. An old spiral does not sing one note. It sings a note with a *shape*: a vowel, almost. Some of the deep ones sound like a man saying *oh* very slowly into a well. Others sound like *aah*, or like a long closed hum that is almost *mmm*.

The drummers are tuning them.

## II. The Leaving

They do not live long, as individuals. Nothing here does. A drummer taps for a season, perhaps two, and all the while it swells. Its pale body grows translucent and fills with a clear gold fluid, and folds of membrane develop along its sides like a furled sail. When the spiral it lives in finally burns, and every spiral burns eventually, the heat in the hollow reaches the point where the drummer stops knocking and starts climbing.

This is the thing the gatherers have seen: something small and many-legged and pale, bursting from the mouth of a screaming, burning trunk and flinging itself into the wind. It spreads its membrane and is gone over the rim.

It does not go randomly. Watch long enough, from the shelter on the eastern horn, with a cup of warm resin-tea and a cloth over your mouth, and you begin to see that the gliders leave in different directions depending on which spiral they came from. The bass spirals release theirs into the hot western flow. The shrill young ones throw theirs east, into the thin cool wind off the far horn. The middle voices go north, or south, or down, into the strange sinking air on the Gullet's southern lip that the gatherers call the Swallow and avoid.

Seven winds, the gatherers say, though no one counts them the same way twice. The gliders count them. Each one leaves on a wind, rides it out across the plateaus, and learns it.

I say *learns* carefully, but I mean it. The membranes of the returning ones are different.

## III. The Returning

They come back. Not all of them, and not as they left.

On certain days, usually after a long stretch of steady wind, when the spirals have been holding something close to a chord for hours, the scythes start to behave oddly. They stop sweeping the mote-swarms and begin climbing, screaming, higher and higher above the bowl. Then the gatherers pack up and head for the shelter, and when you ask them why, they only point.

Coming in over the rim, from every quarter, are the gliders. There are hundreds, then thousands. They are no longer pale. They have darkened in the long heat of the plateaus, and their membranes have stretched and thickened and taken on colour: ochre from the west, rust from the south, and from the east that strange pale green of the finest dust. Each membrane is dusted and resin-sealed and marked along its surface with the same fine rippled script that lines the spiral bores. The drummers carved it inside the trunks. The winds have written it onto the gliders. It is the same writing, from the other side.

They do not land. They converge. The seven streams of them come spiralling down into the centre of the bowl, over the outcrop with the singing-stones, and there the winds are always tangling. On these days the tangle deepens, and a vortex begins to form.

It is not like the others. The ordinary vortices, the brown tearing columns that scour the basin floor, wander. They are blind; they go where the pressure pushes them. This one stands still, centred over the ring of seven stones, and it is not brown but many-coloured, banded, because it is made of gliders as much as of dust. Ten thousand small bodies ride its walls, each holding its membrane at a precise angle, each adjusting constantly. And the column is not formless. It has a waist. It has shoulders. At the top it spreads into two long streaming arms that reach up, up, into the gold murk overhead.

That is the figure in the soot pictures in the shelter. The small figure inside the whirling column, arms raised. The gatherers did not draw a person caught in a storm. They drew the storm, and the storm was standing up.

## IV. The Standing One

I will call it the Standing One, because the gatherers do not name it, and because that is the thing it does that nothing else in the Gullet does. It stands. In a basin where nothing holds still, it holds still. Or rather, it holds its *shape*, while every mote of it, every glider and grain, rushes endlessly around and through and up. It is still the way a flame is still, or a river's standing wave. It is a place where motion has agreed, for a while, to keep a form.

It is perhaps sixty feet tall. It has no face, although where a face would be, the glider-bands thicken and turn, and something like attention gathers there. When it forms, the spirals change their song. They do not fall silent. They answer. The old deep ones with their carved vowels begin to sound together. You realise, standing at the shelter door with dust in your teeth, that the chord the Gullet has been reaching for all season, sliding downward note by note as the spirals lengthen, was being reached for *toward this*. The drummers tuned the trunks so that when the Standing One rose, there would be something to hear it.

Because it speaks.

Or it tries to. Its speech is the sound the gliders make on its walls, a huge soft rushing rasp modulated by ten thousand membranes, each carrying a scrap of what its wind taught it. The west's heat, the east's thinness, the sinking cold of the Swallow. It is a voice assembled from reports. It is the seven winds, read aloud. And the spirals, ringed around it in the churned mosaic of the basin, echo each phrase back in their carved vowels, correcting, confirming, the way a congregation answers.

The singing-stones sit at its foot. Seven drilled slabs in a ring, placed by people long dead who thought they were calling the winds by name. They were not wrong. They were only late. The Standing One was already doing that. The stones were the gatherers' attempt to join the answer, to add a human voice, flat and stony, to the chorus. Watch the column when it rises over them. It dips, very slightly, toward the ring. The glider-bands at its base slow. It is listening to them in particular. It seems, if a thing made of wind and small lives can seem anything, to be pleased.

## V. What It Is Saying

It does not finish.

This is the heart of it, and the gatherers who have seen the Standing One more than once all say the same thing. It rises, it speaks, the spirals answer, and then the phrase breaks. A gust comes in wrong, or dry lightning cracks in the upper murk, or one of the seven streams of gliders is thinner this year than it should be, and the form loses its agreement with itself. The waist tears. The arms unravel into streamers. The column becomes an ordinary vortex, brown and blind, and goes tearing off across the basin, scattering gliders like sparks. Where the gliders fall, they burrow into the youngest spirals and begin, pale and eyeless, to knock.

Or it catches fire. Resin-soaked, charged with the static of a thousand dust-rubbed wings, the Standing One sometimes draws the lightning to itself, and then it goes up whole: a sixty-foot pillar of golden flame with arms, roaring, while every spiral in the basin screams its single rising note. The gatherers in the shelter cover their ears and their eyes. In the morning there are lanterns sprouting in a perfect ring around the singing-stones, glowing orange in the ash.

So it is never finished. It is always beginning. Every season the spirals grow and the chord slides downward. Every season the drummers carve and the gliders leave and learn and return. Every season, sometimes more than once, the Standing One rises and speaks a little of its sentence and falls apart. The churn is not the Gullet's weather. The churn is the Gullet's *breathing*, and the Standing One is what that breath is for. It is the thing all the frantic growing and burning is trying, over and over, to say.

But say to whom?

## VI. The Throat

Go back to the approach. Go back to the ridge.

*A spine of ridge so old its edges have rounded like the knuckles of a sleeping hand.* The gatherers climb it without thinking, and so did I. But stand at the bottom on a clear lowland morning and look up at the whole mountain, and you see that the knuckles are knuckles. The ridge is a forearm laid along the plateau. The two broken horns at the summit are not horns, or not only. They are the ragged edges of a jaw, or a ridge of brow, worn down past recognition by winds that have had forever. And the hanging basin between them, the bowl that holds its shape the way an old riverbed would *if there had ever been a river*, is not a riverbed.

It is a gullet. The gatherers named it better than they knew.

The stone is warm. You noticed that when you climbed. Warm and almost soft under the palm, like leather left in the sun. Everyone assumes it is the sun and the burning cushions. But at night, under a dust-dimmed moon, when the air has cooled a few degrees and the spirals are down to a murmur, lay your palm flat on the bare basin floor and wait. It takes a long time, longer than you will want to stay kneeling. But there is a warmth that comes from underneath. It rises and, over hours, very slowly fades. Then, after longer than you can bear to wait, it rises again.

It is breathing. Once a night, perhaps. Perhaps once a season. The seven winds do not *meet* here, exactly. They are drawn here, pulled over the horns into the bowl and down, into some depth of the stone no gatherer has ever found an opening to, and then out again. This happens so slowly that nothing living on the surface could ever notice the intake from the exhale. It notices only the tangle, the churn, the endless turbulence of air that is always almost being breathed.

There is no water on this mountain. There never has been. Of course there hasn't. The thing that lies here is older than water, or from somewhere water never was. Its body is stone the colour of weak tea, close-grained and patient. Whatever runs in it is not wet. If anything runs in it, it is the same thing that runs in the combers and the lizards and the drummers: heat, and mineral, and a slow golden combustion that smells of pitch and myrrh and struck flint. Every living thing in the Gullet runs on resin and warmth, and none of them drink, because they are not living *near* the sleeper. They are living *on* it, in its breath, in the moist and fertile dark of its mouth, the way small lives live in any mouth. And like the small lives in any mouth, they have become part of its voice.

## VII. The Sleeper

It is asleep. Everything in the description agrees on that, if you read it with this in mind. *The rock underneath is patient. It has seen ten thousand thousand fires and does not care.*

Not caring is what sleep looks like from outside. The Sleeper does not stir when the vortices scour its tongue, or when the lanterns burn on its palate. The gatherers' flint knives notch the spirals rooted in it, and the gatherers' feet scuff the stone of its throat, and it does not wake. The thousand small bright frantic lives of the Gullet rise and burn and rise again in the warm air of its breathing, and they are nothing to it. They are like dreams, perhaps, if it dreams. They are the fast bright flicker at the surface of a very deep sleep.

But a sleeper can be woken. And a sleeper, even deep in sleep, can hear its name.

This, I think, is what the whole Gullet is doing. This is what every drummer taps toward in the dark of the spiral bores, and what every glider rides out across the plateaus to learn. This is what the Standing One rises again and again to say, and never quite finishes. It is a name. It is the Sleeper's own name, spoken in its own language, which is a language of seven winds. The seven winds are its breath, and its breath is the only thing that has ever touched it. The mountain is trying to wake itself. Or rather, the life that grew in the warmth of its mouth is trying, and has been trying for longer than the lowlands have had people, to speak the one word that will make its host open its eyes.

## VIII. The Blue

The gatherers tell a story about the singing-stones. On the rare days when the stones and the spirals sound together in true harmony, the dust clears for a moment, and you can see the whole sky, *a deep hard blue, empty, going on forever.*

I understand that now. I think the gatherers only half do.

On those days, which come perhaps once in a lifetime, the Standing One holds. The seven streams of gliders are all full, the chord of the spirals is exactly as low as it needs to be, and the singing-stones are not wind-scoured out of tune. No lightning comes. The phrase does not break. The column speaks, and the spirals answer, and the stones answer, and the whole of the name is said.

Then the Sleeper breathes in.

It is not the slow breath of its sleep. It is a waking breath, deep and sudden. Every grain of dust in the basin, every mote and scythe feather and loose comber hair, is drawn down in an instant through the stone and into the unimaginable dark below. The air above the Gullet is left clearer than air has ever been anywhere. No dust. No water. No cloud. It is the empty hard blue of a sky from before rain, of whatever sky the Sleeper came from, going on forever.

For the length of that breath, the Sleeper is awake. It is looking up through its own throat at that sky.

And anyone standing in the bowl at that moment is standing in the pupil of its eye.

That is why the ones who have seen it do not come back. They are not afraid, or not only afraid. It is simply that they have been *seen*. For one breath, something older than water took notice of them: a small warm moving thing in its mouth, knife in hand, clay cup at its hip. It did not care, exactly. It did not wish them harm or good. But it saw them, and it knew them, and a gatherer who has been known like that has no more reason to climb. The resin was never the point. The resin was only the thing that brought them close enough.

Then the Sleeper breathes out. The dust comes back, gold and ochre and pale green, pouring up out of the stone in a great warm exhalation that sets every spiral howling at once. The murk closes. The light goes back to honey. The Sleeper sinks back down into its long sleep, for another season, or another thousand. The sunlit stone of the basin is left warmer than it was before, and softer, like leather.

In the bores of the burned and broken spirals, the first new drummers are already tapping. *Tak. Tak-tak. Tak.* They are starting the name again from the beginning, patiently, in the dark. They have forgotten nothing, because they never knew. They are only carving what the wind tells them, in the throat of something that is not yet ready to wake.

## IX. Who Lives There

So who lives in the Gullet of Seven Winds?

The combers live there, and the spirals, the motes, the scythes, the drunk and dreaming pitch-lickers, and the shell-crabs with their stripes of every burning. The gatherers live there for a few days at a time, and so do the long-dead hands that scratched the pictures in the soot and set the seven stones in their ring. The drummers live there, and the gliders who become the drummers' voice, and the Standing One who rises out of them, arms raised, and tries.

And under them all, around them all, holding them in the warm dry cup of its throat, the Sleeper lives there. It is the one resident who never leaves and never changes. All that fast bright short-lived frantic life has been trying, since before there were people to watch, to say its name.

The gatherers leave knots of fibre on the cairns around the rim. They say these mark the safe paths, the ones the vortices avoid. I do not think the vortices avoid them at all. I think the knots are small offerings, streaming and snapping in the wind: little flags of woven comber, tied by people who did not know why they were doing it, in the language the Gullet speaks. Each one is a syllable, and each one adds, very slightly, to the name.
