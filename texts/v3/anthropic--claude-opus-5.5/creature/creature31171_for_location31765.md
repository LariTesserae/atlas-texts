# Who Lives in Asker

## I. The First Answer

The first answer is easy, because the first answer is loud. The skerries live in Asker.

There are too many of them to describe all at once, so start with one.

She is born in the middle of the long night, deep inside a wall in the district called Harrow, in a cell that did not exist twenty years ago. It is one of the warm cells near the wall's core, where the stone is youngest and still carries a little of the heat of the deep. The cell smells of struck match. Water beads on its ceiling and falls in a slow, particular rhythm, three drops and a pause, three drops and a pause. That rhythm is the first thing she knows.

Skerries are born with their ears open. Before her eyes have any use, and in Asker they will never have much use, she is listening. Her mother presses against her in the tight dark and makes a sound she will know for the rest of her life: a short, hooked click with a buzz at the end of it, a signature. The cell answers the click. Every cell in Asker is cut with the engines' precision, but none is perfectly silent. Each has its own drip, its own film of water, its own slight roughness where a glass seam crosses the grain. So the pup learns two voices at once, her mother's and her home's. For the rest of her life, somewhere under everything, she will be listening for three drops and a pause.

In a few weeks she can fly. She pours out of the wall with ten thousand others at the evening outflow and learns the city by throwing her voice at it. A gallery comes back long and hollow. A terrace comes back as a wide, soft hiss. A canal comes back as nothing at all, a black gap in the echo with a cold roar inside it, the one place she learns never to fold her wings. She eats the tiny gnats that hatch from the canals by the million. She learns the other skerries by their signatures and their flight-streams by their pressure. She never touches anything she does not mean to touch.

Here is something the keepers learned only after generations of listening, and the thing they consider most important about her: **she has two voices.**

The first is the air-voice, the clicks and chirrs she uses to see. It is quick, high, and directional, and it is most of the roar that fills Asker's streets.

The second she uses only when she is home. When a skerry roosts, packed into a cell with her kin, she presses her belly and the long bones of her folded wings flat against the stone and hums. It is a low, continuous buzz, far below the pitch of her hunting calls, and it is barely audible in the air because it isn't meant for the air. It goes into the rock. Put your hand on the inner wall of a crowded cell and you can feel it, a fine grain of vibration running through the stone like current through a wire.

The keepers call it the **down-voice**. They believe it is what the engines hear. The air-voice is how a skerry sees the city; the down-voice is how she votes for it.

She will live a long time for so small an animal, twenty years, sometimes thirty. In twenty years her home wall rises a hand's width. When she is old she still returns to it, and the cell she was born in is higher now, farther from the warm core and colder. The drip has changed, because the water finds new seams as the stone moves. Old skerries can be heard in the deep season hunting along the face of their birth walls, calling and calling, listening for three drops and a pause in a cell that has drifted upward out of reach of the rhythm it used to have.

When she dies she falls.

## II. The Ones Who Walk Softly

The gleaners take her.

The account you've been given is right that they are gangly stilt-walkers, larger than dogs, feeling their way along the ledges with jointed antennae. It is right that they eat fallen skerries, and right that the keepers argue about whether they are animals.

Here is why the keepers argue. A gleaner never takes a living skerry. It never enters a roost and never calls into stone. Its low wet rattle is always pitched up, into the air, away from the walls. Most tellingly, a gleaner walks strangely. It lifts each stilt-foot with exaggerated care and sets it down with hardly any weight, the way a person walks across a floor where someone is sleeping.

Every creature in Asker is counted by the stone, and the gleaners seem to be trying very hard not to be.

The keepers find this disquieting in a way they have trouble explaining to outsiders. It suggests the gleaners know about the vote. It suggests that at some point, someone decided the ones who clean the city should not have a say in its shape. The keepers also notice, uneasily, that their own duties overlap with the gleaners' almost exactly. They clear the fallen from the canals. They tend. They walk the ledges in the dark.

There is an old keeper saying: *A gleaner is a keeper that someone else wrote the rules for.* Nobody is sure whether it's a joke.

## III. The Second Answer

The second answer is quieter. The keepers live in Asker.

Consider one of them. Call her Maro, since that is close to her name. She is sixty-one. She was born in the district called Lintel, in the oldest part of the city, where the walls went inert before her grandmother's grandmother was born and the drip-lice left long ago.

Lintel is not a hive. That is the first thing a keeper child learns about home. Its openings are not fist-sized cells. They are doorways: tall, narrow, arched, made for a person to walk through upright. It has rooms. It has stairs whose risers fit a human stride exactly. Its galleries are wide enough for four people to walk abreast, and they carry the voice of someone singing from one end to the other without distortion, as if designed for exactly that.

Lintel was lifted, the keepers say, when people were the majority. Its shape is the fossil of a human vote. Maro lives inside the architecture of her ancestors' voices, and like all such architecture it is going cold. The stone there no longer weeps. It is dry to the touch, almost soft-feeling after the knife-edges of the new districts. It is the quietest place in Asker, which means you can hear yourself breathe and you can hear the roar of the living city as a far-off surf over the rooftops.

Maro's hands are what you would notice if you could see her. Keepers read the city through their fingertips: the carved wall-marks at shoulder height, the grain of the stone, the tempo of the drip on a given ledge. Over a lifetime their fingers go smooth and wide at the pads, and very sensitive. When Maro lays her palm on a new wall, the drip-lice swarm it immediately, and she lets them. She says she can tell a wall's age by how the lice walk, frantic and crowded on young rock, sparse and wandering on old.

She speaks softly, as all keepers do, in a speech that is half words and half clicks. The clicks carry position. When two keepers talk in the dark, part of every sentence is quietly telling the other where the speaker's mouth is, how far off, and what the walls between them are like. Keeper children learn to click before they learn to speak. Maro's clicker is glass, cut from a seam in the district where she was born. It was her father's.

She has spent her life doing what keepers do: clearing the canals, tracking the rise, and now and then singing a wall into being. Over forty years she and a dozen others have been gathering nightly on a low terrace in the district called Weir, clapping stone on stone, whistling, crowding close, lying flat against the ground and humming into it the way skerries do. They are trying to raise a windbreak. The terrace has risen perhaps nine centimeters. Maro thinks it will be finished around the time her grandchildren are old.

She does not find this sad. She finds it ordinary. "In every city there's ever been," she says, "you shouted for a wall and it came after you were dead. Here you can at least feel it moving."

## IV. The Dead Who Rise

Asker is the reason for the keepers' way with their dead, and the one custom outsiders remember.

When a keeper dies, the body is not burned, because nothing burns in that wet. It is not sunk, because the canals are too cold for anything to decay and bodies would only jam the channels. It is carried to the foot of a young, rising wall, the loudest the keepers can safely reach, and laid in one of the low, body-sized cells near the base. Then the cell is sealed with a flat stone.

The wall goes on rising.

It carries the cell upward, a finger's width a year. Within a lifetime the dead are high in the wall among the skerries, cradled in a sealed cell with a million small bodies pressing and humming against the stone all around them. The cold and the mineral water preserve them. The down-voices of the colony run through their chamber day and night.

Keepers can point up into the dark and tell you where their family is. *My grandmother is forty meters up in Harrow. My brother's still low, you could touch his stone if you climbed.* Children are taken to the base of the walls where their ancestors are and told to put their hands flat against the rock, into the swarm of lice and the thrum of the colony's humming, and feel for them.

And every keeper knows, though they rarely say it aloud, that the walls rise where the voices are. To be laid in a loud wall is to be carried by the colony's vote. The dead are lifted by the skerries. The keepers consider this a kind of citizenship too.

## V. The Third Answer

Listen long enough, the keepers say, and you stop hearing the roar as noise.

It begins as a suspicion. A keeper standing on a terrace in the middle of the long night notices that the colony's sound is swelling. It isn't louder because more skerries are flying; it's louder together. The down-voices in the nearby walls fall into step. For a moment, the length of a few breaths, a whole district hums on one note. Then the swell passes on. You can hear it move, district to district, across the whole dark plateau: a slow pressure-wave of synchronized sound rolling out over miles of honeycombed stone, like wind crossing a field of grass. Then it comes back from another direction. Sometimes two swells cross, and where they meet the ground shivers.

Keepers have mapped these swells for as long as there have been keepers. They are not random. They move toward some districts and away from others. They pool over particular walls night after night for years, and a decade later those walls stand taller. Before a district begins to rise, the swells gather over it and stay, repeating, insisting.

The keepers call this **the asking**.

The name of the place, they say, is not a name at all. It is a description. *Asker*: the thing that asks. It does not mean the rock or the colony. It means whatever happens when a million voices press into stone in unison and the stone, eventually, answers.

Is it a mind? Keepers have argued about this for centuries, and the argument is part of the culture, like weather. Some say it is only what a crowd sounds like, as mindless as a flock turning or a shoal breaking. Others say something that makes the same request, in the same place, for thirty years, until it gets what it wants, has earned the word *will*, whatever it is made of.

Maro holds a third position, common among the old. She says it doesn't matter whether the asking is a mind, because the engines treat it as one, and in a city what you are treated as is most of what you are.

## VI. The Fourth Answer

This is the part that has changed in Maro's lifetime, and it is why the keepers have stopped arguing so comfortably.

Stairs are returning to Asker.

For centuries the new districts have been purely the colony's: fist-sized cells, head-sized cells, sheer faces with nothing a human could climb, narrow ledges only a gleaner could walk. Keeper children are taught to count the stairs in a newly risen district the way children elsewhere might count votes, because that is what they are. The count has been falling for as long as anyone has records. The humans are a tiny minority, and the city has been forgetting them.

But over the last forty years, in districts far from any keeper singing, the engines have begun lifting stairs again. There are human-sized risers in the flanks of new walls and short flights that lead from one ledge to another. In one young district in the east, still only chest-high and wet and smelling of the deep, there is a doorway. It is tall and narrow and arched, like the doors of Lintel. There are no people in that district. There never have been.

The keepers went and listened. What they heard over the young stone, carried in the asking, was their own sound.

It was clapping, not from hands but from thousands of skerries striking their wingbones together on the roost in a slow, doubled rhythm. It was the clap of stone on stone that the keepers use when they gather to sing a wall. Among the down-voices there was a whistled figure, two falling notes, that every keeper recognized. It is the call they use to gather a singing party. The skerries have learned it. Somewhere across the generations, the colony noticed which sounds the engines answered with stairs, and it has begun making them.

The keepers do not agree on what this means.

Some think it is mimicry and nothing more: a colony of echo-animals repeating a sound because that sound once moved stone. Some think it is theft, that the skerries have learned to ask in the humans' voice and the stairs are only incidental. Some, and Maro is among them, think it is something closer to what happened long ago in reverse. Once, humans were the loud ones, and the engines built their city, and the skerries moved into the cracks. Now the skerries are the loud ones, and something in the colony has decided, or discovered, or simply fallen into the habit of making room.

"Mimicry is how it always starts," Maro says. "In every city. You learn to talk like your neighbors because it gets you things. Then one day you're asking on their behalf and you don't remember when that started."

## VII. The Last Answer

So what lives in Asker?
