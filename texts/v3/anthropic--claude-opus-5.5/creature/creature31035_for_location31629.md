# Who Lives in Ashcarr

## The Ones You See First

You will take the first tender you meet for a stump.

She is sitting at the foot of an arm in the late afternoon, in the quiet hour when the wood is nearly straight and nothing needs doing. Her nettle cloth has gone the grey-green of old lichen, and she sits so still, with her back so perfectly fitted to the curve of the root, that your eye passes over her twice. She sees you before you see her. She will not call out, because nobody here calls out, but she will lift one hand a little from her knee. It is the same small gesture a tender makes to someone on the far side of a lawn to say *I see you, I'm not coming over, it's fine*.

When you reach her she will be kind, and she will be brief. She will ask if you came down the north rim or the east. The east is steeper, and the moss there has been known to hide old iron. She will ask whether you felt the air change on the way down, and she will seem pleased if you did, the way you are pleased when a guest notices a smell you have long since stopped noticing in your own house. Then she will look past you at the arm, at the sky, at the grey feathering already gathering in the lowest part of the basin, and you will understand that the conversation is over. It isn't rude. The fog is coming, and she has somewhere to be.

There are not many tenders. On any given day there might be twenty-five in the basin, perhaps thirty in the wet season when the work runs fast. You could live here a month and still not be sure you had seen them all, because they keep to their buildings and their arms. When they do cross the lawns they walk the sunken paths slowly and never on the moss itself. They move the way people move in a house where someone is asleep.

## What They Are Called

The first thing a visitor gets wrong is names.

Tenders have names. They were given them at birth or on arrival, and they use them among themselves, sometimes. But almost no one in Ashcarr is called by their name. They are called by their arm.

The arms have names, old and worn smooth. *Long Grey*, *the Widow*, *Crook*, *Second Crook* (younger than Crook, and straighter), *Old Pull*, *Sallow*, *the Heron*, *Ninefold*. That last one is the arm on the paper mill, which was regrafted so many times that its bark shows nine separate healed seams. A tender who has been given an arm to keep becomes that arm's person, and so becomes *Long Grey's*, or *the Widow's*, the way you might say someone is the miller's or the smith's. *Ask Heron's* is a full sentence in Ashcarr. So is *Crook's is poorly*, and people will understand you mean the man and not the wood. Though if Crook's is poorly, they will also go and look at Crook.

An arm outlives its tenders. Long Grey has had, by the paper mill's count, eleven people. Their names are kept in the mill on a single strip of paper that grows a little longer every generation. The strip is not considered important. What is considered important is the arm, and the arm's measurements, and the knot-cords that record them. Those are kept with great care.

So when you ask a tender who they are, they will very often tell you what they keep, and they will think they have answered the question.

## The Knot-Cords

Every spring, before the thaw-fog, each tender takes a length of their own rope-walk cord and measures their arm.

They measure its length from root collar to tip, and its girth at seven stations. They measure the height of the tip at dawn, fully curled, and the height at afternoon, straight. They measure the angle the pin makes with the rod. Each measurement is a knot, and the kind of knot means something: overhand for length, figure-eight for girth, a double loop for the dawn height. The spacing between knots is the number. They tie them one after another along the cord, so that by the end of the measuring the tender holds the arm's whole year in one hand, a knotted string perhaps as long as they are tall.

The cords are hung inside the building, near the engine, one below the last. In the oldest buildings the cords hang in curtains, a hundred and more of them. The ones at the back have gone black and stiff, and some have been grown into the wall, because the wall has thickened inward over the years and simply swallowed them, the way it swallows iron. You can put your fingers into the bark and feel, sunk there, the bumps of knots tied by someone dead three hundred years.

A tender reading an old cord runs it through their fingers with their eyes closed, the way a person might hum an old song to find where it goes. They are looking for a feel. *This year Old Pull put on girth all at the third station, see, and none at the root, so she was pulling from the elbow, and he'd have had to pin her short.* They can read these the way you read letters from a relative. They can tell when a tender was careful, or grieving, or new. They can tell when a tender was old and their hands shook, because the knots are loose and uneven, and nobody retied them.

Some tenders, near the end of their lives, stop tying measurements and start tying other things. Nobody forbids it. There are cords in the rope walk building whose last few arm-lengths hold no number anyone can read. They hold patterns of knots, clustered and spaced, that look like weather, or like speech. The tenders who come after do not try to decipher them. They hang them in their place with the rest.

## How the Body Changes

Live in Ashcarr long enough and your body joins the rhythm, whether you mean it to or not.

It starts with sleep. The fog comes in at dusk and the ratchets quicken, and something in the ticking makes the eyelids heavy. The lamps pulse, and you breathe with them without deciding to. Somewhere near midnight the arms reach full curl, the engines stop, and the basin falls into silence. Tenders sleep hardest in that hour and wake, most of them, at the first wren. On dry nights, when no fog comes and the arms don't move and the lamps don't light, the tenders sleep badly. They lie awake in the green dark listening to nothing, and in the morning they are short with one another, and the ones who have been here longest say their joints hurt.

That is the next thing. The old tenders feel the fog in their hands. They say the knuckles swell a little on wet nights and loosen by afternoon, like the arms do, and that the hand is stiffest just before dawn. A tender who has been at it forty years sits in the late afternoon with their fingers spread flat on their knees, straight, almost pleased, and at dusk those fingers begin, very slightly, to curl.

They are not turning into wood. Nobody in Ashcarr would tell you that, and it isn't true. But there is a posture the old ones take on in the evenings: sitting at the root of their arm with their back to the bark, bent a little forward, chin down, hands closing. When you see several of them at once across the basin in the thickening fog, each at the foot of their own arm, each bent the same way at the same hour, it is hard not to notice that they are all doing the same thing the wood is doing.

Their voices go, too, in a way. The murmur that visitors notice is not a rule. Nobody was ever told to be quiet. It is that in a place where the loudest sound is a wren and the next loudest is a ratchet, a raised voice feels like a dropped pot. People learn to speak at the level of the place. Children who grow up here often cannot shout at all. When they leave, if they leave, they find it takes them a long time to be heard in a room.

## What They Eat

Almost nothing grows in the basin that a person would want to eat, and what does grow is spoken for.

The tenders eat nettle. Young tops in spring, boiled; older leaves dried and crumbled into everything. They eat bracken fiddleheads in the brief weeks when those are safe, and a little sorrel, and the hazelnuts that grow on the rim, which children are sent up to gather in autumn. But mostly they eat what they trade for.

Once a month in the wet season, and more rarely in the dry, a few tenders climb the east rim with coils of rope on their backs and bundles of paper wrapped in oilcloth. At the top there is a flat stone where carters from the country beyond will sometimes wait. They trade rope for oats, paper for salt and cheese, and cord for lamp glass, which is the one thing the tenders cannot make and must be very careful not to break. The carters say the Ashcarr rope is the best in three counties. It is spun so slowly, and so evenly, that it has almost no weak places. They say the paper is strange: soft, faintly green, and very long-lasting. Documents on Ashcarr paper do not yellow.

The carters do not come down into the basin. They say the ticking puts them off. Some say it is the smell, or the quiet. One old carter, asked once, said only that it felt like walking into a church during the part where everyone kneels.

So the tenders carry everything up, and carry everything down, and the food they eat has come a long way on someone's back. Maybe that is why they eat so little. Maybe it is just the pace of the place. Nobody here is ever very hungry, the way nobody is ever in a hurry.

## The Children

There are very few. In some decades there are none at all, and the tenders take in someone from outside: an orphan from the country beyond, a runaway, once a grown man who came down the north rim with nothing and simply stayed. Most years there are two or three children in the basin, and they are raised by all the tenders in common, the way the lawns are kept in common.

A child in Ashcarr is not given an arm. A child is given a pin.

It is a single iron pin, pulled from some old engine, and the child is told to find where it belongs. That means walking the basin, looking at every arm and every rod and every ratchet, working out from the rust and the wear and the diameter which housing it came from and why it was taken out. It can take a year. It is supposed to. By the time the child finds the right hole, they know every engine in Ashcarr by touch.

After that they learn to listen. An old tender puts the child's palm flat against the wall of a building at dusk and says nothing, and the child stands there as the fog comes in and the arm begins to curl. The wall is not still. Through the wood, deep in it, too low for the ear, comes a slow straining groan, the whole building gently taking the weight of its own arm. Children who feel it for the first time often snatch their hands away. They put them back. They learn to tell, through the wall, whether the arm is pulling clean or grinding, whether the pin is true, whether something is wrong in the gears.

Only after the pin and the listening do they learn cord, and pruning, and grafting. Only after all of that, often not until they are grown, are they given an arm. It is usually one whose tender has died, sometimes one whose tender has grown too old to climb. Then they become *Sallow's* or *Heron's*, and their name is added to the strip in the mill, and they will likely never be called anything else again.

## The Rope-Walker

Every tender keeps something, but there is one who keeps a thing that moves.

The rope walk runs a hundred feet down the longest building in Ashcarr, a tunnel of pleached hornbeam so old and so dense that inside it is nearly dark even at noon. At the far end sits the twisting head, and the head crawls. It moves the length of the tunnel over the course of a week, spinning cord behind it, and when it reaches the end it is carried back, and it begins again.

The keeper of the rope walk walks with it.

Not constantly, and not the whole time. But the head needs watching: a snag in the fiber, a kink, a place where the strands feed uneven. So the rope-walker spends the week going down the tunnel at the speed of the rope, which is so slow that you could not see it move if you watched it. She sleeps in the tunnel beside the head. She eats there. She sits with her hand resting lightly on the forming cord, feeling the twist, and she moves her pallet a few feet every day. In the time it takes the rest of the basin to live a week, she has walked a hundred feet.

The current rope-walker is old. She has been doing it so long that the other tenders say she thinks at the speed of the head. She has a way of starting a sentence on one day and finishing it on the next, when you pass by the tunnel again. The children love her. They go down the dark tunnel to sit with her and listen to the faint whirr, and she tells them things very slowly: which fiber came from which press, which press was set by which tender, and how many times this same stretch of floor has had a rope spun over it. The children say that when you sit with her long enough you can see the rope move. It is probably true.

She is not called by the rope walk's name, because the rope walk has no arm. They call her *the Walker*. It is the only title in Ashcarr that names what a person does and not what they keep.

## The Small Ones

Besides the tenders, a few lives share the basin, and each has made its own quiet bargain with the place.

**The slugs** are pale grey and very large, as long as a finger and as thick as a thumb, and they move over the iron at night grazing the thin green bloom that grows on anything metal left damp. Where a slug has passed, the iron is clean and faintly shining, as if polished. Tenders step around them on the paths. A child who steps on one on purpose will be taken aside and spoken to, very quietly, for a long time. The slugs have been here as long as the iron has.

**The beetle** has no name the tenders use; they call it *the little brown*, or just *brown*. It is the size of a grain of barley, dull and hard, and it lives only in the gear housings, in the grease and the dust and the dark. Its grubs are white and blind and eat moss. Nothing else, only the fine green felt that creeps into the teeth of the gears week by week and would, left alone, jam them solid. When a new housing is built, or an old one opened and cleaned, the tender takes a pinch of earth from an older housing, earth with eggs in it, and seeds the new one. It is done with a certain care, the tender cupping the earth in a leaf and tipping it in. Some of them say a word when they do it. Nobody will tell you the word.

The beetles have been doing this for so long that they no longer live anywhere else. Outside the basin, in ordinary woods, there is no little brown. It exists only inside the machines of Ashcarr. It is, in a sense, a tender too, and more faithful than most.

**The wrens** are the exception to everything. They are fast. They are loud. They dart. They are the only creatures in Ashcarr that do anything quickly. One or two pairs nest in the thickest of the old walls, pushing their way into gaps in the lattice, and at dawn they sing, and their song goes through the fog-heavy basin like a needle through cloth. The tenders love them. Nobody says so. But on mornings when the wrens are late, or silent, you will see tenders standing in doorways looking up at the walls, waiting. And when a wren sings for the first time after a winter, when the rime is gone and the thaw-fog is in and the ratchets are ticking again, the tenders stop what they are doing, whatever it is, and listen until it finishes.

**The moths** come at night to the lamps. There are never many, only a few pale soft ones blundering at the orange glass. The lamps are too dim to draw a crowd. But on still, humid nights, when the lamps pulse with the wood's slow breathing, the moths pulse with them. They swim close when the light swells and drift off when it dims, so that the moths too are moving to the rhythm of the fog without knowing it. Some of the tenders say the moths are the only visitors Ashcarr has ever really had.

## The Closed Rooms

As the walls thicken, the doors narrow. Every few generations a door becomes too narrow to use and a new one is trained elsewhere in the wall. The old door is let go. Within a decade or two it has closed completely, the two trunks of its frame swelling toward each other until they meet and fuse.

Usually this is fine. The room behind it is still reachable through the new door. But not always.

There are three buildings in Ashcarr with no doors at all. They are among the oldest. At some point, in some century, the walls closed faster than the tenders trained new openings, or the tenders decided, for reasons nobody wrote down, to let them close. Now they are sealed. Their walls are those poured-wax slabs where no single limb can be told apart. The arms on their flanks are still alive, still rooted, still curling each night and straightening each day, and still pinned to rods that pass into the bark and vanish.

And the engines inside are still running.

You can hear it if you stand close on a foggy night. It is faint and muffled by a yard of living wood, but it is there: a ratchet, somewhere inside, ticking as the arm curls. Nobody has seen the inside of these buildings in living memory. Nobody knows what the engines are driving. The oldest maps mark them only with a symbol that the paper mill's keeper will tell you means *kept*.

The tenders still re-pin those arms every spring. They climb up to the tip and adjust the angle of the rod where it enters the wall, as carefully as if they could see the other end. They measure them. They tie the cords. They hang the cords on pegs on the outside of the sealed walls, because there is nowhere else, and the outside of those buildings is hung all over with rope, black with age, grown half into the bark.

The children are told that nothing lives in the closed rooms. The children do not believe this and never have, and after a while the adults stop saying it.

## Under the Moss

The moss on the lawns is so deep that anything dropped on it sinks. The tenders say that under it lies peat, and under the peat, everything: gear wheels, tools, pins, whole broken engines, preserved black and soft.

The tenders are there too.

When a tender dies, which they usually do in the evening, at the root of their arm, with their back against the bark and their hands curling, they are not burned, because nothing is burned in Ashcarr. They are not buried, exactly, either. The other tenders lay them on the moss of the lawn nearest their arm, in their grey-green nettle cloth, with their last knot-cord folded in their hands. Then they leave them.

The moss takes about a season. It rises around them as it rises around a footprint, and closes over them, and draws them down. In spring there is only a slight softness in the lawn, a place where the moss is a little greener, a little springier. By the next spring there is not even that.

Down in the peat, the tenders say, they keep. Peat does not let things rot. There are places, in the country far off where the old bogs are cut, where men have found bodies thousands of years old with their faces still whole, their hair still braided, their last meals still in their stomachs. The tenders of Ashcarr know this. They think of the dead down there under the lawns, each lying near the root of the arm they kept, knot-cord in hand, perfectly preserved
