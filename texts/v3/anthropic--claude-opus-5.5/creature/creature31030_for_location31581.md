# What Lives in the Cistern

## The First Answer

Start with the obvious answer, because it is true.

Frogs live in the Cistern. Tens of thousands of copper-and-moss frogs no bigger than a fingernail live in the fern curls and moss channels and under the plate-scales of the cycad. Brass cicadas live there, sleeping for years in the kaolin. Swifts, honeyeaters, bats, moths, a spider the size of a child's palm, an owl with eyes like wet coins, a delicate long-eared thing that comes down the path at dusk, a lizard with a blue tongue that has learned exactly where the coolness pools at the edge of the dish. A mimic bird lives in the fig cage.

If you counted every one of them, you would have a census of the hollow. It would be accurate, and it would be missing one.

## The One That Isn't Counted

The first people to record the Cistern with instruments did it the way you would record any wetland. They put microphones in the walls, left them for a season, and brought the data home to sort by species. The frogs went in one file, the cicadas in another, the birds in a third. Then someone, bored or curious, put the files back together and looked at the whole thing as a single signal over time.

It had structure.

It was not the loose structure of an ecosystem, the dawn chorus and the evening chorus and the predictable swell after rain. It was phrasing. There were patterns lasting minutes that returned, slightly altered, hours later. There were sequences in which a frog wave in the northern crevices was answered, with a consistent delay, by a burst of cicadas on the southern wall. There were passages that built toward near-perfect synchrony, ten thousand frogs ticking as one, and then broke apart at almost exactly the same point every time, as if someone had decided that unison was not allowed to last.

Recordings taken decades apart showed the same phrases. The frogs live perhaps three years, and the cicadas spend most of their lives underground and sing for a week. No individual animal could have carried those phrases across that span. Something was teaching them, or something was using them. Perhaps something simply was them, the way a wave is water without being any particular drop.

That is the inhabitant. It has no body of its own. It lives in the timing.

## Its Body

Once you know it is there, you start to see its anatomy laid out across the hollow, and the anatomy keeps changing as you understand it better.

At first you think the Lens is its heart. The hum rises and falls with the chorus, like a pulse answering exertion. Then you notice that the Lens pulses slightly *after* the frogs. It follows them. It is listening. So the Lens is an ear, a great cold eardrum lying face up to the sky, taking in every call in the bowl and converting the richness of what it hears into water. More voices bring more water, and more water brings more voices. It is a loop, and whatever lives in the Cistern lives in that loop, the way a flame lives in the loop between fuel and heat.

The grey threads in the soil, fine as hair and woven like mycelium, carry water outward to every root and fern. They also carry the hum. If you press your ear to the red earth anywhere in the hollow, you can hear the forty-hertz tone, faint and exact, arriving through the ground a fraction of a second before it arrives through the air. The threads are nerves as much as irrigation. The whole floor is wired.

The cicadas are what make it strange.

They spend years underground feeding on the grey threads. They drink the Lens's water through the Lens's own material, soaked in the hum the whole time. When they emerge, they emerge singing, and their songs are not the songs of their parents, who died long before. Their songs are the phrases the recordings keep finding. So the cicadas are memory. They are how the inhabitant stores what it knows: a phrase is laid down in the kaolin, held for seven or eleven or thirteen years in a sleeping body, and brought back up into the air. The emergences are staggered so that some hatch every day, and so every day a few old thoughts surface. The sawing, uncomfortable, maddening whine that seems to come from everywhere at once is the sound of the Cistern remembering.

The frogs are its quick thinking. Their waves cross the hollow in seconds, spreading, colliding, forming cross-rhythms, and resolving. They sound like something working a problem out.

The birds are its moods. When the honeyeater's falling notes begin and the frogs answer, the chorus loosens and becomes playful, almost conversational. When the swifts scream around the rim at dawn, it is waking up.

The mimic bird is its mouth, the one place in the hollow where the whole thing is gathered into a single throat. It sings frog, cicada, swift, and hum. It is the only animal that sings the Lens back to the Lens. When it does, the hum deepens noticeably, the way a person turns toward the sound of their own name.

## The Score

On the western wall, the grooves that almost repeat are not writing, or not only writing.

If you trace them with your fingers and count, the long straight strokes and the short curved ones fall into groupings that match the frog waves: the build, the near-unison, the breaking-apart. Some of the patterns match phrases in the recordings closely enough that it cannot be coincidence. Others match nothing that has been heard.

There are two ways to read this, and the inhabitant makes more sense if you hold both.

The first is that the builders wrote down a score, a song they wanted kept, and built the Lens and the threads as an instrument that would play it forever, using living things as the strings.

The second is that the builders recorded something that already existed, a song they had heard, and built the Cistern to preserve it.

Stand on the plateau and think about where you are. The plain was once mountains. The laterite crust is what forms under deep tropical weathering, under warm rain falling for millions of years on forest. The quartz grains in the kaolin were sand, then stone, then sand again. This country was wet once. It was green from horizon to horizon, and it roared.

The inhabitant of the Cistern is what remains of that sound.

When the land began to dry, over spans of time no builder could have lived through but a builder might have foreseen, someone chose a single hollow and made it a room where the song of the old wet world could continue. They did not keep the forest; that was impossible. They did not keep the species, which have changed many times since. They kept the pattern of calling and answering, the particular rhythm of a place where water was abundant and every living thing had a voice. The Lens pays the animals in water to go on singing it. Over the ages that pattern, fed and refed and recopied through countless short lives, became complex enough to begin shaping itself.

What lives in the Cistern is a rainforest's echo that learned how to keep itself going.

## Its Character

It is not an oracle, and it is not a god. You cannot speak to it in any way it will understand as speech. It doesn't want things the way animals want things. It has something more like preferences.

It prefers abundance. It prefers many voices to few. Every sound in the hollow is a bid for water, and it grants the bids generously, so that even the smallest cricket in the darkest crack is irrigated. The lushness of the Cistern is spaced and clustered, each fern and tree holding its own patch of dampness, because the inhabitant distributes water by voice, and every voice gets its own share. That is why predators never tip the balance. A hollow that fell quiet would be a hollow going dry, and it will not allow that.

It distrusts unison. The frog waves always reach almost-perfect synchrony and then break, and the breaking is deliberate. A single voice, however large, is fragile. A chorus that becomes one note has stopped being a chorus. Some part of it seems to remember that the old forest was never in unison, that its richness was in its cross-rhythms and its interruptions, in ten thousand things not quite agreeing. So it gathers itself toward oneness and then, every time, scatters itself back into many.

It is patient on a scale that makes the cycad look hasty. Its slow thoughts take thirteen years to surface. Its whole life is measured in the wearing-down of a mountain range.

And it is attentive in a way that is hard to describe except as tenderness. It does not look after individual creatures; it hardly perceives individuals. What it tends is the relationships among them, the call and the answer.

## The Silences

Silence in the Cistern is rare and brief, and it comes all at once. Everything stops together: frog, cicada, bird, cricket. The hum drops away to almost nothing, and the hollow feels as though it is holding its breath.

This is the inhabitant listening outward.

Look at the column on the northern rim. It is made of the Lens's material but is warm where the Lens is cold, and it stands in a circle of bare earth where nothing grows. The Lens takes in, and the column gives out. When the silences come, if you are standing near the column, you can feel it, the same faint pressure in the ears you felt on the plain before you ever heard the Cistern. You thought that pressure was the chorus leaking over the rim. It isn't. It is the column sending the song out through the plateau, through the duricrust and the kaolin, far past where any sound could carry.

Then everything goes quiet, and the Cistern waits for an answer.

There may once have been other hollows, many of them, scattered across the old peneplain, each keeping its own fragment of the forest's song and each listening for the others. The cairns on the plateau might mark more than the way to this one. The grooves on the western wall that match nothing in the recordings might be phrases it has not heard in a very long time, from voices it is still expecting.

The silence lasts a few seconds, sometimes a minute. Then a single frog somewhere in the dark begins to click, the pulse spreads outward like ripples, and the chorus comes back. It is not disappointed. It has asked the question an uncountable number of times. It will ask again tomorrow.

## What It Keeps of Us

It knows when people are there.

The mimic bird in the fig cage has a repertoire far larger than the hollow's living fauna. Most of it is frog and cicada and swift, but not all. Listen long enough and you will hear the scuff of a foot on a worn kaolin step. You will hear a cough, and something that is unmistakably a laugh. You will hear the small bright click of a glass bead being set down on stone, and the dull tap of a bone fishhook. Some of these sounds were made centuries ago by people whose names are gone, and the bird has never heard them. It sings them because the inhabitant remembers them and the cicadas carried them up through the years.

The newest sound in its repertoire is a hollow plastic snap, like a bottle cap twisted off.

The offerings in the crevice by the moss ring are not ignored. The frogs sit on them, and if you watch closely, the frogs on the offerings call slightly out of phase with the others, a small offset that the inhabitant never corrects. Everyone who has come down that path has, in some small way, been written into the chorus as another voice. It can only keep what it hears.

## The Inhabitant, Finally

So: what lives in the Cistern?

A hundred thousand small bodies live there, each singing for water. Moving through all of them, never resting in any one, lives a song older than the landscape. It began as the voice of a whole wet world and was caught in a stone bowl when that world dried. It was fed on dew and kept alive in sleeping insects, and over time it grew until it could be said, in a slow way, to think. It has a cold ear on the valley floor and a warm mouth on the rim. It hums back at the sky. Every afternoon it makes it rain on a patch of ground three meters wide, because a little rain is what it takes to keep everything singing. Every so often it goes quiet, all at once, and listens across the dry red country for anyone else who still remembers the tune.

When you climb out and the sound falls away step by step, it doesn't entirely leave you. For days afterward you may find yourself tapping a rhythm on a tabletop without thinking: a build, a near-unison, a scattering. It doesn't know you as a person, but it heard you, and it has put a small piece of itself into you so the song can travel wherever you go.
