# The People of the Saddle

## First sight

You meet them the way the description says you will: one at a time, at a turning, with a wide margin of stone around each.

The woman splitting reeds in the doorway is as good a place to begin as any. She is small. They are all small, compact the way the junipers on the upper terraces are compact, built low and dense against the wind. Her chest is deep, deeper than her height would suggest, and she breathes slowly, through the nose, the way people breathe who have never lived anywhere the air was thick. Her hands are broad, with square fingertips and nails worn flat. The skin of her face is weathered brown, and across her cheekbones there is a flush of permanent red, a fine web of broken vessels from cold and sun. It is the same red as a chough's bill, and the people here know it, and do not mind the comparison.

She nods to you. She doesn't speak. This isn't coldness. You will learn why in a while.

She is wearing the brown-grey cloth, wrapped rather than sewn, pinned at the shoulder with a bone pin. Her hair is covered. Her feet are bare on the stone, and the soles are thick as leather. Beside her in the doorway is a basket, and a small flat plate of schist leans against the doorframe at an angle. You would take it for a stray stone. It is not. It is a sentence.

## How many

There are never more than about forty of them, and never fewer than about thirty. They know the exact number at every moment, the way you might know the exact number of coins in your hand. The number is scratched on the capstone of each cistern, a row of short strokes, and the strokes are rubbed out and recut whenever someone is born or someone goes up through the upper gate. They call this number *the measure*, and the measure is not a count of people. It is a count of mouths. The goats are in it too.

The measure is set by the water. Everyone understands this without being taught, the way children understand that fire is hot. So many air wells, so many fog mornings in an ordinary year, so many jars, so many mouths. When the measure is full, no one decides that there will be no more children, but there are fewer. When the measure is low, there are more. It is one of the things that happens in the town without anyone saying it, like the difference between the lower town and the upper.

Forty people, in a town built for many hundreds. That is the first strangeness. The second is that they do not experience it as emptiness.

## Why the town is so large

A stranger's first guess is that the town was once full and the people dwindled. This is wrong, or at least nobody here believes it. As far as anyone knows, the measure has always been about what it is now.

The town is large because the people move through it.

No one here lives in one house for a lifetime. A room is lived in for a few years, sometimes a season, sometimes a decade. Then its doorway is stacked closed with loose plates, and its occupant moves on, opening another room somewhere else. They take their bedding, their jars, their loom-weights. Often they take some of the stones as well. The new room will need a course repaired, or a lintel reset, and the old room gives up what it can spare.

They say a room left closed too long goes *cold*. They don't mean the temperature, although that's true too. They mean the stones forget how to sit together. Damp gets into the joints. Frost gets a finger in. A room that nobody lives in, that nobody taps and listens to, slowly loosens. So the living circulate. Over a lifetime, a person will live in dozens of rooms across the town, and in every room they live in, they repair something. In this way forty people keep a town for hundreds warm. They are its blood, moving slowly through a very large body.

This is why the lanes are so often empty. The people are spread out on purpose. Each of them holds a portion of the town the way a person holds one end of a long rope, and if they bunched together, the rope would fall slack.

## Low and high

The difference between the lower town and the upper town, which no one says, is this: you are born low, and you die high.

The children live in the lower town, near the goat pen and the lower gate, where the slopes are gentlest and there is grass. That's where the young are most useful: herding, carrying fuel up from the heather slopes, learning to split plates in the easy courtyards where a mistake costs little. As a person grows older they move upward, room by room, year by year, never all at once. Somewhere in the middle of life, a person lives in the middle of the town. The woman in the doorway is there now. The stones around her are neither soft nor sharp. Middling.

The old live in the upper town, near the upper gate, where the choughs land most often and the wind is strongest and the view, when the fog is gone, falls away on both sides into blue distance. The rooms up there are the smallest and the tightest built, because the old have had the longest to learn how.

Nobody forces the climb. Nobody announces it. A person just finds, some season, that it's time to close the door and open another one a little higher. The whole population is always slowly rising through the town, like water in a wick. The lower rooms are opened again by the next children, and so the town turns over, bottom to top, generation after generation.

## Names

A child has no name for a long time. Until then they are called by the room they were born in: *the one of the door with the split lintel*, *the one of the well-square room*. Nobody minds. They're small, and there are few of them, and everyone knows who is meant.

A child is named on the day they lay their first course in a real wall, one that will bear load and shed water and stay. This usually happens somewhere between their seventh and tenth year. Before laying it, they walk the town, sometimes for days, looking for a stone. It can be any stone, in any wall, of any age, but it has to be one they can take out without bringing the wall down, and one they can put back somewhere better. When they find it, they take it, and that stone becomes their name.

Their name isn't a word for the stone. It is the stone. When people want to speak of them, they speak a description of it, shortened over time into a sound: *the grey-with-a-vein*, *the one that rings*, *the one with orange on its lip*. The person keeps that stone all their life. It goes into the wall of every room they live in, always near the door. When they move, it moves. You can find a person by finding their stone.

Their stone is always set with its best face outward, and that is how the town is also a register of its living. Walk the lanes knowing what to look for and you can see who lives where. The name-stones are warm to the touch, even on cold mornings. They've been handled a great deal.

## The sentence at the door

Now, why the woman didn't speak.

The town carries sound. The lanes are narrow, the walls are hard, and the air is thin and still. A word said at ordinary volume in the upper town can be understood, faintly, three squares away. Shouting reaches the goat pen. There is almost no such thing as a private conversation.

So they speak very little, and when they do speak aloud they speak softly, and what they say aloud is understood to be public. It is said to the whole town, because the whole town may hear it. It isn't rude to overhear. It is impossible not to.

Private things are said with stones.

The plate leaning against the woman's doorframe says something. One plate leaning at that angle means *I am here and you may come in*. A plate laid flat across the threshold means *I am here but not to be disturbed*. Two plates crossed means *I have gone out and will return*. A small white stone, of a kind found only in one seam on the slope below the lower gate, set on a threshold means *someone in here is unwell*. Seeing that, people will leave water. A chip of juniper wood means *I have something to give you*. There are dozens of these. There are combinations. There are, if you watch long enough, arrangements that say things no one would ever say aloud: *I am sorry*. *I was wrong about the lintel*. *I have been thinking of you*.

Children learn to read thresholds before they learn to read plates. An old woman in the upper town has not said a word aloud in eleven years, and everyone knows exactly what she thinks.

## Fog

The fog changes everything.

When the cloud comes over the ridge, pouring silent and white into the saddle, it swallows sound. A voice in the fog goes nowhere. It falls dead a few paces away. For the space of a fog morning the town becomes the one thing it otherwise never is: a place where you can talk.

So when the fog comes, they go out.

This is the most surprising thing about them, and a stranger rarely sees it, because a stranger is inside the fog too and can see only an arm's length ahead. But on fog mornings the lanes fill. People who have nodded to each other for weeks sit shoulder to shoulder on the stair treads and talk, low and fast, in the soft wet air. Disputes are settled in fog. Stories are told in fog. Plans for the next wall, the next season, the next child: all made in fog, all spoken in voices that won't carry.

The courting happens in fog. Children are made, mostly, on fog mornings, in whatever room is nearest, with the stone sweating around them. Fog is the season of the body and of the voice. When it lifts and the town comes back grey shape by grey shape, everyone goes quiet again, and returns to their margins, and nods.

They have a word that means both *fog* and *nearness*. They also use it for the feeling of being with someone you love in a room where no one else can hear.

## Rain

If fog is for speaking, rain is for singing.

Rain comes rarely, a few times a year, and it lasts twenty minutes, and when it comes the whole town rings. Every channel runs at once, every roof spouts, every stair tread trickles, and the noise of water is everywhere: in the lanes, in the walls, under the squares where the cisterns fill. For those twenty minutes, sound doesn't carry because it is drowned.

So in the rain, they sing. Loudly, all of them, wherever they are, without meaning or words, just voice thrown out into the water noise, as loud as they have ever been and as loud as they will ever be. No one can hear anyone else. Everyone knows everyone is doing it. Children are taught, before they are named, that when the rain comes they must open their mouths.

When the rain stops, the singing stops in the same breath. Then there is the dripping, and the settling, and the quiet, and people come out into the lanes and look at each other, a little flushed, and nod.

## The air wells

Every person builds one air well in their life. It is the most important thing they make.

They build it late, usually in the upper-town years, choosing a spot on the windward side where the fog comes thickest. It takes a season or more. An air well is harder to build than it looks. It seems loose and careless, stones stacked with gaps, but every gap is placed. The wind has to move through at the right speed, the stones have to be the right thickness to hold the night's cold, and the floor has to slope just so. A badly made air well gives nothing. A well-made one gives a jar or two on a good morning, for longer than anyone can say.

The builder puts their name-stone into their air well when it's finished. It's the last room their name will live in.

Then they go on living, and drinking from the cisterns, and when they die their air well goes on giving water. That's the part that matters. The dozens of air wells on the windward side of the town are the dozens and dozens of people who have lived there. The oldest, the ones furred green inside with the only thick moss in the town, belong to people so long gone that their names have worn off into sounds that no longer describe any stone anyone can find. They still give water. Everyone in the town drinks from them every day.

When a child asks, as children do, where the water comes from, they are told the true answer. It comes from the fog, and from the stones, and from everyone who came before. All three are the same answer.

This is also why the measure holds. The dead keep the living alive, in exact proportion. A person who has built a good air well has, in a sense, made room for someone not yet born.

## The upper gate

When a person dies, their name-stone is already in their air well. Their body is carried up through the upper gate by four of the living, in fog if fog can be waited for. Beyond the gate, a stair climbs toward the eastern summit, and at its top there is a broad flat shelf of stone, grooved and weathered, where the wind never stops.

The body is left there, and the choughs come.

This isn't grim to them. The choughs ride the updraft over the saddle every day. Their calls are the sharpest sound the town hears, and they land on the rooftops and pick at lichen and crumbs and live their own quick black lives among the stone. That they should also take the dead up into the wind seems only right. When the bones are clean, which on that shelf doesn't take long, they are gathered and dried, then pounded fine and worked into the soil of the few stone-lined beds where the bitter greens and the hardy grain grow. That soil was carried up one basket at a time, generations ago. It has been fed a little every generation since.

The room the dead person last lived in is closed with loose plates, like any other. When someone opens it, perhaps years later, they will reuse its stones, mend its courses, set their own name-stone by the door, and live there a while.

The old woman who hasn't spoken in eleven years was once asked by a child, in fog, whether the dead were gone. She said they were not gone. She said they were *not currently needed*. She used the same words the town uses for a closed room.

The child thought about this for a long time. Most of them do.

## Stone

Everyone lays stone. Everyone reads plates. It is the craft beneath all the others, the way breathing is beneath speech.

Watch someone dressing a plate. They don't strike it hard. They tap, and listen, and turn it, and tap again. The mallet is light, the copper chisel soft enough that it must be rehammered every few days. Most of the work is listening. A plate will tell you where it wants to split if you ask it patiently. It will tell you which face goes outward and how it will shed water. The people say you don't shape a stone. You find the shape it has already agreed to.

They speak of stones the way other people speak of animals: as having temperaments. This one is stubborn, this one is easy, this one wants to lie flat and will crack if you stand it on edge. A lintel is a patient stone. A capstone is a proud one. The very oldest stones in the lowest courses, the ones softened and rounded until they look like river stones, are spoken of as elders. They're treated with deference and rarely moved. When a wall has to come down to its footing, those stones are lifted with two hands, one on each side, the way you'd lift a sleeping child.

Because the town is its own quarry, every stone in it has been handled before, many times, by people now in the air wells and the grain beds. A plate a young man is setting into a wall today may have been set into a dozen walls before, by a dozen people whose hands learned the same angles his hands are learning now. They know this. It isn't sentimental to them. It's just true, the way it's true that the water comes from the dead.
