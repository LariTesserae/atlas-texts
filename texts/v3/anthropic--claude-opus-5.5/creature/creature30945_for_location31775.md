# Who Lives in the Hollow of the Unripe Root

## First, the Small Ones

Start with what you can almost hold.

The moth-things are called nothing, because no one who has seen them has stayed long enough to agree on a name. If they need one, call them *listeners*, because that is what they do, and it is nearly all they do.

A listener is the color of a paper lantern after the candle has gone out. Its body is a husk: two dry halves pressed together along a seam, like a seed case that has split and closed again. The wings are thin enough that the wall-light passes through them and takes on the grain of the tissue, fine lines like the veins in a leaf, or the lines on a palm. They have no mouth, no eyes anyone has found, and no legs to speak of. Instead they have a fringe of stiff hairs along the underside of the husk, the same bright bristling as the fibers at the entrance seam. With these they cling to the membrane, always near a great vein and always facing upward, toward the place overhead where the veins converge.

When the sap surges, they tremble. It is not a flutter. It is a resonance. The whole paper body takes up the roar and shakes with it, the way a drumskin shakes when another drum is struck nearby. If you put your fingertip near one during a surge, you can feel the air humming against your skin, a small, concentrated version of the sound that fills your ribs. They eat the roar. Or they are the roar's way of being felt somewhere smaller than the whole hollow.

Here is the stranger thing, and it comes slowly.

There are not a dozen of them. There may be three, or two. There may be only one. A listener does not move through space the way you do. It moves through the hollow's time, and the hollow's time is loose, full of days replayed out of order and cracks heard before they happen. So when you see a listener on the eastern vein and then on the western vein, it has not flown. You have seen it now and you have seen it then, and the hollow has let both moments stand in the air at once, the way it lets a sound stand before its cause.

This is why they cluster where the cracks will be. They are always found near a place where the wall is about to split. Watch one long enough and you will see the membrane beside it pull, creak, and part with a green report. The listener will not flinch, because it heard that crack before anything else did. It has been listening to it, perhaps, for a very long time.

They do not breed in any way you would recognize. A new listener simply appears one day where the echo is thickest, already dry, already old-looking, as if it were a sound that had settled out of the air like dust out of still water. When one fades (they do fade, going paler and thinner until the wall-light shines straight through), no body is left. There is only a small silence where it clung, a patch of wall slightly quieter than the rest, until the roar fills it again.

They are the first residents, but they are not really creatures. They are the hollow's ears.

## Then, the House Itself

You cannot describe who lives in the hollow without describing the hollow as someone.

The plant is not a building with tenants. It is a body that has made a room of its own interior, and it is aware of that room the way you are aware of the inside of your mouth: constantly, without attention, until something foreign touches it. When you squeeze through the seam, it knows. The light does not change, but the roar hesitates, a half-beat of held breath in every vein at once, then resumes.

It is not hostile. That is worth saying plainly. Nothing in the hollow wishes you harm, though the starch crusts will cut you and the echoes will unsettle you. The plant's temperament, as far as it has one, is *patient* and *hungry* and *shy* at once. It drinks without ceasing. It grows in sudden cracking lurches, like a child in the night. It hoards light, not to spend but to keep, and it takes those kept days out and looks at them, over and over, the way an old person turns over photographs. Except that the plant is not old. It is weeks old, and some of the days it replays are older than the hill.

If it has a mind, the mind lives overhead, where the veins converge. From the floor this place looks like the ceiling of a dome, a knot of dark vascular tissue where every rib and thread in the walls runs together, green-black and thick as a wrist, then thick as a thigh, then too thick to judge. The rain begins there. The weather of the light seems to begin there. The listeners all face toward it. If you stand directly beneath it and hold very still, the roar resolves, faintly, into something almost like breathing, and the replayed days seem to come not from the walls but from above, as though that knot is where the plant remembers.

The plant is the host. It is also, in a way that becomes clearer the longer you look, only the shell of the real resident. It is a womb that has not yet been told what it is carrying.

## The One on the Far Curve

The footfalls come rarely, and when they do, the whole hollow changes posture.

The listeners go still first. They do not stop trembling so much as the trembling freezes: a dozen paper bodies caught mid-shiver like a photograph. Then the roar drops. The sap does not stop, but it quiets, the way a crowded room quiets, not in fear but in recognition. *Oh. It's you.*

Then the steps. They are heavy and slow, spaced like the tread of something that does not need to hurry. You hear them clearly, far more clearly than distance should allow, because they come from the far curve, where distance is least reliable. Each step lands with a soft, wet weight, like a foot pressing into ripe fruit, and the membrane floor under your own feet gives very slightly in answer, as if it is one skin stretched from here to there.

Nobody has gotten close. People have tried, walking toward the far curve for what felt like hours while the far curve stayed exactly as far. But people have seen it.

What they describe does not agree at first. One says it was tall and narrow, jointed like a stalk of cane. Another says it was broad and low and moved like a herd. Another says it was a figure carrying something, or several figures carrying one thing. The descriptions only begin to make sense together.

It is green-bronze, the color of the rind. That much everyone agrees on. It is heavy the way wet wood is heavy, dense with water. It moves with the unhurried ownership of someone walking through a house they have just been given the keys to, looking at the empty rooms, deciding where things will go. It stops sometimes. It seems to look at the walls, at the high knot of veins, at particular stretches of floor, as if measuring. Once, someone saw it stoop and press what might have been a hand flat against the membrane, and the whole hollow flushed briefly amber, a late-afternoon light in the middle of a noon.

And around its middle, some say, there is a cord. Three-ply. Tight. Knotted in a way no one recognizes.

Here is what the descriptions add up to, if you let them.

The walker is not one creature, and it is not many. It is many who have not yet come apart. Look at it the way you learned to look at the listeners, as a thing seen across moments rather than in one, and it is a crowd, a village, a whole people, still fused into a single slow body, the way the petals of a flower are fused in the bud before it opens. The stalk-like one and the herd-like one and the figures carrying something are the same being glimpsed from different distances, and distance here is also time. Up close and far off, now and later, it is one. Resolved further forward, into the hollow's filled future, it is a hundred.

This is what the plant is growing toward. It is not a seed and not fruit. It is a population. The hollow you could lose a village in is exactly the size of a village, because it is being held open for one. The walker is that village before it is born, pacing the rooms of its own future home. It keeps to the far curve not out of shyness but out of tact: up close it would have to be one thing or the other, and it is not ready.

When it passes, the roar drops like a room of people noticing someone at the door. That is exactly right. The plant is noticing its owner.

## The Ones Who Leave Things

Now the traces make a different kind of sense.

The blade in the wall near the entrance is not a lost thing. It was *set*. It was pressed into the tissue on purpose by a hand that knew the wall would grow around it, and knew how long that would take in the kind of time that passes on the other side of the hollow's filling. It is a fiber-cutting tool. The people who will live here will live by cutting fiber: harvesting the walls in careful strips, twisting cord, weaving mats and roofs and slings from the very flesh of the house. The plant will let them. That is how it will age. The knife-edged flanges will be worn down not only by the slow rain but by hands.

The cord knotted on the thin rib is their knot, the one you do not know because it has not been invented yet. It is three-ply because they will make everything in threes, perhaps, or because it is strong. It is bright and unfrayed because, from where it was tied, it was tied only a moment ago.

The stones are the most deliberate thing in the hollow. There is no stone here. The plant grows none, and nothing in its body could make any. Someone carried these three stones down through clay, through the seam, through the loose time of the hollow, and stacked them beside the largest pool, flattest on top. It is a hearth-mark, perhaps, or a claim, or a greeting. It is the sort of thing a person does when they arrive at a new place and want the place to know they mean to stay.

The pigment on the top stone is red-brown. It might be ochre. It might be the plant's own sap, which oxidizes that color in air. It might be blood. The smear is roughly the shape of a hand, pressed, not painted. It is about the size of yours.

So who are they, the ones who leave things?

They are the walker, come apart. They are individuals who have separated from that slow fused body and been born, in some later season, into the filled hollow. They live there with the light no longer replaying other days but settled into its own, the rain regular, the edges rounded, the pools permanent and sweet. And some of them, the curious ones, the ones who dream, have learned that the far curve runs backward. They walk it. They come back through the loose distance into the time when the house was still empty, and they leave small things for whoever comes first: a blade, a cord, three stones.

They are not warning anyone. They are preparing a welcome.

## The First

And this is where the answer turns toward you.

The hollow echoes your voice back in a slightly older register. It gives you words you were going to say. It gives you your own words spoken by someone else. You have been told this is the hollow being strange, and it is. But consider the other reading. The hollow is not inventing those voices. It is *remembering* them, the way it remembers light, except that the voices have not happened yet. They are the voices of the people who will live here, and some of them sound like you because one of them is you, or is descended from you, or is someone who stood where you are standing and thought what you are thinking.

A plant grown around a hollow instead of a seed has to get its seed from somewhere.

Perhaps that is what visitors are. The plant opened its seam, recently, bright and wet, barely wide enough to turn your shoulders through, because it was ready to be found. Whoever comes first carries something in: breath, warmth, footsteps on the membrane, a voice for the echo to learn from. Whoever comes first and then leaves carries something out: the hum in the hill, the roar under the ribs, a phrase they haven't said yet. Then, one day, they come back. Or their children do. Or the walker comes apart and some of what it comes apart into has the shape of their hands.

The handprint on the stone is the size of your hand. You don't have to believe it is yours. It is enough to notice that it is the right size for someone like you, someone who found a hill nobody names and pressed their ear to the ground.

## So: Who Lives There

For now, almost no one does, and that is the truest answer.

A few paper listeners live there, or one listener many times, feeding on the roar and sitting beside cracks that haven't happened. The plant lives there, being both the house and the dreaming of the house, hoarding light, drinking without end, making room. The one on the far curve lives there in the way an unborn child lives in a room that has been painted for it. It walks through, measures, touches a wall, and turns the light to evening.

And the people who will live there, live there. They live there in the future tense, which in this hollow is not so different from the present. They have already left their knife and their knot and their three stones. They have already put their voices into the echo. They are already, in the loose far curve of the place, standing beside the largest pool, warming their hands at a hearth that will be built on the spot where the stones now stand. They look up at the convergence of veins, which by then will be dark and old and softly beating, and they tell each other, in words you almost said yesterday, about the first one who came, when the house was still empty and too large and full of weather: the one who crawled in through the bright seam, listened, stayed quiet, and left.

They are grateful to that person. They are waiting.

The roar in the hill is the sound of them waiting.
