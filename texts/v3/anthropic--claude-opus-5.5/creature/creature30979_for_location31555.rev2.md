# Who Lives on the Sill

## The Crew

Everyone who works the stations will tell you that nobody lives on the Sill. It is the first thing they say, a little defensively, as if you had accused them of something. They are *posted* there. They rotate. Three weeks up, one week down, and the Company, what's left of it, a single office in the lowland town with a cashier and a filing cabinet, pays them for the up weeks and not the down.

There are between six and eleven of them at any time, depending on the season, the budget, and who has quit. They are recognizable in the lowland town by their hands, which are permanently pruned at the fingertips and white at the knuckles, and by their voices, which are too loud. On the Sill you speak over the falls or you do not speak at all, and they carry that pitch down the funicular with them and use it in shops and kitchens without noticing. They also stand oddly in the town, weight slightly back, feet a little apart, as if bracing against a current that is not there.

They wear wool in layers that never fully dry: an inner layer that is damp, an outer layer that is wet, and an oilskin over both that makes no difference in a Wall but keeps the wind off. Their matches live in tobacco tins sealed with candle wax. Their tobacco lives in the same tins, and so does their money, and their letters, and in one case a set of false teeth. Every one of them has a smell, a mix of lanolin, peat, rust and woodsmoke, that their families below complain about and eventually stop noticing.

They are not a talkative group, but they are constantly communicating. On the pavement they move in a spread line, each a few body-lengths from the next, and they signal with their hands the way divers do: *deep here*, *fast here*, *slot*, *stop*. Flat palm tilted means the sheet-flow is shifting. A fist dropped means a pothole under the surface. Two fingers pointed at the eyes and then at the horizon means *the Wall*, and when someone makes that sign everyone stops what they are doing and turns to look.

They keep flow books. This is the core of the job, more than the gates or the gauges. Every morning, before anyone wades out, the station keeper walks the nearest channels and writes down where the water is going: which slots are running, which are dry, which weirs are taking flow and which are being bypassed. The books go back ninety years. They are swollen to twice their original thickness, their pages rippled like the pavement, and many of the older ones are unreadable. The ink ran long ago into grey-blue clouds. The crew keep them anyway, in a tin trunk at the central station, and new hands are made to read them, or look at them, through their first winter. Nobody can explain what this is for. It is simply done.

## The Ones Who Stay

Some of them stop going down.

It happens gradually. Someone skips a down week because the gates at Number Two need watching during a wet spell. Then they skip another because the funicular is under repair. Then it has been a year. The Company does not formally allow this, but the Company is a cashier and a filing cabinet, and it sends the pay down anyway, into bank accounts that fill slowly and are never touched.

The ones who stay say the lowland is unbearable. It is dry, and it is *silent*: without the falls, the absence of sound presses on the ears like a hand. They cannot sleep in a bed that does not drum. Their skin cracks in two days. The flat stillness of lowland air, air that is not going anywhere, makes them feel they are suffocating.

At present there are three. Mirela Voss keeps the central station and the turbine, and has for nineteen years. Teo Arrieta, who works the gates at Number Two and Number Three, has been up twenty-six years and is thought to have gone down only twice in the last decade, both times for funerals. And there is a young man called Pim who came up four years ago to fill a gap for one season and has not left. The others do not talk about Pim yet. It is too early to know if he has stayed or if he is only late.

The stayers change. The first thing to go is the stance in the lowland, because they are no longer in the lowland. The next thing is the flow book. Mirela still writes hers every morning, but she writes it after she has already walked out onto the pavement and set the day's route, and the book is more of a record than a guide. Teo stopped writing his years ago. He says he doesn't need it. He says, and this is the part the rotating crew repeat to each other in the bunks with a mix of admiration and unease, that he doesn't read the water anymore. He *knows where it's going to be*.

Asked how, he says: *I watch the Turner.*

## The Turner

For a long time the rotating crew assumed the Turner was a joke. Every isolated workforce has its mascot, the thing that gets blamed when tools go missing, and the Sill has plenty of candidates. There is the small cat whose tracks turn up in the mud and whose body nobody has ever seen. There are the big yellow slugs that get into the flour. There are the eels, which are uncanny enough, sliding over the flooded pavement at dusk as thick as a man's wrist.

But the Turner isn't blamed for things, which is how you can tell it isn't a joke. It's *credited*.

When a slot that has been blocked for a month is found clear one morning, its choke of pebbles and moss lifted out and set neatly on the block beside it, the Turner did that. When the sheet-flow across the eastern basin, which by every rule of the joint-grid should drain north, spends three days draining west into a pothole field where there is no obvious reason for it to go, the Turner is behind it. When a stone the size of a bread loaf, which no flood could have lifted, turns up wedged precisely into the throat of a flute so that the flute's current splits in two, the crew say *Turner* and step around it. Nobody moves it.

The old flow books, the readable ones, are full of this. Entries in cramped Company handwriting note *slot 14 cleared (not by us)* and *stone placed at weir 3 flank, deliberate?* and, in one hand that shows up for about six years in the 1930s and then stops, a long series of sketches of stones arranged in low curving lines across the channels, each with the date and a note of which way the water turned. The last of those entries says only: *It is not random. It is not us. I have seen it.*

What was seen is something like this.

## What It Is

It is an animal, first. Whatever else the Sill has made it into, it is a body, with blood and appetite, and it can be killed.

It is a salamander, or it is what a salamander would become given a hundred times longer than salamanders have had and a place that has never once been dry. It is long, two meters or a little more. It is flat in the way the pavement is flat, broad-headed and low, with small eyes set high and far apart, and a mouth that runs almost the full width of the head. Its legs are short and splayed and absurdly strong, with wide spatulate toes, four in front and five behind, and the toes have grip pads so fine they will hold on wet quartzite in a current that would take a man off his feet. Its tail is half its length, flattened side to side like an eel's, and it uses the tail the way a rower uses an oar.

It has no lungs, or almost none. Its skin does its breathing, and the skin is enormous: loose, folded, hanging in pleated frills along its flanks and throat to multiply the surface. In the lowland air, even in the humid lowland forest, it would suffocate in an hour. On the Sill, where the air is so saturated that water beads out of it onto eyelashes, it can breathe in the open, and it walks from basin to basin across the drift-flooded pavement through the finger-deep sheet as easily as through air, because on the Sill the difference between water and air is only a matter of degree.

This is the first thing to understand about it, and the thing that made Mirela go quiet the first time she thought it through. The Turner can only exist in a place that has not been dry in ten million years. A single rainless month anywhere in its lineage would have ended it. It is a living record of that unbroken wet, the way the moss hummocks are a record of centuries. They are records written in water. The Turner is written in something longer.

Its color is the color of whatever it has been lying in. This isn't camouflage in any active sense. The skin is simply colonized. Algae grow in the folds of its flanks, green-gold in the light. Lichen crusts the ridge of its back. On the old ones, and they are very old, there is moss, actual cushion moss, rooted in the skin along the spine and the top of the skull, so that a Turner lying still in a channel looks like a long hummock, a low green ridge in the flow, and you would walk past it. People have walked past it. Teo has stood on one, briefly. He describes the feeling of a hummock shifting under his boot and then rising, heavily, the way the whole bog-raft rises in a storm, and walking out from under him.

Things live on it. The rotating crew don't always believe this part, but Mirela has seen it and so has Pim. There are frogs, the tiny black ones with orange bellies, the walkers, that ride in the folds of its throat and lay their eggs there in the permanent wet. There are snails. There are tiny crustaceans. There are the larvae of something that nobody has identified, living in the pits of its skin like pores. A Turner is a moving wetland. When it crosses the pavement, a small ecosystem crosses with it, and when it settles into a new basin for the season, the basin gets the frogs.

## What It Does

It eats eels.

That is the heart of it, and everything else follows from that. The eels climb the escarpment out of the warm sea, a kilometer and a half of wet cliff over weeks, and they arrive on the Sill thin and exhausted, and they spend decades in the channels growing fat and dark. The Turner eats them. It eats frogs too, and crabs, and the long forearm-flatworms, and ducklings that ride too close to it on the current. But the eels are what it lives for, the only thing on the plateau big enough and fat enough to feed something its size through the long cold.

Eels go where the water goes. They follow flow. So the Turner has learned to move water.

The stones it places are not random and they are not play. They're weirs, small ones, deflectors and splitters, built across the joint-channels to steer the sheet-flow toward the deep potholes where it lies waiting. It builds low curving lines of cobbles that turn a current a few degrees. It clears one slot and chokes another. A single well-placed stone at the head of a flute can decide which of two basins a whole square kilometer of pavement drains into, and the Turner seems to know exactly which stone, and where. It lifts them in its mouth. The jaw that wide is strong enough for a loaf-sized block of quartzite, carried a body's length and set down, and then it lies with its head against the new stone for a while, feeling the water change.

It is farming, in a way. It's also something older than farming, something closer to what the whole plateau does. The Sill is a machine for moving water, and the Turner has worked its way into the controls.

The crew have come to believe, and this belief has hardened over decades, the way the moss hummocks harden, from a joke into a fact that nobody questions, that a good part of the Sill's famous changeability is the Turners. Not all of it. The floods are floods and the Walls are Walls. But the way a stream that ran north all morning runs east by afternoon, the way a basin fills that has no business filling, the constant reshuffling that makes permanent paths impossible: some of that is the rain, and some of it is animals, patient and cold and very old, rearranging the plumbing of the plateau one stone at a time to bring the eels to them.

There are more than one. Nobody knows how many. Teo thinks eight or nine across the whole table, each with a territory of several square kilometers. Mirela thinks fewer. Pim, who has started keeping his own notes in a hand that looks, disturbingly, a great deal like the 1930s sketches, thinks the number is changing and that there were more once, and that the Company's weirs and penstocks took some of the best basins, and that the Turners are still, a century later, working around them.

## The Turner and the Gates

Here is the thing the stayers don't tell visitors, and the rotating crew only half believe.

The Turners have learned the penstocks.

When a sluice gate is opened on one of the three working penstocks, the flow on that part of the plateau changes dramatically. The weir draws hard, the channels feeding it speed up, and the whole local pattern of sheet-flow bends toward the penstock mouth. Eels are drawn along with it. And more than a few of them, the ones ready to go back down to the sea, are pulled into the iron throat and ride the pipe all the way to the powerhouse at the bottom, where they are shredded in the turbines, or survive and go out the tailrace and down to the sea, depending on luck.

The Turners noticed this. Of course they did; they notice every change in flow. And at some point, nobody knows when, the Turner whose territory includes Number Two began *lying in wait at the weir* whenever the gate was open, taking the eels as they funneled in. Then it began lying in wait *before* the gate was opened. It had learned the sound. The screech of the iron screw-wheel starting to turn, audible even over the falls, told it the flow was about to change.

And then, according to Teo, and nobody else has seen this, but nobody on the Sill calls Teo a liar, it began to lie *against the gate itself* in the days when the gate was closed. It lay with its broad head pressed to the iron in the current, and waited, and when Teo came out to work the wheel it would move aside for him and then settle back in exactly the same place.

Teo says he opens the gate at Number Two, sometimes, when the dispatch from the powerhouse hasn't asked for it. He says he does it when the Turner has been lying against it for a while. He says it seems only fair.

The powerhouse at the bottom logs these openings as operator error. There are a lot of them. Nobody has come up the funicular to ask.

## What It Knows

Pim has a theory, and he explains it badly, over the stove at the central station, while the rain hammers the iron roof and the others pretend not to listen.

His theory is that the Turners can read the Wall.

Their skin is their lung, and the Sill's air is their water, and a change in the air is a change in what they breathe. Before a Wall arrives, long before the crew see the grey face on the horizon, the Turners move. They leave the shallow basins. They go to the deep potholes, the ones that will hold still water even when the channels become torrents. They wedge themselves under the lips of the blocks. And the flow patterns they've spent weeks arranging, the careful little weirs, often get abandoned to the flood, which scatters the stones, and afterward the Turners start again.

So the Turners know when a storm is coming, and they know it early. And Teo, who says he watches the Turner, is not, Pim argues, doing anything mystical. He has simply spent twenty-six years learning to watch an animal that feels the weather in its skin, and now he moves when it moves, and he knows where the water will be because he knows where the Turner will want it to be.

The others nod at this. It sounds reasonable. It is reasonable. But Mirela, who has been listening, says something afterward that nobody writes in any book. She says she has been watching her own hands. She says the pruning on her fingertips no longer goes away even in the drier spells, and that the skin on the backs of her wrists has gone loose and soft in folds, and that she can tell when a Wall is coming now without looking. She feels it, she says, like thirst, a sudden pull toward high rock. She says she doesn't think it is from watching the Turner. She says she thinks it's from living in the same air.

## The Tenants

So this is who lives on the Sill.

There are the frogs, loud and brief, living in cups of rain that last a season or puddles that last an hour. There are the swifts, flying through solid water to ledges nobody else can reach. There are the eels, climbing a kilometer and a half of cliff for a few decades in the channels, and then going back down the falls. There's the cat, whom nobody sees. There are the moss hummocks, each centuries old and holding its weight in water.

There are the crew, posted, rotating, braced against a current in the lowland that isn't there, keeping books they can't read because the keeping is what matters.

There are the ones who stay: a woman with loose folds at her wrists who can feel the weather coming; an old man who opens iron gates for an animal because it seems only fair; and a young man who is starting to draw stones in curving lines across the channels, with the date and the direction the water turned.

And there are the Turners, the true tenants, the ones who were here before the Company and will be here after the last funicular cable rusts through and drops the car into the mist. They are long and flat and green-backed, mossed along the spine, carrying frogs in their throats. They lie against the stones in the dark basins with their huge thin skins breathing the cloud, feeling the water shift, and they reach out, patiently and with great strength, to move one stone a body's length to the left.

In the morning the stream that ran north is running east.

Nobody on the Sill says this is the rain anymore. They read the flow the way sailors read wind, and they know, the way sailors know, that the wind has no mind. But the water here does. It has several, very old ones. They're cold and slow and hungry, and they have been rearranging this oldest of floors since before there were people to walk on it. The water decides, the crew say. And under the water, in the deep potholes, something decides where the water goes.

Before dawn, in drift mode, when the plateau is as quiet as it ever gets, with only the falls, the frogs and the drip of everything, if you stand very still on a high block near one of the deep basins, you can sometimes hear it. Stone grinds softly against stone, somewhere below the surface. It isn't the current. The current doesn't move that slowly, or that carefully, or stop and start again as if listening.

Mirela hears it most mornings now. She doesn't write it down. She finishes her coffee, pulls on her wet wool, and walks out onto the pavement, and she goes where she already knows the water will be.
