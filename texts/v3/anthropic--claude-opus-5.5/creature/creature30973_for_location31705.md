# Who Lives in the Held Summit

## The First Answer

The first answer is the obvious one, and it is not wrong. The frogs live there. The bell-note frogs and the ratchet frogs and the one that hums through the ground. The moss-furred browsers live there, and the booming casque-birds, and the blind white fish turning in the dark of the drip-wells. The glide-birds live there. By some accounts a single one of them may circle the same thermal above the same black tower for two hundred years.

But look at what they have in common. None of them can leave. A bird that crosses the rim drops like a stone. A frog carried out in a climber's pack is dead before the pack is set down. Everything alive in the Hold has lived inside a closed hand for longer than its lineage can remember, and it has shaped itself to the closing. The birds turn before the edge without deciding to. The trees stop at the ceiling. The aroids follow a brightness that is only water on the underside of a lid.

So the first true thing to say about whoever lives in the Held Summit is that they are kept. Whatever else they are, they are kept.

The second true thing is that someone keeps the rope bridge.

## Those Who Stay

They are hard to see. The traces are what the descriptions list: the old huts with trees growing through them, the platform, the ticking thing, the cairns at the Whistles. Travelers who have walked the terraces for days report no one. Then they come back to a spot and find that a fallen branch has been moved off the path. Or they find a bromeliad cup drained and refilled with fresh water, a smooth pebble set in its center. Or they cross the gorge bridge and notice that the knots are new.

They live in the lower world, under the flat interlocking crowns, in the dark second story the canopy makes. Very little light gets down there. What does is green and pearl and never changes with the hour, because the ceiling above the ceiling diffuses everything. It is always a kind of afternoon. They live in the fig-cathedrals, the hollow lattices where the old strangler figs have eaten their host trees and each other and left only the pattern of the eating. The root walls are full of hollows. The hollows are dry, or as dry as anything gets here, and they are warm, because the roots go down into the fevered stone.

They are people. It is worth being clear about that before saying anything stranger. They are human the way the glide-birds are birds. They come from something ordinary and have been kept a long time.

They are small and light-boned, with wide flat feet whose toes spread and grip wet bark. Their skin is always damp. It has the faint sheen of the black towers, and it wrinkles at the fingertips the way anyone's does after a long bath, except that for them the bath has never ended, so the wrinkling is simply how their hands are, finely creased, like the travertine rims. Their eyes are large, and they are dark-adapted to the green gloom under the canopy. When they look up through a gap toward the bright pearl of the Lid, they squint like someone looking into snow.

They move slowly. This is the first thing any observer notices, on the rare occasions anyone observes them. Nothing in the Hold rushes. There is no wind to fight, no winter to prepare for, no storm to run from. The rain falls in the same places it fell last year and a hundred years before. A person who lives there learns the drip-lines the way you learn the stairs of your own house in the dark. They step around the silver threads without looking. They have the gliding stillness of the great birds. Their stillness is not lazy. It is exact: nothing is spent that does not have to be spent.

They cannot leave, any more than the birds can. A child born in that thick hot air, taken over the line, would be struck at once by the full height of the mountain, with no weeks of slow climbing to prepare the blood. It would last minutes. Perhaps a little longer, held against a chest, breathed into. Not long.

They know this. It is the first thing they know.

## Speech in the Troughs

The chorus is too loud for ordinary speech under the canopy. So they do not speak in the ordinary way.

They speak in the troughs.

The frog-calling moves across the basin in waves. A surge begins on the far side and rolls toward you over minutes, and it peaks, and then it passes. Behind each crest there is a lull, never silence but a lowering, a space between waves. That is when the people talk. A conversation among them is a strange, patient thing. Two of them sit together in a root-hollow, and one says a few words, soft and quick, and then the wave arrives and they wait. They wait through the whole crest, looking at each other or at nothing, hands resting on their knees. When the next trough comes, the other answers.

Because of this, nothing they say is hurried, and nothing is said that could not survive a four-minute wait. Their sentences are short and heavy, like the rain. They have no small talk. There would be no point, when every remark has to be carried across the peak of a wave.

When they need to speak through the crest, they don't use voices. They touch. There is a language of pressure on the forearm, the back of the hand, the side of the neck. It is tapped and drawn and held on the skin, and it is fluent and very old. Two of them walking under the canopy through the loudest part of the chorus will go hand in hand. From a distance it looks like tenderness. It is. It is also conversation.

They sing, too, but not against the frogs. They sing with the Whistles. Each of the leaking points around the rim holds its note forever, and the people have tuned themselves to those notes. They know which Whistle they are nearest by the pitch beneath everything, the way a sailor knows a coast by its lights. Their songs are built on those tones: a voice that finds the Whistle's pitch and rises off it, then returns to it. A lullaby in the northeast of the basin is in a different key than a lullaby in the south, because the Whistle there is different. You could map the Hold by its cradle-songs.

The deepest Whistle, the one felt as pressure in the chest, has no song. They do not sing near it. They go there to be quiet.

## The Edge

For the people of the Hold there is only one silence in the world, and it is outside.

Everything inside is sound: rain, frogs, insects, the boom of the casque-birds, the high dry static of the bats at dusk, the hum of the Anchors underneath it all. A child is born into that noise and sleeps inside it and will die inside it. The noise is not something they hear. It is the medium they live in, the way the thick wet air is.

Except at the rim, where it stops as if cut. Put your face through the line and you are in the other world. You feel the thin cold air. You hear your own blood.

They go there. Not often. The rim is dangerous, and the jungle at the edge leans inward and looks afraid, and the people feel that fear too. But at certain points in a life they walk up through the stunted trees and stand at the boundary. Sometimes they lean forward until the whole of the head is out. They hold their breath, because the air there is useless. Then, for as long as the lungs allow, they listen to the thing no one inside can hear, which is nothing.

A child does it once, when it is old enough to understand what it is doing. An elder takes it up to the edge by the hand and talks to it in the troughs on the way, and at the line the elder lets go. The child leans out alone. It comes back gasping, eyes streaming from the cold, and something in its face has changed. It has heard the size of the outside. It has heard that the world does not want it. Everyone in the Hold has done this. It is how they know they are kept.

Lovers do it together, heads side by side through the boundary, silent, until one of them has to pull back. The mourning do it. So do the very old, near the end.

There is no word for it that translates. The closest would be something like *listening to the not-us.*

## The Dead

The terraces spill toward the rim, and in several places streams run off the edge, out through the boundary. The water boils and freezes at once and runs down the outer cliffs as ice. It has been doing this for longer than anyone knows, and the result is the Curtains: organ-pipes and fringes of ice hundreds of meters tall, hanging below the rim like frozen smoke.

The people give their dead to the outflows.

The body is carried up to one of the streams where it runs to the edge. It is laid in the water, and the water is warm, as it always is. The mourners walk with it, hands on it, until the current carries it the last few meters and through. They do not follow it. They cannot. They watch it go out into the silence, into the violet-edged sunlight that none of them has ever stood in, and they watch the frost take it at once. The Curtains take it in.

From inside the Hold, looking out through the boundary, you can see the Curtains hanging down the cliffs, and if you know where to look you can see the shapes. They are not clear. The ice is rimed and fractured and full of old frost, and the dead are only darker places in it, blurs, the way a fish is a blur under ice. But the people know which Curtain holds whom. The newest dead are near the top, just below the lip, where the ice is white. The older ones have been carried down over centuries by the slow flow of the ice, and the oldest are far below, in the ancient blue-black ice near the glaciers, where no eye from the rim can make them out.

So the ancestors are always visible and always out of reach. They hang in the outside, in the silence, in the place where the living cannot go, a few centimeters farther down every year. The Curtains are the people's genealogy. When a child asks where its great-grandmother is, someone takes it to the rim and points.

The cairns at the Whistles are for the dead, too, though not the way a grave is. The Whistles are the places where the Hold loses a little of itself, where the warm breath of the basin escapes forever into the sky and turns to a banner of glitter drifting off over the empty peaks. The people believe, or say they believe, or sing as if they believe, that the Whistles are how the inside speaks to the outside. So they bring things there. They bring shells from the terrace pools, dried white sedge-flowers, bits of colored glass that must have come, long ago, from somewhere else. They stack stones carefully so the escaping air passes over them. They are sending something out along the current. They are sending it to the ones in the ice.

And once, a child's shoe.

## The Shoe

The people of the Hold do not wear shoes. Their feet are wide and gripping and always wet, and a shoe would be a stupidity in that country.

So the shoe came from outside.

No story about it survives, only the object, set carefully into the top of a cairn at the deepest Whistle, the one no one sings near. It is small and made of leather, stitched in a way that belongs to the dry foothills far below. Someone carried it up the whole mountain, across the burning ascent, across the line. Or someone inside was given it and kept it until they understood what it was for.

Here is one way it could have happened, and it may be the true one. Sometimes, not often, over very long spans of time, someone from outside climbs up and crosses in and stays a while. Sometimes they are not alone when they leave. Sometimes they cannot leave with everything they came to love, because what they came to love was born inside, and to carry it out would kill it.

There is a way of being a parent that means walking away across the line and down the mountain and never coming back, so that a child can go on breathing. There is a way of being a child that means growing up inside a closed hand with a single small shoe from a world you will never stand in, and one day climbing to the place where the Hold breathes out, and giving the shoe to the wind that goes where you cannot.

The shoe has been there a long time. The leather has gone green.

## The Counting Thing

Deep in the fig-cathedrals, grown into the roots of a strangler fig and lifted a meter off the ground by the tree's slow swelling, is the instrument: pale metal and crystal, the size of a head. It ticks once every few minutes.

The people know it. They do not touch it. They have a name for it that means something like *the one still counting*, or perhaps *the one that has not stopped keeping track.*

They sit near it sometimes, in the troughs, and listen for the tick. It falls at different places in the frog-waves, sometimes in a lull and sometimes buried in a crest, and there are old people who have spent whole seasons learning its rhythm against the chorus. The pattern of where the ticks fall is said to mean something. It is said to drift very slowly over the generations, and to come back into alignment, and to drift again, and that the alignment and the flowering of the ceilingwood are the same event seen from two sides.

They do not know what it is counting. They are fairly sure it is not counting for them. They are fairly sure that whoever set it there went away, and that it is the last one still doing what they asked.

They feel something like tenderness toward it. It is kept, like them.

## The Hundred and Nine Years

The ceilingwood flowers every hundred and nine years, and when it does the whole basin smells of cinnamon and wet iron.

For the people of the Hold, this is the unit a life is measured by. A person is lucky to see one flowering. A person who sees two is so old they have become a kind of landmark, carried to the rim when they die with great ceremony, placed in the Curtain nearest the deepest Whistle. Children are sorted not by year but by flowering: born before the last one, or after it. Most people's lives fall entirely between two flowerings, and they know the ceilingwood as a story, a set of songs, a smell remembered by the very old and described to the young in the troughs of the chorus. *Like bark and blood,* they say. *Like something sweet that has been hurt.*
