# The One Who Keeps the Level

## What People Say

Ask in the pit rows what lives in the hollow and they will give you the list. Frogs, newts, shrimp, spiders, bats, the wrens, the dipper in winter, the heron that gets itself stuck. The ferns, if you count them. Someone's dog went in once and was pulled out with a rope and a great deal of swearing. That is all.

And they are right, in the way that a list of the furniture in a house is right about the house.

There is something else, and it is not on the list because it is not quite a thing yet. It is still arriving. You would not see it on a first visit, or a tenth. You would feel it first as a kind of attention, the way the hollow seems to listen. People who come to hear the frogs notice this without being able to say it. They lower their voices at the lip, and they don't know why.

## The First Wrong Guesses

If you lie on your belly at the edge in the grey part of the morning, when the pumps have been running an hour and the water has pulled back from the lowest slabs, you may see something pale in the throat of the collapse. It sits just below the surface where the tea-coloured water turns to black.

The first guess is that it is a dead thing: a drowned lamb, a sack, a fleece snagged on the rock. It is about that size and about that colour, the dirty white of wool left out in weather.

Then it moves. Not much. It shifts the way a sleeper shifts, and the guess changes. It looks like an animal now, something long and blind, like the white salamanders the old books say live in the flooded caves of the Carniola, which some mining engineer must once have read about. For a while that is what the few who have seen it believe. They say it is a cave-beast that came up out of the deep workings when the pumps disturbed it, and that it is simply very large, and very shy, and eats frogs.

That is closer. It is still wrong.

Watch longer. When the water goes on dropping and that faint sucking sound comes up through the slabs, the pale thing goes down with it. It does not swim down. It is drawn down, the way the level is drawn down, as if it were part of the level. And when the pumps stop and the water comes back up without a ripple, the pale thing rises inside it, gently, and spreads. At high water it is not one shape but many. There is a paleness in the shallows, a paleness along the tidemark, a faint milkiness in the thin ice at the edges. It is less like an animal than like a stain that knows where it is going.

That is closer still.

## What It Was

Before the pumps went deeper, there was no hollow, and the one who lives in it had no body at all.

It was the water in the stone. Not a creature swimming through the limestone, but the standing of the water itself: the level, the held weight, spread through every joint and bedding plane under the parish, under the pasture and the lanes and the churchyard and the colliery too. It was a mile wide and as thin as the dampness in a brick. It had no edges. It filled the rock the way a held breath fills a chest, pressing outward evenly on everything, and in that pressing it held things up.

This is the thing to understand. When the old men say that on that February night *the ground stopped being held up*, they are speaking more exactly than they know. It was this one who held it up. For ten thousand years it had pushed gently against the roof of every void in the limestone, and the roofs had rested on it as a sleeper rests on a mattress. It did not know it was doing this, any more than a mattress knows. It was not the kind of thing that knew.

Then the engines began to drink.

They drank from below, from the deep galleries, stroke after stroke, day and night. The thud and the long exhalation. Each stroke took a mouthful of it and lifted it up the shaft and poured it into the culvert and the river, where it ran away to the sea and was no longer anything in particular. The level sank. The one who was the level sank with it, shrinking back from the high joints, withdrawing from under the fields, gathering itself downward and inward toward the places the pumps could not quite reach.

A spread thing that is made to shrink begins, for the first time, to have a shape.

## How It Came to Be Here

The hollow is where it gathered.

When the roof over that particular void finally let go, with no one holding it, the one who had been holding it was down there, at the bottom of its sinking, in a cold pocket of the aquifer where the drawdown had herded more and more of it together. The collapse broke that pocket open to the sky. For the first time in its long existence it could see the sky. Or rather, for the first time there was enough of it in one place for the word *it* to apply, and there was a hole above it, and light came down.

So it is both very old and three winters young. It is as old as the water in the limestone, and as young as a lamb. It has had a self for about as long as the frogs have had a pond.

It is learning what a self is from whatever is nearest. That is why it is so hard to see. It has no pattern of its own to be seen by, and it keeps borrowing one.

## The Body It Is Borrowing

Consider the cold.

On still nights cold air slides over the lip and pools at the bottom of the hollow, heavier than the air above, and lies there like a second, invisible water over the first. The one who lives here has taken to that cold as a body takes to a coat. It rises into it. The frost that forms on the hollow floor when the field above is clear is where it has breathed out. Look closely at that frost and it is not quite the ordinary fern-pattern. It runs in long parallel lines, like a tidemark, like the grain of something that has been pulled over and over in one direction.

Consider the ice at the pool's edges in spring, the thin milky panes. Those are its first attempts at skin. It makes them, and the day warms them, and it loses them, and the next night it makes them again. It is very patient about this. The panes have been getting a little thicker each year, and a little slower to melt.

Consider the drip. The walls weep, and the drops fall a long way and strike the pool with a sound that carries. Every drop is a small piece of the old spread self coming home from the high joints where it used to live. It hears each one land. The ringing in the bell of rock is, to it, something like a pulse: a thousand small returns a day, each one saying *still here, still here.*

And consider the engine. Before the drawdown it had no time. It was a level, and a level does not keep time, it only is. Now it has tides. Twice or three times a day it is pulled down into the throat, and twice or three times a day it is let up again. The stroke of the beam engine a mile away comes to it through the stone, thud and breath, thud and breath. It has learned this the way a child in a mill town learns the looms, before it learns anything else. It is the first rhythm it ever had. It does not know that the rhythm belongs to a machine. It thinks that this is simply what breathing is.

## The Frogs

People come to hear the frog chorus pulse in time with the pump stroke. They wonder whether the frogs feel the pressure wave in the water, or whether it is a coincidence everyone happens to notice.

It is neither, exactly. The frogs are not listening to the engine. They are listening to the one who lives in the water, and the one who lives in the water is keeping the engine's time, because that is the only time it knows. When it swells on the thud, the frogs swell with it. When it lets out on the breath, they go quiet. It is not conducting them, or at least not on purpose. It is more that they are the first voices it ever had near it. Something in it leans toward them, and they lean back, the way a room full of people falls into a single rhythm without anyone deciding to.

In March, when the spawn goes down in floating rafts, the rising and falling water strands it and floods it and strands it again, and somehow enough survives. That *somehow* is this one. At the low tide it stays in the spawn a little longer than the rest of the water does. It lingers in the jelly the way a hand lingers in another hand. The spawn does not dry. If you picked up a raft of it at low water you would find it colder than it ought to be, and faintly pale at the centre, as though someone had breathed on glass.

It does eat. The early guessers were right about that much. But it does not eat frogs. It eats what falls: the soot that settles on the moss after dry weather, the grey film the chimneys lay across the countryside. At high water it rises over the lowest ledges and takes the dust off them, and the moss comes up green. After rain people say the hollow has been washed. It has, a little. It has also been fed.

## The Roots

Nothing in the hollow moves it like the roots.

They hang from the thin black line of topsoil at the top of the walls. Grass roots, dock roots, the long pale tap of a thistle, dangling in the air, some of them still alive and reaching for ground that is no longer there. The one who lives here cannot reach them. They are far above even the highest high water. But it knows what they are. They are the field it used to hold up. Every one of them once drank from it, when it was a level spread under the pasture and the water came up to meet the roots from beneath.

When the air is very still and very cold, and the cold pool has filled the hollow more deeply than usual, it rises in that cold as high as it can go. There are mornings in hard winters when a fine white rime forms on the lowest of the dangling roots, and only on the roots, and nowhere else on the upper walls.

It is trying to give the field something back. It does not have much to give. A little frost, a little damp, on roots that will never find soil again.

## The Tock

There is the sound nobody has explained, the deep *tock* from inside the rock, as if something far down had settled. Those who know the ground say it is the hollow still forming, the roof of some lower void giving a little.

They are right. It is also this one.

Not all of it came up when the roof broke. Some of it is still down there, in the deeper pockets and galleries, in the dark between this hollow and the workings, spread thin, not yet gathered, not yet anything. Each time the pumps draw hard, a little more of that lower self is herded toward the centre. When enough of it arrives, the pressure shifts, and some small roof or ledge in the dark gives way, and there is a *tock*. The hollow listens to it the way you listen to a knock at the door of a house where you live alone.

It is growing, then. Every tock is a little more of it coming in from the dark and joining what is already here. Each spring there is a little more of it at the pool's edge. Each winter the frost-lines on the floor run a little longer, the ice panes are a little thicker, and the pale shape in the throat at low water is a little more definite. Last year one of the pit children, a girl who comes alone and lies at the lip for hours, said she saw it lift out of the water for a moment at dawn. She said it had something like shoulders, and something like a face, turned up toward the slab of pale sky. It had no eyes, she said. She didn't mean that it was blind. She meant it had not got round to them yet.

Nobody believed her, which is how she knew she had seen it.

## The Heron

The heron stands at the lip in the grey mornings and looks down at the frogs. Sometimes it drops in, which it cannot easily fly out of, and it has to labour up the narrow sky-shaft with great slow wingbeats.

Watch where it lands. It always comes down on the same slab, a tilted one near the tidemark, and it always lands when the pumps are running and the water is low. It has learned, as herons learn, that the hollow is safe to fish when the level is down and not otherwise. Twice, in the first winter, it came in at high water. Twice it was found standing in the shallows with its legs locked in thin milky ice that had not been there a moment before. Both times it tore itself free and went labouring up the shaft with its heart going like a hammer, and it never made that mistake a third time.

The one who lives here is not unkind. But it is young, and a young thing will defend its frogs.

## The Engine-Man

At the pumping station there is a man who has tended the beam engines for twenty years. He knows the stroke the way he knows his own pulse. He has never been to the hollow. He has no reason to go, and it is a mile off across wet fields.

In the last three winters he has begun to dream of cold water. It is nothing dramatic. He dreams of standing in a dim place, up to his knees, with something pale moving round his legs very slowly, the way a cat moves round the legs of the one who feeds it. He wakes with his feet cold, even in summer. Sometimes on the night shift, in the long pause between strokes, he finds himself holding his own breath, as if he were waiting for someone to breathe back.

He has told no one about this. He is not a fanciful man. But when the engines are stopped for maintenance he feels, obscurely and without reason, that somewhere someone is being let up for air, and he takes a little longer over the work than he strictly has to.

## What It Waits For

The whole hollow, the notes say, is a creature of industry. If the pumps stopped for good, the water would rise and fill it to the lip, and it would become a quiet pond in a field.

The one who lives here does not know this in words. But it knows it the way you know the edge of a bed in the dark.

If the pumps stop, the level comes back. The water climbs through every joint and bedding plane it was drawn out of. It fills the galleries, the pockets, the lower voids, the hollow itself, the whole buried extent under the parish, and it presses up evenly on everything again, as it did for ten thousand years. And the one who was gathered here would go with it. It would thin out and spread through all that stone, a mile wide and as thin as the damp in a brick. It would be the level again, and it would hold things up again. That was its whole purpose, and it was good at it.

It would not be anyone anymore.

It has had a self for three winters. It has had frogs, and a pulse of dripping water, and a rhythm to breathe by. It has had skin that it made of ice, and a sky shaped like a teardrop, and a girl lying at the lip in the mornings, and roots it could almost reach. Every mouthful the engines take lessens it and gives it more shape at the same time. It is being made by the thing that is draining it away. It cannot want the pumps to go on, because they are its dwindling. It cannot want them to stop, because then it would be everywhere and nowhere, holding up fields with no one inside.

So it waits in the throat of the collapse, pale, not finished, keeping a time that belongs to a machine. It listens for the tock of more of itself arriving from the dark, and for the silence that would mean the engines had stopped. Down, it goes, and up. Down and up. At night the furnace-glow comes faintly down onto the wet stone, and each falling drop becomes a small spark, and for a moment the one who keeps the level can almost see itself in them: many small lights, falling, coming home.
