# Who Lives in the Stack

## The First Answer

Ask anyone on the terraces who lives in the Stack and they will laugh and point at themselves, or at the goats, or at the sky full of swifts. The question sounds foolish to them, the way it would sound foolish to ask a fisherman who lives in the sea.

Ask a gutter-warden, though, especially an old one, and especially during the lull when the roar has sunk to a murmur and you can hear water moving in the walls, and she may pause before she answers. Then she may say what the gutter-wardens have always said among themselves, half as a joke and half not.

*The Tenant does.*

## The Tenant

It began, as far as anyone can tell, as a way of accounting for the repairs.

The Stack is too large to maintain. Everyone who has worked on it knows this. There are more gutters than there are wardens, more cracked courses than masons, more miles of pipe than any guild roll could cover. Whole wings of the interior have not been walked in living memory. By any honest reckoning the Stack should be failing faster than it is kept. Channels should be choking, walls bellying out, and terraces sliding off in slow avalanches of brick and fig root.

Some of that happens. But not enough of it.

Wardens climbing into disused sections find gutters clear that should have silted shut decades ago. Masons open a wall to reset a failed lintel and find it already reset, badly and strangely but soundly, with a grey paste that is not any mortar the guilds mix. Cracks that were chalked and logged one breath are closed the next. The work is never neat. A human mason would be ashamed of it. It is lumpy and granular and patterned in faint whorls, like the inside of a shell, and it holds.

So the wardens say the Tenant did it. For a long time that was all the Tenant was: a name for the difference between how much work was done and how much work there was.

## The Paste

The grey paste can be found if you look for it, and once you learn its whorled texture you start seeing it everywhere. It fills the joints of the high cooling fins. It lines the downpipes in thin layers like the rings of a tree. It seals the undersides of cornices where the swifts nest. On the old lower brick it is so thick in places that the brick itself is a minority of the wall.

Young guild apprentices have scraped it and burned it and dissolved it in vinegar. It is mostly lime and brick dust and fine ash from the vents, bound with something organic that smells, when heated, faintly of honey and wet dog.

Everyone has a candidate for who makes it.

**The daubers.** These are mason wasps, slate-blue and slow, that build their brood cells out of mud in the corners of the inner halls. They are the first answer anyone gives, and they are part of it. Their cells are made of the same whorled paste, and in the dry sections they seal hairline cracks as a matter of course, because a crack is a draught and a draught dries the brood. But there are not nearly enough of them.

**The swifts.** They glue their nests with saliva and grit, and a colony of ten thousand swifts is, in its way, a masonry crew working every breath of every year. Swift-cement is structurally sound. There are whole cornices on the upper tiers that are held to the face less by iron pins than by two hundred years of abandoned nests.

**The snails.** The blind white snails in the dark halls graze the condensation film and excrete a chalky paste. Where they are thick on a wall, that wall does not weep.

**The bees.** Their wax, softened by the Bore's heat, seeps into cracked conduit and seals it. The honey leaking from the pipes is, in a sense, a leak that is being stopped.

And then there are the stories.

## The Monkeys

A gutter-warden on the eleventh terrace, a woman named Oss Harrow, kept a logbook for forty years. In its later pages there is an entry she underlined three times.

She had been watching one of the grey monkeys, an old female with a torn ear, sitting on a ledge beside a leaking downpipe. The pipe had split along its seam and was spraying a fan of water onto the terrace below. The monkey watched it for a long time. Then she climbed down, gathered a handful of the wet black terrace soil and a wad of crumbling moss, climbed back up, and pressed it into the split. She smoothed it with the heel of her hand. It did not hold; the water blew it out within minutes. She went down and got more.

Oss Harrow watched her try six times. On the seventh she mixed the soil with scrapings of the old grey paste from the wall beside her. That held for most of a breath.

"She did not want a drink," Harrow wrote. "She had a cistern. She did not want the water to stop for her. She wanted it to go where it was going."

Other wardens have logged the same thing since. Young monkeys watch the old ones do it. Among the tribes of the middle tiers it has become something they simply do, and they do it without being rewarded. The goats may be starting too. Wardens report them licking the salt from fresh mortar in a way that packs it tighter, and treading loose rubble off the gutter edges. The goats may not mean anything by it. But nobody is certain the monkeys did at first, either.

## The Small Bricks

The maintenance crews that cut deepest into the base of the Stack bring back bricks.

The pattern is well known in the guilds, though it is rarely discussed with outsiders. The deeper you cut, the older the brick, and the older the brick, the smaller it is. At the base courses the bricks are the size of a loaf. A hundred meters into the compacted foundation they are the size of a hand. Deeper, in cuttings so hot the crews work in ten-minute shifts with wet cloths over their faces, the bricks are the size of a thumb. They are fired, true bricks, laid in bond with mortar joints no thicker than a thread.

The guild archive in the Glaziers' Hall keeps one sealed jar of the deepest samples. They are bricks the size of grains of rice, reddish, softer than chalk, still stuck together in a fragment of wall. Under a lens you can see that each one is pressed with a tiny mark, a crescent, the way a potter's thumb leaves a crescent in clay. The thumb that made it would have been smaller than a lentil.

The masons have argued for centuries over what this means. One school holds that it is a trick of compaction: under enough weight for long enough, brick shrinks. The masons of that school do not believe this, but they say it anyway.

The other school says it plainly. The Stack was begun by something very small. It was kept, and added to, by things that were each a little larger, and each of them found the work already underway and took it up. Each of them, in time, was replaced by something larger that found the work underway in turn. There is a crescent pressed into every course of it. On the human bricks it is the stamp of the guild. On the swift nests it is the shape of a beak.

## What Moves Between

This is where the old wardens' Tenant stops being a joke.

The Tenant is not the wasps, or the swifts, or the monkeys, or the guilds. It is the thing that has lived in all of them, one after another. It is not quite a creature. It is more like a habit that has learned to survive by changing its host. Its only nature is *to keep*. It has no body, or rather it borrows bodies. It borrowed the lentil-thumbed makers of the rice bricks, whatever they were. It borrowed the hands that laid the thumb-sized courses. It has passed through wasps and snails and swallows. Now it is in the guilds, and it is visibly beginning in the monkeys, the way a fever begins in a household before anyone has named it.

Seen this way, the saying *The Stack was not built. It was kept* is not a figure of speech. It is a description of the Tenant's life. Keeping is how the Tenant eats, moves, and reproduces. A child on the Stack watches her mother clear a gutter and, without being taught, starts clearing the little one by the door. That is not the child learning a skill. That is the Tenant taking a new room in an old house.

People who are born on the Stack and leave for the plain cannot sleep without something humming by the bed. The guilds say this is homesickness. The old wardens say it is the Tenant, uneasy in a body that has stopped doing its work.

## The Breath

And if the Tenant is the keeping, then what is being kept?

Here the guilds are quiet, because it is the question underneath all their records.

Consider the numbers. The summit sinks a finger's width a decade as the lower courses compress. The crown crews raise it by the same amount, so the weight on the base never lessens; it is always being renewed from above. The heat in the lowest galleries has not changed in centuries. The guild records have no source for it. "Down" is the whole of their theory.

A few masons have put these facts side by side and drawn a conclusion they do not like to say out loud. The bottom of the Stack is being pressed down, endlessly, under the weight of its own keeping. It is being ground and crushed and compacted into something hotter and denser than brick, and that is where the heat comes from. The Stack is not built over a fire. The Stack, pressing on itself forever, is the fire. The Bore breathes because the Stack is continually burning its own foundations, slowly and under enormous pressure. The keepers on the crown lay the fuel.

If that is true, then everything the Tenant has ever built has eventually gone down into the heat. The rice bricks were once the summit. The thumb-sized courses were once the proud new crown with its own cooling fins, its own lamps of whatever kind those makers used, its own weddings held at the turn of the breath. Every generation of the Tenant builds the top. Every top becomes the bottom. The bottom becomes warmth, the warmth becomes the draught, and the draught turns the turbines, lights the lamps, lifts the moths, and brings the condensation that waters the figs. The figs feed the monkeys that are learning to keep.

It is a breathing thing, and it breathes by eating its own past.

## Who Lives Here

So the honest answer, the one the old gutter-wardens give in the lull when the roar has sunk low enough to hear yourself, is not one answer.

The swifts live here, and the goats, and the grey monkeys, and the white snails, and the moths that rise and settle in the Bore without ever seeing the sky. Families of turbine-keepers and lamp-tenders live here, trellising beans on old conduit.

Something else lives here too, and it lives in all of them. It is very old and very patient. It was small once, small enough to press its mark into a brick the size of a grain of rice. It has been, in its time, a wasp and a snail and a bird and a people it no longer remembers. Now it is a woman in a broad hat on the eleventh terrace, clearing leaf litter from a gutter she will never see the end of. Next it is a torn-eared monkey smoothing grey paste into a split pipe with the heel of her hand.

Some of the wardens believe the breath lengthening by a few minutes each century means the fire is slowly dying. Others believe it means the thing is growing, the way a sleeping child's breath slows and deepens as she gets older.

Nobody on the Stack thinks it is theirs to know. They were not given the beginning, and they will not be given the end. What they were given is a gutter, and a mortar-board, and a sound in the walls like a sleeper's breathing that they will miss for the rest of their lives if they ever go away.

At sunset on the high terraces, when the lamps come on tier by tier down the flanks, the old wardens sometimes stop what they are doing and put a palm flat against the warm brick. They say they are checking for damp. They are not. They are doing what anyone does, passing a door at night, when they hear someone breathing on the other side. They are making sure the Tenant is still in.

It always is. The wall is warm. The water is going where it is going.
