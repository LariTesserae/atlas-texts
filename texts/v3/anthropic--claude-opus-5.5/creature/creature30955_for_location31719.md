# Who Lives in the Flue

## The obvious answer

The obvious answer is the swifts, and the obvious answer is not wrong. They are the most numerous residents by far, tens of thousands of them, and they have been here longer than any record. After them come the bats, the owls in the square pockets, the spiders at their windfall webs, the beetles and woodlice, the pale shelf-fungi, and the worms turning the black floor. It is a crowded building. Every surface is let.

But every account of the Flue comes back to the same detail, and it is worth taking seriously. *The centre holds nothing at all.* No web spans it. No branch crosses it. No bird perches in it or hovers in it or roosts in it, though swifts are birds of the open air and could. A feather released at the floor rises straight out of the top without touching anything.

In a place where life has crammed itself into every crack, an empty space is not empty by accident. Something is keeping it clear. Somebody lives in the middle.

---

## The stoker

The fen people have an answer of their own, and like most such answers it is half right.

They say there is a stoker. When the works closed, they say, one man did not get the word, or got it and would not accept it. He stayed at the firebox with his shovel and kept the draw going, because a chimney that stops drawing takes a long time to start again, and you never know when the engine will be wanted. He is still down there under the leaf-mat, they say, feeding a fire no one can see. That is why the Flue roars and why it is always faintly warm. When you stand inside and feel the pull at your back, that is him opening the damper to get a look at you.

Fen children are told to say *evening, gaffer* when they go in, and some of them still do. People leave things at the root-arch: a lump of coal kept in a pocket for the purpose, a twist of dry straw, a stub of candle. Things that would burn.

There is no stoker. Nobody is buried under the floor, and no fire has ever been found, though people have dug. Still, the story has understood something the naturalists missed. What lives in the Flue is not a creature that happens to live in a chimney. It is something that *belongs to the chimney*, the way a stoker belongs to a firebox. It is part of the works, and it is still on shift.

---

## The draw

A chimney is not a column of brick. The brick is only the means. What a chimney *is*, the whole reason it was raised, is a movement: warm air going up, cold air coming in behind it, a steady pull that feeds a fire and carries its waste into the sky. Engineers called this the draw, and they spoke of it almost as a living thing. A good stack *pulls well*. A bad one *sulks*. A cold chimney has to be *coaxed*, warmed with burning paper before it will take. Stokers learned the temper of their stacks the way carters learned horses.

When the works fell silent, the brick stayed and the draw should have died. A chimney with no fire under it is only a tall cold room. But this one never quite went cold. The seed in the mortar sprouted, and a tree that respires is warm, faintly, all the time. The leaf-litter rotting in the old firebox gave off its slow compost heat. Then the swifts came, and every one of them is a small furnace, a bird whose heart runs at hundreds of beats a minute. The fire was gone, but there was never a single night without *some* warmth at the bottom of the stack. So the draw never stopped. It only thinned, and learned to live on less.

That is who lives in the Flue. It is the draw: the purpose of the chimney, which outlived the chimney, just as the shape of the chimney outlived the brick. The tree kept the form. The draw kept the function. They are two different tenants of the same house, and they have been married a very long time.

---

## Its body

The draw has no body in the usual sense. It is made of moving air, but it is not the air. The air passes through it the way water passes through a river. A given breath enters at the root-arch and leaves at the rim in a little under a minute, and the draw is the shape that breath is made to take. It is the pull itself.

You can learn its anatomy by standing in it. The *mouth* is the root-arch, where the pull is strongest and coldest at your ankles. The *throat* is the lower third of the column, where the air speeds up, and where visitors feel an odd pressure behind the breastbone, as if something were leaning on them from inside. Above that is the long *gullet* of the upper stack, fast and smooth, where the swifts cling. At the top is the *lips*, the ragged rim, where the rising column meets the weather of the fen and frays into turbulence. That fraying is what makes the whoop in the roar when a gust crosses it.

The roar is not quite the draw's voice. It is closer to its breathing, the sound a large body makes simply by being alive. It changes as little as a sleeper's breathing does, which is almost not at all.

The empty centre is its core, where the pull is purest and nothing is allowed to snag it. A spider that strings a line across the column finds the thread plucked away within the hour. A bat that tries to hang from a filament of root reaching into the open air finds the root gone, dried and snapped by a wind that never lets it rest. The draw does not do this out of malice. A throat keeps itself clear. Anything that lodges in the middle would slow the pull, and the pull is the only thing the draw is.

---

## What it eats

The draw eats warmth. It has never eaten anything else.

In the old days its meals were enormous: a firebox white with burning coal, heat pouring up the stack in a torrent, smoke and sparks and roaring gas. It grew huge on that. Those were its good years, and it remembers them, as far as it remembers anything.

Now it grazes. It takes the respiration of the living wood, a heat so faint you would need an instrument to find it. It takes the warmth of the rotting floor, which rises out of the black mat in a slow invisible fog. Above all it takes the swifts. Tens of thousands of small hot bodies cling to the upper walls, and each one sheds a little heat into the air around it. That heat is what makes the upper stack warmer than the fen, and the upper stack being warmer is what keeps the whole column rising. The swifts are its fuel. They do not burn, and they are not hurt. They only give off what warm things give off, and the draw takes it, as a chimney takes the heat of a fire without the fire being any the poorer for it.

In return, the draw carries them. Swifts are built for the air and are clumsy at everything else. They cannot perch like other birds, and they struggle to lift off from a flat surface. The column holds them up. At dawn they drop from the wall into the updraft and are lifted, effortlessly, straight out of the mouth of the stack into the morning. At dusk they pour down into it in their black spiral, and the spiral is not only the swifts' shape. It is the draw's shape made visible. For those few minutes you can see the thing that lives there, outlined in birds, turning slowly on its axis as it swallows them home.

When you walk in, it eats a little of you too. That is the pull at your hair and clothes, the chill at your ankles, the pressure behind the breastbone. It takes perhaps a degree from your skin and sends it up among the swifts. People who stay in the Flue too long come out shivering, though the air inside is no colder than the fen. They say they feel *tasted*. It is not dangerous. The draw is used to small portions.

---

## What it remembers

The draw has no mind of its own. Air cannot hold a thought. But it lives inside a tree, and a tree is a keeper of records.

Look again at the inner wall. The horizontal bands of rust-red heartwood, a hand's width apart all the way up, are the old brick courses, taken into the wood. The faint crystalline glitter where the light catches them is the tree's memory of fired clay, the silica of kilns and furnaces held in the living grain. The long rust-stains are the iron hoops that once cinched the stack against the force of its own heat. The square-edged pockets where the owls nest were sockets for scaffold beams, or for the fixings of a cleaning ladder. The quarter-circle scar at head height is the arch of the old firebox door, the place where the stoker stood, pressed into the wood like a fossil.

The tree grew up around these things and kept their shapes the way a hand keeps the shape of a tool it has used for sixty years. The draw moves over them every second of every day. Air moving over fluted grain is exactly what makes the roar, and the particular pitch of the Flue, its fundamental and its haze of overtones, is set by those contours. The brick courses tune it. The iron bands tune it. The firebox arch tunes it.

So when mill-workers say the Flue sounds *exactly* like a working stack, they are not being poetic. It is the old chimney's own voice, preserved in wood and played by the thing that used to live on its fire. The draw does not remember the furnace. It is *shaped* by the memory of it, which may be more than most remembering amounts to.

---

## How it counts

The roar never changes, and neither does the swifts' shrieking. A creature that lived only in those sounds would have no sense of time at all, only a single endless present.

But the drips fall at intervals.

Somewhere high in the dark, dew gathers along the old iron-stain bands, thickens, lets go, and falls the full forty metres through the centre of the column. That is the one thing the draw permits in its core, because a falling drop crosses it and is gone without snagging anything. It lands with a soft tick in the shallow basins on the floor. These ticks are the only sounds in the Flue that come *one at a time*.

If the draw has anything like attention, this is where it lives. The draw itself makes those drops. Its rising breath carries the fen's moisture up the stack and presses it against the cooler upper wood, and so the drops condense out of its own body and fall back through it. They are the only thing in the Flue that moves *down*, against the grain of its existence. Each tick is a small, exact event. Here is one, and now here is the next.

A stoker counts shovelfuls, and a chimney that has lost its stoker counts drops. The count never reaches anything. It isn't meant to. It is simply how the draw knows the shift is still going on.

---

## The pools

Something else lives in the pools the drips have worn, and it is the only resident that belongs wholly to the draw's side of the house.

The water is clear and cold, and it trembles constantly in the draught, as if it were always about to boil. Its surface never stills. In it lives a small pale crustacean, a copepod no bigger than a grain of sand. It exists nowhere else in the fen, perhaps nowhere else at all. It is colourless and blind. It feeds on what falls: spores, dust, the fine powder of feathers, the occasional stunned midge knocked out of the updraft by a drop.

Its peculiarity is that it cannot tolerate still water. Taken out and kept in a jar, it lies on the bottom and dies within a day. It needs the perpetual shiver of the surface, the drawn trembling that only this place provides. Somewhere in its long isolation it came to depend on the draw's breathing the way a fish depends on current. It is the draw's one true dependent, the thing that would die first if the pull ever stopped. In a sense it is the draw's child. It is not made from it, but made *for* it.

## The one time the note changed

The Flue has sounded the same for a thousand years, with one exception.

Once, and only once as far as anyone knows, somebody lit a fire inside. Accounts do not agree on who it was or why. Perhaps it was a traveller caught by winter, perhaps a child who had heard the stoker story and wanted to give him something to work with. They built a small fire of peat and dry sedge on the floor of the old firebox, under the quarter-circle scar, and put a match to it.

The witnesses agree on what happened next. The flame did not flicker. It stood up, tall and narrow and perfectly still, drawn straight into the column like a thread being pulled. The smoke did not billow or linger. It went up in a single grey line through the empty centre and out of the top of the stack. The swifts on the walls did not panic. They pressed flatter, and some witnesses say they went quiet.

And the roar deepened. It dropped half a tone, perhaps a whole one, into something fuller and more resonant, the sound of a stack that was finally *pulling well*. For as long as the peat burned, the Flue sounded like a working chimney in a way it had not for a thousand years. People on the far side of the fen came out of their houses to listen.

When the fire went out, the note rose back to where it had always been, and it has not moved since.

The fen people tell this story as a wonder. The ones who stood inside tell it as something closer to a grief. They say it felt like watching someone old be handed a tool from their working life, and see them take it without a word, and use it perfectly, and give it back.

Nobody has lit a fire there since. The coal and candles left at the root-arch are left unlit, which is how they are meant to be left. They are not fuel. They are a courtesy.

---

## What it wants

The draw does not want the fire back. Whatever it was in its great days, it has been small for too long to be hungry in that way now. It has what it needs: the warm wood, the warm floor, the warm birds. It gets the rise at dawn and the return at dusk, the pale blind things trembling in its pools, and the ticks it counts. It keeps the core clear and carries the swifts. It goes on doing what it was made to do, for no engine, with no one on the far end of the work.

That is the answer to what lives in the Flue. It is not a ghost. There is nothing of the dead in it, only a purpose that was never told to stop. It is not a spirit of the tree either. The tree is its house and its instrument, not its self. It is the last worker of the works, still on shift and quietly competent, living on scraps of warmth and fed by birds, sounding the old note through the old shape because that is the only way it knows to be.

The emptiness at the heart of the Flue is not vacancy. It is the draw itself, a column of pure attention, held open to the sky and pulling steadily upward, as it did when the fire was lit and as it will long after anyone remembers that there was ever a fire at all.

When you go in, say *evening, gaffer*. It won't answer. But you will feel the pull at your back, and the pressure behind your breastbone, and the small cold at your ankles where it takes its degree of you. That is it looking at you, briefly, before it goes back to work.
