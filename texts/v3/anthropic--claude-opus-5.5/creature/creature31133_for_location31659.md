# Those Who Live on the Crown

## The Tenants

They do not call themselves citizens, or owners, or even residents. The word they use means something closer to *tenant*, or *one who is staying for now*. Nothing on the Crown can be kept. A house is not property. It is a boat you ride for a while before you have to get off.

At first glance they are ordinary people, the kind you might find in any high, hard place: short, deep-chested, broad across the ribs. Their hearts are large and slow. A stranger's heart knocks in the thin air, but theirs beats with the unhurried rhythm of something that learned long ago there is no point in rushing. They breathe through the nose, deeply and seldom. Watch one of them sleeping and you might wait a long time for the chest to rise.

Their skin is dark in a way that has nothing to do with lineage. At this height the sun comes through a sky gone nearly violet, and it burns without warming. Children are born pale and darken within a year. The oldest people are almost the color of the black glass, with fine grey crazing at the corners of the eyes, as if they too had begun to bloom.

Their feet tell you most. Every Crown child learns to walk on warm stone. The soles thicken into something between skin and horn, and the toes spread wide for purchase on glossy, glass-cast stairs. Even so, they almost always wear shoes: wooden clogs near the crest, felt slippers lower down. Going barefoot is intimate here. You do it at home, on the upper floors, where the stone is only warm and you want to feel the mountain counting through your heels.

## How They Speak

Sound dies a few paces off in the thin air, so the Tenants talk close.

Two people in conversation stand nearly nose to nose. To a lowlander it looks like a confrontation or a courtship. To them it is simply how talking is done. Their voices are low and breathy, with few hard consonants. A shout would carry badly and cost too much air. Over any distance they use their hands: a flowing sign-speech developed across centuries, full of downward sweeps for time and age, upward pushes for *new*, *young*, *soon*. The sign for a person is a flat palm rising slowly. The sign for a house is the same palm tilted and sliding down. The sign for *home*, where you live now, is the two together, crossing.

They have a third way of speaking, the one they use most tenderly. They tap on stone.

The glass carries vibration far better than the air carries sound. Press your palm to a wall on the Crown and you can feel a press close three terraces away, a child running on the floor above, the tick and ping of cooling rock. The Tenants learned long ago to speak through the walls. A family can talk from room to room, even from house to house, with knuckle-raps and fingernail-scratches on shared stone. Lovers in fused buildings tap to each other through the seam at night. Mothers soothe infants by laying them against a wall and tapping a slow pattern nearby, so the baby feels it through its whole body. Most of these patterns have no words. They are older than words. They are the patterns of the mountain itself, the counting, imitated and returned.

This is the first thing to understand about them: they answer the mountain. When a wall pings in the night, someone in the house, half-asleep, will often reach out and tap it once, gently, as you might pat the flank of an animal that has shifted in its sleep.

## Counting

Children on the Crown learn to count by listening.

At bedtime, on the upper floors, a parent lies with the children in the warm dark, and they count the mountain together: tick, tick, tick-tick, a long silence, a far-off click. The numbers rarely add up to anything, and that is not the point. The point is the attention, and the understanding that the stone is never silent and never finished.

A clear ringing ping, the rare one, the sound of some deep stress giving way, is an event. The whole household stops. Someone notes which wall it came from and what hour. A child born in the same hour as a ringing ping is considered marked. Such children are often named for the sound. Ping-names are short and bright, a single sung vowel: Ii, Eh, Aah. People who carry them tend to become mold-keepers or sail-riggers, work that needs a steady hand and a certain patience with sudden things.

They do not keep time with clocks. They keep it by the presses. The great slow groan of a mold closing comes through the stone every few hours, felt more than heard, and people say "two groans past the fog" or "meet me at the fourth groan." A day of work is a certain number of groans. A lifetime is too many to count, and they don't try.

## The Climb

A house on the Crown drifts downhill a few dozen meters in a lifetime and takes six hundred years to reach the cloud. But a family does not stay with its house forever. The house is warmest and best when it is young, near the crest, glossy and sharp. By the middle slopes it is comfortable. Lower still it begins to cool, and the fog comes more often, and the sails catch well but the walls begin to cloud, and the mites arrive.

So every few generations a family makes the Climb.

It is the great event of a Tenant's life, more important than marriage and nearly as important as death. When the elders judge the house has drifted low enough, the family petitions the foundry guilds for a place in a new casting. There is a waiting list, of course. There is always a waiting list, because the mountain makes only so much new stone. When a place comes open, the family packs everything it owns and walks uphill.

They don't hire porters, and they don't use the lifts. They carry their things up the stairs themselves, through the fused districts, past houses stamped with the same arches as their own, only sharper and newer and hotter. The whole neighborhood turns out to watch, standing in doorways with hands raised in the sign for *rising*. In the new house, which still smells faintly of sulfur and has floors hot enough that they must lay down wooden boards for the first year, the oldest member of the family presses a palm to the fresh wall and taps the family's pattern into it for the first time.

The pattern is the only thing a family truly owns. Each has one, a rhythm of taps handed down and never written. In the old house the pattern has been tapped into the same stones so many thousands of times that people swear the walls have learned it. Late at night, they say, an old house will tick the family pattern back to itself, faintly, long after the family has gone uphill.

The house they leave is not empty for long. Someone always needs it. There are younger couples, sail-menders just starting out, families whose own houses have drifted lower still. The Crown is a city where everyone is always moving up one rung while the ladder slides down beneath them. Most people, over a lifetime, travel only a few hundred meters. Every one of those meters is uphill, climbed against a ground that has been falling the whole time.

## The Dead

Nothing on the Crown is buried. There is no soil to bury anything in.

When someone dies, the body is washed with cistern water, the most precious thing a family can spend, and carried up to the crest by the family on foot, in a final climb. At the foundries the mold-keepers take it. The heat at the mouth of the upwelling is enough. It happens quickly and without smoke. The mold-keepers gather what remains, a small weight of pale mineral ash, and mix it into the parting-slurry they brush onto the inner faces of the molds before each pressing.

So the dead go into the walls.

Not into their own family's walls, necessarily. The next pressing might become a stair, a lintel, a water channel, or the inner face of a stranger's bedroom. The ash leaves no mark. There is no way to know which stone holds whom. But everyone knows the city is made this way, and has been for centuries. Every wall is faintly ancestral. When a Tenant lays a hand on stone, they are touching the drift of the dead: grandparents, guild-founders, children who did not live, all pressed into the city and carried slowly down the mountain.

This changes what the descent means.

Seen one way, the Crown is a city of the living climbing upward. Seen another, it is a city of the dead walking downward, one hand's width a year, toward the cloud. The two currents pass through each other every day. At the crest, the stone holds the newly dead, and people there speak of them as near. On the middle slopes the walls hold people from three hundred years ago, whose names survive only in guild-rolls. In the lowest districts, where the walls crack and slump and slide away into white, the stone holds people no one remembers at all.

When a façade finally sheds and slips soundlessly into the cloud, the Tenants say that someone has *gone on*. They do not say who. They don't know. They only know that the dead were carried six hundred years down the mountain and have now, at last, been set down.

## The Old Who Walk Down

Most of the living climb. A few, at the end, do not.

Every generation there are some who have lived a long time, whose hearts have slowed until even the Tenants think them slow, and who choose not to make the last climb to the crest. Instead they walk *down*.

They go to the lowest districts, the chalk-marked houses at the edge of the cloud, where no one else will live. They take very little: a felt blanket, a water-flask, sometimes a small cast-glass bowl. They move into an abandoned house and stay there. Their families visit at first, bringing food, but over time the visits thin. Everyone understands this is how it is meant to go. The ones who walk down are not exiled. They are honored, with a quiet, slightly frightened reverence. They are called *the Settling*. You do not tap on their walls.

What they do down there is not entirely known. They sit in doorways looking out over the cloud sea. They sleep in the cooling rooms. Some are said to stop eating the grain that comes up the lifts and to eat nothing at all, or nothing anyone can see. The fog comes up to them more often than to anyone else on the mountain. It fills their houses and their lungs, and many of them develop the grey-green crazing around the eyes and across the backs of the hands. Children who visit say the old ones have grown very still and very quiet, and that when you sit beside them you can hear them counting under their breath, in time with the stone.

Eventually the house they have chosen cracks, leans, and goes on into the cloud. No one sees it happen. The family finds the doorway gone, a fresh edge of broken glass, and nothing beyond it but white.

## The Mites

The glass-mites are the smallest tenants on the Crown, and in their way the most numerous.

They are nearly invisible one by one: grains of pale dust with legs, too small to make out without a lens. Together they form a faint grey haze in the corners of old walls, a moving bloom that shifts when you breathe on it. They eat the mineral salts that weep out of aging glass, and in eating they pit it, softening edges and dulling gloss. They are the Crown's only decay, and decay is, in its way, a kind of life.

At the crest there are none. The stone is too hot and too new. They appear on the middle slopes, sparse and shy, and grow thicker going down. In the lowest districts they are everywhere. Walls there are furred with them, and the fog condensing on the stone runs grey with their bodies.

Some families scrape them off. Others leave them, and a certain kind of old family is quietly proud of its mites, of a house that has lived long enough to be eaten. There is a saying: *a wall with no mites has no memory.* No one is sure what it means. Perhaps only that the mites come to what has lasted. Or perhaps something stranger. The mites eat the bloom, and the bloom rises out of the stone, and the stone was pressed with ash. So there is a small, uncomfortable thought the Tenants do not often say aloud: that the mites are, very slowly, eating the dead.

If they are, they do it gently. And they carry what they eat nowhere but further down.

## The Swifts

Above all of this, the vent-swifts turn.

They are the only tenants of the Crown that never walk on it. They are born on it, from eggs laid in warm chimney-vents and hatched by the stone's heat without any brooding at all. The mother lays and leaves, and the mountain does the rest. The chicks emerge into the dark of the vent, climb toward the light, and on their first day they fall out into the updraft and are caught. They may not touch stone again for years.

They eat what the wind brings: the lowland's floating life, gnats and ballooning spiders and winged seeds lifted thousands of meters by the mountain's warm breath. They are the one link between the Crown and the living world below that does not run on cables. Every swift is built from things that grew somewhere green.

The Tenants love them with a love that is almost mourning. The swifts are silent, as everything here is silent. On still evenings, forty or fifty of them spiral over the foundries, rising in the shimmer so high they turn to specks against the violet, then to nothing. People say the swifts are trying to reach the top of the sky. People say the swifts are the city's thoughts. Children draw them in the margins of their slates as small dark crescents, sickle-shapes, like the curve a hand makes when it reaches.

When a swift dies in the air, which is how swifts die, it falls. It may fall onto the crest, and then the mold-keepers take it and give it to the molds the way they would give a person. That is the oldest law of the Crown, older than the guild stamps: whatever falls on the hot stone is poured.

## The Walker at the Edge

And then there is the thing at the cloud-line.

It is seen at dawn and only at dawn, when the fog is lowest and the light comes sideways across the cloud sea. It is tall, far taller than a person, and pale, the grey-white of fog or of glass gone to bloom. It walks slowly along the ragged lowest edge of the city, where the houses lean and crumble, and it stoops over the broken stone as if it were grazing. It moves on legs that are too long and too thin, and it steps with enormous care, the way a heron walks through shallows.

No two witnesses agree on how many legs it has. Some say four. Some say six, or eight, or that it has a great many small legs beneath a few large ones. A sail-rigger once claimed to have watched it for the better part of an hour, and said the number changed while he watched. It would lift one long limb, and when that limb came down there were two. It paused over a fallen façade, and some of its legs seemed simply to settle into the rubble and stay there, while the rest of it moved on.

Most people treat this as a tale. The mold-keepers do not. They have the longest memories on the Crown, because they keep the guild-rolls, and there is an old notation in the rolls, a stamp found on no mold: a spiral of dots, each dot smaller than the last, trailing downward into nothing. The keepers call it *the mite-sign*. In the margins of the oldest rolls it appears beside the records of the Settling, the old ones who walked down.

Here is what the mold-keepers believe, when they can be persuaded to say it. It is only a belief.

The mites grow thicker the lower you go. At the cloud-line they are everywhere, in the walls, in the fog, in the grey runoff. And there, in the cold and wet, with more of them than anywhere else, they sometimes *gather*. Not the way dust gathers in a corner. The way birds gather in a flock, or cells in a body. Enough of them together, in the right fog, at the right hour, become something with a shape: long-legged, because long legs are the easiest shape for many small things to hold, and pale, because they are made of what they eat, the bloom of the ancestral glass. It grazes because it is still eating. It walks the edge because that is where its food is, the oldest walls, the most-eaten dead. It sheds legs because it is not one creature. It is a slow agreement among a great many, and the agreement sometimes loosens.

The keepers say more, more quietly. They say the Settling are not alone down there. They say the old ones who walk down sit in the doorways and count, and the mites come to them, as they come to anything that has lasted, and the crazing on their skin is not age. They say that when an old house finally goes into the cloud and the family finds only a fresh edge and white beyond it, the thing walking the cloud-line at the next dawn is a little taller.

The Tenants do not fear it. They are not a fearful people, and fear costs air. But they do not go down to the cloud-line at dawn, and when they see the pale shape from far above, stooping among the ruins in the grey light, they raise a hand and make a sign that belongs to no other purpose: palm open, turned down, pressed slowly toward the ground. It means something like *rest*, or *you may stop now*, or *thank you for carrying them*.

## What Lives Here

So who lives on the Crown?

The Tenants live here: slow-hearted, dark-skinned, close-talking people who climb their whole lives against a ground that is always sliding down, who tap patterns into stone and are, they say, sometimes answered.

The dead live here, pressed into lintels and stairs and bedroom walls, walking downhill a hand's width a year, for six hundred years, toward the cloud.

The swifts live here, above it all, never touching it, spiraling up out of the vents and into a sky that grows darker the higher they go.

The mites live here, in the walls and the fog, eating the bloom of the ancestral glass, making the only decay and the only soil the Crown has ever known.

And at the bottom, in the dawn fog, among the broken houses where the city finally ends, something pale and patient lives, tall and many-legged and never the same shape twice. It is perhaps made of the mites, perhaps of the Settling, perhaps of both. It grazes on the last of the old stone and stoops at last into the white. It is what the mountain makes of those who have finished climbing.

The mountain is a conveyor turned inside out, new stone rising at the crown and old stone carried down to the cloud. Everything that lives on it has learned to move against that current or with it. The living go up. The dead go down. Somewhere in the middle, in the warm house with the family pattern worn into its walls, a child lies on the upper floor in the dark, feeling the heat come up through the stone, and counts.

Tick. Tick-tick. A long silence.

Then, far off and clear, a single ringing *ping*. The child's hand goes out, the way it has seen its parents' hands go out a thousand times, and taps the wall once, gently, in reply.
