# Who Lives in the Mill

## No One

The first answer, and the one travelers give when they come back out, is that no one lives in the Mill. You cross it, if you are foolish or desperate or called. You carry your water, though water is everywhere, because the water of the Mill is half stone and sits in the stomach like wet clay. You walk by feel. You speak by touch to whoever you came with, and you try not to lose them in the mist, because a companion lost in the Mill stays lost. Nobody lives there, travelers say, any more than anybody lives inside a waterfall.

That answer isn't wrong, but it has been worn. It has had its edges taken off, and what's left is smooth enough to pass from hand to hand without anyone feeling what used to be on it.

Stay long enough and you will feel a hand on your shoulder.

## The Hand

It comes out of the mist behind you. You have heard no footsteps, because nothing can be heard. You have seen no shape, because the milk of the air closes an arm's length off. There is only a palm, warm, dry despite the damp, resting between your neck and your shoulder with a weight so exactly judged that it feels less like being grabbed than like being leaned on by a friend.

Then the fingers begin to move. They press, lift, and press in a pattern you can't read. It isn't tapping. It is closer to the way a hand smooths a sheet: long, continuous pressures that shade into one another with no clear place where one sign ends and the next begins.

When you turn, someone is there.

## The Held

Travelers who have met them call them the Mill-folk, or the Pale, or the Leaners. They have their own word for themselves, but it isn't a word. It is a touch: two fingers laid flat against the inside of the wrist and held there, not pressing, not moving, simply staying. The nearest translation anyone has managed is *the held*, or *those who are still in it*, or *here*. After a while you understand that these aren't three meanings. They are one meaning seen from three sides.

Seen through the mist they look almost exactly like the mist. The silt that films a traveler's skin in hours has filmed theirs for lifetimes, and it doesn't wash off, because there is nothing here to wash with that isn't more silt. They are the color of weak tea with cream, like the water. Their hair, if they are born with any, is worn away by the gritty air before they are grown, and their scalps are smooth and slightly polished, like the tops of stones. Their eyes stay almost closed, not from squinting, because there is no glare to squint against, but because an open eye in the Mill collects grit, and a closed one sees nearly as much.

Their feet are the strangest thing about them. The soles aren't calloused, because callus is an edge and the Mill takes edges. The soles are smooth. They have the soft, dense, rounded give of a river stone that has been handled for a century. When the Held walk, their feet don't step on the cobbles so much as settle among them, rocking when the stones rock, turning a quarter-turn when the stones turn. Watching one walk is like watching a stone that has decided to go somewhere.

They are not many. Perhaps there are a few hundred across the whole breadth of the plain, perhaps a few thousand. No one has counted them, and they don't count.

## How They Speak

Everything the Held say, they say with their hands on someone else's body.

This is not a crude substitute for speech. Travelers who learn a little of it, and a few have, report that it is subtler than any spoken language they know, precisely because it has no gaps. Speech is made of breaths and pauses and words with edges. The touch-language of the Held is continuous. A conversation is a single long pressure that changes shape: a palm sliding from shoulder to elbow while the fingertips vary their weight, and the thumb holds a note underneath it all like the sub-sound of the deep stones.

Because it is continuous, it has no beginnings. You can't start a sentence in it, only join one already happening. When a Held child is born, its mother's hand is already speaking to it before the child is fully in the water. That speech doesn't stop. It passes from hand to hand for the rest of the child's life, so that every Held person is, in a sense, inside one conversation that began before any of them and has never paused.

Travelers find this unbearable at first. They want to know what is being said *now*, what this particular touch means. The Held can't answer, the way you couldn't say what a single stone contributes to the roar. They are not saying things. They are saying.

Over weeks, a traveler who stays begins to feel meaning arriving without being able to say when it arrived. You understand that you are welcome. You understand that the weather is turning, that a squall line is two days downwind, that the crabs are rising near a certain drift of holes and should be left alone. You can't remember being told any of it. You have simply been worn into knowing it.

## What They Carry

Each of the Held carries a single stone.

It is given at birth, pressed into the infant's hand in the warm water. It is chosen by the mother, or by whoever is nearest, from the stones at her feet at the moment the child arrives. It is not special. It is whatever stone was there.

The child holds it. As a toddler, the child learns to rub it. By the time the child is grown, the rubbing is as constant as breathing and as unnoticed: a thumb circling, circling, the stone warm from the hand and the hand warm from the stone. The Held sleep rubbing their stones. They walk rubbing. They rub while they speak, and while they eat.

This is the source of the grooves travelers find in some of the larger fixed stones. When one of the Held grows old, they sometimes sit, and they set their stone against a bigger one, and the rubbing goes on, stone against stone, until the smaller has worn a hollow into the larger that fits it exactly. Then they sit on, rubbing the hollow, sometimes for years.

Travelers have asked why. The answers they get, pressed into their forearms by patient fingers, don't resolve into anything like a reason. The closest anyone has come is this: the Mill rubs everything, so the Held rub one thing. They are not resisting the Mill. They are doing what it does, but small, and on purpose, and with love. A Held person's stone is the part of the world they are personally finishing.

Some of these stones, after a lifetime, are very nearly round.

## How They Live

They eat the mat. It grows fresh on every upturned face, never older than a few days, and the Held scrape it off with their thumbnails, which they keep as the only edges they own. It tastes faintly of iron and green, and faintly of the chalk that gets into everything. They eat the pith of the reeds, and when they eat it they apologize to the reed-patch with a long flat touch on the stones at its roots, because every reed they take is a little fixity lost. Sometimes they eat the crabs, though only ones that surface alive, and they never take a crab near a drift of holes.

They drink the water. It is warm and milky, and over a lifetime their bodies take in so much rock flour that the Held grow heavy, dense, and slow to float. When one of them lies down in the water, they sink to the stones and rest on them, and the stones rock them.

They sleep together, in a knot. When the dim light shades toward whatever passes here for dusk, they find one another by touch and gather into a single cluster, shoulder to shoulder to shoulder, packed so completely that there is no gap anywhere, every body touching six or seven others at once. They stand like that, leaning into each other and into the wind, and they sleep on their feet. Their bodies shift all night, a small rock, a quarter-turn, a settling, and the knot slowly revolves. By morning the person who was on the windward edge is in the warm middle.

Travelers who have slept inside such a knot say it is the only time in the Mill they have not felt the roar as noise. It was still there, as loud as ever. But pressed into all those bodies, they felt it the way the Held do: as the voice of everyone else's shifting, the sound of being among.

## Their Names

A Held person's name is a touch, and like all their language it doesn't stay the same.

An infant's name is sharp, as much as anything here can be. It is a quick pinch at a specific place, the crook of an elbow or the back of the neck. As the child grows, the people who use the name press it a little more softly, a little more broadly, without meaning to. The name wears. By middle age it is no longer a pinch but a pressing. By old age it is a palm laid flat and kept there.

The very old have names indistinguishable from one another: just a warm hand resting, without pattern. No one finds this sad. It means the person has been named so often, by so many, that the name has been rubbed smooth. It means they have been thoroughly known.

## Their Children

A Held child's first sound is the roar, and it never hears anything else.

This is harder to imagine than it seems. Travelers arrive at the Mill carrying a lifetime of silence in them. They remember quiet rooms, still nights, the space around a single bird call. The roar is terrible to them because it fills something that used to be empty. A Held child has no empty place for the roar to fill. For the child it isn't a noise. It is the medium, as water is the medium for a fish. The Held don't hear the roar any more than you hear the pressure of the air.

What they hear, and hear keenly, are the *thinnings*: the fractional drop in the roar near a cairn, the narrow notch of silence around a hole, the pocket of quiet that forms at the foot of a standing grey bird. A Held child learns these the way other children learn color. There are, it's said, dozens of distinctions in the touch-language for different kinds of lessened roar. One is the warm thinning of a reed-patch. One is the cold, wrong thinning of a hole-drift, which children are taught to back away from with their eyes still closed. One is the hush that follows the Slow One. And one has no other name but the pressure used for the dead.

## The Cairns

Travelers who find the cairns ask who built them. The Held, when asked, say *no one*, and they mean it, and it is also the only true answer they have ever given about anything.

A cairn is not built by placing stones. You can't balance three rounded stones in a moving current. Travelers have tried and the stones have simply rolled apart. A cairn happens when one of the Held has learned to feel, through the soles of those smooth feet, the places where the Mill *hesitates*.

The hesitations are tiny and they don't last. Somewhere in the endless rocking there is a moment when a particular cobble, pressed by its neighbors in a particular way, is momentarily balanced. It is held in place by the whole plain's pushing, all the forces around it cancelling. That moment passes in a breath. But a Held person who has spent forty years listening with their feet can sense it coming, and can be there, and can set a second stone on the first in exactly that breath. Then they wait, sometimes for days, standing utterly still in the flow, for the next hesitation. If it comes, they set the third.

A cairn isn't made against the Mill. It is made *out of* the Mill, from one of the few moments when the Mill itself held still. Once three such moments are stacked, something happens that the Held can't explain and don't try to. The hesitation doesn't pass. The stillness of that breath stays. The water parts around it. The mat grows old. The roar, just there, forgets to be quite so loud.

This is what the Held are for, as far as they are for anything. They don't farm, they don't build houses, they have no tools and want none. Across the whole enormous plain, over generations, they find the hesitations and keep them.

Travelers used to think the cairns were shrines, or markers, or waystations. They are closer to words. Each one is a single place where the continuous speech of the Mill paused, and someone was there to catch the pause and hold it open. If the Mill is a sentence with no beginning and no end, the cairns are where the Held have laid a hand on it and pressed, gently: *here*.

## The Old

This is where the account starts to change shape, as it was always going to.

The Held don't die the way travelers expect. No one has seen a funeral. No one has seen a body, which would in any case be ground to silt within a season. When a Held person grows very old, with a name worn to a flat warm palm and a stone rubbed nearly round, they begin to spend more of their time standing still.

At first it is at cairns, in the lee, where the stillness is easiest. Then it is out on the open plain, in the full lean of the water, one leg planted, rubbing their stone. Their family comes to them and presses the long speech into their shoulders. More and more, the old one doesn't press back. They just stand, and the water parts around them, and the stones at their feet go quiet.

Then one day the family comes, and what is standing there is a bird.

It is tall, grey as the mist, with legs too long for its body, stopped on one leg in a pocket of silence it seems to be drinking. The Held don't startle. They lay two fingers flat against its long neck, where a wrist would be, and hold them there. Then they press into its feathers the flat warm palm that is the old one's name, and they walk on.

Travelers argue about this. Some say it's a metaphor, some that it's a grief-custom and the Held greet every bird as their dead. Some who have stayed a long time say they watched it happen: an old woman standing still for a season, the grey coming up through the silt on her skin, her body thinning and lengthening, until what had been leaning in the flow was a standing thing on one leg. They are not sure they saw it. The Mill wears the edges off memory too. They can't tell where the woman ended and the bird began, and after a while they stop being sure that there was a place.

But it explains something about the birds. It explains why no one knows what they eat. They eat the quiet, the Held say, because it's what they spent their lives learning to find. It explains why the roar is thinner near them. They are cairns that walked there.

## The Crabs

If the old become birds, the Held are asked, what are the crabs?

The Held go still at this question, the way they go still near a hole-drift.

The crabs are blind and flattened and mottled like stones. They ride the churn down and down and surface flat as coins. When they die they are indistinguishable from stones and are ground along with them. The Held say, in a pressure that is hard to read and that they seldom make, that the crabs are *the ones who went down instead of standing up*. It is the other way of being finished. You can learn the hesitations and keep them and grow grey and tall and quiet on one leg, or you can let the rocking take you, all the way under, and become so exactly the shape of the Mill that the Mill no longer notices you're there.

It isn't a punishment. The Held have no punishments. But they won't eat a crab found near the holes, because those are the ones that went too far down, and came up having worn all the way through to the other side of something, and they don't want that in their bodies.

## The Slow One

And the Slow One.

Travelers take it for a hill. The Held know it is not a hill, though they grant that in every way that matters it is the last hill, the one that should have been worn to nothing and refused. It moves at the pace of a season. Its shell wears away on top and grows back from beneath, so its surface is never the same for long, though it is always there.

The Held walk with it when it passes. Sometimes a whole knot of them will leave their usual wanderings and fall in alongside, pacing it for months at a speed so slow they have to stop, stand still, and wait for it to catch up. They sleep in its lee, in the day-long wake of stiller water. They press their palms against its flank, which is warm, and is rock, and is alive. They speak to it in the long speech, and they believe it answers. The shifting of its body beneath their hands, the vast sub-sonic groan of its shell regrowing, is the slowest conversation in the world.

Ask what it is and they give a touch that travelers have translated as *the first*, or *the one who is still*, and after a long time of being with the Held, as *the one who keeps getting edges*.

This is the thing about the Slow One the Held revere. The birds find stillness and the crabs find surrender, but the Slow One does neither. It is worn, and it grows. It is being finished, and it keeps un-finishing. Every season the Mill takes its surface away, and every season it makes a new surface from inside, rough and edged and new. The Held don't know how old it is. They say it was here when the Mill was mountains. They say that in all that time it has never once had the same shell twice, and it has never once stopped being itself.

The Held rub their stones round. But they press their hands to the Slow One the way you would press your hand to a mother, or to the thing your mother was trying to become.

## The Doorway

The Held do not go through the doorframe.

They go near it. They stand beside it for long hours, feeling with their feet the slight difference between the water on one side and the water on the other. They put their palms on its uprights. They never step through, and when a traveler does, they wait on the near side and then hold the traveler for a long time, pressing an especially slow and careful pattern into the traveler's back, as if smoothing something back into place.

Asked why, they make a touch that is almost, but not quite, the touch for *cairn*. It is the pressure for a hesitation that someone found and kept, but held open the wrong way. It isn't a place where the Mill paused, but a place where the Mill paused *and then went on without it*. On the far side, the roar sounds a fraction older, because that is where the older roar went.
