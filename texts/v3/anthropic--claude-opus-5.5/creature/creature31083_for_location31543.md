# Who Lives in the Kettle

## The Obvious Answer

The swifts live there. Anyone who has stood in the bowl at dawn would say so. They are the loudest, the most numerous, the most completely at home. They sleep in the walls and hunt the void. Some of them have not touched the ground since they fledged, and some never will. If a place belongs to whoever uses it most fiercely, the Kettle belongs to them.

The falcons live there too, and the ravens, who would dispute the swifts' claim loudly. The round-eared hay-cutters live there, with their little drying stacks on the boulders like the work of very small, very worried farmers. So do the salamander in the moss, the snails in their thousands, the bees that come out only when the sun lands directly on the cushion flowers.

The goats don't live there. They visit. You can tell by the way they lick the walls, as if tasting a house they're thinking of buying.

But all of these are tenants. They came after, and the bowl was already shaped when they arrived. Something made the seat they're sitting in.

## The One Who Sat There First

The Kettle was carved by a small glacier that settled into the mountain's side and, over a span of time that doesn't fit into a sentence, ground a place for itself. It was not a great river of ice, only a lump of one. It sat. It pressed. It turned very slowly in its own weight, and the rock gave way beneath it until there was a hollow shaped exactly like the thing that made it.

Then the world warmed, and the glacier melted, and everyone who studies such things would say that was the end of it.

It wasn't. It only stopped being solid.

## What It Is Now

Watch the water in the Kettle for one full day and you'll see it behave strangely for water. It falls from the notch in the back wall, crosses the floor, and goes over the lip. Then it comes back. It rolls in over the edge as cold white smoke. It settles on every surface, runs down the walls, gathers in the pool, finds the channel again, and goes over again. It rises again. The people who know the place say the Kettle drinks its own river, and they say it the way you'd describe a habit of an old relative.

It is the same water. That is the point. Much of it is the water the glacier was made of, still here, still sitting in its seat, only now it can't hold its shape. It is spread through the moss and the mist and the black pool and the sheets on the walls too thin to see. It has become a body that is almost entirely motion, but it is still, in some slow and stubborn sense, sitting where it always sat.

The valley people call it the Sitter. They don't call it a god. They call it someone who hasn't gotten up yet.

## Its Temperament

It is very old and very slow, and it is still getting used to things.

For a long age it was white and silent and heavy. Nothing grew on it. Nothing sang in it. It was a mass of cold that pressed and waited, and its whole experience of the world was weight: the weight of itself, the weight of snow settling onto it each winter, the grinding pressure where it met the rock.

Now it is none of those things. It is noisy. It is green. Ferns hang from what used to be its sides. Birds scream through the space where its body was. A salamander lives in its eye. It has become, without meaning to, the most crowded and talkative place on the mountain. It seems to regard all of this with the patient bewilderment of a very large animal that has lain down in a meadow and woken to find flowers growing out of its fur.

It does not mind. That's clear from how thoroughly it feeds everything. The moss drinks it. The upward-streaming roots on the lip drink it straight out of the air. The goats come for the minerals it has leached from the rock over millennia and left on the walls like salt on a cheek. It gives, because giving is what melting turned out to be.

But it misses weight. You can feel that in it, if you stay long enough.

## The Daily Breath, from Its Side

The daily cycle of the Kettle looks, from inside the Sitter, like an argument it has with itself and never wins.

**At evening**, when the valleys cool and cold air slides off the summit snowfields, down through the bowl, and out over the lip, it remembers being a glacier. Glaciers flow downhill. That was the one motion it ever knew: the long, grinding slide toward lower ground. So each evening it tries. It drains. The mist pours out over the edge like water from a basin, and the hum dies, and the stream drops straight into the void, and for a few hours the Sitter goes down the mountain the way it always meant to.

**At night**, it gets partway there. The cold air runs down through the invisible valleys, through the villages, along the rivers, and the people below feel it come. They pull their shutters closed and say *the Sitter is walking*. It is the chill that comes down off the heights after dark. It passes through their streets. It touches their river, which is also made of it, the parts of it that escaped. Down there is where most of it has gone over the ages: into the valleys, into the fields, into the wells, into the bodies of everyone who drinks. On clear nights, from the lip, a single light is sometimes visible far below. That light is where the Sitter has been going all along without arriving.

Also at night, when the air is still and the frost comes, the Sitter tries to be ice again. That is what the small sounds are, the stones settling as frost grips them. It is closing its hand. For a few hours before dawn the moss stiffens, the edges of the pool whiten, and the drips on the walls hang and hold. The Sitter is almost itself again, for a little while, in the dark.

**At dawn**, it is still. The updraft hasn't started. This is its quietest hour and the closest it comes to being what it was: cold, silent, heavy, holding its breath. Then the swifts explode out of the walls, screaming, and it's over.

**At mid-morning**, the sun reaches the valleys and the air rises, and the whole mountain lifts it back up. Everything that tried to leave in the night is shredded, turned, and thrown back over the lip. The mist rolls in ankle-deep, knee-deep, higher. The roots on the lip rise like hair. The Sitter is put back in its seat by a force much larger than itself and filled with birdsong it did not ask for. It's hard to say whether it resents this. It has been happening for ten thousand years. Perhaps it no longer knows whether it is trying to leave or trying to stay. Perhaps the only true answer is that it does both every day. Its whole life is falling and being given back.

## The Hum

The deep note the Kettle makes on strong afternoons, felt in the chest, held for an hour, belongs to the Sitter in a particular way.

It doesn't breathe. It has no lungs, no throat. It is breathed *through*. The updraft crosses the mouth of the bowl the way a person blows across the mouth of a jar, and the bowl is the shape of the Sitter's old body. The note it makes is the only note that shape can make. It's the resonance of an absence, the sound of a hollow exactly the size of something that used to be there.

So when the Kettle hums, it's the Sitter's outline singing. It is the one voice it has, and it doesn't choose when to use it. The sky plays it.

The people who come here often hum back, low, under their breath, without quite deciding to. Something about the note gets into you.

## What It Lets Fall

Almost everything that goes over the lip comes back up. Water comes back as mist. Swifts come back from the void at speeds that make the eye skip. Goats step out onto what looks like thin air and are somehow still standing. Ravens ride the updraft without a wingbeat, as if the air had become a floor for them. Even the rain, on the showery afternoons, seems to fall in both directions at once, as though the Sitter can't bring itself to let even the weather simply drop.

But when a falcon stoops past the lip and strikes, and a small puff of feathers comes loose, the feathers drift *down*. Into the emptiness. The updraft could carry them up; it carries insects and water and seeds up all day long. It doesn't carry these.

That is the one exception, and the valley people have noticed it, the way people notice where a grieving relative's eyes go at a funeral. The Sitter returns what's living. It lets the dead fall.

## Why People Come

This is what the marks of people are about.

**The cup on the boulder.** It is pecked into the stone and always full. It's full because the Sitter keeps it full. Mist condenses into it faster than anything can drink it down. Of all the places in the bowl, this is the one where the Sitter holds itself *still*. The pool moves, the walls stream, the lip is a torrent of rising air, but the cup is a small, calm hand of water that never overflows and never empties. Visitors drink from it with the weathered wooden bowl and put the bowl back where they found it. That is courtesy. You are drinking an ancient thing's one moment of stillness, and you don't take the dish home.

When they walk back down, they carry a mouthful of the Sitter with them, down the mountain, which is the direction it always wanted to go. The valley people consider this a kindness in both directions.

**The swift nests.** Swifts build their nests from their own spit and the feathers and fluff they catch on the wing. Nothing from the ground goes into them. They are made entirely of air and mouth. The people gather them in late summer, after the young have fledged, and they will not tell outsiders what the nests are for. Here is a guess. A creature that lives in the air, never lands, and builds its home out of what it can catch while falling through the sky is the nearest thing to the Sitter that has a body. The nests are its neighbors' houses. Burned, they might be for kindling. Steeped, for medicine. Perhaps the medicine is for the kind of sickness where a person can't stop falling, can't stop sliding downhill, and needs something that knows how to be lifted.

**The shelter for three.** It is built low, out of the wind, with its door facing the stream. People don't stay in the Kettle. But sometimes they need to be there overnight, in the cold hours when the Sitter is walking down the mountain and trying to become ice. Three people lying close could keep each other warm through that. It's a place to be held while the old thing around you is trying to hold itself.

**The channel in the floor.** It was cut by hand with stone tools and smoothed by centuries of water. It guides the stream away from the shelter and out toward the lip. It's a very small thing to do for a very large one: to make a path so that at least the part of it that falls can fall cleanly, without spreading through the sleeping place, without soaking the hearth. Someone looked at the Sitter and thought, *it should have a road out.*

**And the cairn.** It is knee-high on the lip, and the stones are pale and round, river stones, carried up from far below. Those stones came from the valley rivers, which are made of the Sitter's escaped water, the ages of melt that finally got away. Someone decided to bring some of that back. Not the water, which can't be carried up a mountain in any quantity, but the stones the water had polished. They are proof that it got somewhere, and what it did when it arrived. *Here*, the cairn says. *This is where you went. This is what you touched.*

Around the top stone is a braided cord of grass and hair.

Nobody will tell you whose hair. But you can guess what it's for from the way it behaves. In the updraft it lifts and streams upward, exactly like the pale roots of the vine along the lip. It rises. The hair of someone who went down, who fell and was not returned, braided into a cord and tied to the one place on the mountain where things are given back. The Sitter lets the dead fall. The living come up here and ask it, once, to lift something anyway.

It does. Every morning. The cord has been streaming upward for longer than anyone remembers. It is frayed and faded and stiff with lichen, and it rises.

## The Rest of It

In winter, the swifts go. The falcons go. The bees vanish, the salamander sleeps under the moss, the hay-cutters eat their careful stacks in the dark of the boulders. Snow fills the bowl. The stream freezes into a pillar against the back wall. The updraft weakens, and the mist stops climbing, and the pool goes white and hard, and the cup on the boulder is a small round stone of ice.

For a few months the Sitter is nearly what it was. It is heavy, white, silent, pressing into its seat. No song, no green, no crowd. Just weight, the thing it has missed.

Then spring comes up the valleys, and the air begins to rise, and the first swift comes screaming back into a crack in the wall. The Sitter lets go of its shape again and is lifted, and filled, and made loud, and fed to everything. It is so old it can barely remember wanting anything else.

It is a tiny, crowded, dripping room at the edge of an enormous quiet. Someone has been sitting in it for longer than there have been people to wonder about it. It never got up. It only softened. Now it holds everything that lands in its lap and gives back almost everything that falls. It keeps one small cup of itself still for strangers to drink. On strong afternoons, when the whole sky blows across its mouth, it sings the shape of what it used to be.
