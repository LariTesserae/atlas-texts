# What Lives in the Unfinished Range

## The Sourness

The account says nothing lives here but the plants. That is nearly true.

Go back to the chord. It is vast and stacked and *faintly sour*, grinding against itself where the harmonics nearly align and don't quite. It is easy to take that sourness as a flaw, the way a new instrument is always slightly out of true until it settles. But the chord has had a thousand years to settle, and many thousands before that, and it has not. The sourness stays. It moves around. Some of it is alive.

Old tuners had a word for this. When you tune an instrument so that most of its intervals come out sweet, the error you couldn't eliminate collects in one bad interval, and that interval howls. It throbs. Two tones that almost agree set up a slow *wah-wah-wah* between them, a pulse that is neither note and belongs only to their disagreement. The tuners called it the wolf.

There are wolves in the Unfinished Range. They are not animals. The account is right that there are no animals, and never have been. A wolf here is a beat: a throbbing interference between two overtones that ought to agree and refuse to. It has no body because it doesn't need one. It lives in the argument.

## Finding One

You cannot hear a wolf, any more than you can hear anything else here. You find one the way you find everything on the mountain: through the plants, through your bones, and through the stone under your hands.

Stand on a node line long enough and you learn its ordinary tremor, the faint, even quiver of the fronds as they lean toward the loud rock. Then one day the quiver changes. The fronds begin to shiver in two rhythms at once, a fast tremble riding on a slow sway, as if each leaf were being pulled by two hands. The effect moves along the lattice in a slow wave, crossing the slope over hours. Seed-heads in its path burst out of season, one species and then another, in little glittering puffs that don't follow the usual fuse-burn sequence. They come in patches, in a blotch that drifts.

Then you feel it yourself. Your molars ache on one side and then the other, alternating. Your balance develops a faint seasick lean that corrects itself and returns, corrects and returns. The black glass under your palm, cold a moment ago, is slightly warm, because rock that is made to disagree with itself heats as it rubs. In the deep shadows the rime of frost sublimates away faster, leaving dark patches in a shape that looks like nothing and moves.

Then comes the worst part, or the most intimate. Your heart tries to match it. The wolf throbs at some slow rate, one beat every few breaths, and your pulse drifts toward that rate the way two pendulum clocks on the same wall drift into step. It takes a while, and you can resist it by moving. If you sit still, you find after an hour that your heartbeat and the wolf's beat are the same, and you can no longer tell which one you are feeling.

That is the "high thin harmonic, almost melodic," that the account mentions drifting in for an hour and fading. It isn't a harmonic. It is a wolf passing through, and your body noticed before you did.

## What One Is

It is worth being precise, because a wolf is a strange kind of thing to be.

It exists only where two overtones of the mountain's note nearly coincide. The mountain is enormous and its note has dozens of overtones, each strongest in some regions of rock and weakest in others. So a wolf has a location: a patch of slope, a length of ridge, a curve of cliff, wherever its two parent tones overlap strongly enough to disagree audibly. It is spread across that region like a bruise. At its center the throb is strong and the fronds shiver hard. At its edges it fades into ordinary chord.

It lives on a knife-edge, which is fitting for a place where every edge cuts.

If its two tones ever came into perfect agreement, the beat would slow, and slow, and stop. Two voices singing the same pitch make no wolf. Consonance is death. If its two tones drifted too far apart, the beat would quicken into a buzz, then a rasp, and then cease to be a beat at all. It would come apart into two separate, innocent notes with nothing living between them. Too much difference is also death.

So a wolf spends its whole existence leaning. It presses on its two tones, very gently, holding them near each other but never together. It cannot sing; it has no voice of its own. What it has is influence over the voices it lives between, a push on the vibrating stone that is too small to measure in any single second and adds up over centuries.

Whether a wolf knows it is doing this is impossible to say. It behaves the way a flame behaves toward air. It moves toward what sustains it and away from what would end it. It avoids resolution. It drifts across the slopes toward regions where its tones overlap more richly. Over many observations it also does one thing that is harder to explain as mere physics. It turns toward new sounds.

Strike two slabs together near a wolf and, within an hour, the throb will be closer. It has come to taste the strike, the brief new tone you added to the chord, looking for something to almost agree with. It finds nothing that lasts. Your strike fades, and the wolf drifts back to its old argument. But it came.

## The Fraction

The account promised to return to the fraction of a tone. This is where it belongs.

The note is the same note it was a thousand years ago, give or take a fraction. The drift is small and slow and relentless, and it is why the node lines crawl across the slopes, why the forests die back behind and push out ahead, and why the Silence House is failing. The account gives no cause for it.

The cause is the wolves. Each one leans on its two tones, holding off agreement, and the whole chord bends very slightly under all that leaning. The mountain's note is not drifting on its own. It is being pushed by the things that live inside it, the way a riverbed shifts because of what swims in it.

Look again at the strata. The account says each band in the cliff is a period of slightly different pitch, frozen into stone. That makes the cliffs a record of the pushing. Most bands are broad and even, laid down during long quiet stretches when the wolves were few or balanced against each other. Here and there a band is thin, a little discolored, faintly wavy along its length. That band is a wolf, or more precisely the centuries during which a particular wolf lived near this rock and bent its note. Where the wavy band stops, the wolf ended. Read a cliff face carefully and you are reading a genealogy of disagreements.

A harder thought follows. The mountain is trying to finish. That is what the intended rock is, and what the faint outlines and wrong shadows are: the shape the note implies, the shape it would settle into if it ever came to rest. A chord that resolved would know its final form. The haze would condense and the ledges would complete. The peaks would come down out of the violet air, grain by grain and then all at once. Some day after that the rain might come, and the edges would begin to soften, and the mountain would become an ordinary mountain, finished and old.

The wolves prevent this. They do not mean to, if they mean anything. They are only alive, and staying alive means keeping the chord from resolving, and a chord that never resolves never lets the stone decide what it is. The range is unfinished because it is inhabited.

## Birth

New wolves come from the future.

As the mountain grows, new overtones enter the chord. Each new spire, as it thickens out of haze into stone, adds its own resonance. Most new tones settle in harmlessly. Occasionally, though, a coming spire grazes an existing overtone. It lands near it without landing on it, and a beat begins between them. Most such beats are just beats. They throb for a while and fade as the new rock finds its pitch. A few catch. They begin to lean, and then there is one more wolf.

The wrong shadows mark where this will happen. The long blue shadow lying across the snowless slope, cast by a spire that won't exist for four hundred years, lies across the ground where that spire's tone will someday meet another. The node-forests grow unevenly under the wrong shadows, thinner and more restless. The plants there shiver sometimes for no present reason.

Then there is the echo. Strike stone near a node and feel the answer come back late from empty air, from the outline of a cliff that hasn't formed. Most of the time the answer is only your strike, returned. Sometimes the answer comes back *wobbling*. It is your strike and not quite your strike, carrying a faint slow throb that you did not put into it.

The honest reading is that something is already there in the unmade cliff. It doesn't live yet, but it will. The sound went out, found the shape of the future, found the wolf that will be born in it, and came back carrying its pulse.

## How Many

They are few, and they are far apart, like everything here.

At any one time the basin holds perhaps a dozen. They keep their distance from each other, because two wolves close together disturb each other's arguments. When two do meet, one of two things happens. Sometimes they cancel. Their leaning opposes, their tones resolve, and both go quiet within a day, leaving a stretch of slope where the fronds lie strangely still. Sometimes they merge into a single, larger, slower wolf that spans two ridges and throbs once every few minutes.

The old wolves are all like this: vast, slow, and dim. They have absorbed many smaller ones over the millennia, and their beats have grown so wide and gradual that the fronds hardly register them. You feel an old wolf in your inner ear as a gradual tilt of the whole world that takes a quarter hour to swing one way and another quarter hour to swing back.

There is one older than all of them.

The account describes the fundamental as a pressure, a *slow pulse in the gut, one throb every few seconds*. But a pure tone does not pulse. A single note, however low, is steady. A pulse is a beat, and a beat takes two.
