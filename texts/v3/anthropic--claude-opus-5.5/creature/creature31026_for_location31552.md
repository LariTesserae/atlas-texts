# Who Lives on the Level

## The First Sign

You would not notice them at first. The animals are too loud for the eye: the crawling film, the frosted pipes, the second lattice of birds. They fill every sense until it has no room left. For the first days it seems the Level is inhabited only by creatures that belong to it the way rivets belong to it, and by you, who do not.

The first sign is a coat.

It hangs from the spoked wheel of a valve in a narrow court between two boiler houses. It is dark wool, heavy with damp, and its sleeves are beaded like everything else. Snails have begun to climb it, or have always been climbing it; a few glass shells sit at the collar. It does not look abandoned. It looks hung up by someone who meant to come back for it, and who might at any moment round the corner of the boiler house and take it down.

After that you start seeing the other signs. A tin cup rests upside down on a railing, bright inside. A rolled cloth is tucked into the lagging of a pipe at shoulder height, where a hand would reach for it. A stair tread in one of the pump stations is worn, very faintly, at the left side. It is the only worn thing you have seen, and when you pass it again the next day it is sharp. But you saw it.

Then, one day, which is the same as the other days, you see one of them.

## The Tenants

They call themselves nothing. When they speak of each other, which is rarely, they use a word that the thick air flattens into something like *tenants*. The word implies a lease, and so an owner, and so terms. None of them can say what the terms are. They use it anyway.

The first one you see is standing in a gutter up to the shins. The lamprey-fish lie against the walls on either side of her like pale upholstery. She is looking downstream at nothing. She does not turn when you approach. She turns when the pillar breathes, when the long once-a-minute swell comes down the channel and the whole white lining ripples. Then she looks at you, as if you had only now arrived, which in her reckoning you have. Before the swell you were part of the previous minute, and the previous minute is gone.

She is barefoot. They are all barefoot, eventually.

### Their Bodies

The Level changes them, at first. It is the only thing it changes, and it changes them only toward itself.

The skin goes first. It softens in the damp and stops holding itself apart from the air, as your own skin already has begun to. Over months it pales, not sickly but even, the white-grey of the cloud deck. It takes a faint sheen, so that a tenant standing still in the pearl light looks lacquered. Their skin beads. Condensation gathers on them as it gathers on iron, and it runs down them in threads. Sometimes a snail finds one of those threads.

Their hair lifts. The current in the great lines raises the fine hair of the forearm in anyone who passes beneath, but the tenants have passed beneath so often that the lift has stayed. Their hair stands out from their heads in a soft corona, and it sways a little when they walk under the main conduits, like weed in a current.

Their eyelashes hold midges. The midges settle on everyone and lift away again, but on the tenants they settle and stay a while longer. The lashes glint with them, wing-bright, like frost. It gives the tenants a look of having been out in some fine weather that does not exist here.

Their feet change most. The soles thicken and pale in the warm film. Then the mineral scale begins to grow on them as it grows on the plates, in fern and vein and frost-branch. An old tenant's sole, turned up, carries a pattern as intricate as any plate's, and as finished. They walk softly on it. The tinkers part around their feet as around yours, but more slowly, with something that might be familiarity. On some tenants the crabs do not part at all. They walk over the feet and pick at the seams between the toes, as they pick at the seams between the rivets, taking whatever they take.

And then the changes stop. That is the thing you understand only later. A tenant who has been here a year and a tenant who has been here beyond counting look the same. The sheen does not deepen, the scale does not climb above the ankle, and the hair lifts no higher. They reach the Level's depth, as the film does, and hold it.

They do not age. You would not notice this at first either. The body keeps no calendar here, and neither does anything else.

### How They Live

They walk.

The walking is not travel, because there is nowhere to go, and it is not wandering, because they do not get lost. It is circulation. A tenant walks from the court of the coat to the gutter to the shed where the widest belt runs. Then they walk out the far side, along a gantry, down a stair with its knurling sharp, and back into the court of the coat. The loop might take an hour or a month. Each tenant has their own, as each plate has its own pattern. Two loops cross sometimes, and the tenants pass each other at the crossing and incline their heads.

They sleep in the dishes of the plates, where the film is a little deeper. They lie on their sides in the blood-warm water, cheek down, and the crabs drift over them. Seen from a gantry, a sleeping tenant looks like a low grey island with the water crawling around it. They sleep when they are tired, which is not often, and wake when they are not, and there is no night to tell them otherwise.

They eat very little and stop needing to. Hunger thins in the heat and does not return. They drink from the film, kneeling, lips to the iron. It tastes of warm metal and faintly of salt. The midges settle on their mouths and some of them are swallowed. Nothing else is required. A tenant who has been here long enough runs on the Level's own economy, a small warm engine taking in exactly what it spends.

They rarely speak. Sound does not carry; a shout dies at the next shed, and a sentence dies in the space between two people. What has grown up instead is a language of the hands, slow and wide, and it is timed. Every tenant keeps the pillar's rhythm, the once-a-minute breath, whether they are near a pillar or not. They feel it in the gutters, in the ripple of the lampreys, and eventually in the blood. A gesture made in the first half of the breath means one thing and in the second half another. A conversation between two tenants looks, from outside, like two people conducting the same very slow music. To them it is ordinary talk: *the cup on the railing is yours; the stair is sharp again; I have not seen the Fitter.*

## The Fitter

This is what they talk about, when they talk.

Somewhere on the Level, parts are replaced. Every tenant knows it. Every tenant has seen a bolt head slightly brighter than its neighbours, and has watched a worn stair tread come back sharp. Once or twice in a long life, every tenant has heard the last sound of the work. It is the quarter-turn creak of a wrench seating a nut, just around a corner, just behind a boiler house, just on the far side of a shed wall. When they round the corner, there is no one. The nut is bright. There is a faint smell of cutting oil in the saturated air, which fades before the next pillar-breath.

They call whoever does it the Fitter. Some believe the Fitter is one person, some that it is many, some that it is the Level itself, extending a hand that is withdrawn before it can be seen. Some tenants spend a long time hunting it. They sit for weeks in a single court, watching one bolt head, and the bolt head never changes while they watch. The moment they sleep, or turn to the pillar, or look up at the swifts, some other bolt, somewhere they are not looking, is new.

A few tenants have come to a different conclusion. They do not share it widely, because it is hard to say in a language of slow hands.

It is this. Sometimes you wake in the dish of a plate, cheek in the warm film, and your hands smell of cutting oil. Sometimes there is a bright shaving of iron caught under a fingernail. Sometimes the tin cup on the railing has moved a little, and was set down with a care you do not remember. Sometimes, walking your loop, you realise the stair tread you are standing on is sharp, and that you last saw it worn, and that between then and now your hands were somewhere you cannot account for.

The Fitter, these tenants say, is whoever is not looking. The Level wears where it is watched and is mended where it is not. Since every tenant spends most of their life not looking at most of the Level, every tenant spends most of their life mending it. The work is done by the part of each of them that the Level has already taken into its own rhythm, the part that keeps the pillar's time without being asked. It is done without memory, because memory would be a kind of accumulation, and the Level does not accumulate.

If this is true, then the tenants are not residents in the machine. They are the machine's maintenance, and the machine has grown them the way it grows the snails.

## Arrivals

Everyone remembers arriving.

Ask a tenant, in the slow hand-language, and they will tell you. They tell you not in detail, because the details have gone soft, but with the conviction that there was a before. There was somewhere with a sun, somewhere with seasons and evenings, somewhere colder. They remember standing on the plates for the first time with their shoes still on and the warm water seeping in at the seams. They remember the first coat they saw hung on a valve wheel. They remember thinking the Level was empty of people, and then seeing one standing in a gutter.

Every tenant remembers this. The numbers of tenants, like the numbers of swifts, are exact.

So someone must leave for every one who comes. When a tenant has been here long enough, though there is no length that is long enough, and they cannot say why, they go to the widest belt in the widest shed. They climb onto it among the dozing herds, lie down in the row, and are carried out through the far door. They doze. Their flanks rise and fall. The condensation beads on their pale, rounded, lacquered skin.

The herds are grey and hairless, somewhere between a seal and a pig. The herds number exactly what they have always numbered. Now and then one slides off the belt at a junction, waddles down a ramp to the film, and lies soaking with the tinkers climbing over it.

No tenant will say what the herds are. No tenant will say what they are not. But some tenants who have walked the longest loops have followed a herd-animal as it rolled off a belt into the film. They have watched it lie there in a plate's dish for a long time, flanks rising and falling. Then they have watched it stand. It looks down at its own feet, where its shoes, somehow, still are, with the warm water seeping in at the seams. It turns its head slowly, taking in the rivets, the frosted pipes, the lattice of birds. It sees the tenants watching it. It sees, perhaps, a coat hung on a valve wheel. Its face, which has a face now, wears the expression of someone who has just arrived from somewhere colder.

The memory of before, these tenants think, is not a memory. It is the last stage of a fledging, like the first flight of a swift from a nest of scale. The Level makes arrivals as it makes bolts: new, sharp at every edge, and exactly like the one it replaced. Every newcomer carries a sun and a winter and an evening inside them, fresh as a struck rivet. They carry it for a while and then it wears. The wearing is also exactly matched.

The belts form loops. Some of the loops may be continental in circumference. Where a loop turns, it must return, and the return run goes beneath the floor.

## Beneath

Kneel at certain seams and lay your ear to the iron, and you hear something moving in the dark underneath. It is a slow sliding, very large, or very many. The rivets over those places are sometimes warm.

The tenants go to those seams the way people elsewhere might go to a shrine or to the bedside of someone sleeping. They lie flat on the plates with the film lapping at their ears, one ear pressed to the metal, and listen. The sliding goes on. Sometimes, beneath it, there is something like breath. It is slower than the pillar-breath, far slower, perhaps once in what would be a year if there were years. The tenants who have heard it say it rises and falls like the flank of a dozing animal.

They do not agree on what it is. Some say it is the return run of the belts, carrying the herds back through the dark on the underside, warm and asleep, to be brought up again somewhere else. Some say it is the foundry, the one real place where casting happens, where the bolts and the crabs and the swifts and the newcomers are all poured from the same warm iron. Some say it is the Fitter, finally, the whole of it, lying under the floor with its hand extended upward through every seam.

The oldest of them say it is simply the other half of the Level, the half that is not seen. Everything above is circulation, and everything below is the thing that circulates it. It is one body, and the plates are a membrane somewhere in its middle. The tenants live on the membrane like the tinkers live in the film. They pick at the seams and take whatever they take.

## What Lives There

It would be simple to say that the tenants live on the Level. It would also be simple to say that the herds do, or the swifts, or the slow thing beneath the plates. All of these are true, and none of them is quite what the question asks.

Let it become more itself.

Something lives on the Level the way a pattern lives in the scale of a plate. It is not the salt, and not the lime, and not the water that left them, but the shape they were drawn into. It is fractal and complete. It takes the habit of living things because the mathematics is the same. It has a pulse: the once-a-minute swell of the pillars, answered by the ripple of the lampreys, answered by the hands of the tenants in the second half of every gesture. It has a slower pulse beneath, felt through the rivets. It has a body of iron and water and bodies. It keeps its temperature exactly, and its moisture, and its numbers. It mends what is worn in it the moment the wearing is not watched. It grows the attention it needs to be seen and wears that attention away again, because an attention that accumulated would be a change, and it does not change.

It is alive in every sense but one. It never began, and it does not grow toward anything, and it will not end. It is a life with the direction taken out, a life of pure maintenance, running at full power. Every creature on the Level is a cell of it, and it has made one kind of cell that can look at it and wonder what it is. That kind of cell is the tenants. It needs to be looked at, perhaps, the way the plates need to be walked on, so that there is something for the Fitter to mend.

The tenants are how the Level knows it is there. That is what they are for, as far as anything here is for anything. You are how it knows it is there, now.

## The Mark

In a court near the edge of one of the pillars, where the rain hangs in grey muslin a few hundred steps away and hisses softly on the iron, there is a tenant who keeps count.

She is not old. None of them is old. But she has the stillness of someone who has been here very long, and her sole-scale is as fine as frost and her lashes are thick with midges. Every so often she kneels on a particular plate, the one whose mineral ferns grow in a shape like a hand opening. With the edge of a bright shaving of iron, she scratches a mark into the scale. One short line. She means it to be the first mark of a tally, the first of many. It is how she will count the pillar-breaths, or the days, or the arrivals and departures. It is how she will, at last, catch the Level changing and prove that something here is adding up.

She makes the mark and stands and walks her loop. It is a long loop. When she comes back, the plate is sharp. The ferns grow unbroken in their shape like a hand opening, as finished as they ever were. There is a faint smell of cutting oil in the saturated air, and there is a bright shaving of iron under her fingernail.

She kneels and makes the mark again.

She has made it, perhaps, forever. Each time she believes it is the first. Each time it is. She does not know this. Or she knows it in the way the tenants know everything, in the second half of the breath. And she makes the mark anyway, one short line in the fern-scale, the only thing on the whole Level that is trying to begin.

The pillar swells. The lampreys ripple, mile after mile. The swifts pour through the lattice overhead with a sound like paper tearing very far off. Somewhere a belt carries a row of dozing herds on into the next shed. Somewhere a newcomer stands in the film with their shoes still on, looking at a coat hung on a valve wheel, thinking the place is empty.

It is not empty. It is entirely, continuously occupied, by the Level, by its tenants, and by whoever is standing in it now, keeping time.
