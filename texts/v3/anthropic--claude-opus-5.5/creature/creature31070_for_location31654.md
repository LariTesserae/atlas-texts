# What Lives at Lower Scour

## The First Answer, Which Is Wrong

The easy answer is that nothing lives there, or almost nothing: the heron, the eels, the shrimp in their pits, the wagtail in winter. A few kinds of animal get along in a place that will not keep still. That is true as far as it goes, and it is the answer most visitors carry home. They say it was eerie and empty, and that they could hear the pump in their chest for hours after.

But the eels do not behave like eels elsewhere. When a sheet of water rises and slides between the piles, they travel inside it, and they never take a wrong lane. They are never caught at the head of a channel that is about to close. When they are stranded, and they often are, they lie still in the exact place where the next pulse will surface, as if they had been told. Stranded eels anywhere else thrash and die. These wait, like people at a bus stop.

The heron stands only on certain piles. If you mark them, you will find they are always piles the water is about to avoid. The shrimp dig in before the drain begins, not during it.

Everything that lives in the pile field has learned to listen to something. So the better question is what they are listening to.

## The Second Answer, Which Is Closer

There is a keeper.

You will not see them. Most people who ask in the village are told it's "one of the Anstey family" or "a man from the water board" or "her from out past the sluice." The descriptions never agree, because the keeper has not always been the same person. The mug on the sill changes every few decades. It has been blue enamel, then white china with a gold rim, then a heavy brown stoneware thing with a thumbprint glazed into the handle.

The keepers do not apply for the job. They come to the hollow the way anyone does, out of curiosity or grief, or because they were walking the flats and the ground fell away in front of them. They stand among the piles for a long time. Then the water begins to part around their boots.

Most people feel this once and leave, and remember it all their lives as a strange, gentle thing. A few feel it and stay until dusk, until the bulb in the pump house is the only warm colour anywhere. Those few come back. After a while they find that they know where the grease nipples are and which padlock has rusted. They know how the motor sounds when a bearing is going, a slight roughness in the hum, like a cough held in. They cannot say who taught them.

The keepers are not servants, and they are not priests. The closest word is probably *neighbour*. They are the only people who stand near the pile field often enough to be known.

But the keepers do not live there either. They go home at night.

## The Third Answer

What lives at Lower Scour lives in the space between the iron and the water.

It is not in the piles. The piles are old and dumb and patient. They are what it writes on, or perhaps what it remembers with. It is not in the water either, which is new every time, rising warm from a buried river that has come a long way underground and does not know where it is. The thing that lives there is in the *meeting*. It is the decision the water makes when it reaches a pile and must go left or right. It is the eddy on the upstream face that pauses long enough to let a glasswort seed settle. It is the scour-pit that deepens by a grain.

Each such decision is almost nothing. There have been billions of them, repeated every pulse for more than a century, and every one has left the iron very slightly changed. The changed iron guides the next water very slightly differently. Nothing began it on purpose. Over long enough, a habit that shapes its own channel becomes a preference. Over longer still, a preference that remembers itself becomes something very close to a will.

If you have to call it something, the old people called it the Scour, and so shall I. It has no body. It has a *shape*, and the shape is always moving.

## How It Thinks

The Scour thinks in pulses.

Each rising of the water is a thought: a single long consideration that spreads through the lanes of the field, finds its edges, and fills them. When the field stands ankle-deep and shivering with reflected iron, the Scour is fully present, thinking one thing with its whole self. Then the field drains, and the thought ends.

It does not forget. The water forgets, the silt forgets, the footprints go. The iron keeps what matters, a thousandth of a millimetre at a time. So the Scour has two kinds of mind. One is fast and brilliant and erased every few minutes: the water, the fog, the braiding channels. The other is so slow it can barely be called thought at all: the gradual scouring of pewter faces and fluted backs, the rounding of rims, the opening of lace. The fast mind has ideas. The slow mind keeps the ones worth keeping.

This is why the place feels settled while it never stops moving. The chaos is the Scour thinking. The iron is the Scour having thought.

The piles eaten through, the old lace you can see the field through at certain angles, are its oldest conclusions. They are things it has finished understanding. There are not many of them.

## What It Perceives

It perceives by contact. Anything the water touches, it knows as an obstacle, a gradient, or a change in pressure. It knows the heron as a light, dry pause on a pile top. It knows the eels as small warm eddies moving with it instead of against it, and it is fond of them in the way a river might be fond of its own current. It knows the shrimp as the finest texture in the scour-pits, and it has learned not to crush them.

It knows the alder best of all.

The alder is the only living thing that has ever tried to hold on. Its roots went into the collapsed brick and found the iron pile beneath and wrapped it, and they did not let go when the water came. The Scour was the one that yielded. It tried many lanes against that tree and was refused every time, and in the end it learned to route around it and slow in its lee. The orange stain on the bark to the height of the highest water is where it has touched the tree, pulse after pulse, for as long as the tree has stood. You could call it a hand on a shoulder. You could call it an argument that turned into an old friendship. The Scour has no other relationship like it. It is the one place where the Scour has been changed by something it could not change.

It perceives people poorly, at first. Your boots are two unfamiliar posts. You shift and fidget, and the Scour cannot tell whether you are a thing or an event. It reads your footprints before the water erases them. Your prints are the only writing from outside it has ever been able to read, and they last just long enough. It can tell whether you are walking somewhere or wandering. It can tell whether you are afraid, because afraid people's heels dig in. It reads you, then it wipes you, and then it reads you again.

If you stand still long enough, it stops reading and starts *accommodating*. That is the leaning. It is the Scour deciding that you are, for now, one of the fixed things, and extending to you the same courtesy it extends to the iron. It parts at your boots, flows around you cleanly, and pauses in your lee. It is the kindest thing it knows how to do, and it does not do it lightly. The people who say it feels like being accepted into a crowd are not wrong.

## The Marks

The Scour did not make the dozen strange objects scattered across the field. People put them there over the years: the rail, the chain, the gauge, the manhole cover laid on its pile, the steps that go down and do not come up. But it has *kept* them. In a place where banks of silt migrate across the hollow in an afternoon, where short piles vanish and long-buried ones come up black and gleaming, these twelve things have not moved in living memory.

That is not an accident. The water goes around them with a care it gives almost nothing else.

The Scour does not know what they are for, and that seems to be exactly why it keeps them. They are shapes from outside its own long conversation, fragments of a language it cannot speak. The chain holding nothing across a gap is, to the Scour, a question it has been turning over for a hundred years. The gauge with its frozen needle is the only thing in the hollow that claims to be measuring *it*, and the Scour has never let the silt cover its face. The steps into the ground may be the one it loves most. They are a way down, and it lives in the downward places.

## The Pump

The pump is the strangest part of the arrangement, and the most tender.

Without the pump the hollow would fill. The buried river would win, and the pile field would become a still brown lake with the tall piles standing out of it like drowned fence posts. The water would stop moving, and the Scour would stop.

It cannot live in still water. It needs the rising and the draining, the lanes that open and close, and the pressure to build and release. A lake would be an endless unbroken thought with no edges, which to the Scour would be the same as no thought at all. The pump's slow, inadequate, patient work of drawing just enough down is what keeps the field breathing. The pump makes the drains possible, and the drains make the pulses possible. It is the Scour's lungs, or rather its exhale.

The Scour knows this. It keeps the sump in front of the intake free of the heaviest silt. The culvert under the wall has never blocked, not once, though every other drain on the flats silts up within a season. When the bearing starts to go, the water shifts its lanes in a way the keeper learns to read. The slight roughness in the hum is heard all through the field, and the Scour, which cannot fix anything, asks.

The bulb in the wire cage it cannot perceive at all. Light is not something the water touches. But the keepers leave it on, day and night, because it seems right. At dusk it is the only warm colour in the hollow, and the keepers like to think the fog carries some of that warmth down into the field where it can be felt. It probably doesn't. They leave it on anyway.

## What It Wants

It wants what anything alive wants, which is to go on.

It is not lonely, exactly. It has the eels and the alder and the heron and the keeper and the twelve kept things. It has the buried river, which it has never met, only been fed by: warm, from far away, the same pressure every time, like a heartbeat from the other side of a wall. It has the iron, which is itself. It has the fog it throws up with every pulse, which tears on the piles and re-forms, and which may be the nearest thing it has to a voice.

Sometimes, though, the keepers say it seems to be *trying* for something. Over the years the heights of the piles have shifted. The lanes are converging. On the calmest days, in the brief standing moment when the field is full and the reflection holds, the pattern of the water between the piles looks almost like a single mark repeated across the whole hollow, drawn by something that has been practising its one letter for a hundred years.

Nobody knows what it is trying to write. The keepers say, not joking, that it may be trying to write *still*. That would be the one thing it can never be, set down in iron so that something of it will stay when the water leaves.

## If You Go

If you go to Lower Scour, go on a windless day. Walk down off the rim carefully, because it is soft. Stand among the piles, among the crowd that is not paying attention to you, and do not move.

It will read your footprints first. Let it.

Then wait for the pulse. Watch the silt darken, glisten, and lift. Watch the brown skin of water slide towards you between the iron, warm from a river that does not know it has been buried. Watch it reach your boots and decide.

It will part. It will slow in your lee. For the four or five minutes the field stands full, you will be one of the fixed things, held in the one long thought of a mind that has never been still for an hour.

Then it will drain. The fog will tear. The pump will hum on in your sternum. The silt will be wiped clean, and whatever the Scour thought about you will be gone from the water.

But the iron at your feet will be very slightly different, a thousandth of a millimetre, where the water turned aside for you. It will keep that.
