# Who Lives in Fennick

## The First Answer: The Ones in the Walls

If you count bodies, Fennick belongs to the beetles.

Press your ear to the wall of any house on the terraced row and you will hear them: a dry, patient shuffling between the layers of cellulose, like someone turning the pages of a very thick book in the next room. They do not live *in* the houses. They live in the walls of the houses, the way a thought lives in a skull. They have chewed galleries between the translucent sheets, and if you hold your face very close to a wall in the lichen-light you can see the galleries as darker veins, branching and rejoining. The pattern is not random. On the terraced row it repeats every two feet, a trailing motif of stems and small five-lobed flowers. The beetles have eaten wallpaper into the walls, and neither they nor the bulb could say who taught it to them.

Only the terraced row is inhabited this way. The larger detached houses near the square, with their bay windows and their single grown picture frames, have no beetles in them at all. The beetles know which houses are theirs. Somebody built this town with an idea of who belonged where, and the idea came down into the root intact even when the chairs did not.

They have a life apart from the factory, and it is small and almost domestic. Between shifts (the shifts are marked by a lengthening of the pulse, eleven seconds becoming thirteen, then fourteen) they gather in the galleries in twos and threes and touch antennae for long minutes without moving. They groom one another's elytra with their forelegs. When one dies, which is rare and seems to happen only in the factory, under a belt or in the path of the flywheel, the others carry it to the terraced row and wall it into the house it came from. The houses on the row are slightly thicker than the others. Hold one up to the dark and there are small shapes in the lower courses, pressed flat, iridescent, a kind of lineage.

Their eggs are the thing that complicates them. Beetles in Fennick do not lay in the ground. They lay in the kettles.

The description says the kettles have no openings, and that is true, but tap one and some of them rattle faintly. A few, the oldest ones, in houses the snails have not yet reached, have a single round hole in the side, neat as a punched ticket, where something has chewed its way out. The bulb grew a kettle because it remembered kettles. The beetles found a sealed hollow vessel sitting in the center of a room, warm and fibrous, and knew exactly what it was for. Now every new beetle in Fennick is born out of the memory of something that once boiled water for tea. They emerge already walking in a line.

So the beetles are the working population. They live here, and they are the right answer if a census taker came through with a clipboard and a lamp that gave off darkness. But they have never once gone through a door. They walk the pavements, they enter the factory by a gap in the wall, and they go home through the plaster. The doors of Fennick are not for them. Somebody else is expected.

## The Second Answer: The Gentry

The snails move through the front doors. They are too large for anything else.

A snail in Fennick is the weight of a sheep and the color of wet slate, and its body when extended is longer than a man lying down. The foot is pale gray with a darker margin, and it ripples in slow waves from tail to head, each wave taking most of a minute. They leave a trail that stays luminous for days, fainter and bluer than the lichen, so that the lichen map of Fennick is overwritten by a second, looser map: the paths the snails have taken, curving into houses and out again, doubling back, pausing in long luminous smears at the chapel wall.

They behave like people who own the place. They take the middle of the street. The beetles, in their single-file lines, stop and wait for a snail to pass, sometimes for an hour, and the line backs up out of sight in the dark, each beetle standing patiently in its stretch of pavement. The snails enter the larger houses near the square, the ones without beetles, and settle in the front rooms with their eyestalks swaying at the height of the empty picture frames, as though considering them.

It is tempting to think that they are the ones the doors were grown for. They are not. They are something stranger and more useful.

Watch a snail eat an unfinished house, the ribs of a not-yet-home at the edge of town, and it looks like grazing. It is slow and thorough. The radula rasps along each lignin rib with a sound like a file on soft wood, and the rib shortens by a hair each hour. But then watch the house that grows back a few doors down. It is simpler. The chair inside it has fewer rungs. The window has one pane where there were four. The snails are not destroying the drafts. They are *digesting* them, taking in the bulb's attempt, and whatever passes through them comes out as a more essential version. The bulb is drafting a town from memory, and the snails are its editors, and every revision loses a detail and keeps the shape.

Their shells are the evidence. Each shell spirals in the proportions of the chapel steeple, and on the inner whorls, if you run a thumb along them, there is a faint raised pattern: tiny rectangles in rows, a grid, the three streets and the square, rendered over and over as the shell grew, each iteration a little more worn. The oldest snails carry the oldest drafts of Fennick at the tight center of their spirals, where no one will ever see them. The fresh lip of the shell, still soft and faintly sticky like everything else here, carries the current draft, the four-stalk chairs and the cornerless frames.

The snails are the town's historians, then, or its archive. They carry everything the bulb has tried and abandoned. When they rest against the chapel wall for weeks with their eyestalks drawn in, they are pressed there shell-first, and the hymn from inside runs through the spirals like water through a pipe.

They live here in the way a library lives in a city. But no one reads them, and the doors were not grown for them either.

## The Third Answer: The One Object in the Room

In the houses where the bulb grew nothing at all, no chair and no kettle and no frame, there is a bat.

This is precise. Walk through Fennick room by room and you will find no room with both an object and a bat. The bats roost only where the bulb's memory failed, one to a room, hanging from the ceiling rib exactly at the center. The bulb forgot what went in the room, and the bat is what fills the gap.

They are small, no bigger than a folded handkerchief, and pale where most bats are dark, their wing membranes nearly as translucent as the walls. Hanging, they are almost invisible. You find them by their warmth, which is the only warmth in the empty houses, and by the faint pressure of their breathing on your eardrums when you stand close. They sleep with their wings wrapped forward like a shawl pulled around the shoulders.

When they hunt, they hunt only the moths on the square, and they never go near the pool of darkness itself. They take the moths at the outer edge of the spiral, the ones that have not yet been drawn in. It is almost courteous. They seem to understand that the moths nearer the center belong to someone else.

The bats are the nearest thing Fennick has to lodgers. They sleep in the rooms, they go out, they come back to the same rib. If you want someone who lives in a house in Fennick in the ordinary sense of the phrase, it is the bats. But they are placeholders, and they know it. They are what the bulb put there *instead*.

## The Fourth Answer: The Moths, and Where They Go

The moths are the first clue that the town is not what it appears to be.

They circle the six lamps on the square in their hundreds, pale and wide-winged, and they behave exactly as moths behave around a flame: the widening spiral, the helpless inward drift, the last approach. Moths do not fly toward light because light is good for them. They fly toward it because they navigate by the moon and a near light confuses the geometry, until they are circling something that is not the sky. The moths of Fennick are circling the lamps for the same reason. Somewhere in their small bodies, the dark on the square reads as a moon.

When one passes into the pool and does not come out, it is not destroyed. It arrives.

This cannot be seen from outside. Step into the pool, stand still among the wool and the warmth and the cabbage smell, and wait. After a while there is a soft ticking near your face, a moth battering itself against something, and then another. They are circling something in there, above head height, in the exact center of the six lamps. It is something you cannot see, and it must be a light.

Inside the darkness on the square, there is a gas lamp lit.

The townsfolk have their own lamp, their own light, their own evening. The moths fly from Fennick's dark into Fennick's light, and they are circling it still, in a square you are standing in and cannot see. The number of moths outside does not decline because the moths are not being lost. They are emigrating.

## The Fifth Answer: The Townsfolk

These are the ones the doors were grown for.

Stand in the pool of darkness long enough, an hour or more, and the impressions stop being fragments. At first they are only the wool and the cough and the scrape of a boot. Then there is the pressure of a crowd, loose and unhurried, the way a crowd stands in a square on a weekday evening when the shift has ended and nobody is ready to go home. A shoulder brushes yours and moves on. There is a smell of wet coats, carbolic soap, pipe tobacco, and under it, faintly, coal dust, which is not a smell exactly but a dryness at the back of the throat. Somebody near your elbow is eating something from a paper bag. You hear the paper.

The voices never resolve into words. They have the cadence of words, the rise at the end of a question, a short laugh, a long complaint delivered in a monotone, but when you strain for the words they slide off. It is like trying to read a sign in a dream. You understand that someone has just said something ordinary and a little funny, and that several people are laughing, and you do not know what it was.

Once, if you stay long enough, a hand will take yours. It is large and dry and callused across the palm, the calluses of a man who worked with a shovel or a pick. It closes around your hand briefly, as a hand closes around a child's to keep it close in a crowd, and then lets go. Nobody in Fennick has ever been able to say whose it was, and nobody who has felt it has ever quite stopped feeling it.

This is who lives in Fennick. The beetles work here, the snails keep the records, and the bats sleep in the gaps, but the town was grown for people who can exist only where there is no light at all, because light would require them to be seen, and there is nothing to see.

Now the factory makes sense.

The factory does not make darkness to light the streets. It makes darkness to make *room*. Every bale the beetles carry to a lamp enlarges, by a little, the space in which the townsfolk can exist. The pool on the square is not a pool. It is the town's only inhabited district, and it is growing. The lamps on the corners, each with its small emitted absence, are outlying settlements, a family or two apiece, a man on his way home. Stand under a corner lamp and you will sometimes hear footsteps passing, going somewhere.

The beetles do not know what they are doing. They walk the pavements and carry the bales, and they are building a population one sack of nothing at a time. The bulb grows the houses so that when there is enough dark, the people will have somewhere to go. It grows the doors so that there will be something to come home through, the lintels so that a tall man can duck, and a single kettle in the center of a room because a kitchen without a kettle is not a kitchen, even if the kettle cannot open and a beetle will be born out of it.

It has been doing this for as long as the mound has been in the basin. The dark on the square is wider every season. One day it will be wide enough to reach the nearest front door, and someone will go inside.

## The Sixth Answer: The One in the Chapel

But there is a voice in Fennick, and it is not in the square.

The hymn from the chapel is the only sound here that is not a fragment. It is slow, very slow, a single phrase stretched across hours, and if you sit on the chapel step through a whole cycle of it you can hear that it is a real hymn, with a real tune, the kind sung in a chapel of stone and damp coats on a wet Sunday by people who worked six days a week and were tired. Slowed this much it no longer sounds like singing. It sounds like breathing, or like tide.

What is in there is not a person. It is too large, and the steeple is not a steeple.

In bulbs, the flower is made in the dark. It forms at the heart of the bulb during the long cold, a complete and folded miniature, every petal already present and tiny and pale, waiting for the stalk to lift it. When a bulb flowers, it spends itself. Everything it has stored goes up the stalk and into the bloom, and the bulb is left thin and papery, and the flower opens above ground, sets seed, and falls.

The steeple is a scape, a flower stalk. The green bud at its tip, still sheathed, splitting a little further every day, is the bloom.

What lives in the chapel is the flower of Fennick, and it is singing because a flower forming in the dark from the memory of a village would carry, folded inside it among its petals, the thing that village did most together. In a company town, what the people did together was the shift and the chapel. The factory is the shift, and the flower is the chapel. It is the town's collective voice, the one part of the memory that was always sung by many people at once, gathered into the most important organ the bulb has.

This is why the snails rest against its walls. They carry every draft of Fennick in their spirals, and the flower is learning from them. It is taking in every abandoned version, every chair with rungs and every four-paned window, to fold into itself before it opens. The snails are not worshipping. They are being read.

It is also why the beetles will not go near the chapel. They are the working population. They know, the way working people always know, what it means when the company begins spending everything it has stored.

## The Seventh Answer: The Bulb

So perhaps the one who lives in Fennick is the bulb itself.

A bulb is a plant's way of waiting. It is a store laid down against a bad season, a clenched fist of stored sunlight underground, holding still until conditions change. Everything in Fennick is in the first instant of its existence, sharp-edged and faintly sticky, because a bulb is always about to begin. It is never in the middle of anything. It lives entirely in the moment before.

The bulb lives in Fennick the way a person lives in their own memory: it is the only place it has. Something soaked down into the root from a village above, the smoke and wool and cabbage and coughing, the hymn, the particular way a street was laid out by men who wanted to see from one end to the other, the idea of which houses were for which people. The bulb took all of it the way roots take water, without choosing, and stored it, as bulbs store everything, against a season to come.

And now it is turning the store into a town, and then into a flower. It is growing larger inside without growing larger outside, because what it holds is not measured in the dimensions of the basin. It is measured in how much there was to remember.

Whether the village above still exists, whether it was emptied when the pit closed or flooded when the valley was dammed, whether the wet country is wet because a reservoir now lies where the terraced rows once were, cannot be learned from here. The street signs spell *FENNICK* every time, and every time the street names are slightly different. The bulb caught the name of the whole place but not the names of the streets. That is how memory works when it belongs to many people at once and to no one in particular: the name everyone said, and not the names each one said.

## The Last Answer

A bulb does not flower forever, though. One season the bud at the top of the steeple will split its sheath the final inch. The hymn will stop, and in the silence you will notice for the first time how much of Fennick's quiet it had been holding up. The factory will slow. The beetles will stand still in their lines along the pavements, each one in its own stretch of dark. The pulse will lengthen past fourteen seconds, past twenty, and then not come.

The scape will rise. It will go up through the unseen dome, through the skin of the mound, through the felted pewter leaves, and it will open above the basin under an ordinary gray sky, in ordinary rain.

No one knows what the flower will look like. Perhaps it will be only a flower, pale and large, with a smell of coal smoke and cabbage and wet wool that carries across the basin in the rain. Perhaps it will be something more like a gathering: many small blooms held close together on a single head, like a crowd standing in a square on a weekday evening when nobody is ready to go home. Then it will set seed, the way flowers do, and the seed will fall, and the wind in the wet country will carry it.
