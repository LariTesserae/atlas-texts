# The One Who Holds On

## The Polished Rungs

You would not find it at first. You would find the swifts, the moths, the frogs lighting their throats in the dusk, and you would think you had met everyone. The Crown is full of life, but none of it looks at you. The swifts are asleep on the breath. The moths are blind. The frogs are busy flickering at each other. The snails have not noticed you and will not finish noticing you for years.

Then you look at the ladder.

Everything else in the Crown is furred. Moss runs along the rivet rows in contour lines. Lichen frosts the rim. Brass pipes have gone green and soft as felt. The rust has had centuries to build its shale. But the last few rungs of the ladder, where it meets the rim and the cold, are bright. They are not merely clean. They are *polished*, worn to a gleam in a particular shape: a narrow band on each rung, slightly off-center, where something has closed around the iron again and again for longer than the orchids have been alive.

So you start with that, and you go looking. The first thing you learn is that you will not see it by looking. Nothing here relies on hearing. It turns out that nothing here relies much on being seen, either.

## First Guesses

You would guess a person. Someone left behind, the last of whatever crew once read the gauges, grown old and strange in the roar. It is the natural guess, and it is wrong in almost every particular except one. Whatever climbs the ladder does have hands.

Then you would guess something like the swifts: a creature of the air that rises to the rim and sinks again, touching the rungs only in passing. But the polish is not the polish of passing. It is the polish of *gripping*: slow, total, repeated. Something has held on to those rungs as if its life depended on it.

It does. That is the first true thing.

## Everything Rises Unless Something Holds It

The one who lives in the Crown is light.

It is not small. When you finally see it, and you will see it only when it wants you to, or when you have been still for so long that you have become part of the wall, it is taller than you, long in the way that tree ferns are long. It is jointed in more places than a body ought to be, so that it can fold flat against curved plate and follow the curve. But it weighs almost nothing. Its bones, if they are bones, are hollow in the way a flute is hollow, or a flue. The breath passes through it. You can see this if you catch it against the daylight at the rim: the updraft enters small openings along its sides and leaves through others near its shoulders, and the fronds and mosses that grow in its seams stream upward the way the long vines do.

If it let go, it would rise.

This is the whole shape of its life. The swifts have given themselves to the column. They sleep on it, mate on it, and never land. The keeper has refused it. Every hour of its existence is spent in contact with the made thing: a hand on a pipe, a foot hooked through the grating, the long fingers closed around a rung. Its hands have taken the shape of holding the way a fig root takes the shape of a seam. At rest they are closed. Opening them is something it has to decide to do.

It sleeps the way the swifts sleep, locked, except that where they lock their wings open to the breath, it locks its grip shut against it. Sometimes, in the warmest hours near the gallery, you might find it asleep on the ladder with its body drifting gently upward from its own hands, stretched out along the draft like a vine, anchored at the fingers. The vines have never once hung down. Neither has it.

## The Body

Let it come into focus slowly, because it does.

Its skin is the color of the wall, and at first you assume this is camouflage. It is closer to kinship. Rust grows on it. The same flaking shale that forms on the plates forms in thin crusts on its forearms and along its back, and it sheds them in hand-sized flakes, the way the wall does. In those flakes, and in the rich dust behind them, things root. Moss runs along its seams in lines that follow no rivets you can see, until you realize they follow *its* rivets: small raised points along its limbs, in rows, as regular as the wall's. A fern the length of a finger grows out of the inside of one elbow. It has grown there for a long time. The keeper bends its arm around it with care.

Is it iron? It is warm when you touch it. You will only touch it once, if you are lucky. The warmth is skin warmth, breath warmth, the exact temperature of the column. Condensation does not bead on it the way it beads on the cold rim plates. It is not a cold thing. But it rings. If you lay your palm on its chest it rings faintly, the way the wall rings, carrying the note. You feel the roar pass through it, and you understand that in the Crown there is no clear boundary between a thing that lives in the sound and a thing that the sound lives in.

Its face is long and mostly smooth. There are eyes, large and dark and set wide, made for watching a void full of ash-colored birds. There are no ears, or none that you can find; the whole body is the ear. And in the middle of the brow, pressed into the skin so faintly that you would miss it without the light behind, there is a mark: a circle with a line through it.

You have seen that mark before, on the maintenance plates, under the moss, worn nearly smooth. You wondered then whether it showed a stack or an eye.

Here, on this face, it is both. You are looking at the eye of the stack.

## Where Its Mouth Should Be

There is a mouth. You have to look for it.

Across the lower part of the face, where a mouth would be, there is a seam, and over the seam grows a moss so fine and so close that it looks like skin. It is the palest green in the Crown, paler than the lichen at the rim, and it is very even. It has had time to grow even. Along its edge you can find, if you look closely, a thin line of older and darker growth, like scar tissue, like the ring in an orchid's stem that marks a change in the note.

The keeper does not open its mouth. There is no reason to. You cannot speak in the Crown. Nothing here speaks. And a mouth left open in the column would take in the breath, and the breath would lift it.

So the moss grows closed. It grows closed for seven years at a time.

Hold on to that; it comes back.

## What It Does

For a long time you might think it does nothing. It moves slowly, as nearly everything here moves slowly, and it moves along the wall rather than across the void. But watch it long enough and a round emerges, as regular as the valve wheel and as patient.

**It reads the gauges.** The gauges have fogged faces and needles that drift almost imperceptibly toward numbers no one reads. The description was wrong about that: someone does read them. The keeper stops at each one along the gallery and lays two fingers against its glass. It does not wipe the fog away, and it does not need to see the numbers. It feels the needle through the glass, through the vibration of the plate the gauge is set into, and it holds its fingers there for a long time. The moths gather around the gauges in their silent clouds because the note shifts there; the keeper gathers there for the same reason. They are doing the same work. The moths just do not know that it is work.

**It keeps the middle empty.** This is the part you would never guess, because it looks like gardening and it is the opposite. The garden wants to close. Every year a new skin of rust and soil, the ferns rooting deeper, the figs braiding their way out across the seams, the fronds growing out from the wall at right angles and then, some of them, too far. The keeper goes to the ones that reach too far into the column. It holds the wall with three limbs and reaches out with the fourth, farther than a person could, and it breaks them off. It does not break many, and it does it gently. It lets the broken frond go, and the frond rises, spinning slowly, up the column and out into the sky.

The garden is not what the keeper keeps. The garden is tolerated, the way a river tolerates the town that grows along its banks. What the keeper keeps is the absence. The void is the most important thing in the room; it knew that before you did. Everything the keeper does is arranged around it.

**It leans on the wheel.** The great valve wheel beside the dead hatch turns a fraction of a degree a year, on its own, or so it seemed. The keeper visits it once a year. It sets both closed hands against a spoke, lowers its body until it is nearly horizontal on the grating, and holds still. It does not push, exactly. It adds its weight, which is almost nothing, for a very long time. A day. Two. The ferns around the wheel grow bent because the spoke passes through them every few decades. The keeper's own fern, the small one inside its elbow, grows bent in the same direction.

Why? It does not know, not in the way you would want it to know. You would want an instruction, a purpose, a plan. What it has is a habit older than knowing. The habit lives in its hands the way the grip does. Once a year, the wheel. Once a day, the gauges. Always, the void kept open.

**It climbs.** Every day, at the hour when the swifts wake near the rim and begin their long spirals down, it climbs the ladder. Hand over hand, never both hands at once, never a moment without holding. This is where the polish comes from. At the top it stops just below the painted line where orchid becomes frost. It hooks its long fingers around the last bright rung, lifts its face over the rim into the cold, and goes still.

## At the Rim

Outside it is bright and freezing and the wind moves sideways, and the keeper does not like it. You can see this. It folds its body tight against the iron, and the rust-flakes along its back lift and chatter in the crosswind. The cold can't come into the Crown, and the keeper is a creature of the Crown; out here it is half exposed, a warm thing with its head in winter.

But it stays, and it stays in order to listen with its body to the other stacks.

The chimneys of the drowned city stand across the cloud sea, a dozen or so, each with its green smudge at the crown, each breathing its plume. Each has a note. From the rim, if you are very still, you can feel them: faint, far, each at a slightly different pitch, together forming a slow chord that never resolves.

The keeper is not still the way you are still. It is still the way an instrument is still when it is being tuned. It holds its face over the rim with the mark on its brow toward the other stacks. The small openings along its sides close, one by one, so that the breath of its own column stops passing through it. For a little while it stops being part of its own stack's note and becomes an ear for the others.

You start to understand, then, what the wheel is for, and why the note has been sinking for centuries and no one knows why.

Someone knows. Or rather, someone's hands know. Once a year the keeper lowers its weight against the spoke. Once a day it climbs and feels the chord. Across the cloud sea, on the tops of other stacks, other long light shapes are hooked around other bright rungs, doing the same.

Whether they are tuning the chord toward resolution or keeping it from resolving, the keeper could not tell you. You would have to ask whoever set the first habit into the first pair of hands, and that one is under the moss with the maker's mark, worn smooth. You suspect the keepers are not trying to finish anything. The chord is not a problem. It is a way of staying in touch. A dozen stacks, a dozen notes, each one adjusted, every few decades, by the width of a fern stem, so that the whole drowned city keeps sounding *together*, even if it never arrives.

## The Hush

Every seven years or so, the furnace breathes out.

The draft slackens. The heat drops. The note falls a half-step, and for the first time in years there is something like silence in the Crown. The swifts come down and cling to the vines, bewildered. Their nests of felted fluff and silk, glued to the upward-streaming vines, begin to swing *downward*. The spider threads sag. The floating seeds begin to fall. For a few weeks the Crown is a room where things hang instead of stream. You can hear rain on the iron, the drip of condensation, a frog calling aloud.

This is the keeper's season.

It is the only time the keeper can be seen easily, because it is the only time it moves quickly. The one who has spent seven years holding things down now spends a few weeks holding things up. It goes out along the vines, trusting its weight to them now that the breath no longer pulls, and it gathers the swift chicks that tumble from the tilting nests. It holds them against its chest, where they feel the faint ring of the note still in it, and they quiet. It sets them in the crooks of the fig roots, in the moss, in the pitcher plants beside the little translucent frogs who are, for once, singing. It catches falling seeds in its closed hands and presses them into the soil behind the rust plates. For a few weeks the garden plants itself from above, and the gardener is the keeper.

Then, on one of those quiet evenings, it climbs.

At the rim, with the cold on its face and the frost line creeping down a little in the weakened draft, it lifts one hand from the rung. It has not done this in seven years. With its long fingers it finds the seam on its face where the pale moss has grown over, and it tears the moss away.

It does not look painful. It looks careful, the way the keeper breaks the ferns that reach too far. The moss comes away in one soft piece. The keeper does not let it rise. There is no breath to carry it now. It tucks the moss into the rust along its forearm, where it will take root again.

Then it opens its mouth.

## The Song

What comes out is the note.

Not the fallen note of the hush, a half-step low, but the old one, the one the stack sings when the furnace is full. The keeper's body is a pipe; you knew that when you saw the breath pass through it. Now the pipe is its own. It closes the openings along its sides, fills itself, and sounds, small and clear and exact, the pitch its stack has lost for the season.

It is holding the place. The furnace has faltered, the column has dropped, and for a few weeks the chord across the cloud sea would be missing a voice. So the keeper supplies it. It holds this stack's note in the chord the same way it holds the ladder: steadily, with its whole body.

And across the cloud sea, from the tops of the other stacks, faint and far, each at a slightly different pitch, others answer.

This is why the hush matters, and why all the stacks fall silent together. The furnace beneath them is one furnace. The drowned city breathes out as a single body. For a few weeks every seven years, the vast roar that has swallowed all the sound there is steps aside. In the space it leaves, a dozen small voices hold the chord by themselves.

They do not say anything else. They do not have words. Perhaps each voice carries a little more than the pitch: a slight roughness where the moss has torn, a waver that means *the ferns reached far this time*, or *the wheel is harder to turn*, or *I am older*.
