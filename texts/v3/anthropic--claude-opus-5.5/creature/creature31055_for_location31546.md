# Who Lives in Weir-in-the-Cloud

## The Loud People

Start with the people, because they would insist on it, and because they would insist loudly.

They call themselves nothing in particular. Traders from the eastern valley call them *the Weir-folk*. Traders from the west use a word that means something like *those who shout in fog*, and the saddle people have adopted it with pleasure. They are short and broad through the chest, built for ladders and steep stone. Their calves are thick as fence posts. Nearly all of them have a small white scar somewhere on the shin or ankle where a leech was burned off in childhood with a coal, and they compare these scars the way other people compare birthmarks.

Their voices are the first thing any stranger notices. There is no such thing as a quiet conversation in the saddle. Between the frogs, the clappers, the dripping, the pigs, and the swifts, a murmured word simply disappears. So the people pitch their voices high and forward, into the front of the face, a carrying half-song that can cross a lane in fog. Even lovers speak this way. Even grief is loud here. When someone dies, the mourning is shouted from the rooftops, because otherwise the dead might not hear it over the water.

Sometimes children go down the mountain for a season, to be fostered with trading partners in the valley towns. They come back complaining that down there they couldn't sleep. The silence pressed on their ears, they say. They lay awake listening for the clappers and heard nothing, and felt as if they had gone deaf, or died.

## The Keepers of Small Rights

Everyone in the saddle owns something, and almost nobody owns land.

What they own are *rights*, and the rights are specific. A family holds the water from one channel, from the third clapper to the stone basin with the chipped lip. A woman named Oru-of-the-Laurel holds the right to string the fourth net from the north cliff, as her mother did and her mother's mother. The oak called Grandfather Left-Hand, the one whose largest limb bends down and back, holds a platform, and the platform belongs to a lineage of eleven living people. They argue constantly about whose turn it is to sleep on the side nearest the trunk, where it's warmest.

The rights are remembered, not written. The town has a sort of living ledger. Certain old people, usually women and usually the ones with the worst knees, sit in doorways and know who is owed what. When a dispute comes up, you go and shout it at one of them. She shouts back what her grandmother told her, and that settles it. If two of them disagree, they shout at each other, and the town gathers to watch, and eventually one gives way. The case is then considered closed for a generation.

These memory-keepers are called *stones*, the same word as the old granite paths. It is a compliment. It means you have been walked on for a very long time and still hold your shape.

## The Netters

In migration season the town becomes a different organism.

The net-crews go up before dawn in the dark and the fog, feeling for the poles by memory. Nobody carries a light near the nets, because light turns the birds. They work by touch and sound. An experienced netter can tell from the thrum of the cord, through the pole and into her palm, what has struck the mesh: the soft double-tap of a thrush, the heavy slap of a pigeon, the frantic buzz of something small. A netter's hands are the most respected hands in the saddle. They are criss-crossed with fine cord-cuts and very gentle. They can take a tangled warbler out of a net in the dark in five breaths without breaking a feather. Such a netter is said to have *listening fingers*.

There is a strangeness to the netters in season that the valley traders notice and don't like. They stop sleeping properly. They doze in the afternoons, sitting up, and wake at dusk alert and restless, turning their faces toward the western lip of the col. They start to talk less, which in this town is almost frightening. Their families know to leave them alone. *She's gone up into the birds,* people say. *She'll come down after.*

## The Children on the Bridges

The upper level of the town, the lashed walkways at head height, belongs to the children and the dogs.

The children of Weir are fearless in a way that horrifies visiting parents. They run the wet bamboo barefoot, because shoes slip and toes grip. They drop from bridge to roof to branch. They fight the macaques daily and with real hatred, and they respect them the way you respect a rival clan, with feuds and grudges passed between generations of monkeys and children. There is a particular old male with a torn ear whom the children call the Tax Collector. Every child in town has personally been robbed by him. He is the most hated and the most beloved figure in their world.

Every child also knows, without being told exactly, that they are waiting to be the youngest one who can climb to the highest roof. That child carries the first bird. The honor goes to whoever is youngest and able, so it can't be earned by age. Some years it goes to a tiny, determined four-year-old, and the whole town holds its breath while she climbs. Some years there is no small child brave enough, and it falls to a gangly nine-year-old who is secretly embarrassed. Either way, the child who does it is called *bird-hand* for the rest of their life. Old bird-hands are treated with an odd tenderness. They are expected to be a little foolish, a little lucky, and strangely good with animals.

## The Animals Who Have Moved In

The line between the people and the other residents is thinner than it looks.

The black pigs are not really kept. They are *negotiated with*. Each pig considers one house its own and sleeps under that floor. It is fed there, and it will defend the house against other pigs, dogs, and occasionally drunk men. The pigs root the soft mat between the stilts and keep it from swallowing the poles. Some people believe the houses would sink without them.

The dogs are small, rough-coated, and devoted to shouting. They are indistinguishable from their owners in this respect. The ponies in the hollow oaks are elderly-minded creatures that seem to know the trail better than their drivers and simply refuse to go when a slip is coming. People listen to them.

Then there are the birds that stay. Every migration a few of the exhausted travelers never leave. A thrush takes a rafter and does not go on. A warbler with a bent wing settles into the moss of some wall. The saddle people call these *stayers* and consider it lucky to have one in the house. Over five centuries the stayers have interbred with the resident birds. The saddle now has its own odd little populations: a thrush that sings a valley song with a mountain accent, and a flycatcher found nowhere else, pale and fog-colored. The people say it is the child of every bird that ever rested here and couldn't bear to leave.

The big crested jay deserves its own line. There is always one dominant jay in the saddle, and the town speaks of it as a single individual, though it obviously can't be. It is always *the* Jay. The Jay steals. The Jay announces. The Jay knows when the fog is coming a full minute before any person does, and screams about it. By now, when the Jay screams, the whole town reaches for its nets or its washing.

## The Thing That Is the Town

The longer you stay in Weir-in-the-Cloud, the harder it becomes to say where the people end.

The walls are woven by hands and then finished by moss. The ladders take root. The stone walls are held up by oak roots now, and the oak roots are held in place by the stones. The water runs from roof to roof, house to house, used and passed on, so every family drinks water that has already passed through a family above it. The pigs keep the stilts from sinking. The children keep the monkeys off the racks, the monkeys keep the children quick, and the hawks keep the smaller birds low in the fog, where the nets are.

After a while it starts to feel as though one creature lives here, not many. It has a thousand bamboo veins and a skin of moss. Its lungs are the chimney-holes and its heartbeat is the uncountable clatter of the clappers. It keeps its memory in old women called stones. It feeds twice a year when the sky pours through it, and it is constantly, patiently wounded and healed. A terrace slumps, a thatch tears, a stilt tilts, and immediately someone is lashing, digging, re-seating. Nobody plans the repair. It happens the way a scab forms.

The saddle people half-know this. They have a word, *weir*, the same as in the town's name. It means a woven fence set across a stream to catch fish while letting the water through. The town is a weir set across the river of cloud and wings that pours over the mountains. They catch some of what flows and let most of it pass. That is the whole art: *let most of it pass*.

This is where the rules come from, and where they stop being merely practical.

## The Open Gap

No net may cross the central gap. It must stay open.

The traders assume this is conservation, that the old people knew that if you net the whole pass the birds stop coming. That's true, and the netters will say so if asked. But ask one of the stones, very late at night, loudly, with enough rice-beer in both of you, and she may tell you something else.

The gap is kept open, she says, because it is *in use*.

Something else crosses there.

The people have no single name for it. Some call it the Long Crossing. Some call it the Guest, or Grandmother Fog, or simply *the one who goes through*. Nobody claims to have seen it. There is nothing to see. But everyone in the saddle has felt it, usually once or twice in a life, and they describe it the same way.

You are standing on the upper steps, they say, in the fog, and the noise *stops*. It doesn't fade. It stops. The frogs, the clappers, the dripping, the pigs, the far shouting across the bridges, all of it falls away at once, and there is a silence like the one the children complain about in the valley, only enormous and very near. The fog moves past you, as it always does, pouring over the western lip. But now you understand, suddenly and completely, that it isn't weather. It is something walking. It is very large, very old, and in no hurry at all, and it is crossing the mountains at the one low place, the way everything must.

Then a clapper knocks. A frog starts up. The noise comes back like water filling a footprint, and the fog is just fog again.

## What the Guest Is

The stories diverge here, and that is appropriate, since nothing about the Guest holds still.

Some say it is the migration itself: not the birds but the *going*, the pull that lifts a thrush off one continent and sets it down on another. They say it has a body made of all that wanting, and it crosses here because this is the lowest place.

Some say it is the cloud-river, the great wet breath of the lowlands that climbs the western face every day and gives up its water on the moss. It is alive, they say, and aware of the town the way a sleeper is aware of a blanket.

Some of the stones, the very oldest ones, say it is something much stranger. They say it is making a single crossing so slowly that the whole five hundred years of Weir-in-the-Cloud are one of its steps. The town lives in the space of one long footfall. The birds and the fog and the seasons are only the weather it trails behind it. When its foot finally lifts, the saddle will be empty, and somewhere far down the far valley another notch will fill with moss and stilts and shouting.

Nobody tries to settle which story is true. It isn't that kind of question. The saddle people don't worship the Guest. They don't pray to it, and they don't make offerings, exactly. They do what the town does with everything: they leave room.

## The Night of the First Fog

Now the rules make a different kind of sense.

Nobody nets on the night of the first fog of the season. That is the night the Guest comes through most fully, pouring the whole season in with it. Every net is furled, and every family stays inside. Oddly, in a town that shouts everything, people *whisper* that night. It is the only night of the year they do. Children who have never heard their parents' voices below a half-song lie in the smoke and hear them murmur, and remember it all their lives. The clappers keep knocking and the frogs keep calling, because the Guest does not mind the town's noise. It is the town's *silence* it seems to want, one night a year: the people falling quiet and letting it pass without being caught at.

Nobody takes the hawks, because the hawks ride the Guest's own updraft through the notch. They are its outriders, or its thoughts. The netters say a hawk sliding past at rooftop height is the closest thing to seeing the Guest's face that any living person will manage.

And the first bird caught each year is let go from the highest roof by the youngest child who can climb there.

That one the stones don't explain. But anyone who has watched it happen understands.

## The Bird-Hand

Picture it. It is the first clear morning after the first fog. The whole town is out, packed onto the bridges and roofs and worn steps, shouting as usual, jostling, pigs underfoot, the Jay screaming, a goat on somebody's thatch. Then a small child climbs the ladder of the highest house. Every year it is the same house, an old tree-seat whose rungs have gone concave, and the child climbs slowly. The town gets quieter, rung by rung, until by the time the child reaches the top there is nothing but frogs and water.

The child stands on the roof in the fern and white orchids, with the wet sun glittering off everything. Held against their chest, cupped in two hands the way the netters taught them, is the first bird of the season. It is usually something small and brown, heart going like rain.

The child opens their hands.

For one breath the bird sits there, too surprised to move. Then it goes, not down into the forest to rest, but *up and through*, straight out over the open central gap where no net has ever been strung. It follows the hawks, the fog, and the long invisible thing that is always crossing.

And the whole town, every loud voice in the saddle, lets out a single enormous shout. It is not a word. It is the sound people make when they have let something go on purpose.

Then everyone goes back to work. The nets go up. The monkeys hit the racks. The water keeps running down its thousand channels, used and passed on, used and passed on. Somewhere a terrace wall starts to slump, and someone goes to fix it.

Something vast is crossing through, very slowly, in the fog, in the gap the people keep open for it. That is who lives in Weir-in-the-Cloud: the loud people, the stayers and the stones, and the creature they make together out of moss and water and habit. It is a weir across a river of wings, catching a little and letting the rest go by.
