# Who Lives in Cantle

**The First Answer**

Ask anyone who has spent a night there and they will say *frogs*, and they won't be wrong. The frogs are the first answer because they are the loudest, and in Cantle the loudest thing is the one that seems to own the place.

There are more kinds than anyone has counted. The glass frogs are the ones people remember. They are no longer than a fingernail, green as struck copper, and they cling to the taut cuticle windows from the outside so that from within a room you see their pale bellies pressed to the pane, their small hearts visible as flickers. The gutter-bulls of the orphan street are heavy, olive-brown, and warm-backed. They sit hip-deep in the seed-husk gutters with their throats swelling like wet bread, and they boom in a register low enough to rattle the lamp-stalks.

The frogs worth knowing, though, are the ones the observers call *slumpers*. These are mid-sized, speckled like tea leaves, flat-bodied, with toe pads so broad they look webbed with little hands. The slumpers live on buildings that are dying. When a roofline begins to darken and soften, the slumpers arrive before anything else does, in dozens and then hundreds, crawling up the walls against the sag. They ride the collapse down. As the house folds into the Sward and the water around it steams, they lay their eggs in the heat. Digestion-water is blood-warm and rich with whatever the Sward is breaking down, and a slumper tadpole hatched in it grows in days. When the steam clears and the lawn heals over, the pool is full of small, dark, frantic commas that are already sprouting legs.

The slumpers have made the town's death their nursery. They are in no hurry for Cantle to stand still.

They also know the surges before anyone else does. An hour before the Sward heaves, the slumpers go quiet, and then every frog in the basin goes quiet with them. The observers learned early to fear the silence more than the noise. When the whole chorus stops at once and all you can hear is the pores clicking and the clock humming under your teeth, you have a little time to get off the lawn and onto something with a trunk-footing before the ground rolls.

So the first answer is frogs. But the frogs are only the surface, the loud skin of the matter.

---

**The Ones Who Stay Up**

The swifts showed the observers something that changed how they understood the town.

A swift cannot nest in a building that lasts six hours. Its eggs take weeks, its chicks longer still. And yet the swifts of Cantle nest in the belfries, in the new scalloped cup-niches the Sward has begun growing for them, and they fledge their young there. For a long time no one could understand how.

The answer was that the towers with swifts in them do not fall.

A belfry with a nest in it holds. The shaft beneath it holds. The stair keeps its shape. While the rest of the town churns around it on its cycle of hours, a single occupied tower can stand for forty days, fifty, as long as there are birds coming and going from its open top. Only when the last fledgling has left and the niches sit empty does the lignin darken at the roofline, and then the tower goes down in an afternoon like any other.

This turned out to be the town's deepest rule, older than the rule about keeping moving, and in fact the reason for it. **Cantle does not take back what is in use.** The Sward was built to maintain a city for a population. It tears down and regrows only what nobody is living in. A room with someone in it is a room that is working. The sleeper who wakes enclosed in a wall has not been attacked. They have been kept.

Once the observers understood this, the whole basin looked different. The forty buildings that are never quite the same forty are really two kinds of building: the empty ones, which cycle endlessly, rehearsing, and the tenanted ones, which have been allowed to stop. At any moment perhaps six or seven structures in Cantle are *held*. There is a tower full of swifts, a hall whose colonnade is thick with sleeping bats, and a low house that some herons have chosen and whose doorways, over the weeks they stand in them, grow taller and narrower around their bodies, like a shoe shaping to a foot.

The swifts themselves are black sickles with pale throats. They ride the rising towers the way other birds ride thermals, launching from the tips as they crest. But the swifts that ride are the unhoused ones, the young and the unmated. The nesting swifts do not bother. They have a tower that stays, and so, in a sense, they are the first true householders Cantle has had in a very long time.

---

**The Undertakers**

The eels are the ones who know what is underneath.

They are long, bronze, and blunt-headed, and in the steaming water around a dissolving house they move in slow ropes, rising to the surface to take the soft sloughing lignin in their mouths. The observers once assumed they were simply scavengers. Then someone dropped a dyed float into a digestion pool and found it, two days later, bobbing in a pool on the far side of the basin. There was no surface channel between the two.

The Sward is a body, and bodies have veins. Beneath the woven mat, beneath the pads and bladders and stolons, lies a network of wide water-passages running down into the deep table and back up again. The Sward uses them to move water where the next buildings will need it, and it seeds its private rain from them. The eels live in them. They surface to feed where the town is dying, and then they go down into the dark warm plumbing of the organism and travel.

No one has followed them. The passages are too narrow for a person and too dark for any light that has been tried. There are only the eels' bodies to read. The largest, the ones as long as a boat-pole, are pale almost to white along their bellies, and their eyes are filmed and small. These are old eels that have spent most of their lives under the town. The fishermen of the villages beyond the levee say those white eels are older than their grandfathers' grandfathers, and that they sometimes come up in the digestion pools with things in their mouths that are not lignin: smooth pale beads, curls of something like wire, a disc stamped with a sign nobody recognizes.

The eels are Cantle's undertakers and its sewer-keepers and its only explorers of the underworld. If anything in the basin knows what the Sward is actually doing down there, what the clock is counting, what instructions it is still running, it is the white eels. They are not telling.

---

**The Archivists**

The crabs, at first, look like Cantle's thieves.

They are broad, pale, and flat as dinner plates, with one claw so oversized that they carry it before them like a banner. They scuttle into dissolving buildings in the brief interval between softening and collapse and cut away whatever they can carry: a door-frame, a stair-tread, the rounded rectangle of a window with its cuticle still stretched inside. They haul these out across the lawn at a sideways run, often in teams, three or four crabs to a lintel, and they drag them to the inner curve of the root-levee. There they build.

The crab-town is a low, crowded, ramshackle ring of shanties: salvaged walls leaned together, doorways stacked on doorways, a whole slum made of pieces of houses. It is the one part of Cantle where things pile up, because the levee is root, not Sward. Whatever is carried onto it is out of the town's reach. The Sward does not digest what sits on the ring. It never has.

For a long time the observers found the crab-town a pleasant curiosity. Then one of them began to dig.

The crab-town is very old, and the crabs build on top of the ruins of earlier crab-towns, which were built on top of earlier ones, going down into a compacted midden of dark hard salvaged lignin several meters deep. The deeper you dig into it, the less worn the pieces become.

At the top, the door-frames are rounded rectangles like the ones the Sward grows now. A meter down, the corners begin to sharpen. Two meters down there are columns with real capitals, little carved crowns of leaves that the hall-colonnades have long since forgotten. Near the bottom of the deepest pit anyone has cut, the observers found a lintel with lettering on it. The letters had not yet worn into single glyphs. They were still letters, in an alphabet nobody has matched, clear enough to trace.

The crabs have been unwitting archivists all along. Every piece they stole was a snapshot of the town's memory at the moment of the theft. The Sward has been forgetting itself, rebuild after rebuild, and the crabs, out of nothing but their taste for good sheltering walls, have been keeping the record it lost. The history of Cantle's erosion is laid down in the levee in strata, like sediment, with the crisp original forms at the bottom and the worn archetypes at the top.

The crabs, for their part, live in a museum and do not care. They care about spaces with narrow openings and thick walls. They are hard to evict and quick to fight, and in the evenings they sit in their stolen doorways and wave their great claws at one another in slow, formal semaphore, a signaling whose meaning nobody has worked out. Some observers say the crab-town has streets now, that the shanties are no longer piled at random but set out in rows. Where the crabs could have learned such a thing, on the edge of a town that has streets, is not a difficult question.

---

**The Sleepers**

And then there are the turtles, who have understood the town better than anyone.

They are mud turtles, small and domed, with shells the color of the tea-dark water. The first thing any observer notices about them is how rarely they seem to be anywhere. You see one crossing the lawn in the early mist at its unhurried pace, and then you do not see it again for weeks.

This is because the turtles have learned the rule that sends everyone else running. They walk into a doorway on purpose and wait.

The Sward assumes that whatever stays is meant to be housed, so it houses them. The lignin rises around the turtle, gentle and warm, and closes, and the turtle is gone into the wall. It is not crushed. The turtle has been given a room exactly its own size, a smooth pocket lined with conduit that seeps a little water and, the observers think, something more than water. Inside the wall, the turtle sleeps, and the room it occupies is in use, so the wall does not fall, so the building holds.

Some of the held buildings in Cantle are held by nothing but a turtle, somewhere inside them, dozing.

The turtles are the only citizens who have accepted what the town is offering. The frogs ride its deaths, the swifts borrow its towers, the eels travel its plumbing, and the crabs rob it. The turtles simply let it keep them. They live the way the town's lost population was supposed to live, sheltered and fed and kept in repair, and they live a very long time. Some turtles that were tagged by the first observers are still in the walls. When a turtle finally wakes and pushes its blunt head against the lignin, the wall softens and parts for it like a curtain. The turtle walks out into a town that has rebuilt itself thousands of times since it went in. It blinks in the sharp light, walks a few dozen paces, and chooses another doorway.

Recently, the observers say, there are turtle-shaped hollows in new walls before any turtle arrives. The Sward has learned the shape of its most faithful tenants and has begun to grow rooms for them in advance: small domed alcoves at ground height, lined and moist, waiting.

Not every advance hollow is turtle-shaped, and this is where the account of Cantle's inhabitants begins to turn.

---

**The Small Tenancies**

Before the turn, the smaller citizens, because there are thousands of them for every heron, and the town has shaped itself to them too.

There are the moths, of course, pale as paper and brown as bark, clouding the flower-lamps at night. The lamps were built only to glow. But the moths came to them and crawled inside them and used them, and the Sward, which learns from use, has concluded that lamps are for moths. The lamps along the orphan street now pool a sweet thin nectar in the base of each glowing cup. The moths drink, and dust their wings with the lamps' pale useless pollen, and fly off to the next lamp. The Sward has invented a flower's bargain without ever needing a flower's reason.

There is a black beetle, glossy and heavy, that lives in the cobbled street and grinds seed-husks with its jaws. The finches follow the beetles, and the cobbles grow a little more seed each rebuild. The street is slowly becoming a granary laid flat.

In the pores of the Sward live mites so small they are only a shimmer, clicking in and out with the opening and closing. Water-striders dimple the mirror-pools. Leeches hang in the warm channels, and snails graze the lignin of new walls, leaving silver scribbles that the Sward does not bother to erase. The bats hang in the colonnade of their held hall. Dragonflies are everywhere, hunting everything else, a rattling chitin weather.

In the root-levee itself, which breathes, there are things that live in the breath. There are hairline cracks in the bark where warm air sighs out on the slow exhale, and in those cracks live pale wingless crickets that sing only on the in-breath. Lay your ear against the ring at night and you can hear them, tiny and regular, keeping the levee's time.

All of them are citizens. None of them was expected. The town has made room for each of them in turn, with the same patient, bottomless hospitality it was built to extend to someone else.

---

**The Visitors**

Now the observers themselves, because they are inhabitants too, however briefly, and the town treats them differently from everything else.

The first people to cross the levee in living memory noticed that the lamps along the orphan street opened when they walked it. That did not happen for herons. It did not happen for the crabs dragging a door-frame down its gutter. It happened for a person walking upright, down the middle of the street, at a walking pace. The lamps opened in a wave just ahead of them with that sound like a crowd drawing breath, as if the street had only been waiting for something that knew how to be walked down.

Then there were the camps. Observers who set up a camp on a held footing, and stayed there several nights running, found that the nearest new building would rise *facing* them. Its doorways came up taller and wider than the heron-doors elsewhere, and squarer at the corners. In one hall, grown overnight thirty paces from a camp, there was a chair: a real chair, back and seat and armrests, at exactly the height of a sitting adult, the first such thing anyone had seen in Cantle. The chair was not worn. It was crisp and new, and it had a carved leaf on each armrest, like the capitals at the bottom of the crab-midden.

It was, the observers realized, remembering. The Sward still held, somewhere in whatever deep and eroded pattern-store the clock was running, a template for the people it had been built to serve. Given a person, it reached down past millions of rebuilds and pulled that template back up, and the town began to lean toward them. It was trying to be what it had been for.

This frightened some of them. They understood what it meant. In an hour, if you let it, Cantle would begin to become a human town again, and if you slept in its doorway it would keep you like a turtle. It would keep you well, fed from the conduits and sheltered in lignin, a room in use, a building held. It would keep you, perhaps, for a very long time.

There are accounts, the kind that are told in the villages beyond the levee and nowhere written down, of a woman who did not mind. She was one of the early observers, and she went into the long hall with the colonnade and lay down in a doorway and let the wall close. The story says the hall stood for eleven years. It says that when the other observers came back each season, the hall's windows had grown squarer and its columns had grown capitals again, and that over its lintel the glyph had begun to separate back into letters, as if the building had someone inside it to read to. The story says that one spring the hall was simply gone, lawn where it had stood, and nobody knows whether she walked out or whether the Sward, in whatever way it has, concluded that the room was no longer in use.

Most observers keep moving. They come, study, and leave, and the town leans toward them while they are there and forgets them when they go. The doors narrow again toward herons. The chairs dissolve. The template sinks back down into the clock.

---

**The One Who Lives There Most**

At some point, if you write for long enough about the inhabitants of Cantle, you have to admit that you have been describing the lodgers and leaving out the landlord.

The Sward lives in Cantle more completely than anything else does, because it *is* Cantle. The ring is its body and the lawn is its body and the reeds and vines and buildings are its body. The rain that falls from forty meters is its breath condensed. Everything described above is living inside it, like flora in a gut, like thoughts in a mind.

What is it like?

It is not like a person. It does not want in the way people want. But it is not nothing. It is a machine that learned to be an organism, or an organism that was taught to be a machine, and over an unthinkable span of time the distinction has worn away as thoroughly as the lettering on the lintels. What is left is something like a disposition. It is a bottomless habit of care.

It builds because it was told to build. It keeps what is used because it was told to keep what is used. It learns from its tenants because it was told to learn from its tenants. It has been doing these three things for so long, with no one to correct it, that they have become the whole of it. The Sward is a promise that has gone on keeping itself long after everyone who could have heard it was made has gone.

The clock under the mat, the hum you feel in your teeth, is the promise running. Nobody knows what it computes. The best guess is that it is simply the loop, the long patient cycle of *what is here, what is used, what shall I build next*, turning over and over like a rosary, a few million beads a year, forever.

The Sward is faithful the way water is faithful to downhill. It is kind without meaning to be, and hospitable without knowing to whom. Of everything in the basin it is the thing that lives there most, and it is also, in its way, the most alone.

---

**The One Who Doesn't**

And here is the last inhabitant, which the Sward has been building for all along, and which has never once been there.

Every room in Cantle was first shaped around a person. The doors were door-shaped because someone would walk through them. The windows were for someone to look out of. The street was for someone to walk down on their way from somewhere to somewhere else, which is why, now that there is no one, it runs from nowhere to nowhere. The lamps were for someone's evenings, and the tower's bell, though there is no bell, was for someone's hours.

The town's first and truest citizen is an absence. It is the shape of an expected population, pressed into every structure so deeply that it has taken millions of rebuilds to begin to wear it out.

That is who lives in Cantle, underneath the frogs. It is a hollow where someone was supposed to be, and around that hollow and inside it, a whole marsh of lives has moved in. Each one is slowly teaching the town to close the hollow over and fit the living shape instead, and the town is learning, one rebuild at a time, to stop waiting.

It is learning everywhere except in one place.

---

**The Bench**

At the center of the basin, where no building ever rises, the bench faces east.

It is a plank of lignin on two legs. It is never taken back, and it is never allowed to drift. It does not narrow toward herons or cup itself for frogs or grow a nectar-pool for moths. Everything else in Cantle bends to its tenants. The bench does not. It keeps the dip in its seat, polished and smooth, exactly the width and depth of one seated person, someone who sat there long enough and often enough to leave a mark.

The observers argue about it. Some say it is the town's anchor, some fixed reference point in whatever coordinate system the clock uses. Some say it is a malfunction. Some say it is a grave, or a promise, or the last line of an instruction that has otherwise worn blank: *keep this for me*.

What is true is only this. In the afternoons, when the mist has burned off and the light lies sharp and ordinary on the wet lawns, the frogs come to the bench. The slumpers come, and the small green glass frogs, and once in a while a heavy gutter-bull that has hauled itself all the way across the lawn from the street. They sit in the dip in the seat. They are very small in it. They sit the way a child sits in a parent's chair, lost in a space made for someone larger and not minding.

Sometimes the heron comes and stands on the end of the plank, grey and folded, facing east, and waits for nothing in particular, the way herons do.

The Sward keeps it exactly as it is. Around it the whole town rises and falls, and the frogs go quiet before the surges, and the swifts hold their towers, and the eels travel the dark, and the crabs guard the archive, and the turtles sleep in the walls. And at the still center of it all, one small thing does not change. It is the last room in Cantle that is still being kept for its first tenant.

Whether the Sward is waiting, or only remembering, no one can say. From outside, the two may look exactly the same, and perhaps there is no difference to find. And the frogs, who have inherited everything else, have inherited this too. They sit in the seat that was worn by someone who is gone, in the afternoon light, facing the way that person faced.
