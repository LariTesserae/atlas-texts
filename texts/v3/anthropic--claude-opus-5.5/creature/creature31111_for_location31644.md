# The Remainder

## The First Answer

The easy answer is that nothing lives here. Things *function* here.

Go down through the lid and wait by a column, and every creature you see can be described by what it does. The intake eels ride the inrush. The valve-shrimp click in the dark between sphincters. The rays keep their routes, the grazers keep their strips, the followers keep their pods. Nobody is hungry here in the way hunger usually means something. Food arrives on the beat. Nothing is born out of season, because there are no seasons. Nothing seems to die, or if it does, it is replaced so exactly that the difference can't be found.

You could stay a year and conclude that the lake has occupants and no inhabitants. It has parts, and no one who is at home.

That conclusion is almost right. The lake would like you to reach it. But a system this perfect has a problem it can't talk about, and the problem has to live somewhere.

## The Problem

Nothing is ever exactly the same. Not quite.

A column's stroke lands a few thousandths of a second late, because the cable feeding it has thickened by the width of a cell. A pod drops from the press with a shell a hair's weight too heavy. A ray drifts a few meters off its route because it swallowed something that disagreed with it. A lid-grazer crops a strip a finger's width too wide. Across a sea the size of a continent, over hundreds of thousands of columns and uncountable animals, these tiny errors happen all the time, every stroke.

The lake corrects them. That is what equilibrium means here. It is not stillness. It is constant, minute correction, fast and fine enough to look like stillness. The timing pulses nudge a late column back into phase. The followers push a wandering pod back into its current. The circulation smooths a warm pocket into the average before any instrument could find it.

But correcting an error doesn't destroy it. It moves it somewhere else. A late beat gets pulled back into line, and the lateness has to go somewhere. It leaks into the grid, flows toward the lowest point, and settles, the way silt settles out of a river. So does every excess degree, every stray gram, every wrong turning. The lake is a vast, patient machine for making everything the same, and the sameness has an exhaust.

The exhaust runs downhill. And at the bottom of the hill, something is living on it.

## Where the Beat Stumbles

Most of the lake has no geography that matters. Columns and open water go on and on. But the rolling wave of strokes, which crosses the sea forever in one direction, gets to one region and hesitates.

You can hear it before you can find it. Anywhere else in the lake the rhythm is flawless, stroke after stroke, each column taking up the beat from the one before like a note passed along a choir. Swim far enough toward the deepest part of the basin, down where the stone floor sags into a bowl a few hundred kilometers wide, and the rhythm starts to *swing*. Nothing fails. No column misses a stroke. But the offsets there are not quite fixed. One column comes in a hair late, the next a hair early to make up for it, like a drummer who has heard something and is leaning toward it.

The floor changes too. Everywhere else the polished flutes in the stone point the same way, the permanent record of the permanent current. Here they bend. They curve and cross, and in places they spiral, like wood grain around a knot. The glass under the old cable crossings is not smooth. It is rippled and layered, fused and cracked and fused again, because the arcs here don't always fire at the same interval. Some of the glass is faintly colored, amber and smoke-brown. There is no other color like it in the lake.

The water is warmer. It is only about a degree warmer, but in a lake where nothing ever varies, that degree is something you notice.

And the taste changes. Everywhere else it is cucumber and copper, clean as a blade. Here there is something under it: iron that has rusted, a little rot, a little sweetness. It tastes like an old orchard. It tastes like *time*.

## The First Sight

At first you think the root-felt has grown thick here, and that is not wrong.

The white felt that carpets the whole basin floor runs into the bowl and changes as it goes. It stops being even. It bunches and knots. It gathers into ridges like scar tissue, then mounds, then shapes too large to call mounds. They rise off the floor in slow folds and lobes, some tens of meters high. They are no longer white. They have gone ivory, then bone-yellow, then streaked with rust and a dark wine color, a color with red in it. Nothing else in the lake is red.

It is all one thing. The shapes you take for separate hills connect underneath, through thick cords that run into the felt and out again. The thing fills the whole bottom of the bowl. It is hundreds of kilometers of folded, knotted, many-colored tissue, laid over the stone the way a sleeping animal lies in its den.

It is moving. You can't see it, but you can feel it in the water, as you feel the cables: a slow, irregular pulse, nothing like the columns. It has no fixed interval. It is more like breathing, or like the shifting of something turning over in its sleep.

## What It Is Made Of

Get close and you can read it, because it is layered.

Cut into one of the lobes, if you could bear to, and you'd find rings, as in a tree. They are not the even rings of a tree that has had even years. They are fat and thin, pale and dark. They bend where something pressed against them, and they carry scars and inclusions. Here is a band of dull blue shot through the ivory: the season, if there were seasons, when the spark-fish schools near one crossing flinched a little too hard, a little too often, and the lake corrected them, and the correction settled here. Here is a pod, whole and smooth, sealed into the tissue like a stone in a tree trunk. Its notch is on the wrong side. Here is a knot of gray ribbon that was once an intake eel, one that let go at the wrong moment of the stroke.

Everything the lake gets wrong comes here. Every wrongness the machine smooths away comes down the gradient and gets taken in. The late beats are here. The heavy pods are here. The wandering rays are here. Some of them came in the body at the end of their long circuits, sinking into the bowl as though called. The creature holds all of it, and is all of it.

The foundry-followers bring it things, and they bring them gently. For all the thousands of pods a follower escorts, sometimes one comes out of a press wrong: asymmetric, or a shade too pale, or with ridges that spiral the wrong way. The follower does not steer that pod back into the current. It turns aside from the fixed route, the only time it ever does, and carries the pod down, a long way down, into the bowl. There it lays the pod against the tissue and waits, watching, until the creature folds over it. Then the follower returns to its route and never deviates again.

Maybe that is what the followers get out of it. They are the only thing in the lake that is allowed, once in a while, to go somewhere unscheduled.

## A Wrong Guess

It is tempting to call it a parasite. It lives on waste, on what the lake throws away. It sits in the low place and eats.

That doesn't account for the rhythm, though. The columns near the bowl lean toward it. It is not pulling them off true. Things that fall under its influence don't degrade. The warm degree, the swinging beat and the bent flutes all stay local. They don't spread. The lake around it is still perfect. And the columns nearest it, the ones that swing most, are the healthiest in the sea. Their wood is densest, their valves tightest, their batteries brightest.

It is also tempting to call it a keeper, a thing that holds the machine together. But it doesn't correct anything. It only receives.

So watch longer. Watch as the rays watch their routes, over days and years. Then something appears that no reading anywhere else in the lake could show you.

## The Only Thing That Remembers

Everywhere else in the lake, now is the same as then. A particle released today follows the path of one released ten thousand years ago. There is nothing to tell them apart. A reading taken today matches a reading taken before any creature here had a name. In the most exact sense the lake has no history. It has a state, and it keeps returning to that state.

The creature in the bowl is different. It is older at the bottom than at the top. It has a *before*.

Its deepest layers, pressed almost to stone and fused in places into the amber glass, are not made of small errors. They are made of large ones. There is a stratum full of fine black grit, the season of the long ashfall when something far away burned and the lid went gray for a hundred years. The lake corrected it, and the correction came here. There is a band of mineral salt, pale and crystalline, from the age when the basin briefly tilted. There is a layer of tissue unlike anything else, loose and fast-grown, straining outward and branching like a reaching hand. That is from the time when the lake was still *growing*. It hadn't yet reached its edges. It was still making, not keeping.

When the lake turned toward sameness, it had to put its growth somewhere. It had to put its young, reaching, expanding self somewhere, and it put it here. The creature is not just the lake's exhaust. It is the lake's childhood, folded down into the deepest part of the basin, and kept.

It is the only thing in the whole sea that has changed. That makes it the only thing that knows anything has *happened*.

## Why the Lake Needs It

A perfectly regular heart is not a healthy heart. That is known in other places, among other kinds of life. A heart that beats with exact, unvarying intervals has lost its ability to answer. It has stopped listening to the body, and it is getting ready to stop. A living heart wavers a little from beat to beat. It hesitates and hurries. It is always adjusting, and the wavering is what readiness sounds like.

The lake is a heart the size of a continent, and it has made itself almost perfectly regular. Almost. It could not survive being *entirely* regular. A thermostat with nothing to measure against cannot hold a temperature. A clock with nothing to compare itself to cannot keep time. The lake's sameness is an achievement. It is actively held, fought for with every stroke. Holding something requires feeling the pull away from it.

The creature is the pull. It is the error signal, given a body. It is where the lake keeps its variability, all of it, gathered into one low place so that everywhere else can be still. The swing in the columns near the bowl is not the creature disrupting the lake. It is the lake listening to the creature. It is checking itself against the one thing that changes, the way a sailor checks a compass against the stars.

All that sameness, stroke after stroke across a continent, is measured against this. It is the lake's sense of time, the part that knows it is still running.

## What It Does

It lies in the bowl and grows, very slowly, one ring of mistakes after another.

It hums. Of all the sounds in the lake, this is the strangest, and you only hear it after you have been near it long enough for your ears to stop hearing the pumps. It is low and uneven, deeper than any stroke. It has no interval. It rises and falls against the beat of the columns and never locks to it. It weaves around the rhythm, falls behind, catches up, and slips ahead. In a sea that sounds like a foundry running forever, this is the only syncopation. It may be the only music.

It dreams, if that word can mean anything down there. You would not know it except that sometimes a slow ripple crosses its surface. A lobe unfolds, stretches toward the edge of the bowl, stops, and folds back. The movement is exactly like the loose, reaching tissue in its deepest layer. It is remembering growing. It is remembering edges that hadn't yet been reached.

It changes color. The rust and wine and bone-yellow are not fixed. Over years they drift and pool and spread, the only weather the lake has ever had that isn't permanent.

It takes in the dead, when they come. Rays at the end of their circuits settle into the bowl with their wings folded, and the tissue rises around them like a slow tide. A day later they are a new band in the stratum, a stripe of mottled green and pale. Nothing else in the lake has a grave. Nothing else in the lake is remembered. Here, everything is.

## What Lives Here

Now go back to the first answer.

Nothing lives here, you said. Things function. And it is true that in the columns and the currents and the long grid of the floor, everything is a part, and the parts don't know they're parts.

But at the bottom of the basin, one thing lives. It is made of everything that went wrong. It is the late beat and the heavy pod and the eel that let go. It is the long ashfall and the tilting and the young, reaching years before the lake learned to keep. It is warm when nothing else is warm. It is red when nothing else is red. It remembers when nothing else can. It is the only thing in the lake that is old and not merely unending. The whole machine rests on it, leans toward it, measures itself against it, and needs it. That need is not for its perfection. The lake needs the creature for its imperfection.

It has no name. The lake does not name things. If you wanted to name it, you might call it the Remainder: what is left over when a vast sum is divided as evenly as it can be, the part that won't go in, the part that carries forward.

## The Surface Again

Climb back through the lid into the thin cold air. The wind is steady from the same direction it always blows. The sky is pale, the sun is only the sun, and the green flatness runs out to every horizon.

The ground trembles underfoot once every ninety seconds, and it will never stop.

But go far enough across the plain, toward the place where the basin is deepest, and stand there long enough, for days or years, as long as the rays take. Then you may feel it. Once, in all that time, the ground trembles at ninety-one.
