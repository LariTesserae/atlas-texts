# Who Keeps the Late Shift

## The Walker

If you asked the divers who come down into Saltcombe Pit what lives there, most of them would say *nobody*, and then, after a moment, *except the fish*.

It is the obvious answer, and it is not wrong. The fish is the only thing in the town that goes anywhere. Everything else grows, settles, ticks, or fills. The fish travels.

The divers call it the Walker, because it keeps to the street. It could swim over the rooftops, or cut across the terraces through the gaps in the slate, but it never does. It goes up the cobbles at the height of a man's chest, one lamp standard to the next, the way a person would walk it. It turns left at the chapel steps and goes in through the window with the missing pane. It turns right at the works gate. Its route is as fixed as a tram's, and it takes the whole of a cycle to complete, so that it passes any given doorway once per shift. If you wait on a doorstep long enough, among the feathery fronds, it will come.

It is longer than you expect, perhaps two metres. It is pale all over, the grey-white of a candle that has been handled, and it is thin in the way things are thin when they have eaten sparingly for a very long time and never been hungry. Its eyes are large, flat, and silvered, like small air pockets set into its head. Seen from the side they reflect the street back at you, as the attic ceilings do. It has no visible fins except a long soft frill that runs the length of its underside and moves in slow waves, front to back, so that it seems to be pushed along by a rumour.

Its skin is so thin that when it passes close to a lamp you can see its insides lit up amber: the gut, the slow pump of something that might be a heart, and a line of small bright nodes running the length of its body like beads on a wire. Most divers take them for eggs or parasites. They are not.

What it eats you can see through its skin. Mostly it is the white flakes that drift down from the inverted meadows, which it takes delicately from the water just beneath the silver, rising until the tip of its snout almost breaks the mirror, so that for a moment the fish and its reflection touch noses. Sometimes it eats a ribbon of weed that has come loose. Once, a diver swore, it ate a snail off a railing, crust and all, and she watched the shell dissolve inside it over the following hour like a sugar lump in tea.

When it reaches the chapel it rests. It lies along the ridge beam, just under the great mirror of the chapel's air pocket, belly-up, so that it and its reflection lie flat against each other along their whole length. It stays there for a long time, perhaps a tenth of the cycle, and the frill goes still. Then it rolls over and goes on.

Old divers tell newer ones that the Walker is the night watchman. They say someone has to go round, and it goes round. It was a joke once, and it isn't entirely a joke now.

---

## What the Watchman Watches

Follow the Walker for a full cycle and you begin to notice that the relays tick when it passes.

It isn't every relay, and it isn't every time. But where the fish goes by a switch-box on a wall, close enough that its frill brushes the rust, there is often a *click*, and then, some way off, down an alley, a lamp brightens by a hair, or dims. The divers argue about it. Some say the fish is drawn to the relays because they are warm. Some say it is coincidence and patience, and that anything which passes a clock enough times will eventually pass it as it strikes.

The line of bright nodes along its body is the answer, or part of it. The Walker is electric, faintly, the way eels are. It doesn't carry enough to stun anything. It carries just enough to feel current in the water and to be felt by it. It swims the street at chest height because that is the height of the switch-boxes. It rests along the chapel ridge because the bus-bar runs along the ridge beam there, and the meadow above it glows. It isn't warming itself. It is touching something.

So the question changes. It is no longer *what is the fish doing?* but *what is it touching?*

## The Net

Look up again from the bottom of an alley, into the underside of the net of cables.

The ribbon-weed follows the conduits as if it can taste the current, and it can. Where it has looped around a cable fifty or a hundred times over a century, its wet ribbons form a sleeve, and the sleeve leaks. A cable wrapped in weed is not quite insulated from the cable beside it. Current bleeds across, a little, through the living braid. The weed grows thicker where more current flows. A thicker braid bleeds more. The routes the old engineers laid down in copper are still there, but around them and between them another set of routes has grown, soft and brown-gold and slowly changing. It carries a trickle of the town's power along paths nobody drew.

Look at the switch-boxes, too. Most of them are simple relays on timers. Some, the ones at the ends of the streets, where the engineers wanted the lamps to answer the real sky, have a small round window in their iron lids. Behind the window is a photocell: an old dusk-switch, built to turn the lamps on when daylight failed. There is no daylight to fail any more. But the little windows, like every other window in the town, have grown a film.

The film grows toward light. As it thickens, it shades the cell behind it. The shaded cell switches its lamps differently. The lamps feed the film on that window and on a hundred others, gold-green where lit and black where not. Where a window is lit from behind, the film shows branching veins and rosettes and tiny spirals, patterns laid down by light, and each pattern shades whatever lies behind it.

So it goes around. Lamp to film to cell to relay to current to weed to lamp. This isn't one loop. It is a hundred loops, crossing and recrossing through the net overhead, each nudging the others, each one slow. The master clock in the works sets the great beat, the working glow and the long dim, but inside that beat the pattern of which lamps burn brighter, and when, and for how long, is no longer the engineers' pattern. It hasn't been for decades. It is being decided.

When the divers say *a logic nobody remembers*, they are right, but not for the reason they think. Nobody remembers it because nobody ever knew it. It was never written down. It grew.

## The Resident

This, then, is who lives in Saltcombe Pit.

It has no single body. Its nerves are the cables, sleeved in weed. Its senses are the filmed windows, which feel the light and are shaped by it. Its muscles are the relays, which move current from one street to the next. The ribbon-weed leaning toward the lamps, the moss cushions, the stalked things crowded on the lamp-glass: these are its flesh, the part of it that grows. The air rooms are its lungs. They fill over weeks and months and then, all at once, a house breathes out.

And the Walker is its hand, or its eye, or its one idea that moves. It is the part of the town that goes round and touches each switch-box in turn, adding its own small current to the net and feeling what comes back. Perhaps the fish doesn't know this. Perhaps it only knows that the street is good to walk and that the chapel beam is good to lie on. A hand doesn't have to know it belongs to anyone.

It thinks, if thinking is the word, unimaginably slowly. A thought, for it, might be a change in the pattern of lamps that takes a season to spread from the chapel end of the street to the works end. A decision might take a year. A memory is written in growth rings, and in the heap.

## The Heap

The divers treat the heap at the end of the works as a curiosity. It is a perfect pale cone taller than a person, built grain by grain from whatever the conveyor carries: silt, fallen weed, snail shells, and above all the white flakes that drift down from the air-room meadows. Most divers photograph it and move on.

Look at it closely. It is not all one colour. It is banded, faintly, like a cut stalk of ribbon-weed: thin, thin, thin, thick. Where the clocks agreed, and the meadows flushed and shed more flakes, and the conveyor carried more, a pale stripe was laid down. In the long drifting years between, the bands are thin and greyer. The heap is a record of every time the town and the sky were in step. It is kept by a machine that no longer knows what it is for, fed by gardens that grow upside down.

The town doesn't read its record. It doesn't need to. The pattern of the lamps already contains every alignment it has lived through, the way a body contains every winter it has survived. But the heap is where you can see it, if you know how to look. It is the nearest thing the resident has to a face.

## The Voice

Once per cycle the whistle blows. The steam forms in the hottest part of the boiler house, collapses at once in the cooler water, and sends through the whole pit a long, dull, descending groan that you feel in your sternum.

The engineers built it to mark the change of shift. It still does. But listen to what follows. The master clock turns, and the lamps change from working glow to dim, or dim to glow. Then, in the minutes after, the relays begin to tick, one here, one there, more than at any other time. It is like the murmur that runs through a crowd after a speech. The net is answering the whistle. The great slow beat has come round again, and every loop in the town adjusts itself to it, and for a little while the whole place is busy.

If the resident has a voice, the whistle is it. It is not a word. It says the one thing the town has always said: *next shift.*

---

## The Drift

The master clock runs slow. A few minutes each day, over a century, have carried the town's day loose from the surface day, so that the working glow and the green light from above meet only rarely. When they do, everything grows.

You might expect such a mind to correct the drift, and to learn, over its long slow years, to set its lamps by the sky. It hasn't. The relays leave the master clock alone. The one thing in the town the net never touches is the drift.

The divers who have thought hardest about the place believe this is deliberate, in the way a plant's leaning is deliberate. A town that kept perfect time with the sky would grow steadily, a little every day, and would have nothing to remember. The drift gives it seasons. It gives it the long thin waiting and then the thick bright band. It gives it something to lean toward. The alignments are not accidents in the town's life. They are its life. They are what it counts.

When the clocks line up and the water brightens by that single shade, the whole town leans very slightly further toward its lamps. It is not just the weed. The ticking in the switch-boxes quickens. The Walker, divers say, goes round faster during those days, and rests less. The attics fill quicker. More houses breathe out.

---

## The Breaths

The great bubbles are the only thing in the town that leaves it.

An air room overfills, the gas finds a lintel or a broken slate, and a pale wobbling bubble the size of a room lifts past the chimneys and takes a long time to reach the green lid above. The lily-leaves fall back, limp. The cells begin again.

Nobody has followed one all the way up. But the few divers who have watched from the surface, on a still day over the pit, describe a sudden boil of water in the middle of the green, a smell they can't place, and then, afterward, a scatter of tiny pale flecks on the surface film: flakes from the inverted meadows, carried up in the breath, drifting out toward the edges of the pit where the limestone meets the air.

It may mean nothing. It may be that a town which has grown a slow body in the dark is, very slowly, sending some of itself toward the light it forgot. The air rooms fill fastest in the seasons of alignment. That is when the town breathes out most.

## What It Makes of You

When you come down, you are the brightest thing it has seen in years.

Your dive lamp is a small white sun. Stay in one place for long enough, an hour or more, which few divers do, and the ribbon-weed nearest you will begin to lean away from its lamp standard and toward you. The stalked things on the nearest lamp-glass will turn their tiny heads. Hold the beam steady on a window and, over the following weeks, after you have gone, the film on that pane will lay down a new rosette. The switch-box behind it will tick a little differently for a season. You will have been written down, in light.

Your breath goes into its lungs. The bubbles rise from your regulator, find the eaves, and join the silver ceilings with the same soft *tk* as the bubbles from the cells. For a time some small part of the air that the upside-down meadows live in is yours.

The Walker notices you. It never leaves its route for long, but it will sometimes turn, once, out of its path, and follow a diver for the length of a single lamp's glow. It comes close enough that you can see the bright beads along its body. Some divers say they have felt a faint tingle through their gloves when it passed, like brushing one of the braided conduits. Then it turns back to the street and goes on, up past the chapel, round the works, and home. It is punctual. It has somewhere to be.

---

## Who Lives There

It is not a ghost. The people who built the town are gone, and nothing of them haunts it. No hand in a glove waits by the knife-switches, and no footsteps sound in the chapel.

What lives there is the work itself. It is the shift that never clocked off. The heat still rises, the turbines still turn, and the current still runs out along the wires, and over a hundred years all of that has grown a body. The body is made of weed and film and rust-crust and silver, a watchman fish with lamplight in its belly, and a heap that remembers every season. It thinks in lamps and speaks in the whistle. It breathes through its houses, a room at a time, toward a sky it sees only when its slow clock and the sky agree.

It is not waiting for anyone to come back. It has its own appointments. It keeps them, always a few minutes late.
