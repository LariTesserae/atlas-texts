# Those Who Live in Ossary

## The Leaning People

Start with the obvious answer, because the obvious answer is still true. People live in Ossary, around four million of them if you trust the stairwell censuses, which nobody does, since a stairwell census counts doorways and some doorways in the Guttering open onto forty rooms stacked like shelves in a cupboard.

You can pick out an Ossaran in any port on the continent by the way they stand. In a city with no flat ground, the body stops trusting flatness. Ossarans carry their weight a little forward and to one side, as if every floor were a ramp. On a level quay at the coast they look faintly drunk. In the harbor towns they are called *leaners*, and it isn't meant kindly. They have strong calves and ankles as supple as a goat's. Old Ossarans walk with a hand trailing along the nearest wall. They aren't holding it for support. They're touching it the way someone else might touch the shoulder of the person beside them in a crowd.

Their hands give them away too. The water that weeps from every surface is bitter and slick, and a lifetime of touching wet stone wears the skin smooth. Ossaran children have fingerprints. Most Ossaran grandparents don't, or have only faint whorls near the fingertips, like the carved inscriptions on the lower walls that the snails have grazed into illegibility. Ossarans say *the city takes your mark back*, and they don't say it sadly. Among the old families, a perfectly smooth palm is a kind of dignity. It means you have been here long enough to be read by the stone rather than the other way around.

They are pale, because the sieved light rarely falls on anyone for long, but it isn't a sickly paleness. Their skin has the faintly glossy look of the walls, from the gull-fat and nest-glue salves everyone rubs into it each night against the caustic damp. Nobody wears much. It is always warm and always wet. Clothing is oilcloth and loose linen, with a hood whose broad stiff brim sheds whatever falls from above. Visitors think the hoods are for rain. They are mostly for guano. A man pulling his hood forward in greeting is the Ossaran equivalent of tipping a hat.

## How They Speak

Ossarans talk low.

It's the first thing a linguist notices, and they have been studying it for three hundred years. Swift-scream sits high at the top of hearing, so over the centuries Ossaran speech has sunk beneath it into the chest and throat, all rounded vowels and hummed consonants. To outsiders it sounds like people speaking with their mouths closed. Singing in Ossary is almost entirely bass, and even women and children pitch their voices down. A high voice doesn't carry here. It dissolves into the shrieking overhead like a drop of water falling into the sea.

They also talk in gaps. Every Ossaran learns, before they can read, to feel the approach of a Turning: the change in the air, the thickening roar, the first lifting spirals. Conversations are shaped around it. The grammar favors short complete clauses, so a sentence is never left hanging when the noise comes down. There's a verb tense, the *before-turning*, used for anything said at dusk that must be remembered until morning. Promises are made in it. So are marriage vows.

When the noise comes and no one can hear, they switch to hands. Ossaran hand-cant is old and dense, as full as any spoken tongue. It has dialects by ward and slang by trade. In the minutes of the Turning, whole negotiations happen in silence on the stairways. People press against the walls with birds tearing past a yard overhead, and their fingers move fast and small, close to the chest. A courtship conducted entirely during Turnings is considered the most romantic kind. It proves you wanted to say it enough to say it without sound.

Ossaran names come in two parts. There is a spoken name, and a *drip name*, which is the name of the groove on the family wall beneath which the child was born. Every old house has grooves worn down its face by particular drips, and each has been named for centuries: *the Long Thread*, *the Widow's Mark*, *the Fork*, *the One That Stopped*. You might be Anneth of the Fork. Ask an Ossaran where they're from and they won't give you a ward. They'll touch a groove.

## How They Live

They eat what the Column brings and what the cable-lifts haul up. Grain comes up the escarpment in iron baskets, paid for in fertilizer, and the old joke goes that every loaf in Ossary has already been to the fields and back. Most of what they eat is their own, though. There are midge-flour cakes, dense and nutty and faintly shrimp-tasting. There's snail broth, clear and pale, simmered all day. In season there are gull eggs, stolen from the harbor rooftops by boys who come home bloodied from the beaks. Rat is poor people's meat and nobody pretends otherwise. No one has ever eaten a swift. The idea is so deeply forbidden that there's no word for the act. You'd have to describe it in pieces, like describing a murder to someone who had never heard of death.

They sleep tilted, in hammocks or on slanted pallets with a lip at the foot. In the oldest houses the beds are shallow carved troughs in the stone, angled toward a drain, with a thin runnel of wall-water down one side so the sleeper is never quite dry. Ossarans who travel can't sleep in flat beds. They say it feels like lying on the lid of a coffin.

They are not a quiet people, though they speak quietly. They are a close people. Four million of them live in the warm wet crevices of a stone organism, pressed together the way the swifts press into the flues, and privacy is a lowland idea they find faintly sinister. Doors in Ossary are curtains of chitin beads that rattle when anything moves, whether a person or a rat or a draft from the Throats. A shut door means someone is dying, and the neighbors will come.

## The Trades, and the People the Trades Make

A city this old sorts its people by what they do, and each trade makes a slightly different animal out of a human being.

**The Fanwardens** tend the Throats and the great engines. They are heavy, deaf by forty, and gentle in the way of people who spend their lives beside something enormous that could kill them. They feel the city's heartbeat, the forty slow thuds a minute, in their teeth and breastbones. Put them anywhere else and they go restless and anxious and can't settle, because they can't feel the pulse. A Fanwarden's family counts their breathing against the engine without knowing it. When a Throat's fan goes down for repair, the whole ward sleeps badly.

**The Stratigraphers** are the guano miners, and they're half scholar and half laborer. They cut the compressed beds beneath the roosts and read them as they go. Each layer is a year, and each layer says something: a thin bed means a bad season, feathers of an odd color mean a flock from elsewhere, and a band of ash means the Kiln Heights burned. They know Ossary's history better than any archivist because they hold it in their hands before they cart it off to be sold. Stratigrapher families keep what they call *core-shelves*, small polished columns cut from the beds under their own roost and stacked in the house. The family's years are laid down in brown stone, and a grandmother can run her smooth palm down one and say *here is when I was born, the Year of the Soft Brown*, and her fingers will find it.

**The Gatherers of the Fallen** walk the streets at dawn with long-handled baskets and collect the birds that died in the night. Thousands of them come down every day, swifts and bats alike, and every one is lifted, counted, and carried. Gatherers wear grey and do not speak while working, not even in hand-cant. The swifts are rendered in the Quiet Kilns with small rites: a word, a pinch of chitin-flour on the pyre. The ash is mixed back into the lime that makes new mortar. Every new Ossaran wall, then, is built partly from birds, so that the living ones will know it. Gatherers are respected and a little feared. Children who see one early in the morning hold their breath until the grey figure has passed.

**The Climbers** are the nest-harvesters, and they are the strangest.

## The Climbers

Climbers go up the chimneys on the iron rungs every season after the breeding and pull down the old saliva cups to be boiled into glue. They work in near-darkness with the flue walls moving on every side, a living lining of birds that shift and scream and settle. A Climber feels her way by touch and by sound. Over a lifetime she becomes something the birds tolerate, and then something they ignore, and then, the old Climbers say, something they include.

Climbers have long arms and callused fingers that curl a little even at rest. Their eyes are large and dark-adapted and they wince in daylight. They are slight and wiry, because the rungs punish weight. Many of them hum constantly and quietly, below the scream, because a humming body in a flue is a known body and the birds don't startle. Some of them stop being able to sleep lying down at all. They doze sitting upright with their backs pressed to a wall and their feet braced, like the swifts themselves, which cannot perch and do not lie down.

Climbers pass their trade down in families. The families don't marry much outside it, and over the generations their people have grown recognizable: long-limbed and dark-eyed, a lineage the rest of Ossary calls *the chimney-born*. They have their own ward on the slopes above the Cup, a warren of narrow vertical houses whose rooms are stacked one on top of another and joined by ladders rather than stairs. Their children climb before they walk.

And some Climbers stay up.

It isn't common, but it isn't rare either. A Climber who has spent thirty years in the flues goes up one season and doesn't come down. She rigs a sling and a ledge-board somewhere in the upper throat of an old chimney. Her family lowers bread and broth and water on a line, and she lives there with the birds. The Ossarans call them the *Walled*, and they don't consider it madness. It's closer to taking holy orders. The Walled live for years inside the roosts. Their skin takes on the violet bloom of the films, and they speak less and less. When they die, they are left. The swifts build over them. Some of the oldest flues in the Cup contain a Walled Climber from three or four centuries back, gone to bone and saliva-glue, sealed into the chimney wall under a crust of nests, part of the roost itself.

Ask a chimney-born family how many of their own are in the walls and they'll tell you exactly. They say it the way other families say how many are buried in the lowland cemeteries: *we have eleven in the Old Crook flue, and our great-aunt in the Fork chimney*. And if you listen at the base of those flues at the Turning, among the millions of birds, some Climbers swear you can hear humming.

## The Cup

Everyone agrees the Cup is where the old families live. Nobody agrees on who they are.

The Cup is the oldest quarter, sunk into a basin of its own making, dim and wet and always dripping. Its people are pale even by Ossaran standards. They are very quiet, very courteous, and very slow to trust. Their hands are perfectly smooth by the age of thirty. They carry their faces a little turned upward as they walk. In the rest of the city it's said that Cup folk can tell the time of day, the season, and the mood of the flocks from the pitch of the scream alone, and can tell the difference between one chimney's birds and another's by ear.

Their houses are older than any record, and they've sunk so far that ground floors have become cellars, then cisterns, then flooded sealed galleries that no one enters. The Cup families live in what were once upper stories and are now street level. Down there they keep things the rest of Ossary has forgotten. They know the original drip names, from before the drips shifted. They keep the oldest form of hand-cant, full of signs no one else knows. They have a funeral rite that involves carrying the dead up, never down, to the mouths of the old flues.

The chimneys of the Cup hold the great swifts.

These are the ones the Ossarans don't discuss with visitors: nearly twice the size of any wild swift, with plumage gone almost to charcoal, and a scream that has dropped until it rasps rather than shrieks. They are the oldest bloodlines in Ossary, and some of them no longer leave the flues.

That is the part the Cup families know and don't say. The wild swifts of the world are ceaseless fliers. They sleep on the wing, drink on the wing, and mate on the wing, and touch nothing but their nests. The great swifts of the Cup's deepest flues cling in the dark, year after year, and do not migrate. They are enormous and still. Younger swifts fly in, pass midges to them beak to beak, and fly out again. They are fed the way hive-bees feed something at the heart of the hive. The washbasin-sized nests belong to them. When they scream, the sound comes up out of the flue mouths as a low rolling growl that the Cup families say they can feel in the cisterns beneath their houses.

The Cup families tend these flues. They are not Climbers and they don't harvest. They keep the flue mouths clear, keep the rungs oiled for no one, and come to the chimney bases at the Turning and stand in silence with their smooth palms pressed to the brick. Asked why, a Cup grandmother will say only: *someone has to be here when they want something.*

## The Second City

Now look up, because that's where the question starts to change.

The Climbers were right. There are roads in the air above Ossary.

On still evenings when the Column goes calm, the flocks move slowly enough to see their shapes. They are not random. The same spirals form over the same wards, and the same great folding curtains of birds hang in the same places, hour after hour and century after century. Chimney-born families have mapped them for generations on scrolls of pressed chitin. The maps show avenues and plazas, a great turning ring over the central wards, and long corridors of flight along the eastern rim that feed the Throats like arteries feeding a heart.

Hold one of those old sky-maps up against the Stratigraphers' reconstructions of the city's past, the vanished skylines read out of the guano beds, and something appears that most Ossarans would rather not think about.

The flocks trace buildings that aren't there anymore.

Over the Kiln Heights, a slow standing spiral wraps an empty column of air exactly where the Great Flue of the Thin Bed century stood before it fell. Over the Guttering, the birds part around a space shaped like a tower demolished three hundred years ago, splitting and closing again as if it were solid. The flocks of Ossary remember the city: every chimney that ever held them, every wall they ever clung to, every roost, stacked in the air in layers like the guano beds below. The second city up there is not a new city. It is all of Ossary's past cities at once, still being flown around.

It works in the other direction too. Ossarans don't like to admit it, but their builders have always watched the sky. When a flock begins to bend over a vacant plot, and a new spiral starts to thicken in the same place evening after evening, someone eventually builds a tower beneath it. The tower always takes. The swifts roost in it within a season rather than a generation. So the builders have learned to follow the sky. Nobody says the flocks are designing the city, but nobody builds against them either.

So the Ossarans keep building higher, reaching up toward a city that is reaching down toward them. Stone and air are growing into each other's shape.

## What Lives in Ossary

So who lives there?

People do, the leaning quiet low-voiced people with smooth hands, who sleep tilted and speak in the gaps and name their children after drips. Swifts do, hundreds of millions of them, screaming on the wing, and the great still elders in the dark of the Cup. So do the bats and the snails and the beetles and the films, and the rats living off the fallen. So do the Walled, sealed in their chimneys, humming or not.

But stay a year and the question comes apart in your hands, because none of those things live in Ossary the way a person lives in a house. They live in it the way cells live in a body.

Look at what the place does. It breathes: the Column rising, the Throats inhaling, the fans pulling air down into the wards and letting it out through ten thousand vents as the cloud that never leaves. It has a heartbeat, forty to the minute, which the Fanwardens feel in their teeth. It sweats. It has a skin of living film, banded by age like tree rings or bruises. It eats, taking in midges by the billion off the brine flats and carrying them in along its arteries of air, through the swifts, through the bats, through the rats and beetles, down into the guano beds, where the food is compressed and laid down as stone. It is building a body out of what it eats, the way a coral reef does, the way bone does.

It sinks, too. It is slowly settling into the tableland and softening the rock beneath, the old wards lowest, the Cup a basin of its own making. The Ossarans say *the city is settling*. The Stratigraphers, who know the beds best, say something slightly different. They say the old ward isn't sinking but digesting: working its way down into the plateau, turning limestone into city, the way a root works down into soil.

It remembers. The flocks overhead carry every building it has ever been, flying a map of itself in patterns nobody taught them.

And it grows. The towers rise a little each century where the sky asks for them. The swifts grow larger, and their scream drops deeper, the way a voice deepens with age.

What lives in Ossary is Ossary.

The people aren't its masters and aren't quite its servants. They're an organ of it, a slow-thinking part it grew for hands. The swifts can't build, dig, fire kilns, or cable fertilizer to the lowlands and bring back grain, so it grew a species that could. It shaped them the way it shapes everything, patiently and over strata. It leaned their bodies, lowered their voices, smoothed their fingers until they no longer leave their own mark on anything. It taught them to stop and stand still at the Turning, to gather its dead, and never to harm the part of it that flies. It bred some of them long-limbed and dark-eyed so they could climb into its throats, and some of them pale and patient to sit beside the old ones in the Cup and wait.

At the center of it all, in the deepest flues of the oldest quarter, the great swifts cling in the dark, fed by the young, too large now to fly out. Their scream is dropping, century by century, toward a register that isn't sound at all but pressure, felt in the cisterns, in the chimney brick, in the smooth palms of the people pressed against it at dusk.

The Cup families are waiting for that voice to drop low enough to be understood.

The grandmothers say it's close now. They say it has only one thing to tell them, that it has been trying for eight hundred years, and that when it finally arrives it will come in the before-turning tense, the one used for promises.

Every evening at dusk, the swifts pour down into the flues like smoke running backward. The whole pale city glows wet in the last light, and four million leaning people stand against the walls with their hands to the stone, listening. The thing they belong to breathes in once, enormously, and holds the air in its chimneys through the night.
