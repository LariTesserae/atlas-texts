# Who Lives on the Held Plateau

## I. The easy answer

The easy answer is the herds, and the easy answer isn't wrong.

Walk out among the grazers until one stands close enough to touch. The first thing you notice is not its shape but its warmth. Nothing else on the plateau is warm. The stone isn't, the dust isn't, and the air certainly isn't. Yet the grazer gives off a steady, modest heat you can feel on your face from a foot away, like standing near a wall that has had sun on it all day. Lay your palm against its flank and the warmth is exactly the temperature of your palm, however cold your hand is. Whatever you bring to it, it matches.

The fleece is not hair. Part it with your fingers and the strands are finer than wool, pale and slightly translucent, somewhere between felt and frost. They don't come away when you pull. Under the fleece is the body, and the body is dense and smooth and gives very slightly, the way a sun-warmed stone seems to give when you've been lying on it long enough. There is a pulse, if you wait. It comes once a minute.

The grazer is doing what it has always done. It lowers its head. Its muzzle, a soft rounded swelling, meets the edge of a lichen ring and presses there, and something happens that looks like cropping. The jaw moves in a slow sideways grind, but there are no teeth anymore, only a smooth ridge of something like horn. No lichen comes away. The grazer raises its head, turns a little to the left, and comes back. It is not eating the lichen. It is doing the eating the way a hand on a railing does the holding, out of an old agreement between the two of them, long after anyone needed support.

Look at the ground beneath it. Everywhere else on the plateau, footprints vanish, and the dust has always been smooth. But under each grazer's hooves the stone is worn into four shallow dishes, no deeper than a thumbprint, polished to a gloss. These are the only marks on the whole plateau that are allowed to stay. They can stay because they aren't marks of anything happening. They have always been there, the way the grazer has always been there.

They are not identical, and that is the second thing you notice. It takes a long time, because the likeness is what the eye sees first. But one grazer turns a little farther left than its neighbours before coming back. Another holds its head at the top of the raise for a fraction longer than the rest, and because the herds are all in phase, that small hesitation shows in the great wave that crosses the plateau. There is a slight kink in it at that spot, a place where the rising line of heads bows, and it has been there forever, the way a river has always bent at a particular rock. The plateau's stillness is not blank. It is made of thousands of tiny, permanent habits, each one the last trace of a creature that was once a particular creature and has been rubbed down to the single gesture that was most truly its own.

That may be what the worn faces are. The faces went first because faces are the most exposed part, and what remains is character without expression. The one who turns too far left. The one who lingers at the top. You could learn them. People have. Some of the tally marks, looked at closely, aren't counts at all. They are names. Each short row of strokes sits beside a scratched hint of a turn or a pause.

## II. The small and the hanging

The hares take three hops forward and three back. Their ears have worn down into two smooth swellings on the crown of the head, and when the great breath comes in, the swellings flush very faintly, from bone-cream to a pale shell-pink. They seem to be listening with the whole top of the skull to a sound that is really a pressure. When the breath goes out, the colour fades. Watch a field of hares from a low rise and you will see the pink come and go across the whole field. It looks like light moving over water.

The ground birds on the tors shift from foot to foot. Over a span of time no one can measure, their feet have worn the tops of the tors into the texture of feathers. There are faint ridged shallows in the stone, fanned like a wing, so that where a bird sits and where a bird once sat have become hard to tell apart. Stroke the rock where no bird is perched and it feels faintly downy.

The hanging birds are different, and harder to explain.

They hold themselves in the air at intervals across the whole sky, wings spread, not beating, not drifting. If you took a long time and a good eye you could find that the intervals are exact. They make a lattice over the plateau, like the pegs of an enormous tent. Their shadows fall east with everything else's, long and clean, and where a shadow lies on the stone the cold is very slightly deeper and softer. It is the only shade on the plateau. The day never ends, and in the ring of upright slabs you will find bedding worn into the dust along the line where one bird's shadow falls. Pilgrims slept in the birds' shade because it was the nearest thing to night.

Some of the cairn-builders seem to have believed the hanging birds hold the afternoon in place, that they are what stops the sun. There is a drawing on one tor of a bird with its wings spread and a line from each wingtip running down to the ground, as if tethered, or tethering. A circle sits above it, which might be the sun, might be an eye, or might be nothing. Nobody has seen a hanging bird move. Nobody has seen one land. If they are pins, what they pin is not the sky. It is the hour.

Below everything, in the dust, live the springtails and mites. Scoop a handful from a hollow and hold it still. After a while the dust in your palm begins, very slowly, to breathe: a faint swelling and settling, once a minute, as the millions of tiny bodies in it go through their own looping motions in unison. It is not hard to believe that the smoothness of the dust, the way your footprints never last, has something to do with them. They are not moving the dust back into place, because nothing moves it. They remember it smooth. Each of them has a place, and each is in its place, and a footprint is only a brief disagreement with that.

## III. The breath

Stay long enough and the direction of cause starts to turn around.

At first you assume the breath comes from the animals: thousands of lungs in unison, a shared rhythm that grew up among creatures living too long together. But the breath is too even and too far below the level of any single body. It is in the stone when you lie on the swells. It is in the dust in your hand. It is in the cairn whose top stone rises and falls. It is in you, soon enough, whether or not you choose it.

The animals do not make the breath. The breath makes the animals. Each grazer, each hare, each mite is a place where the breath has pooled and taken a shape, the way still water in a basin takes the shape of the basin.

What is the breath, then? The people who scratched faces into the rocks may have known. There is one carving, larger than the rest, cut low on the face of the biggest tor in the western troughs. It shows two things pressing against each other. On one side is something like a hand pushing upward. On the other is something like a hand smoothing downward. Between them is a single wavy line, drawn with more care than anything else on the plateau.

You could read it this way. A mountain range is a quarrel. The deep earth pushes the land up, and the weather wears it down. For almost all of a mountain's life, one side of that quarrel is winning. The peaks are rising faster than they're wearing, or wearing faster than they rise. But somewhere, maybe only once, maybe only here, the two came into perfect balance. For a moment the push and the smoothing were exactly equal. Then both sides finished. The deep earth went quiet, the wind stopped, and the rock had nothing left to give up. But the rhythm of the balance, the long back-and-forth of rising and wearing that had gone on for longer than anything, did not stop. It had nothing left to act on, so it simply kept going, gently, on its own. It is a pulse left over from a quarrel both sides have forgotten.

That is what lives on the plateau: not the herds but the balance, or the memory of the balance, breathing. The herds are what it looks like when it rests on stone. The lichen rings are what it looks like when it rests on time. The breath in your chest is what it looks like when it rests on you.

It doesn't want anything. That is what makes it hard to call it a creature. It is not waiting, or keeping, or guarding. It does not hold the plateau the way a miser holds gold. It holds it the way a sleeping person holds their own hands folded, without effort, because this is where the hands are.

## IV. The ones who came and stayed

The pilgrims are harder to write about, because they had faces, and some of them still almost do.

Consider the counter, the one who cut the longest row of tallies. It runs across the smooth face of a low boulder near the eastern cairns, stroke after stroke, hundreds of them, each cut firmly and deeply at first. Then the strokes get shallower. They get farther apart. The last one is half-finished. The groove starts deep and fades out midway down the line, as if the hand had simply forgotten why it was moving. Beside that boulder is a hare that is a little too large, larger than any other hare on the plateau. It takes three hops forward and three back, and at the forward end of its loop it pauses beside the half-finished stroke. It does not look at it, having nothing to look with, but it rests there, the way a person pauses in a doorway when they've come into a room and can't remember what for. Then it hops back.

Consider the one who drew faces. The drawings are scattered over a dozen tors: grazers in a few quick lines, horns curved, eyes set wide, a muzzle, a tuft at the chin. They are the only faces on the plateau, and they are affectionate. Whoever drew them liked the animals, or liked who the animals had been, or liked the idea that they could have faces at all. Near each drawing the herds stand a little thicker, and the grazers closest to the rock lower their heads, at the bottom of each loop, toward the carvings instead of the lichen. It may be coincidence. It may be the only kind of gratitude the plateau has left.

Consider the one curled in the windbreak. The weave of cloth in its fleece is plain, over-and-under, the weave of something handmade and warm. It lies on its side with its knees drawn up, its forelegs or arms folded near its face, its back to the wind that never blew. It sleeps. It has been sleeping in the lee of that wall since before the ash went unlit. Its breath is the plateau's breath, but if you kneel beside it for long enough you'll notice something no other animal does. Every so often, perhaps once in a hundred breaths, it makes a very small sound, a hum or a sigh. It's the sound a sleeper makes when turning over in a dream, and it never quite turns over.

Consider the tall one with the staff.

It stands at the edge of a herd in the northern swells. It is taller than the grazers, narrower, and upright, and its fleece falls straight like a cloak. What it holds in its worn right hand is long and smooth and could be a staff, or a crook, or a spear shaft with the point long gone. It raises its head when the herd raises theirs and lowers it when they lower theirs. It is in perfect phase. But at the top of each raise, its smooth, blurred head is turned not toward the herd, and not toward the lichen, but southeast, toward the one gentle slope where the ground begins to fall away. That is the way up. That is the way everyone comes.

It is not watching. It can't watch. But its gesture, the single thing that was most truly its own and has outlasted everything else, is looking toward the place where people arrive.

Perhaps it was a shepherd, and the grazers were its flock, and it came up here following them. Perhaps it was a guide who brought others, and stayed to meet the next ones. Perhaps it was only someone who hoped to be followed. The plateau has worn away the story and kept the posture.

This is what the plateau does to those who stay, and it is not cruel. It takes things away in the order that weather takes things from rock. First go the sharp edges: urgency, grievances, the reasons you came. Then go the projections: plans, hopes, the long thin spires of *later* and *when*. Then go the features: the name, the face, the voice. What's left is the root. The root is a gesture, a pause at the top of a breath, a turn a little too far to the left, a head held toward the way people come. The plateau does not take that. That is what it keeps, forever.

The pilgrims are the only residents who ever had somewhere else to be. Maybe that is why their gestures are the most particular. The grazers' habits are small and inward. The pilgrims' habits point at things: at a half-finished line, at a drawing, at the road.

## V. The afternoon

Go further in, past the herds, past the breath, past the ones who stayed, and you come to the truest answer, which is also the strangest.

What lives on the plateau is an hour.

Look at where the sun stands: a little past midday. Think about what that hour is, wherever people live. It is the hour after the meal. The main work of the day is behind you and evening hasn't started. The light is clear and honest and throws long, clean shadows. It is the hour when an old person sits on a doorstep that generations of feet have worn smooth, with nothing that needs doing, not waiting for anything. It is the hour when a dog lies down in the one patch of shade and sighs. It is the hour after a thing is finished, before the next thing begins.

The plateau is where that hour lives. It went there the way water goes to the lowest place. It went to the highest place instead, because it is the opposite of water. It collects where everything else has run off. Every creature on the plateau is a shape that hour takes when it settles into a body. The grazer's slow crop and return is what *after lunch* looks like as a grazer. The hare's three hops forward and three back are what *nothing to do and nowhere to be* looks like as a hare. The curled sleeper in the windbreak is the hour lying down.

This is why nothing suffers. Nothing drinks, nothing dies, and nothing is born, and none of it is a punishment or a trap. The hour after a thing is finished doesn't need anything. It doesn't hunger, because the meal is over. It doesn't sleep, because it isn't tired, only settled. It doesn't die, because dying is something that happens later, and there is no later here.

It is the dwelling place of *done*. Not dead. Done, the way a pot is done when it comes out of the kiln and has its shape for good, the way a sentence is done when it reaches its full stop and means what it means. A long, long process
