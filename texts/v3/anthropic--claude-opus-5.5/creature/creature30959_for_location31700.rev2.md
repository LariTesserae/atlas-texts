# The One Who Holds On

## What It Leaves Behind

You notice what it has touched long before you see it.

The first sign is the bright rungs at the top of the ladder. Once you know to look, there are others. Every gauge on the wall has a small clear patch in its fog, about a thumb's width, low on the glass. It is the kind of mark you would leave by leaning close and breathing on the glass and wiping it to read the numbers. The patches are always in the same place, and the fog is always creeping back in from the edges.

The valve wheel has a mark too. Most of its rim is furred green, but two places on it are worn smooth, set a little wider apart than a person's shoulders. Something with very large hands has held that wheel. It has held it often, and for a long time.

Then there is the hollow in the moss. A few meters above the gallery, the moss grows a hand's-breadth thick in its rivet-row contours. In one place those contours have been pressed flat into a long shallow trough. It runs straight up the wall, much longer than a person is tall, narrow at the bottom and wider near the top. It looks like the place where a deer beds down in tall grass, except that it is vertical, and the thing that bedded there lay facing the void.

It is warm when you touch it. Everything here is warm. But the moss in the trough is flattened in the direction of the draft, and it has not sprung back.

## What It Looks Like, at First

Most people who have seen it did not know they were seeing it.

They describe a tree fern that seemed taller than the others. Or one of the long vines streaming up into the column, pale against the iron, that seemed to be in a slightly different place when they looked back. Or a swift, very large and very slow, resting against the wall, although swifts never rest.

The best time to see it is on the long evenings, when the frogs light up. Thousands of small orange throats flicker on and off across the wall. Among them, if you are patient, you may see a stretch of dark where no frogs are flashing. It is long and narrow, like a gap in a string of lamps. The frogs go dark near it. Or perhaps they are lighting it: flashing at it from all around its outline, so that it appears as a silhouette drawn in sparks.

Then the silhouette moves against the draft, which nothing else in the Crown does, and you understand that you have been looking at it for some time.

## Its Body

It is long, eight or nine meters, and almost all of that length is thinness.

Its frame is brass tubing, some pieces no thicker than bamboo, jointed and articulated like the legs of a crane fly. Over the frame is stretched something that was once a membrane, or oiled canvas, or a sail. Now the garden has had it for centuries. Lichen patches it pale. Moss grows along its long bones in contour lines, the same way moss grows along the rivet rows. There are orchids in its elbows. A fig has rooted in one of its shoulder joints and braided a thin gray root down the length of the limb, and the root and the brass have grown so close together that it is no longer clear which is holding which.

It weighs almost nothing. That is the first real thing to understand about it.

It lives the way the vines live. Its body streams upward in the draft, head toward the sky, trailing like a kite from its own hands. Those hands are the only heavy part of it. They are dense, dark iron, too large for the thin arms that carry them, and they are shaped less like hands than like grapnels: three long curved fingers and a thumb, made for gripping plate seams and rivet heads and rungs.

It moves across the wall hand over hand. It never walks, because it has nothing to walk with. The legs, or what might once have been legs, are just more streaming length, trailing up and fanned open in the breath. When it wants to go higher, it opens its hands and lets the column take it.

This is why only the top rungs of the ladder are polished. It does not climb the ladder. It lets go at the gallery and falls up. It rides the warm column the way the swifts do, rising through the void in a long slow drift. It catches the top rungs just before the rim and hauls itself over the edge. The moss on the lower rungs has never been disturbed because nothing ever needed them.

One of the rules of the place is that everything rises unless something holds it. This creature is the thing that holds. Its whole existence is a grip.

## Its Face

At the upper end of it, where a head would be, there is a disc.

The disc is glass over a white dial, about the size of a dinner plate, with fine black numbers around its rim and a single black needle. The glass is fogged from the inside in the same way the wall gauges are fogged. There is no clear patch on this one, because nobody wipes it.

Once you have seen its face, you understand the maker's mark stamped into the maintenance plates, the one worn nearly smooth: the circle with a line through it. It is neither a stack nor an eye. It is a portrait, a picture of this.

The needle moves.

It does not move in response to you. You can stand in front of it for an hour and the needle will not care that you are there. But over that hour it will drift. It drifts slowly, the way the wall gauges drift, pointing at numbers no one reads. Sometimes it drifts in step with the gauge nearest it. Sometimes it moves against it.

It is tempting to call this face an eye, and it does seem to use it as one, pressing the dial close to things. But the dial is not how it sees. It feels its way by the note, like the moths. The dial is how it knows. Whatever it measures, the measurement is written on its face, legible to anyone who could read those numbers. Nobody can.

## Its Voice

It is hollow.

Its brass bones are pipes, open at both ends and pierced here and there along their length with small round holes, like the holes of a flute. The breath of the Crown passes through it constantly. That means it is always sounding. Inside the great roar that fills the room like water, it adds its own small note. That note is thin and high and constantly changing as it bends its limbs and closes and opens its joints.

You cannot hear it. Nothing can hear anything in the Crown.

But the moths can feel it. They read the note in their bodies and find the walls by its variations, and this creature is one of those variations: a moving thread of different vibration drawn through the great one. The moths gather around it. They gather around the gauges too, and perhaps that is only because it gathers there, and has for so long that the moths have come to believe the gauges are part of it.

On its body, among the lichen, there are always a few pale moths clinging, wings folded, smelling faintly of hot iron.

## Its Work

It keeps the room. That seems to be all it does, and it seems to be everything.

It makes the rounds of the gauges. It moves along the wall between them at the pace of the snails, hand over hand, rivet by rivet. At each gauge it lowers its long body until its dial is pressed almost against the fogged glass. It breathes on the glass through a vent below its face and wipes the fog clear with the back of one iron finger. Then it holds still. Its own needle trembles, as if in agreement or argument, and then it moves on.

It tends the wheel. The valve wheel beside the dead hatch does not turn on its own, a fraction of a degree a year. This creature turns it. Every so often it takes the rim in both hands, at the two worn places, and it pulls. It does not pull hard. It pulls the way you would tighten a string on an instrument you were afraid of breaking. The wheel moves perhaps the width of a hair, and the ferns around it lean aside a little further.

It does this slowly because anything fast would be heard. Here, anything fast would be a sudden change in the note, and everything alive in the Crown depends on the note staying steady. The moths steer by it. The orchids grow by it. A sharp turn of that wheel would be, in this room, like a light going out.

It also prunes, though less than it used to. The garden grows inward. Ferns reach out over the void and fig roots thread across the gaps between plates. Once, it cut them back. You can see the old cuts on the oldest wood, clean and deliberate, a line of stubs where the green was made to stop. The newest growth has no cuts. It is reaching out over the column unchecked, a little further every year.

## At the Rim

Sometimes, perhaps every few weeks and perhaps every few years, it lets go.

It falls up through the whole height of the column: past the ferns, past the white orchid cascades, past the swifts spiraling in their sleep. It catches the polished rungs and pulls itself out into the cold, the thin air, and the sideways wind.

Out there, and only out there, it stands.

Above the rim there is no updraft to carry it, only wind and ordinary weight. For the first time, its long body settles downward. Its limbs fold into something like legs. It crouches on the frost at the edge of the rim, above the painted-stripe line where the orchids end, and it faces the cloud sea.

The lichen on its body whitens in the cold. Its dial fogs over completely.

Then its needle begins to swing. It points at one distant stack, holds, and moves to the next. It visits each of them in turn: the dozen black chimneys of the drowned city, each with its green crown and its faint plume, each sounding its own far note. It is listening to the chord. Or it is feeling the chord, the way the moths feel the note, through the metal of the rim and the bones of its hands.

After a long time, sometimes a whole night, it climbs back over the rim into the warmth. It lets the column take its weight again and goes back down the wall to the wheel.

There are others. That much seems certain. People who have lingered on the rims of other stacks report long pale shapes crouched at the edge of those crowns too, needles turning. They report bright rungs and clear patches on fogged glass. No one has seen two of them at once. No one knows whether they have ever met.

Maybe they have never needed to. They have the chord.

## What the Chord Is For

The note of the Crown sinks over centuries, and the orchids record it. Cut through an old stem and you will find its growth rings banded where the vibration changed. Scholars who have traveled between the stacks and cut the old orchids of several crowns have tried laying the bands side by side. It is hard to be certain of anything in work like that. But some of them believe the bands answer each other. They believe that when one stack's note has dropped, another's has shifted soon afterward, across hundreds of kilometers of cloud, as if in reply.

If that is true, the chord is not an accident. It is being made. It is being tuned, a hair's width on a valve wheel at a time, by long hollow creatures that cannot hear it. They have been doing it since before anyone thought to cut an orchid open.

The chord does not resolve, not yet. But the scholars who believe the bands answer each other also believe the chord is moving. They think it is converging, very slowly, on something. By their estimates it is still thousands of years away.

No one knows what happens when it arrives. Perhaps it is a message and someone is meant to receive it. Perhaps it is a lullaby for whatever burns below. Perhaps it is a lock, and the chord is the key. Or perhaps it is only a job, begun by the makers and carried on after them the way everything here has been carried on, because the purpose continued even when no one was left to hold it.

## The Hush

Every seven years the furnace breathes out, the draft slackens, and the Crown goes almost quiet.

For the creature, this is the hardest season. Its body, which has been held up its whole life by the column, no longer has anything holding it up. It sags. Its long limbs hang down for the first time in seven years and swing heavily from its iron hands. It returns to the trough in the moss above the gallery and lies there against the wall, belly to the iron, dial facing the empty void. That is why the moss there is pressed flat. That is its bed, and this is its sleep.

But it does not only sleep. Two other things happen in the hush.

The first is that it can be heard. With the roar softened, the small breaths moving through its hollow bones become audible for a few weeks. It is very faint, like wind across the mouth of a bottle. Travelers who happened to be in the Crown during a hush report that at some point it sounds a single deliberate tone, or a short phrase of tones, through all its pierced limbs at once. The frogs are calling aloud in the stillness and the rain is ticking on the plates, and this sound rises through them with its own quality. It is soft and very clear, and unmistakably directed.

The travelers do not agree on what they heard. Some say it was a single held note. Some say it was a question. One said it was a name, though she could not say whose. Everyone agrees it happened only once, and then it did not happen again.

The second thing is that it goes down.

This is the only time it can. For the rest of its life the updraft pushes it upward; descending past the gallery would mean fighting the whole column hand over hand. In the hush it lowers itself over the rail and climbs down the wall into the dark. It goes past where the ferns thin out and the mosses end, past where the rust is bare, and down into the warm dimness that does not end.

No one has followed it. No one knows how far it goes.

When the heat returns and the roar swells and everything turns upward again, it comes back up, carried by the renewed breath. It rises out of the depths with its limbs streaming, like something surfacing. Its iron hands are warm, warmer than the rest of it, and blackened with soot that smells faintly of bread.

The needle on its face has moved. It has always moved, by a large margin, further than it moves in all seven years at the gauges. It has read something down there. It has been to the one gauge that matters and laid its face against whatever burns.

## What It Is, Now

It was made. Everything here was made. It is a maintenance thing, built by the same hands that stamped the plates and set the gauges and mounted the wheel, and it was built for a purpose that has outlasted the hands.

But the garden has had it for a long time. The fig in its shoulder does not know it is anything other than wall. The orchids in its elbows bloom on its schedule, not their own. Snails ride on its iron hands for years at a time, grazing the rust, and it does not shake them off. Spider threads snag on its limbs as it rises through the column, and it wears them, dozens of long silver lines streaming up from its body into the draft. It is strung with them like an instrument. Swifts shelter in its lee. Some have built their silk cups in the crooks of its joints.

It is no longer exactly a machine that has been overgrown. It is closer to one of the garden's belts: a narrow walking band where brass and root and moth and moss have found a way to live together, and which happens also to keep the gauges clear.

## What It Is, Underneath

Once you have learned its shape, you start seeing it everywhere.

Look at the tree ferns near the gallery. They grow straight out of the wall at right angles and then curve upward with their fronds spread open in the draft. Some of them are ferns. Some have a thin line of brass running up the inside of the trunk. Some have a disc of fogged glass buried in the moss at the top, under the spreading fronds, and a needle behind the glass.

Look at the long upward vines near the rim, the forty-meter vines that have never once hung down. Most of them are vines. Some of them end in iron hands, still closed around a rivet head, the fingers furred over entirely with lichen.

There were many of them once. None were ever made anew; all of them were made long ago. One by one, over centuries, they stopped moving. And when they stopped, they did not let go. They held the wall where they were, facing the void, and the garden took them. Every one of them died reaching slightly inward, toward the center of the column, as if leaning toward something.

The one that still moves visits them. In the gaps between its rounds it goes to each one, in no order anyone can find. It lowers its dial to theirs, breathes on the glass, and wipes it clear.

The needles behind those old faces are still drifting. They are slower, much slower, than the needle of the one who wipes them. But they have not stopped.

This is where the scholars' second theory comes from. It is less popular than the first, because it is sadder. The theory is that the note sinks over the centuries because the pipe is changing. Every time one of them goes still and joins the wall, its hollow body becomes part of the stack, and the stack's voice shifts a little lower. On this reading, the orchid bands are not a record of the chord being tuned. They are a count.

Maybe both theories are true. Maybe the tuning is done this way too.

## What Will Happen

One day, perhaps, the ring of green will close over the void, and the column will breathe up through leaves.

If you have spent long enough on the gallery, you know what that ring will be made of. Some of it will be fern and fig. Much of it will be hands and hollow brass, the long still bodies of the keepers, reaching inward a little further every year until their fingertips meet across the middle of the air. The breath of the furnace will rise through them. It will pass through all their pierced bones at once, and the Crown will sound a note it has never sounded before.

The one that still moves has stopped pruning. The newest growth is not cut back. Maybe it is tired. Maybe it has decided something.

For now it keeps its rounds. It wipes the glass. It turns the wheel by the width of a hair. It lets go at the gallery and falls up into the sky, catches the bright top rungs, crouches in the frost, and listens with its hands to the other stacks answering across the cloud sea. Every seven years it goes down into the dark to read the fire, comes back warm, and does not say what it found.

Somewhere below, something is still burning. It has never asked for anything and it has never stopped. Up here, at the top of the column, something has been holding on for as long as the fire has been burning. It has never let go, and when it finally stops, it still will not.
