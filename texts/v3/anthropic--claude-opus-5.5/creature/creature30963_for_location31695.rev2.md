# Who Lives on the Saddle

## The count

There are thirty-one of them, most years. Some years there are twenty-nine, and once in the memory of the oldest woman there were thirty-six, which is remembered as a strained and anxious time, the way other places remember a famine. Nobody keeps the count on purpose. They simply know it, the way you know how many steps lead to your own door.

They are not a tall people. The air up here is thin and makes its demands, and over many generations the bodies that did best were compact and deep-chested, with a slow, steady heartbeat that doesn't hurry for anything. Their hands are broad. The pads of the fingers are flattened and hard from a lifetime of lifting plates and feeling along edges for the line where the stone wants to split. Their skin is brown and weathered by high sun, and fine lines fan from the corners of their eyes, because they squint against the glare off the fog-banks below. Their eyes are dark, and when they look at a wall they look at it the way a scholar looks at a page.

They dress in the dense grey-brown cloth of goat hair and heather fiber, wrapped rather than cut, pinned with bone. From a little distance, sitting still against the schist, a person here is hard to see. This is not camouflage. It is only that everything here is made of the same few materials, and the people are too.

## The woman in the doorway

Her name is Ashke. She is the woman you passed, splitting reeds with her thumbnail. The reeds come up from a seep far down the lower slope, a half-day's walk, and she will make them into the fine mats that line the cistern-mouths and strain the grit from the water. She nodded to you because a nod is what you give. Speech is given less freely.

The people of the saddle speak quietly, almost always. It isn't shyness. The lanes are narrow and the walls are hard, and a word spoken at ordinary volume travels three turnings and arrives, slightly warped, in someone else's doorway. A raised voice here is a thing everyone hears. Over time the people have grown a manner of speaking that stays close to the body: low, unhurried, full of small pauses. When two of them talk in a lane, their heads lean in, and from a distance it looks like prayer.

For longer distances, they knock.

## The knocking

This took a long time to understand, if you are a visitor, and some visitors never do. The sound of the mallet on the chisel, dressing a plate, travels further and more cleanly than any voice. Every stoneworker's rhythm is a little different: the weight of the strike, the pause between, the way the blows cluster. People know each other by it. Sitting in her doorway, Ashke can tell you that Oru is working on the upper terrace wall this morning, and that he is tired, because his strokes are coming in threes instead of fours.

And the knocking is not only work. There is a set of patterns, older than anyone knows, tapped on the stone itself with a knuckle or a small hard pebble kept in the fist for the purpose. Come. Fog is coming. The goats are out. Someone is ill. Someone is born. Someone has died. The walls carry these messages through the town. A knock on the outer face of a house in the lower town can be felt as the faintest tick in a wall up near the upper gate, if you have your palm against it and know what to feel for. Children learn to rest their hands flat on stone the way children elsewhere learn to cup a hand to an ear.

So the town is quiet, but it is not a silence of no one speaking. It is a silence full of listening.

## The margins

Why do they live so far apart, in a town built for so many more?

They will tell you, if you ask, that it is for the wells. Each household tends a cluster of air wells on the windward face: clearing the channels, restacking a stone the frost has shifted, scraping the lichen from the catchment floors but never, ever the moss inside. A household lives near its wells. The wells are spread out along the windward faces, so the households are too.

That is true. It is not the whole truth. The deeper reason is harder to put into words, and they don't often try. It has to do with sound, and with water, and with the arithmetic of a place that can hold only so much life. A person here seems to need a certain amount of stone around them, the way a juniper needs a certain amount of cracked rock to root in. Crowd them and they grow uneasy, short-tempered, sleepless. The thirty-six-person year was not remembered as a hard year only because the cisterns ran low. It was remembered as a year when people could hear each other breathing through the walls at night, and couldn't bear it.

So they spread out, and the town holds them the way a hand holds a few seeds: loosely, with room between.

## Stone-reading

Every child here learns to read stone before anything else. There is no writing on the saddle, not in the way you would mean it. But there is a great deal of reading.

A plate is read for its grain: where it will split cleanly, where it will crumble, which face will shed water and which will drink it and crack in the first frost. A wall is read for its courses. And this is where the reading becomes something more, because every builder lays stone a little differently. One sets plates with a slight upward cant. One favors long thin stones and packs the gaps with slivers. One always turns the rusty lichen-edge outward. After a lifetime, a person's hand is as recognizable in a wall as their knock is in a lane.

So when an old man walks a child down a lane, he is not only teaching them how to build. He is teaching them who built. That course is Hesk's, your mother's father's father. Those, with the slivers, are Dannat's. Below that, the soft rounded ones, those are older than anyone's name, but someone laid them, and look how well: not one has slipped.

The walls are the genealogy. The town is the book of everyone who has lived in it, and the lower down you read, the older the hands.

## The rooms that are shut

Now the closed rooms.

When someone dies on the saddle, there is no burying them. There is no soil to spare; the little there is was carried up in baskets, and it is for the bitter greens and the hardy grain. And the stone is too hard and too precious to cut a grave in.

So the dead are carried up. On the morning after a death, when the fog has lifted, a few of the living carry the body, wrapped in its own cloth, up the path beyond the upper gate to a flat shelf of rock just below the higher summit. They lay it there, open to the sky, and they come back down without looking behind them.

The choughs come.

This is why the choughs are treated as they are. This is why no one throws a stone at them, why the red-billed birds that land on the rooftops are left in peace to pick at lichen and crumbs, why there is always a little spilled grain on certain terraces. The dead go into the choughs. The choughs ride the updraft over the saddle, round and round in the clear air, and call their sharp calls, and then drop down to walk on the roofs of the houses where the dead once lived. When a chough lands on your roof, you do not say "a bird is here." You go still for a moment, and then you go on with your work, a little more carefully.

Meanwhile, down in the town, the dead person's doorway is closed. The family stacks it shut with loose plates, the way it has always been done: no mortar, nothing binding. It can be undone in an afternoon. The room behind it is not a tomb, because there is no one in it. It is a room at rest. It will be opened again.

## Opening

When a child is born here, the people do not say "a child is born." They say "a house is opened."

It is not literally the child's house, at first. An infant lives with its mother. But at the birth, somewhere in the town, a closed doorway is unstacked. The room behind it is aired and swept. Its stones are looked over, one by one, to see what the frost has done. Perhaps the room is used for storage for a while, heather bundles and fuel. But it is understood that this room has been opened because of the child. When the child is grown, it will live there, or its stones will go into the house the child builds.

A young person's first real work, the thing that marks them as grown, is to take down a closed house, a house of the long-dead, and build its stones into a wall of their own. They lay one full course, by themselves, from the old stones. Everyone comes, at some point in the day, to look at it and say nothing, which is praise. Now that person's hand is in the book, over the hands of whoever came before. And the stones they used carry the older hands inside them: the lichen, the wear, the angle of an old chisel mark turned inward and hidden.

So the arithmetic holds. A house closed, a house opened. A course taken down, a course laid. The total amount of wall stays the same. The number of people stays roughly the same. It is not a rule anyone enforces. There are no laws on the saddle in that sense. It is more that everyone can feel the balance, the way a stonemason feels whether a wall is plumb by laying a palm against it and waiting.

## The others

The people are not the only ones who live here, and they would be the first to say so.

**The goats** are wealth, yes, but they are also a kind of household member, each with a name, each with a known temper. The formal respect you noticed is real. A goat is spoken to politely. When one dies, it is not eaten. It goes up the same path as the people do, though to a different shelf, and the choughs take it too.

**The cats** belong to no one because they belong to the town. They move freely through the closed and open houses alike. The cats are the only creatures allowed inside a shut room; they slip through the gaps in the loose-stacked doorways, and people say that when a cat is seen coming out of a closed house, that house will be opened soon. This is said lightly, and believed completely.

**The lizard** is thought to be very old. Not one lizard, of course; there have been many. But the people speak of it in the singular, the lizard, as if the same grey creature had been sunning itself on the south walls since the lowest courses were laid. You cannot look at it directly. Everyone knows this. It is considered a lesson: some things are only there if you let them stay at the edge of your eye.

**The moss** in the oldest air wells is the only thick, soft green thing in the whole town. No one touches it. If a person has a fever, or a grief too large to speak, they may go to their household's oldest well and sit beside it with their back against the stones, in the damp and the faint hum of the wind moving through, and smell the green. That is all. They sit there. Afterward, they are a little better.

And **the fog**. The fog doesn't live here, exactly. It visits. But it visits so faithfully, and gives so much, that the people speak of it as the town's oldest and most generous guest. When it pours silently over the windward ridge and swallows the town, people step outside their doors and stand in it for a moment with their faces lifted and their eyes closed. They say, quietly, what might translate as welcome back, or as thank you, or as you again. The fog does not answer. It beads on their eyelashes and on the wool of their cloaks, and it runs down the stone into the channels and the cisterns, and that is its answer.

## The one who is the town

Here is the thing that takes longest to see, and that the people of the saddle perhaps never quite say, because it is too large and too obvious to them, like the mountain itself.

Imagine you could watch the town not for an afternoon but for a thousand years, the whole span pressed down into a single long breath.

You would see the walls moving. Slowly at first, then clearly: courses lifting away here, settling down there, doorways closing and opening and closing, rooms appearing and dissolving like cells. The whole town would seem to ripple, to shift its weight, to breathe. The stones would travel. A single plate might go from a lower-town wall to a well-head to a lintel in the upper town to a stair to the landing that now leads nowhere, and back down again, over twenty generations, turning a new face outward each time.

And moving among the stones, you would see tiny warm points of light: the people. Thirty-some of them, always about thirty-some. They would flicker out and new ones would kindle, but the number would hold, and the lights would keep moving through the lanes and between the houses, carrying stones, carrying water, knocking on walls.

From that distance, it would be very hard to say that the people live in the town. It would look much more as though the town were alive, a single slow creature of stone fitted into the notch of the mountain, and the people were the way it moves. Its hands. Its blood, carrying water from the wells to the cisterns to the mouths and back out through the channels. Its memory, reading its own walls. Its breath, the fog drawn in and let out. The choughs are the part of it that flies. The lizard is the part of it that watches from the edge of the eye. The cats are how it passes between its open and its sleeping rooms.

At night, when a stone somewhere settles a hair's breadth deeper into its bed, the people who are awake hear it, and do not startle. They say, if they say anything, that the town is turning over in its sleep.

## Morning

But up close, none of that is visible. Up close there is only the lane, and the cool shadow, and the morning sun just now reaching the upper edge of the south wall.

And at the turning, Ashke in her doorway, splitting reeds with her thumbnail. Somewhere above, Oru's mallet goes tap-tap-tap, three strokes, a pause. He is still tired. On the roof across the lane a chough lands and walks a few stiff steps and tilts its red bill at her, and she goes still for a moment, and then nods to it, the same nod she gave you, and goes back to her work.

The fog will come in an hour. She can feel it in the stone.
