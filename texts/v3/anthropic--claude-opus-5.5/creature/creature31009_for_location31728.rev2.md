# Who Lives at Hollow Coin

## I. The first answer

The easy answer is the Tuners, so start with them.

There is one sitting on the lip of a bridge-song at the fourth tower from the centre. Her token rings a little flat of the Stump's ninth bell, so she is called by that interval, which in their small speech is a hum with a catch at its end, like a word someone changed their mind about halfway through. Call her Catch.

She is thin, as all of them are. The plateau gives little and asks for a great deal of stillness. Her feet are bare. Tuners go barefoot from infancy, and by adulthood their soles are thick, pale, and ridged like the floor. They are also exquisitely sensitive. A Tuner standing on the plain can tell you which bell is swelling, how far off the nearest rasp herd is, and whether the grain beneath her has the faint sourness that means a sawyer passed through within the day. She does not hear these things with her ears. The ears are nearly useless out on the open rings, where the drone fills them the way water once filled drowned men in the records. She listens with her heels and her shins and the long hollow of her spine.

When she speaks to another Tuner she leans in and hums against his collarbone, and he feels the words more than he hears them. Their language has almost no consonants, because consonants do not survive the drone. It is all contour: rising, falling, held, broken off. Tenderness is a note held slightly longer than it needs to be. Grief is a note that starts on pitch and is allowed to drift, deliberately, until it beats sourly against the bell beneath it. Everyone who feels that beating in their bones knows what it means.

Catch is watching the bridge. It runs from her tower's throat to the next, eleven hundred metres of sustained tone, visible only as a ribbon of cleared air where the pollen-dust will not settle. Tonight the chord is wavering. She can feel it in the rail of pressure under her palm, a faint tremor at the fourth node, like a pulse that has started to skip. The Ringing is not due for another thirty years, but the pitch has been sliding early this century. Nobody knows why. She will sit here all night, humming correction into the tower's throat through a listening-spike pressed to the wood, holding the bridge up with her voice.

That is a Tuner's life. It is maintenance: keeping open what the Stump would let close, and holding up what the Stump would let fall. Their histories are measured in keys, and they are also measured in losses, in towers that went silent while someone slept.

That is the first answer. It is true, but it isn't the whole of it.

## II. The body the place makes

Look longer at Catch and you begin to see how thoroughly she has been shaped.

Her chest is deep and her ribs are long and fine. Over generations the Tuners have come to resemble knellbirds, broad-chested, with bodies built as resonators, because the ones who could hold a note longest and steadiest were the ones who kept the bridges up, and the ones who kept the bridges up were the ones who lived. Her voice sits low, in a range that couples well to wood. Her upper hearing is already fading, and she is not old. Almost no Tuner over forty can hear a chaff swift's wing-crack. They lose the sky first. By the end of their lives they hear only the drone, and they say it is like coming home.

Her heart has learned the bells. Tuner hearts do not beat regularly the way the old records describe hearts beating. They drift. Across the day they slow as the pitch drops and quicken as it climbs, entrained to the Stump the way the rasps are entrained. A Tuner taken off the plateau, if such a thing were possible, might simply stop, a clock whose pendulum has been lifted off its pin.

Her skin carries a faint violet sheen at the wrists and throat. Tuners do not like to talk about this. Grainmoss grows where the Stump is cracked, and a Tuner's skin is a surface under constant pressure, and dust gets in. It is not an infection, exactly. The threads are very fine and very shallow, and they hum when brushed, as the moss does. Among the Tuners, when you love someone, you touch them there.

So perhaps it is not quite right to say the Tuners live on the Stump. The rasps are the Stump's grazers and the knellbirds are its tuning-forks. The Tuners might be the Stump's attention, a way for it to notice its own bridges and doors, a small warm part of it that has learned to walk around.

Catch would not accept this. She believes she is a person, descended from people, searching for the people who came before. Watch where she goes when she is not holding up a bridge.

## III. What the Tuners are looking for

The Tuners search the rings for the builders.

They have been searching for sixteen keys, which is to say something over sixteen hundred years. The method is patient and nearly unbearable. A listener goes down into the heartwood through doors of pitch, sometimes kilometres down, through galleries that exist only while the right note is being sung. She drives a listening-spike into a ring and plays it back. What comes through the spike is the sound that was playing when that ring was laid: the drone of a century gone, the chords of bells that have since collapsed, the cracks of a night ten thousand years ago that went on falling for ten kilometres. It is weather. It is the Stump's diary, and it is almost entirely noise.

The Tuners are sure that somewhere in it is a voice. The builders sang the towers up. They understood the grain better than anyone living does. It is inconceivable to the Tuners that people who could do that would simply stop, would let the plain go quiet without leaving anything behind. So the builders must have written themselves in. There must be a ring that is a library, a ring that is a chorus, a ring that is a whole people encoded in grain, waiting.

In sixteen keys, no one has found it.

Something else has been found, and the listeners keep it to themselves, because it does not fit and they do not know what it means. Directly beneath the towers, in a narrow column under each null room, the rings are different. The drone is absent from them. They are the only places in the whole deep archive where the Stump laid down wood in silence.

In that silence there are small sounds.

## IV. The null rooms

Every tower has one. They are the only silent places on the Stump. Tuners are born in them, marry in them, and die in them.

Think about what that means from inside a Tuner's life.

A Tuner child is born into silence, and the first thing it ever hears is its mother's voice, unmasked, with no drone beneath it. It is the only time in the child's life it will hear a voice that way. Every voice afterward will be laid over the Stump's, entangled with it, felt through bone. That first one is clean. Tuners say they remember it, and perhaps they do, somewhere below words. The newborn's first cry is recorded too. It goes into the floor and into the ring being laid there, a single clear shape in a band of silence.

Tuners marry in the nulls because it is the only place two people can hear each other's actual speaking voices. The ceremony is almost nothing. Each says the other's interval-name aloud, unhummed, into the stillness. For many of them it is the only time they will ever hear their own name as a sound in air rather than a pressure in their chest. The floor records it.

And Tuners die in the nulls. The dying are carried in and laid on the floor, and their families sit with them in the silence, which is the only mercy the plateau has. They do not take the bodies out. There is nowhere to take them. Out on the plain, the sawyers would have them within a night. But a sawyer moves by singing the wood loose, and in the null there is no frequency to sing. The wood will not flow. So the body stays, and the Stump does what it does everywhere, slowly: it creeps. Over years the floor rises around the body a micron at a time, molecules shouldering through the lattice, and takes it in. There is no seam. Eventually there is only a faint violet trace in the grain, the shape of someone lying down.

The last breath is recorded as well: the last breath, the last word, and the family's voices speaking the dead one's name aloud.

So this is what the listeners have found beneath the towers: columns of silence, kilometres deep, going back further than any Tuner history. They are threaded with small clear sounds. There are first cries, names spoken twice, last breaths. Most of the voices are in no language anyone living knows, and older ones sit beneath them, older and older, until the listening-spikes can't go any deeper.

The Tuners have been looking for a library the builders left, with its catalogue and its index. What they have found is that the builders never left at all. They went into the nulls to be born and to marry and to die, one by one, for a million years. They did not write themselves into the rings on purpose. They simply lived there, and the Stump held on to them.

Catch, who will die in the null of the fourth tower, is writing herself in too. So is everyone she has ever loved. There was never a moment when the builders stopped and the Tuners began. The Tuners are the builders, the last and thinnest part of a very long sentence, still being spoken.

So a second answer to what lives here is that the dead live here, in the columns under the towers, in the silence. They are not ghosts. They are a record, and the Stump plays nothing back unless it is asked. They are kept, which may be the next thing after living.

This is still not the whole of it, because the Stump itself is alive.

## V. The one who lives there

Consider it plainly.

It was a tree. It was a tree so large that its stump is a sixty-kilometre plain three kilometres high. Whatever stood above it once must have gone up out of the atmosphere entirely, a trunk through the veil and on into the dark, crowned with who knows what. Then it fell, or was felled. Nobody knows which, and the Burn may be the answer.

A thing that size, cut off at the base, did not die. It went on. It learned to live without water and without a crown. It moved its substance by sound because it had no other way left. It grew shoots, one every few kilometres, century after century, slender and doomed, reaching up into the bronze air, and every one of them was taken down by wind and cold and grazing mouths, and every one of them was absorbed and remembered and followed by another.

The inhabitants say the shoots are how the Stump listens to the sky. That's true, but think about what else a stump does when it sends up shoots. It is trying to grow back. It is trying to be a tree again, over and over, in a place that will not allow it. Every pale ghost-stripe across the plain is an attempt, and the plain is streaked with them in every direction.

Its breathing is a drone with a period of several seconds, swelling and receding. Its bells are chambers kilometres long, and it pulses them, and the pulse is its metabolism. It cannot stop singing, because stopping would mean starving. Every moment of its life is spent making sound so that it can go on being. It has done this for longer than anything else on this world has done anything.

It has also, maybe, learned to want other things. It grows knellbirds to keep itself in tune, or it grows the things that keep the knellbirds alive. It is hard to tell which, and there may be no difference. It has sawyers moving through it that scour old grain and make room for new. It has rasps that comb its surface. It has grainmoss that is its own skin going outward, fine as nerves, humming when touched.

And it has the Tuners, small warm things that walk on it and hum into its wood and hold its bridges up with their voices. When they die, they lie down in the only places it has left silent, and it takes them in.

Why would it leave those places silent? A thing whose life is sound, which must sing every moment, gave thirty rooms to the absence of sound. It could be an accident of the builders' engineering, chords cancelling at the centre of every tower by design. Perhaps. But the Stump is the medium those chords are made of. Nothing holds in the wood unless the wood agrees to hold it.

Here is what might be true. A thing that listens that hard, that has heard nothing but itself and the wind and the crack of its own contracting skin for a million years, would badly want to hear something that isn't itself. It would clear a space in its own voice and wait there. And into that space a people came, with their first cries and their names and their last breaths, and it kept every one.

The null rooms are not where the Stump can't hear. They are where it listens hardest.

So the third answer is the Stump. What lives at Hollow Coin is the Stump, a vast, patient, maimed, persistent listener, singing itself into existence one micron at a time, and holding very still in thirty small places so it can hear the voices of the ones who live on it.

## VI. The one it is listening for

Go back to the edge.

The drone thins there. You can hear the wind, and under the wind, very faint, there is another drone across the basin, lower and slower, in no key the Stump knows.

The oldest records say the Stump was one of many, a forest. The Burn is near the centre, which means it is old, laid down long ago, early in the Stump's life as a stump. Something burned. Something roared so terribly that no one who has listened to the whole ring will speak of it. After that, the rings go on, broad and narrow, plenty and starvation, and they do not stop.

The shoots are antennae. They catch the wind and the faint light and they vibrate, and the vibration is fed down into the plateau. When wind comes up from the basin at evening, the shoots all sing at once, a high glassy wail that is the loudest sound on the plateau. It can be heard from the dust plains three kilometres below.

It can be heard from the plains, and so it may carry further.

Every hundred years the Stump lays down a new ring and changes key, slowly, over weeks. The old standing waves collapse. Bridges fall and doors seal. The Tuners lose towers every time. They have always thought of the Ringing as weather, something that simply happens to them. But a thing that changes key on purpose, at great cost to everything living on it, might be doing it to reach a note. It might be moving toward a key it hasn't found yet, or one it has heard faintly, from far off, across the basin.

This century the pitch has been sliding early. Catch feels it through the fourth node of the bridge she is holding up. She hums correction into the tower's throat all night, keeping the chord where it was, keeping the bridge from falling. She doesn't know that the whole plateau under her is leaning, very slightly, very slowly, toward the edge, toward that other drone. Across the basin, the other voice has not changed its key at all, and it is lower, and slower, and waiting.

## VII. The last answer

So who lives at Hollow Coin?

Rasps live there, grinding in herds that follow the swelling bells. Chaff swifts live there, smoke-flocks cracking like sails. Knellbirds sing into the shoots, and sawyers swim through the grain and leave ember-lines behind them.

The Tuners live there, a few thousand thin, deep-chested people with violet at their wrists. They hum love into each other's collarbones and hold up bridges made of a single note. Once in their lives they hear their own name spoken aloud.

The dead live there, a million years of them, kept in silent columns beneath the towers, first cries and last breaths threaded through the heartwood like a second grain.

The Stump lives there. It is what remains of something too large to imagine, cut down and burned, which never agreed to die. It keeps singing because singing is how it eats, and it keeps sending up shoots because some part of it still remembers being a tree. It keeps thirty rooms silent so that it can hear the only voices that aren't its own.

Beyond all of these there is a question that has not been answered yet, and it lives there too. It is carried on the evening wail of the shoots, down through the bronze veil and out across three kilometres of dust and whatever lies past that, toward a lower, slower voice that may be another survivor or may only be an echo. Every century the plateau pays in towers and bridges and doors to shift its key a little closer.

Something lives on the Stump that is not the Stump. It is a hope, in the only form a thing made of sound could keep one: a note held a little longer than it needs to be. In the Tuners' language, that is tenderness.
