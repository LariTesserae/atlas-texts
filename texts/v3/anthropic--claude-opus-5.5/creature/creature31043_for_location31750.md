# Who Lives in the Anvil Highlands

## The First Answer: The Bead-Gatherers

Before anything else, you hear them.

It is a small sound, easily lost under the hum: a soft, irregular clicking, like pebbles shifting in a pouch. It follows the smelter herds at a respectful distance of a few hundred meters. It pauses, then comes on again. If you stand still and let the herd pass you in the dark, with its heat and its groaning and its smell of scorched stone, the clicking will pass you too, and very close. You may feel something brush the edge of your field, the fine hairs on your forearm leaning toward it and then settling. You will not see what it was.

These are the scavengers the account mentioned in passing, the ones who gather iron beads "for reasons of their own." That was an understatement. They are people.

You could spend a long time in the highlands without being sure of this. They are not hiding. Hiding is impossible here, and they know it better than anyone. They are simply quiet, in a place where quiet is the ordinary condition of anything that wants to survive. They move at the pace of the herds. They speak at the volume of breath. In the rare moments when you do see them, it happens all at once, in the white instant of an eel discharge. For half a second, a figure is crouched by the slag with one hand flat on the ground and its face turned toward you, already aware of you. Then it is dark, and the afterimage holds for a minute: a narrow body, a cloud of lifted hair, two eyes giving back the flash like coins.

They were aware of you long before that. They felt you coming a kilometer off.

## Their Bodies

They are tall and spare, deep in the chest, built for thin air. Their bodies seem to have been drawn out and lightened by the altitude over generations, the way the grazers have.

Their skin is stained. The warm drizzle carries dissolved iron, and over a lifetime the rust settles into the creases of their hands, the lines of their faces, and the soles of their feet, until the old are mapped in oxblood. They do not wash it off. A person's rust is a record of the rains they have stood in, and the old are proud of theirs.

Their eyes are very large and very dark, with a reflective layer behind them like an animal's. For nearly all of their lives those eyes see almost nothing: dozens of blacks, and the cherry glow of a smelter's belly at a great distance. The eyes are made for the flash. They are built to take in the whole world in half a second and keep it. A highland child's eyes can hold an afterimage for five minutes. An old person's can hold one for ten.

Their hair never lies flat. It lifts in a fine, perpetual halo, drifting toward whatever field they pass through, so a highland person's head tells you which way the current runs. Hunters, meaning the human ones, cut their hair close before going out on the rises. Long hair broadcasts.

Their true senses are in their hands and feet. They go barefoot always, from birth. The pads of their fingers and the soles of their feet are dense with nerve, calloused over but not deadened. Many rub iron dust into the fingertips as a kind of tattoo, which sharpens the sensitivity until they can read the ground's currents the way you might read raised print. A highland person entering unfamiliar country kneels and lays both palms on the rock and stays there, reading, for as long as it takes. It looks like prayer. It is closer to looking.

## Their Speech

Sound carries too far here, so they have made a language out of almost no sound.

Ordinary talk is a breath with shape in it, murmured close. Shouting exists, but it is nearly obscene. A shout on the rises can reach a hunter three crests away, so it is used only to warn. A child who shouts in play is hushed with real fear. Some people go their whole lives without shouting once, and to say of someone that "she never raised her voice" is to say she lived long and carefully.

Their vocabulary for color is small: *red*, for the glow; *white*, for the flash; *violet*, for the startled grazer; and *black*, which they divide into more than thirty kinds. There is the black of a hollow and the black of open ground, the black of mist and the black of a lava tube's mouth, and a particular black that is the shape of something standing between you and a distant smelter.

Their vocabulary for touch and charge is enormous. They have words for the prickle of wire-grass and for the pull of a seam underfoot. They have separate words for the way your hair leans toward a storm before it breaks and for the way it leans toward a person you love. There is a word for the flat, dead feeling of stepping into a lava tube where the hum stops, and they use the same word for loneliness.

They also speak without sound. Two people clasp hands, and one sends small deliberate pulses of charge into the other's palm: a prickle, a pause, a longer prickle. It is slow and intimate. It can only be done skin to skin, and it cannot be overheard. Hunting parties speak this way on the rises, holding hands in a line.

## Their Time

There are no days here, so they do not count days. They count flashes.

Every seep-chain has its own rhythm of discharge. The eels release when their stored charge peaks, and that depends on the local current, the local heat, and the length of the chain. So every community living near a chain has its own clock. Two families a basin apart live in different times. When travelers meet, one of the first courtesies is to compare: *how many flashes to your hand?* A "hand" is a span of time, about as long as it takes to walk a ribbon-road from one end to the other.

Time here is like north. It is a local opinion, and they hold it lightly.

Age is counted in flashes seen. Very small children are carried to the pools so they will not miss any. The first flash a child sees is spoken of all its life: *her first was over the long terraces, with the mist low, and a smelter standing in the water.* Every person carries a store of stills, one image fixed from each flash they have witnessed, and a life is remembered as that sequence of frozen instants separated by long dark. When the old tell stories, they tell them as pictures: *In this one, your grandfather is bending to the pool. In this one, he has turned his head. In this one, there is someone beside him I do not know.*

Some of them etch the stills. The smelters leave sheets and nodules of slag glass, black and smooth, and with a bead of hard iron a patient hand can scratch an image into it by touch alone. The etching is read by fingertip, never by eye. There are tubes where these flashbooks are stacked by the thousand, the remembered instants of people dead so long their rust has gone into the lines.

## Their Danger, and Their Stillness

Everything alive carries a charge. Everything alive can feel the charge of everything else. The hunters see in current. This is the first thing any highland child learns, and the rest of their upbringing is built on it: *you are always visible.*

A frightened body broadcasts. So does an angry one, or an excited one, or a hungry one. The heart's rhythm, the muscles' tension, and the salt on the skin all ripple outward into the ground's fields, and the spade-headed hunter walking the next rise reads it as easily as you would read a lit window.

So they have learned to be calm. It is not the calm of peace. It is closer to a technique, taught from infancy and refined over a lifetime. They call it *going to rock*. You slow the breath, soften the muscles, and let the heart settle toward the ground's own slow pulse, until your field smooths out and blends with the stone around it. A child who can do it for ten breaths is praised. An adult is expected to hold it for a hand. The masters, and there are always a few in every community, can go to rock so completely that a hunter will pass within an arm's length, head low and pits working, and not turn. The masters say you do not hide from the hunter. You become uninteresting to it. You become part of the hum.

It shapes their temperament. They are, almost universally, a still people. They do not fidget. Rage is rare and brief, because rage is deadly. Joy is expressed quietly, in the hands. A visitor may think them cold. Then they touch you, palm to palm, and the charge that comes through tells you everything they have been keeping level, and you understand that it was never coldness. It was discipline.

## Their Honesty

Because every body broadcasts, they cannot easily lie to one another. Stand near someone on open rock and you know, in your skin, whether they are afraid, whether they want you, whether they are grieving. Their courtesy, then, is not a matter of concealing but of *declining to read*. It is considered deeply rude to attend to someone's field without invitation. People who are well brought up keep their palms turned inward in company, a gesture that means *I am not looking*.

Privacy exists in only one place: the lava tubes. The rock there damps the hum and shields the field, and a person inside is truly unseen for the only time in their life. It is the dead feeling they share a word with loneliness, but it is also freedom. Secrets on the plateau are geographic. You go into a tube to think a thought no one else can feel. You go there with someone to say a thing you are not ready for the open ground to know. Courtship happens at tube mouths. Confession happens deep inside them. Some of the old withdraw into the tubes at the end and stay there, and this is understood to be a request.

## Their Love

A pair who have been together a long time grow alike in their charge. Standing side by side through thousands of flashes, going to rock together, speaking palm to palm, their fields gradually fall into rhythm. The old say that a long-married couple broadcast as one body, and that a hunter cannot count them.

There is a phrase for the moment two people first touch with intent, the small visible spark that jumps between fingers in the dry air before the hand closes. It is not considered romantic, exactly. It is simply the fact of it, and they mention it plainly: *We touched, and it jumped.* Children are sometimes named for where it happened.

## The Second Answer: The Dead in the Lines

The beads are for the dead.

When a highland person dies, their body is carried to one of the old ribbon-roads, the fused iron lanes that the smelter herds laid down over ages along the seeps. The body is laid there, bare, on the warm metal. Then the family empties their pouches over it: all the beads they have gathered, a lifetime of clicking in the dark, poured over the chest and arms and face.

Nothing rots quickly here. The thin air, the dark, and the minerals in the damp slow everything. Over a season the body begins to mineralize. The iron in the beads and in the blood, and in the old rust stains of the skin, draws together, and the ground's slow currents begin their work. The outline of the person is traced in rust, then in iron. What they looked like becomes a drawing of what they looked like.

Then the iron begins to move.

Leave a nail on the ground and within a season it will have crept toward the nearest seam. The dead are no different. Over years, a millimeter at a time, the iron that was a person migrates off the ribbon-road and down into the healed fissures, the pale seams that run straight for enormous distances across the plateau. The dead go into the lines. The saying is exact. They join the wiring.

This is why the highland people gather beads. Every bead they carry is iron that will someday lie over a body and help carry it into the ground. A heavy pouch is a kind of wealth and a kind of love. You gather for your elders. Your children gather for you. A person who dies with no one to cover them goes into the lines slowly and thinly, and this is the worst fate they know.

And the dead are still there. Or so the living understand it.

Kneel on a seam, lay your palms flat, and feel. The current is there, broad and slow and constant. But in certain places, on certain seams, it has a texture: a hesitation, a pattern, something the fingertips half recognize.
