# What Lives in the Warm Throat

## At First, Nothing

The first answer is that nothing lives there, apart from everything that obviously does.

You could stand in the room for an afternoon and list them all: the bats, the crickets, the glass shrimp, the eyeless fish, the geckos, the silent frogs. You could take the fig for the landlord, since it is the largest and oldest living thing you can see, and the one that built most of the architecture. That would be a reasonable account. It would even be true, as far as it goes.

But the silence doesn't fit it.

The soft floor, the wet air and the hanging roots account for some of the quiet. They don't account for all of it. Speak a word in that room and pay attention to what happens to it. It doesn't fade the way a sound fades in fog. It shortens. The end of it goes missing. You say *hello* and the room hears *hel*, and the rest is gone, as if something reached out and took the tail of it before it could finish.

That is the first sign. It isn't a creature yet. It's a gap where one would be.

## The Warmth That Isn't Weather

The second sign is the heat, and it takes longer to notice, because you already have an explanation for it: the hot rock below and the water seeping up through the crack.

The explanation is fine. But stay long enough and the heat stops feeling like a condition and starts feeling like a temperature, the way the difference between a warm stone and a warm hand is hard to name and impossible to mistake. The condensation on the walls of the throat is the temperature of skin. The pool is the temperature of a bath, or of the inside of a mouth. Nothing in the room is ever cold, including the corners, the high places among the bats, and the floor under the deepest litter. Heat from a single source should have a gradient, and this has almost none. The whole chamber is held at one steady warmth, the way a body holds itself.

Lay your palm flat on the flowstone rim of the pool and keep it there. After a while, and you have to be patient, you may feel the warmth rise very slightly and fall very slightly, on no schedule you can predict. It isn't a pulse. It is closer to what you feel lying beside someone asleep.

## The Breath

Now look again at the day.

The pool fills by a finger's depth from dawn to dusk and drains back through the night. The warm wet air rises up the throat all day long. In the late afternoon, when that rising breath meets the cool coming down, the room rains. At dawn it sweats.

In any cave you'd call this hydrology, and here too the word covers most of it. But if you have been lying on the soft floor for a day, feeling the warmth and listening to the words you don't say go missing, you'll find that another word has settled over the first one without asking permission. The word is *breathing*. It is one breath, in through the night and out through the day, so slow that a single exhalation lasts from sunrise to sunset. The rain is the moment the breath turns. The dawn sweat is the smaller turn at the other end, the way a sleeper's breath catches briefly before it deepens.

The place is called a throat for a reason, and whoever named it may have known it. The room is not the thing. The room is the mouth of the thing, or the space just behind the mouth. The throat above is its throat. The crack at the bottom of the pool, where the limestone meets the older, hotter rock, leads down toward whatever the rest of it is.

Nobody has gone down there, and the crack is too narrow. You wouldn't want to anyway, any more than you'd want to climb down someone's windpipe to see if they're really alive. It is enough to know that it goes down, and that it is warm all the way.

## What It Eats

It eats sound, though "eats" may be too coarse a word.

It has no teeth and doesn't hunt. It seems to take what is offered, and in that room the offers are small: the patter of the rain, the dry shuffle of beetles in the litter, the tick of a cricket's leg on a root. It takes the edges of these, the ends, the echoes. Nothing in the room ever echoes, because an echo is the part of a sound that comes back, and none of it comes back.

The bats are safe. Their voices are pitched too high, above its hearing or its hunger, so they shout all night in a room that swallows everything else. They may be the only creatures there who have never had to learn to be quiet. Some of the others have learned. The frogs are the clearest case. Frogs call, because calling is what frogs are for, and these don't. You could say they lost the habit in the dark. You could also say that long ago, frogs in that pool called and had their calls taken before the calls could reach another frog, until the ones who were born quiet were the only ones left to breed. Or you could say the frogs gave their voices to it willingly, as a tithe for the warmth, and now they sit in the shallows of its mouth, damp and dun and content, and it keeps them warm and they keep it company.

None of these stories is the answer. The frogs don't call. That's all anyone knows.

## The Tenants

Once you have seen it this way, you can't see the crowd the same way again.

Everything in the room lives inside it the way small lives live inside any large body. The beetles turning bat droppings into soil are its digestion. The moss swelling in the rain is the lining of its mouth. The eyeless fish drifting pink through the pool are something like the creatures that live in a gut, necessary and unnoticed. The fig is not the landlord. The fig is the only other large thing that has ever loved it.

That may sound too sweet, so look at the evidence. The fig does not grow the way figs grow. Its roots don't spread across the ground; they go down, all of them, straight into the throat, as though the tree understood from its first season that the only real water and warmth for miles lay in that direction. It has been reaching down for centuries. Below, the warm breath has been reaching up the whole time, feeding it, keeping its leaves greener than anything on that dry hillside has any right to be. The hanging roots with swollen wet tips that haven't yet touched the floor are the fig still searching. When a root does touch down and thicken and fuse, the thing below has, in the smallest way, been touched. The colonnade on one side of the room is centuries of that.

The glow on the root-tips at night is the part hardest to talk about plainly. It is a film of fungus or slime feeding on what the tips exude, and that is true. But the glow is brightest on the roots that are closest to landing, and it gathers on them the way warmth gathers in a hand just before it closes. The white moths circle it without taking anything from it that anyone can see. Maybe they are just moths, drawn to light. Or maybe they have been living in that breath so long that they circle the places where the tree and the thing below are about to meet, the way small birds gather at a wedding.

## The People

The people who came here knew it was there. You can tell from what they did.

They built a wall around the pool on the side nearest the throat, knee-high, carefully fitted. A wall that low keeps nothing in or out. It marks a threshold. It says *this is the edge of the mouth; beyond here you are inside it*, the way you would build a low fence around a sleeping animal to remind yourself where it starts.

They left pots in the niche by the colonnade. Nobody knows what was in them. They may have held food or seed, or water from somewhere else. My guess is that at least one held nothing but breath: someone who could not say a thing out loud went down the throat, spoke it into a pot, and sealed it, and left it for the room to take slowly, the way the room takes everything. The fig found that pot and filled it with root and cracked it outward without breaking it, which is a very gentle way to open a thing.

And they left their hands.

## The Hands

The hands are on the lit wall, the one the throat-light crosses. The flowstone has been growing over them ever since. Some are clear. Some sit under a frosted skin of mineral. One is nearly gone, with only four fingertips above the flow, as if someone is sinking.

This is where it becomes most itself.

The mineral water is the slowest machine in the room. Grain by grain it turns everything it touches to stone: the wall, the pot, the edge of the pool. You could call that burial. But look at what it has covered first and most carefully. It hasn't coated the colonnade evenly, or the walls in general. It has gone for the hands. It has laid itself over them in thin, clear layers, not scraping them away, not staining them, keeping every line of every finger visible beneath it, as if pressing its own palm flat against each one.

The people pressed their hands to its wall in ochre, and it has been pressing back ever since, at the only speed it has. The smallest hands, the children's, are held most lightly, the mineral over them still thin as breath on glass. The one that seems to be sinking isn't sinking. It is the hand it has held longest. Of all the people who came down the throat, that one, whoever they were, has been held by it for the most years. In a few more centuries the fingertips will go under too, and that person will be wholly kept.

It does not forget, which is the other half of its appetite. It takes the ends of sounds and it never gives them back, and a thing that never gives anything back is either a thief or a keeper. Everything in that room suggests it is a keeper. It has kept the wall, the pots, the hands and the frogs' old voices. It has kept, in some sense, every word anyone ever spoke down there: the tails of them, the parts that would have echoed. They are still in it somewhere, down the crack in the hot rock, held the way the hands are held.

## The Seat

That brings you to the stone by the pool, the flat broad one worn smooth on top in a way stone doesn't wear by itself.

Sit on it. It is warm, warmer than the floor, warmer than the flowstone rim. From there you can see the whole room: the hands, the light moving, the hanging forest, the throat going up into brightness. It is the one place in the room where you can see everything it has kept, all at once.

If you speak from that stone, something different happens. The words still go short and the ends still go missing. But sitting there, you may feel the warmth under you rise very slightly as they go, the way a listener leans in.

It was a place to sit. The people wore it smooth by coming back and sitting there and talking, not often and not for long, but more than once. The stone is its lap, or its ear, or the spot just inside its lips where you can put your mouth close. It kept that stone clear. The mineral has crept over the wall and the pots and the hands, but it has never crept onto the seat. It is waiting for someone to sit there again.

## What It Is

It has no name that anyone remembers. The people who built the wall must have called it something, and whatever they called it, it took the ends of that too.

If you need a way to hold it in your head, hold it like this. Somewhere below that room, in rock older and hotter than the limestone, lives something very large and very slow and very warm. It does not move, and it may never have moved. It breathes once a day. It is kind in the way deep things are kind, without much notion of what an individual is, but with a great patience for anything that stays near it. It feeds a tree on a hillside where no tree should be green. It keeps a crowd of pale small lives in the steam of its mouth. It has taught the frogs to be quiet. It swallows every sound that comes to it, not out of hunger exactly but because keeping is the only thing it knows how to do with what it's given. It has been holding a dozen human hands against its wall for longer than there have been cities.

The room is not growing and it is not collapsing, because it doesn't need to do either. It found its shape a long time ago. What lives there found its companions a long time ago too: the fig reaching down, the frogs sitting still, the bats shouting above its hearing, the people who came and went and came back. Now it keeps them all, and fills with them, and breathes.

## After

When you climb back out, the surface feels thin and harsh and loud, because for the first time in hours every sound you make is arriving whole, echo and all, and nothing is taking any of it.

For a while afterward, when the afternoon cools, you'll half-expect it to rain. That isn't just a habit your body picked up. When you spoke down there, the ends of your words went missing, and they didn't go nowhere. A small piece of your voice is down the crack in the hot rock now, held in the warm dark with the frogs' old calls and the words of the people who left their hands. That piece of you is still on its schedule, turning at the top of the long slow breath, and it rains every late afternoon, whether or not you're there to feel it.
