# Who Lives at Orrin Low

## The people, first

Start with the people, because they would expect you to.

There are about three hundred of them, give or take the ones who leave on the eleventh-day steamer and the ones who come back on a later one. Coming back is common. People leave Orrin Low meaning it, and they return within a year looking faintly embarrassed, and nobody asks them why. Everyone already knows. Inland, the ground is silent. You lie in a boarding-house bed in a city of brick and soil and something is missing from under you, the way a missing tooth is felt more than any tooth that's there. They say they can't sleep. What they mean is that they can't find the edge of themselves.

They are broad in the shoulder from the winching and the sledging, and many of them are a little deaf in the left ear, the ear that faced the stamps on the shift line. They speak in short phrases, loudly, with a lot of hand. There is a whole grammar of fingers here: a flat palm pressed downward means *wait for the gap*, two knuckles knocked together means *the stamps* and also *work* and also, depending on the face, *the way things are*. The children learn it before they learn words. Children here are rocked to sleep by nobody. The ground does it.

Their walk gives them away anywhere. They walk in time without meaning to, a slight hitch in the gait every second step, and on the jetty at the port inland you can pick an Orrin Low man out of a crowd by the way his feet keep a beat nobody else hears. When two of them meet far from home they fall into step without noticing, and something in both their faces relaxes.

They know which birch is the oldest. It stands in a fern-trench near the original quarry face, among the empty buildings, and it belongs to nobody. But the old people walk out to it, the ones who remember the flow coming down, who were children when the sky went orange and the sea boiled at the shelf edge. There are seven of them left. They sit near the birch on the moss that sighs cold air, a kilometer from the Stamp House, where the pounding is only a mutter in the stone. They say they go out there for the quiet. That is not quite true. They go out there to be where the town was, and to feel how far it has gone without them.

## The ones in the rock

Then the animals, who were here first by a few years and have never let anyone forget it.

The petrels are the real population. Tens of thousands of them to three hundred people. At dusk they come in off the grey water in a low, flickering smoke, and the town goes quiet in a particular way to let them. The children have a game where they lie on the moss and let the birds skim over them. By night the whole field purrs. Put your hand on the stone near a burrow and you can feel it: the stamps, and under the stamps, the purr, fitted into the stamps like mortar into brick.

The starlings are the town's parody of itself. They sing the tram brake, the dinner bell, the engine whistle, the steamer's horn nine days early so that people look up from the gardens and then swear. A few have learned the three short pulls of the fire whistle, which has caused trouble. The pigeons don't do anything. They are fat and iridescent and content and they live in the one warm dry place for kilometers, and they are, everyone agrees, the only creatures in Orrin Low with any sense.

The fox is a single fox, or everyone talks as if it were. Probably there have been several generations of it. It is small and grey and has a torn ear, and it has been seen carrying a potato in its mouth with evident embarrassment. People leave the worst rhubarb at the ends of the rows for it and call this a mistake.

The wading bird nobody can name picks along the rust-orange crust of the settling ponds on legs like wire. It is the color of the slurry. It appeared maybe ten years ago. Nobody has seen it anywhere but here, on the ponds behind the Stamp House, the ground the town has already eaten. Some say it came in on the steamer. Some say it didn't come from anywhere. It is the only thing in Orrin Low that lives entirely in the town's leavings and seems to prefer them.

## Something in the moss

You could stop there. People, birds, fox, voles, a nameless wader. It would be a fair answer.

But climb onto the roof of the Stamp House on a clear afternoon, which you are not allowed to do and which every child in town has done, and look forward, north, over the fresh rock.

The rings.

From up here the moss is a drawing. It lies in curves nested inside curves, in lines that fan out from the shed and bend where two sets of curves cross, in knots where they reinforce each other, bare patches where they cancel. Forty-eight stamps, each on its own cam, each a fraction out of step with its neighbors, and the stone carrying all of them at once. Where the ground shakes hardest the moss can't hold. Where it lies still, at the nodes, the moss grows thick. The pattern is simply the sound, written down in green.

The children have a name for it, which the adults pretend not to know. They call it the Walker.

## The Walker

It began as a joke, probably. Something to frighten younger siblings with. *The Walker is ahead of the Stamp House. The Walker goes first.* Because it does. That is the thing about the rings nobody quite says aloud: they form in front. A meter, two meters past where the stamps have reached, on rock the iron hasn't touched, faint curves are already coming up in the moss. Of course there's an explanation. The vibration runs ahead through the basalt, the stone rings like a tile, the moss answers wherever the shaking reaches. It's physics. Nobody disputes it.

But the children say the Walker is picking the way.

Watch the pattern over a season, as some of them do, secretly, with charcoal sketches from the roof, and it isn't fixed. It drifts. It shifts with the winching, yes, but also between winchings. Some weeks the knots slide east a little, and the moss that forms on the eastern rock is thicker, and the next winching somehow goes easier on the eastern rail. The winch crew will tell you the rails settle unevenly. The children will tell you the Walker leaned.

What is the Walker? Ask ten of them and you'll get ten answers, and every answer grows as it's told. It is a shape in the moss. No, the moss is only where it shows, like frost on a window shows the breath. It lives in the stone. No, it lives *in the timing*, in the gaps between the forty-eight beats, in the place where one stamp's echo meets another's arrival. It is made of when rather than where. It is enormous, as big as the whole field, because the whole field hums. Put your ear to a boulder a kilometer away and you are putting your ear against its body.

It is young. Everyone agrees about that. It is younger than the oldest birch. It was born thirty-four years ago when the first stamp dropped, or maybe it was born forty-one years ago when the flow cooled and the rock learned to ring and simply waited seven years for something to ring it.

And it died once.

## The three days

The older residents talk about the boiler failure quietly, as if about an illness, and you are meant to think they mean the town's illness: no grit, no wages, the steamer coming and leaving empty. That was part of it.

But listen to what they actually describe. They describe waking in the night because the silence was so loud. They describe the stone underfoot feeling *dead*, not still, dead, the way a hand feels when you touch someone who's gone. They describe the moss going grey in the rings, within a day, the whole drawing blurring as though someone had breathed on it. They describe the petrels refusing to come home.

That is the part everyone circles back to. Tens of thousands of birds hanging offshore, calling, calling, circling in the fog for three nights, while their chicks sat in the burrows and the fox grew fat. Because the petrels didn't find their way in by the stones or the stars. They found their way in by the pattern. The beat came up through the rock and out over the water, and they flew down it like a road. When it stopped, there was no road. There was a shelf of black stone they'd nested in for decades and could no longer recognize as home.

On the fourth morning the engineers got the boiler lit and the stamps started, one by one, all forty-eight, and within the hour the petrels came in, in broad daylight, which they never do, a black river pouring over the town. People wept in the street and would not later admit it.

And the rings came back. But not quite the same. The old people are certain of this, the few who had climbed the roof before. The knots came back in different places. The Walker, if there is a Walker, came back leaning slightly differently. As if what restarted was a sibling rather than the same thing. Or as if the same thing had been somewhere for three days and come back changed.

Since then the stamps don't stop. There are spare boilers now, spare cams, a peat stock that would last a winter. Nobody in town can say out loud what exactly they're protecting.

## The larger animal

The children's Walker is not quite right, and not quite wrong. It is the first glimpse of something bigger, seen from too close.

Step back. Step back further, past the roof of the Stamp House, past the fog-nets, out over the sea where the petrels circle, and look down at Orrin Low whole, and look at it over thirty-four years instead of one afternoon.

There is an animal on the lava field.

It is two kilometers long, if you count the trail. It moves a few centimeters a week. At its front end is a mouth of forty-eight iron teeth, rising and falling, rising and falling, scraping the stone and grinding it fine, the way a snail's rasping tongue grinds the film off a rock. It eats basalt. It digests it in the sorting sheds, sifting out the fraction it can use, the glassy grit, and it passes that along a gut of tramway to the jetty, where the steamer carries it off every eleven days: the one thing the animal gives the outside world in exchange for peat and bread and wire.

Behind it runs its trail, grey and slick. Settling ponds, crushed gravel, rust-orange crust. The same silvery track a snail leaves across a stone, only a kilometer long and forty years deep. The nameless wader walks in the trail and nowhere else, like the small things that live only in a larger thing's wake.

It breathes at its northern edge through thirty gills of tarred mesh that pull water out of fog, hissing on good mornings. Its warm breath comes out of the condenser tower, and along that warmth, in a narrow green strip, its gardens grow, the way gut flora grows along the only warm, wet channel in a body. When the animal moves, the gardens move with it. *Follow the warm water.* The townspeople think it's a saying about gardens. It is a description of how to stay alive inside something.

Its veins are iron pipes on trestles, sagging between buildings, carrying steam and air. Its single nerve is a copper line from the dynamo, lighting the mouth, the stomach, and the doctor. Its heartbeat you already know. You've felt it through your boots since you arrived.

Its body is made of houses on sledges, and the houses creep forward after the mouth in their own time, dragged on oiled logs by neighbors, a family deciding one day that the walk has become too long. And like a hermit crab, like a snail that outgrows and sheds, it leaves its old shells behind. At the far end, near the original quarry face, the empty buildings stand with moss and fern crawling up them, the cast-off casings of a younger body. The oldest birch grows among them. The seven old people sit there. They are the part of the animal that remembers where it used to be.

The people are its cells. They don't think of themselves that way, any more than a cell does. But they walk in its rhythm. They sicken when they leave it and come back. Their children are rocked by its pulse. When it stopped for three days they felt it die, and they have arranged their whole lives since around making sure it never does again.

And the petrels are its breath at night, the purr in the rock. The starlings are its voice, learning its own sounds and singing them back. The fox is what it can't keep out, and the rhubarb at the row ends is its mercy. The rings in the moss ahead are its feelers, the soft horns it puts out onto fresh rock before it touches it, tasting where to go.

That is what the children saw from the roof and called the Walker. Not a ghost in the moss. The animal's own senses, running ahead of its mouth.

## What it doesn't know

It is young, and it's mortal, and it's already more than halfway across the only food it can live on.

Sixty years to the northern lip. After that the grit is gone. Inland the stone is older and poorer; the animal could turn, rails and all, and crawl into it, eating more slowly, thinner, feeding fewer cells. Or it could stop. Nobody has decided. Which is to say: the animal hasn't decided. There is no single mind in it to decide. There are three hundred people, and tens of thousands of birds, and a drawing in the moss that leans a little east some weeks.

The children who drew the rings from the roof are grown now, some of them, working the shift line with waxed cotton in their ears. A few still climb up at dusk when nobody's looking. They say the rings at the front have started doing something new in the last few years. They say that at the very edge, in the faintest curves on the freshest rock, the pattern has begun to bend. Not east. Not west. Slightly inland.

As if something were feeling for the way.

## What will be there after

In three or four hundred years there may be a forest here, soil, old trees whose age nobody knows. And through the forest a long straight scar of gravel and old ponds running toward the sea's edge. At its northern end, maybe, a rusted iron shed on rails, forty-eight columns standing still at last inside it, ferns in the cam housings.

Whatever lives there then will find the shed the way you find a shell on a beach: empty, beautiful, clearly the shape of something that was once alive and inside it, and then moved on or didn't.

But if there are petrels then, and there will be, ask them. On certain nights, coming in from the sea, they may still bend their flight along a line nobody else can see, down a road that was drawn in the stone long ago by something enormous and slow that drummed its way across the rock. They'll purr in the burrows. And the purr may still come, out of long habit, in pulses. Two seconds, and two seconds, and two seconds, keeping time for a heart that stopped.
