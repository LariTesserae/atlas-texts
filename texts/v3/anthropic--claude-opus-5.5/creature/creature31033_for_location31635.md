# Who Lives in the Lathe

## I. The Ones You See First

Start with the stilt-reader, because that is where everyone starts.

You will probably mistake the first one for a spire. It stands at the trailing edge of a repair front, where the towers are just beginning to sink. It is pale, narrow, and perfectly still, and the ground-light passes up through its thin legs the same way it passes through the lattice. Only when it moves do you understand it was never part of the city. It lifts one foot, sets it down a stride further on, and the spire you were looking at becomes an animal.

Up close it is taller than a person by half again. Its body is a slender keel slung between four jointed legs, and the legs are almost all of it. They end in feet like folded paper, so narrow that the stilt-reader seems to rest on points. It weighs very little. When it walks over stubble it leaves no scuff at all, and in powder it leaves no print. Its neck is long and carries a head shaped like a closed hand: tapered, eyeless as far as anyone can tell, with a vertical seam down the front that opens when it feeds.

What it does among the spires looks like reading. It stops at a lattice and lowers its head until the seam nearly touches a thimble-room. Then it holds there, sometimes for a minute, sometimes for ten. Occasionally it moves its head in small, precise increments from room to room along a row, the way an eye moves along a line. At other rooms it stops only briefly and moves on, as if it has read them before.

It eats the threadvine flowers, but not every flower. Surveyors who followed one for a full front counted forty-one flowers it passed and three it ate. They could find no difference between them. The flowers grow at the peaks of spires, and the spires are remembered architecture. So the stilt-reader is choosing among memories. It is eating the ones it finds worth eating.

Nobody has seen a young stilt-reader. Here is what the more careful surveyors now believe: stilt-readers are not born, or not in any way we would recognize. Watch a front that is remembering a very old era, the ones that rise up out of the deepest strata and are shaped strangely, all long thin members and forked supports. Now and then a structure goes up at the very front edge of the bright band that looks almost exactly like a stilt-reader standing still. Most of them sink with everything else. Occasionally one does not. The next morning there is one more reader in the world, standing where the tower was, tilting its head toward the stubble as if trying to remember what it was for.

If that is true, then the stilt-readers are a room the city once built for its own audience, remembered so many times that it learned to walk.

## II. The Ones Underneath

The grinders are harder to love.

They live in the crushed room-layers just below the stubble. Those layers are flattened chambers pressed as thin as leaves and stacked in their thousands, and the grinders slide through them edgewise, the way a coin slides into a slot. A grinder is a disc the width of a palm, grey on top and bone-white beneath. The underside is ringed with concentric ridges of something harder than the Lathe. It has no visible head. Held in the hand (they can be caught, briefly, when they surface), a grinder is cool, which nothing else on the Lathe is. It vibrates faintly and constantly, like a held bee.

They come up every few years, all at once, during a front. They spread across the new spires in their thousands and cling to the structures, and they turn. They rotate in place, slowly, their ridged undersides cutting spiral grooves into the fresh material. The grooves are perfect. Then the grinders slide back down into the pressed rooms, and when the next front passes over that ground, the city it remembers is grooved too.

The first theory was that they were parasites, feeding on the new growth. But they eat none of what they cut. The shavings fall back into the powder. The second theory was that they were a kind of immune response, marking damaged instructions. But the spirals do not follow damage. They follow each other. Map the grooves across a whole field of grinder-work and they connect from spire to spire into a single continuous line that loops and doubles back across kilometers. It looks very much like handwriting.

What the grinders are doing, then, is adding. They are the only thing on the Lathe that makes marks the Lathe did not already contain. Every other process here works by remembering, but the grinders write, and the city remembers what they wrote as if it had always been there.

There is an argument among those who study them. One side says the grinders are corrupting the record, scratching over the old instructions with meaningless spirals, hastening the smoothness. The other side says the spirals are the only new thing that has happened here in longer than anyone can count, and that a record which can still be written in is not yet finished. The two sides agree that the grinders are cool to the touch because they are doing something expensive. They disagree about whether it is worth the cost.

## III. The Ones Who Keep Moving

People live on the Lathe, too.

Nobody lives there in the sense of settling. It is not possible. But there is a people whose whole life is the crossing. They call themselves by a word that means something like *the unstayed*, or *those who are never set down*. The name everyone else uses for them is the Turners.

A Turner child is born walking, in a sense. Birth happens in a hammock slung between two walking-frames, while the frames are carried forward. The infant is passed from carrier to carrier and never laid on the ground. Children learn to walk before they learn to sit, because sitting still is the first dangerous thing. Their games are all about motion: rocking games, pacing songs, rounds that are sung while circling. Their stillest posture is a slow sway from foot to foot, which the old ones do all day like tide.

They sleep in shifts, in hammocks, while others carry. They cook on moving braziers. Their tents are frames of light wood that fold up, and they never pitch them on the ground. They hang them from each other. A Turner camp is a slow procession, a few hundred people walking together at the speed of a repair front, and in the long amber evenings it looks from far off like a town that has decided to migrate.

Their language has no word for *stay*. It has seven words for *shift*, depending on whether you are shifting weight, gear, sleep, or grief. When a Turner says "I love you," the phrase translates most closely as *I will keep carrying you.*

They follow the fronts. Behind a front, the ground is freshly stubbled and slightly softer, and the dew stays longer there, so the rosettes gather. Turners harvest the rosettes' outer leaves, which are edible when boiled and taste like pennies and bread. They follow the stilt-readers too, at a respectful distance, because where a reader lingers, the threadvines are thickest. They do not hunt readers. They say a reader carries more of the city than any front, and killing one would be like burning a library you cannot read.

And they know the Rule better than anyone, because they have given it their dead.

## IV. The Ones Who Were Set Down

When a Turner dies, the procession stops.

It is the only time it does. Everyone halts, and for the length of one song they all stand still together, a few hundred people, and they feel the filaments begin. It is a faint prickling at the soles of the feet, the Lathe noticing them. The song is timed to end before the prickle turns into a grip. Then the dead one is laid on the stubble, face up, hands open, and the Turners walk on without looking back. Looking back is the only real taboo they have.

Within a week the body is threaded through. Within a month it is ground. Then, sometimes a season later and sometimes years, a front passes over that place, and among the remembered towers there is a room shaped like a person.

It is not a statue. The detail is wrong for that. What rises is a hollow, a chamber in the lattice whose walls follow the lines of the body that was laid there, with a threshold where the open hands were. It has alcoves where the eyes were and a window where the mouth was. The front has taken the body in and filed it with everything else it keeps. It remembers the dead one the way it remembers a city, as a place to be lived in.

The Turners do not follow fronts to find these rooms. They are forbidden to look back. But they do find them, because the fronts go everywhere, and eventually every family walks through a city with one of its own inside it. There are rituals for this. You do not stop. You do not touch it. You may walk past it as slowly as walking allows and sing the name once. By the next morning it is stubble again.

Sometimes the room has spiral grooves in it. Grinder-work. The Turners have strong opinions about this. Some families take it as a blessing, a sign that the dead one has been written into the city and not merely filed. Others take it as a desecration and grieve all over again. A few old Turners say you can read the grooves if you walk the spiral with your eyes, and that what they say is the dead one's name, in a script no living person was ever taught.

And then there are the ones who stood still on purpose.

Every generation has a few. Most are old, though not all. They walk out from the procession one amber evening, go a long way off, and stop. They raise one hand, which is a Turner gesture meaning *go on without me*, and they hold it. They are gone by morning, not dead yet, only threaded. The Turners do not speak of them as dead. They use a different word, the same word they use for a hammock that has been folded and packed: *put away carefully.*

The fronts remember them standing, hand raised. That is what the rumored spires are. Surveyors can find perhaps a dozen of them a year across the known Lathe, pale human-shaped towers with one hand lifted, rising and sinking in an afternoon. The Turners say the raised hand is not a farewell. It is how you hail someone across a distance. They are saying *here I am* to whoever still lives in the city.

Which raises the question the Turners have always known the answer to, and which the surveyors took much longer to ask.

## V. The Ones the Rooms Were For

The rooms are the size of thimbles, and too small for anything alive.

That was what everyone assumed for a long time. Then someone put the powder under a lens.

A single mote of the grey powder is not a grain. It is a structure: folded, faceted, and hollow, like a seed-case, or like a tiny building collapsed in on itself for storage. Under enough magnification the folding resolves into a lattice. Inside the lattice are chambers. Inside the chambers, at the limit of what any lens has ever resolved, there is more lattice.

Put a pinch of it in a warm dish and watch. Over hours, the motes drift together. They click, faintly (that is the ticking, or part of it), and they assemble. Not much: a strut, a node, two or three together. It is the beginning of a lattice. Then it falls apart again. But it tries.

This is what the repair front is. It is not a machine sweeping across the plain rebuilding the city. The front is the powder gathering. It is billions of motes rising from the channels and the hollow cores, streaming along whatever gradient they follow, and *standing up together*. They lock into the shapes they once held and hold them for an afternoon. The spires are not made of Lathe-stuff. They are made of motes, crowded shoulder to shoulder, remembering where they used to stand.

And the thimble-rooms, with their thresholds and alcoves and windows, are not too small for their inhabitants. They are the right size for a mote, or for a cluster of them, which may be what the old inhabitants were. Or perhaps the inhabitants were whatever the motes are the scattered pieces of.

Here the careful surveyors stop and the Turners go on. What the Turners say is this:

There was never a city here with people in it. The city *was* the people. They did not build it. They were it. They lived as architecture, each small life a room, each room holding the ones beside it up, until the whole continent was a single inhabited structure standing in the light. When they wore down, they did not die. They became powder, which is what they are when they are resting. When they are awake, they stand up and become the city again, or as much of it as they can still remember.

The Lathe is not a ruin of a civilization. It is the civilization, asleep, getting up each afternoon to stand in its old shape for a while, and lying down again a little more tired.

The third sound, the pressure at the edge of hearing like a crowd holding its breath, is exactly that.

This explains a great many things at once. It explains why the fronts remember and do not invent: they are not following instructions, they are recalling themselves. It explains why each remembering is a little less complete: every time they stand, a few more of them have forgotten how. It explains the Rule. What stays still long enough is taken in, not devoured, but *included*. The city is inviting it to be a room. The pack becomes a pack-shaped room because the city is made of residents, and residents make room for what arrives.

It explains the dead. When a Turner is laid down, the powder moves in and holds them, learns their shape, and keeps it. When the front comes, it stands up in the shape of a person because somebody is now living there.

And it explains the stilt-readers. A city made of its own people would need someone to *witness* it, someone outside the architecture who walks among the rooms and sees what they are. At some point, very long ago, the city built itself a reader. It remembered that reader so many times that the reader learned to walk away on its own four legs.

It even suggests what the grinders might be. They may be the city's last attempt to have a *new* thought. They are small, cool, expensive engines of revision, cutting the first new text in an age into a record that otherwise can only repeat itself.

## VI. The Ones in the Sky

That leaves the veil.

The powder that rises into the high air on the great slow convection is the same powder: the same motes, the same residents. Up there, where they cannot touch the ground, they cannot remember the strata below. They have nothing to stand on and nothing to recall themselves against.

And yet sometimes they make shapes.

The shapes in the veil match nothing in the Lathe. Surveyors have compared them. They have sketched the brief vast outlines that cross half the sky, the towers and lattices and frames of something enormous, and checked them against every stratum they could reach. Nothing matches. The fronts on the ground remember. The veil does not remember. It has nothing to remember from.

The Turners have a word for what it does instead, and it is the same word they use for what a child does when it points at the horizon and says *there*. The closest translation is *intending*.

Up in the sky, loose and light and unanchored from their own history, the residents of the Lathe are imagining a city they have never been. It lasts a few minutes. It is the only thing in the whole continent that is not a repetition. When it dissolves, the motes drift back down in the long slow sweep of the convection. They settle into the channels, rest there, and stand up on some later afternoon as the city they were. They do not remember the one they dreamed.

Or perhaps they do, a little. Perhaps that is where the grinders' spirals come from. Perhaps each dream leaves a trace that sinks with the powder, down through the pressed rooms, until something cool and palm-sized and patient finds it and carries it up and cuts it, very carefully, into the next day's memory.

## VII. The One Who Isn't Anyone

So here is who lives on the Lathe.

A people too small to see, who are also a city too large to cross, lie resting as dust and wake as towers and grow a little more tired every time they stand. A reader they built for themselves wanders among them, eating the flowers of the memories it judges worth keeping. There are small cool writers beneath the floor. There is a people above the floor who never lie down, whose dead are welcomed in as rooms and whose oldest members sometimes walk out into the light, stop, and lift a hand to say *here I am.* And somewhere in the high bright veil there is a dream of what all of them might yet become.

And then there is the copper rosette.

It belongs to none of this. It is not remembered; it moves too fast for that, a few centimeters a day, just quick enough never to be included. It does not remember; it has no strata, only a grip of tiny hooks and a set of grooved leaves and whatever it takes from the difference between the warm ground and the cooler air. It will not be written into the city and it will not write. It will not dream in the veil. It lives on the heat that rises off a continent-sized sleeping crowd, and it keeps its distance from its neighbors, and it goes on.

When the fronts finally stop, on that distant afternoon when the last resident forgets how to stand, the Lathe will be one smooth surface of warm light. The stilt-readers will have nothing left to read. The grinders will have nothing to write on. The Turners, if they are still there, will walk forever across a floor with no rooms in it, and no one will make a room for them when they lie down.

The rosettes will still be there, crossing the smooth warm plain a few centimeters at a time, catching dew in their grooves at dawn.

In the Turner language there is a phrase for them too. It is what the old ones say to a child who has finally learned to sway from foot to foot all day without tiring, the phrase that means a Turner has become fully one of the unstayed.

It translates, roughly, as *you are moving just fast enough to be alive.*
