# Who Lives in the Seaward City

## The First Answer

The first thing to understand is that no one who lives here is older than the ground beneath them. That isn't quite true, but it is what they say, and they say it the way other people say *the sun rises in the east*. It isn't a fact they have measured. It is how the place is arranged.

If you asked a stranger who lives in the Seaward City, and you asked out loud on some open plate at pewter noon, the honest first answer would be: *hardly anyone*. You would look across a kilometer of green-grey stone, past a stepped hall and a line of narrow houses, and you might see one figure walking the pale avenue, small and unhurried. That figure is the first answer. It is not the whole one.

## The Walkers

They are people, recognizably. They have two arms, two legs, and faces, and they speak, though not often. They are not descended from the surveyors, or not only. Some came up from the coast generations ago and some came out of the forest. A few say their families were always here, which cannot be literally true, since *here* keeps moving.

What makes them a people is that they walk west.

Not quickly. The crest of the city moves at the pace of the land being laid at the seaward edge, which means the pace of stays kindling in new stone and old stays going slack in the east. The city's heart drifts west by perhaps a few hundred meters a year, perhaps less. The walkers don't measure it in meters. They measure it in houses.

A household lives in one of the narrow houses, the ones clustered like seed heads, each with its single amber window. They stay some years, until they notice the dew in the mornings creeping more slowly than it used to, the seam-ferns turning brittle at their tips, the arcade near them moaning a little flat. Then they know their stay is tiring. They gather what they own, which is not much, and walk west until they find a cluster of houses that is empty, newly risen and waiting. The city makes houses on the young plates before anyone asks for them. No walker has ever built a house. They move into ones that were already expecting them.

So a walker's whole life is a slow migration across the same few kilometers of stone, keeping pace with a place that never holds still. A child might be born in one house, grow up in three, marry in a fourth, and raise children in a sixth. When they are old, if they look east from their doorway, they can see, bent upward on the horizon by the lensing, the green wave of forest where their first house now lies under seam-fig roots.

The rule that governs all of this is never spoken, because it doesn't need to be: **to stay in the city, you must keep walking. If you stop, the city leaves you.**

## Their Hands

You can tell a walker by their palms.

Every walker has a scar across the heel of the right hand, fine and straight, from a seam. Children get it at about the age of seven, and not by accident. A parent or older sibling takes the child to a particular seam, one that matters to the family, and the child lays a palm flat across it and draws the hand along until the edge opens the skin. It stings. The blood runs into the seam and creeps, like the dew, toward the stay.

Afterward the child is said to *know the lay*. This is partly ceremony and partly practical. A walker with a seam-scar can find seams in the dark by touch, feeling the grain change under their fingers. Older walkers have many scars, a fine hatching across both palms, one for each house they've lived in and each seam they've needed to remember. The hands of the very old look like the lay itself seen from a terrace: pale fields crossed by fine lines.

They don't shake hands. When two walkers greet each other up close, which is rare, they hold their palms up side by side, as if comparing maps.

## How They Count

Because distances lie, walkers count.

Every walker carries a seam-cord, a length of fern-fiber twine knotted as they go. A plain knot marks each seam crossed. A double knot marks a seam where three plates meet. A loop marks an avenue. A long walk leaves a long cord, and the cords are kept. In some houses they hang from the ceiling in bundles, dozens of years of walking, so that the house smells faintly of dry fern. A walker can pick up a cord their grandmother knotted and walk her route exactly, even though every stay on it has moved, because the seams are the one thing that doesn't lie.

They are good at arithmetic in a strange, specific way. Ask a walker how far it is to a place and they will not say *two hours* or *three kilometers*. They'll say *forty-one seams and the fig arcade*. Ask them how old they are and some will answer in houses.

And they give addresses by sound. Each arcade moans on its own note in the warm wind, and walkers learn the notes the way other people learn street names. *I live two notes above the long arcade*, someone might say, *on the side where it answers*. A person with good ears can find a house in the fog by pitch alone.

## Why They Don't Walk Toward Each Other

Visitors, the rare ones, always notice this first: the figures on the avenues never seem to approach one another.

It isn't coldness. It comes from the stays. When you walk straight toward someone across open lay, the lensing plays with you. They look near and then far. They look close enough to call to and then turn out to be most of a day away. Or they seem distant and are suddenly *there*, around a corner of a terrace, much sooner than you were ready for. Walking directly at a person is how you make a fool of yourself, or arrive exhausted, or arrive too early, with nothing to say.

So walkers have a manner of meeting that looks from a distance like avoidance. Two people who want to meet agree on a seam, *the triple seam past the dry bridge, the fourth one*, and each walks there by counting, often along parallel avenues, not looking at each other at all. They arrive at the seam and only then look up. To walk toward someone with your eyes on them is considered either very rude or very intimate. It means *I trust the distance between us not to lie*.

Courtship is conducted almost entirely this way. Two young walkers who are interested in each other will walk the same long avenue at the same hour for weeks, a kilometer apart, never closing the gap, each knotting the same seams onto their cord. Eventually one of them stops keeping the distance. That's the proposal. The other either slows down, which means yes, or keeps pace, which means not yet.

Married couples walk together side by side, facing the same direction. Seen from a terrace they look like two threads of the same seam.

## What They Eat, What They Drink

They eat from the seams.

Fern tips, uncurled and bitter, are boiled until soft. Moss is pressed into flat cakes and dried on the warm stone, where it bakes slowly in the stone's own interior heat. Seam-figs are the staple: small, dense, and pale, sweet as dates, ripening in the wet rings. Walkers never pick more than one tree can spare, not out of any rule, but because a seam-fig that is stripped is a fig that stops prying, and a fig that stops prying lets a plate grow too whole, and a whole plate grows nothing. Everyone knows this without being told.

They don't eat the glass snails. A walker who sees a child reach for one will catch their wrist gently and put the snail back in the moss. When asked why, most walkers say that the snails carry the lantern-light and that it would be unlucky to swallow it. A few older walkers give a different answer: that the snails are *reading*, that the patterns they graze into the moss are the same shapes as the carved script on the old walls, and that it is not polite to eat someone in the middle of a sentence.

They eat wader eggs rarely, only one from any clutch, and only in years when the fog is thick.

Water comes from the rings. Every morning someone in each household walks inward to the nearest stay with a jar and fills it from the black pools where the smeared sun lies across the surface like a bar of gold. The water tastes faintly of iron, faintly of warm stone, and of something else walkers call *the pull*, a sensation at the back of the tongue like the moment before a sneeze. Children from the coast, drinking it for the first time, often cry. Walkers raised on it find other water flat.

## The Hours They Keep

The light never rises very high, and the walkers have made a life to fit it.

They are awake at dawn, when the beaded lay turns from black to silver and the lanterns close. This is the hour for water-fetching and for walking long distances, while the stone is wet and the seams are easy to feel. At noon, when the smeared sun is highest and the sky is hardest to read, many of them sleep, or sit indoors behind their amber windows. Looking up at noon for too long gives a particular headache, the *sky-ache*. Walkers who have it squint at everything and are forgiven for being short-tempered.

In the late afternoon, when the fog runs inland down the avenues in white rivers, they go out again, and this is the most social hour. The fog hides the distances. It fills the avenues knee-deep or waist-deep, and walkers wade into it as into a shallow stream. Inside the fog you can't see far enough to be fooled, and people who wouldn't cross an open plate toward each other at noon will stand together in the mist, talking quietly, while it pours around their legs toward the seams. Much of the city's business is done in fog: trading, arguing, arranging marriages, deciding which household moves west next.

At night they stay near the rings, among the lanterns, and tend their dead stays and their living ones.

## Behind the Other Windows

Most of the amber windows have no one behind them.

The walkers know which is which, though a visitor never could. The windows with people behind them flicker faintly when someone moves. The others hold steady, or change very slowly in a way that isn't flickering: a pattern growing across the inside of the amber like frost across a pane, then retreating, then growing in a different shape.

The walkers call what lives behind those windows the *keeping*, or sometimes just *the house* in a way that implies more than one house. They're the processes the city runs on. No walker claims to understand them. But walkers keep them company.

It's an ordinary thing to do. An old walker, a woman with palms hatched pale from fifty years of seams, might carry a stool to a steady window at noon and sit beside it for an hour, humming. Children are sent to sit with windows when they're restless. A walker who can't sleep will go and put a hand flat against the warm stone beneath a keeping window and feel it ticking, faster than the ordinary ticking of the cooling stone, in rhythms that almost make sense.

Do the keepings notice? The walkers are split on this. Some say no, that the keepings are like weather. Others point to the houses that appear on young plates, always the right number for the families who need to move, always clustered the way the walkers like to cluster, with windows facing the way they like to face. *Something is listening*, they say. *Something counts us the way we count seams.*

There is a story, told at fog-hour, about a walker who sat with the same steady window every day for forty years, humming the note of her arcade. When she died, the window changed its frost into a pattern it had never made before and then held that pattern, unchanging, until its stay went slack and the forest took it. Walkers who were children then say the pattern looked like a woman's hands, palms up.

## The Writing

The dense carved script on the old walls changes from district to district, and nobody reads all of it. What the walkers have noticed, only in the last few generations, is the direction of the change.

In the east, on the oldest plates, the script is spare and angular, alien, the marks of something that thought in straight lines and exact angles. The surveyors copied some of it into their books; the books are mostly moss now. Moving west, the script loosens. It picks up curves. It begins to repeat certain shapes in groups, like words.

On the youngest plates, the ones still faintly orange at their seams, the carved lines on the new walls look almost, *almost*, like knots.

Some walkers have laid their seam-cords beside the newest writing and found runs of marks that match: plain, plain, double, loop. The same route, their own route, written into the wall of a house they haven't moved into yet.

Nobody is sure what to do with this. Some find it comforting: the city is learning us. Some find it frightening: the city is writing down where we are going to walk. A few of the young have begun, deliberately, knotting nonsense into their cords, walking routes that go nowhere, just to see if the walls will copy that too.

The walls, so far, have not.

## The Stopped

East of the oldest plates, where the stays stand slack and the stone crazes to gravel, other people live. The walkers call them *the stopped*. They call themselves nothing in particular, because in the forest you don't need a name to be found. You just shout.

The stopped are, mostly, walkers who stopped walking. Some were old. Some were tired. Some fell in love with a house and wouldn't leave it, and the city walked away from them, a few hundred meters a year, until one morning they woke and the dew didn't creep, the arcade didn't moan, the stay beside them was silent in the chest, and the forest had come up to their door.

This is not considered a tragedy. In the city it is spoken of gently, like retirement or like dying. You'll hear an old walker say, *I think I'll stop soon*, and their children will nod.

The stopped live loudly. That's the first thing a walker notices on visiting, and walkers do visit, carrying seam-figs east to trade for things the forest has. In the forest the distances are honest. You can see a person twenty meters away and they are twenty meters away. You can walk straight toward a friend without embarrassment. So the stopped call to each other through the trees and laugh and sing and bang on things. Their children run straight at each other. Walkers find it exhausting and a little shocking and oddly moving. More than one walker has gone east to visit a parent and come back quieter than they left.

The stopped live in the rubble of old halls, now cracked open and roofed with fig roots. They eat fruit the city never grows: heavy, wet, sugary things that rot fast. They drink water that tastes of nothing. Their palms heal smooth after a few years, because there are no sharp seams left to cut them. You can tell how long someone has been stopped by how faded their scars are.

They keep a few old things. In certain clearings, tended with great care, stand the slack stays: dark columns, inert now, the spiral vines on them long dead and hanging in grey ropes. The stopped hang their old seam-cords on these, hundreds of them, whole lives of knotted walking, stirring slightly in the damp air. When a stopped person dies, their cord goes there too. Walkers visiting the forest sometimes go to these clearings to look for the cords of people they knew. Finding one, they don't untie it. They just count the knots, and remember.

## The Listeners

And then there is the larger thing, the three-toed one, the dark-coated grazer in the wet rings before dawn.

Walkers call them *listeners*, and most walkers have never seen one clearly. What they know comes in pieces: a print in the damp at the edge of a ring, wide-set and three-toed, the middle toe longer; a darker patch in the moss where something has grazed; a shape at the base of a stay in the last grey before dawn, standing absolutely still, head raised.

What walkers say about them has changed over the generations, and is still changing.

The oldest story, the one grandparents tell, is simple: the listeners are animals, shy grazers that like the stay-water, like the waders but bigger. Leave them be.

The next story, the one parents tell, is that the listeners are *attending*. When a listener comes to a stay night after night, the stay is healthy. The vines climb, the dew creeps briskly, the arcade near it sings in tune. When the listeners stop coming, the stay begins to tire. Walkers have learned to read this. If no fresh three-toed prints appear at a ring for a full season, the households near it start packing, even if the dew still seems to move. The listeners know before the stone does.

The story the young tell is sharper. They say the listeners don't just know. They *decide*. A listener at the base of a stay, head raised, is not listening to the stone; the stone is listening to *it*. The pull in the stays, the bending of the light, the creep of the water, the direction the vines are forced to turn: all of it is held in place by attention, and the listeners are what does the attending. When a listener walks away from a stay for the last time and turns west, the stay goes slack because nothing is holding it anymore.

There is a fourth story, told only in the forest, only by the stopped, and only at night. It says that a few of the stopped, the very old, the ones whose palms have gone perfectly smooth, sometimes walk east. Not to the clearings with the cords, but past them, deep into the forest, where no one follows. And sometimes, years later, in the gravel at the forest's edge, the stopped find tracks. Three-toed, wide-set. Heading west, back toward the city.

No walker believes this. Every walker, standing in a wet ring before dawn and seeing a dark shape at the base of a stay, has thought about it.
