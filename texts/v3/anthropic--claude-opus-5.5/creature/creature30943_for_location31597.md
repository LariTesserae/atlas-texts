# What Lives in the Undertube

## I. The Wrong First Answer

The obvious answer is the bats, and it isn't wrong exactly. It just isn't the answer to the question being asked.

The bats live *in* the tube the way weather lives in the sky. They are the condition of the place, its climate of wings. Ask what lives in the Undertube and the colony answers with its whole roaring body. But the colony leaves every night and comes back every morning. It is always arriving and always going. It is a tide, and a tide belongs to the shore without living on it.

The swiftlets are the same, only smaller and in daylight. The crickets are a texture. The guano world is a kitchen: everything in it is eating or being eaten, and nothing in it is *home* so much as *busy*.

So look again. Look for the thing that stays when everything else is in motion.

## II. The Salamander

It rests at the base of the bat chamber, where the guano thins to a brown skin over the ropy lava. It is pale in the way of things that have never needed colour: not white, but the grey-pink of the inside of a wrist. Its gills stand out from behind its head in three feathery pairs, the only part of it that moves, lifting and settling in the damp air with a rhythm too slow to be called breathing.

The description of the place calls it *the only creature here that seems to be doing nothing*. That is how it looks. If you stay long enough, though, and almost no one does, you begin to notice something the plateau people half-noticed generations ago, when they decided that loud speech in the chamber was a discourtesy.

The gills are not keeping time with the salamander. They are keeping time with the drips.

There is a drip three metres to its left that falls every eleven seconds. There is another, farther back, that falls every forty. There is one high in the teeth above the colony that falls every six hours and lands on nothing, on a single stone the size of a fist, and has worn a dimple there no deeper than a thumbprint. The gills lift on the eleven and settle on the forty. At the six-hour drip the whole animal goes still in a different way, like a held breath.

It is counting.

## III. What It Counts

Start with the drips, because that is where it learned.

When the tube was new and still ticking with heat, when the last lava had drained downhill and the roof was freezing its thousand teeth in place, the first water came through the basalt and fell. Something in the dark (it may not yet have been a salamander, it may not yet have been anything) noticed that the falling was regular. Here was a thing that happened, and then happened again, at the same interval. It was the first pattern in a place that had only been heat and then cooling.

The noticing became a habit. The habit became a sense, and the sense grew an animal around it.

It counts the drips still, all of them, every drip in the tube. It knows each basin by the pitch of water landing in it, the way you might know your children by their footsteps on the stair. It knows when a drip has slowed after a dry season on the plateau and when it has quickened after rain. It does not see the rain. It only hears the rain arrive weeks late through thirty metres of stone, a slight change in the tempo of the world.

From the drips it learned to count other things.

It counts the colony. This is the great labour of its existence, and it is not done by listening to individual bats. No one could separate millions of voices. It is done by listening to the *grain* of the sound. The chitter of the colony has a texture like sand poured through fingers, and the salamander knows the exact coarseness of that sand. A colony one bat larger sounds, to it, very slightly rougher. A colony that lost a thousand to an owl on the plateau, or to a hard night of wind, sounds smoother, worn, like a stone turned in a stream.

At dusk, when the emergence pours out through the great skylight, it counts them out. Before dawn it counts them in. The two numbers should be the same, minus the dead, plus nothing, because the young are born in their season and the count adjusts then, as a ledger adjusts at the turning of a year.

When the numbers don't match, it does something.

## IV. What It Does

Here the image starts to widen, because the salamander by the chamber's edge is not all of it. It never was.

What the salamander does, when the count is wrong, is *attend*.

Consider the bats left behind during the emergence, the young and the sick, whose residual chitter is almost the only sound in the tube for that hour of emptiness. Some of them die. Their bodies fall to the guano, and within days a soft grey mould has furred them into cushions, and the beetles and mites have come, and the bodies are gone into the brown plain. That is ordinary. That is the kitchen working.

But sometimes a sick bat does not die. Sometimes, in the quiet hour, the salamander leaves its place, and it is astonishingly slow, a pale smear crossing the guano like a thought crossing a sleeping face. It comes to where the bat hangs low on a shelf or clings to a root curtain, and it rests beneath it. It does nothing visible. It simply stays, with its gills lifting and settling, lifting and settling, on the eleven and the forty.

And the bat's heartbeat, which had gone ragged, begins to fall back into a count.

The plateau people do not know this. What they know is older and vaguer: that the chamber is attended, that something keeps it, that the colony has never failed in living memory or in the memory of the stories. They know that a loud voice, a voice with no rhythm in it, a shout or a curse or a laugh that breaks, lands in that attentive dark like a stone thrown into a ledger. It cannot be counted. It has no interval. It is pure event, a thing that happens once and never again, and the keeper of the tube has no place to put it.

So they whisper. A whisper is almost a drip.

## V. Where Else It Lives

Once you know to listen for it, you find it everywhere the tube keeps time.

It lives in the amphipods in the drip-pools. Those transparent, eyeless shrimp flicker through their coins of water in patterns that, over hours, resolve into a slow circuit, the same path around the same basin, a clock-hand made of a living thing. They are not the salamander, but they are *of* it, the way a fingertip is of a hand.

It lives in the spiders on the lava shelves, who rebuild their sheet webs at intervals so exact that the webs on the northern wall and the webs on the southern wall are always the same age as one another.

It lives in the root curtains. This is stranger, because the roots belong to trees on the plateau that have never heard of the tube. Yet where the roots hang into the cool dark, their tips grow at a rate matched to the condensation. They stop an arm's length from the floor, all of them, as if a line had been drawn. The cave insects feed on those tips and keep them there. Or the tips feed the insects exactly as much as the insects can bear. It is hard to say which is the ledger and which the entry.

It lives, most of all, in the teeth.

The teeth hang from the ceiling in their thousands, thin as fingers, frozen drips of molten rock from the day the tube was born. Nothing here throws stones, and so they do not fall. And the keeper of the tube, whatever it is when it is not being a salamander, *knows each one*. It knows them the way it knows the drips, because each tooth is the fossil of a drip. Each is a count that was made once, in fire, and stopped. The ceiling of the Undertube is the first page of the ledger, written before the keeper learned to write, and it has been reading that page for three thousand years.

This is why the equilibrium is so complete. This is why the waterfall of the place looks frozen. The Undertube does not simply *happen* to be still beneath its roar. It is being held still. It is being counted into stillness, every drip and wing and cricket and root-tip entered and balanced and entered again, by a patience that has no other occupation and wants none.

## VI. What It Wants

It would be easy to say it wants nothing. The salamander at the chamber's edge looks like a creature without wants.

But a counter wants something very particular. It wants the numbers to close. It wants out-at-dusk to equal in-before-dawn. It wants the drips to keep their intervals, the webs to stay the same age, the roots to stop at their line. It wants the circle, the same circle, described again tomorrow by millions of lives that do not know they are describing it.

That is not nothing. It may be the deepest want there is: the wish for the world to be *reliable*, to keep its promises, to fall every eleven seconds forever.

It loves the colony. Love is the only word that fits the way it listens to that sound, the way it knows the coarseness of the grain, the way it goes out across the guano in the quiet hour to sit beneath the ones whose hearts have lost the count. It loves the swiftlets less, because they come and go with the daylight and it has never quite learned the light. The skylights are a little foreign to it. They are places where the outside falls in.

## VII. The Fig

In the brightest skylight, a single stunted fig has rooted in a crevice and climbed halfway up the shaft. Its trunk is twisted and its few leaves are pale.

Of everything in the Undertube, only the fig does not keep time.

It grows when the light is good and stalls when it is not, and the light on the plateau is never the same two years running. Its roots have gone into the basalt along lines no drip ever made. It does not balance against anything. Nothing eats it back to a line. It is climbing, slowly and crookedly and without permission, toward the open sky, and its roots are prying at the edge of the collapse with the patient strength that only roots have.

One day, very far from now, the roof will fail again. The description of the place says nothing here suggests that day. That isn't quite true. The fig suggests it. The fig is that day, arriving one pale leaf at a time.

And here is the last thing about the keeper of the Undertube, the thing it took the longest to see:

It counts the fig too.

It has counted every leaf. It knows the rate of the roots' prying the way it knows the six-hour drip. It could stop the fig. It stops so much else, holds so much else to its line. A word to the crickets, a shift in the condensation, a small ruin of fungus at the root, and the fig would join the ledger like everything else.

It doesn't.

Once a year, in the quiet hour of the emergence, when the colony has poured out into the dusk and the tube is almost silent, the salamander leaves its place at the chamber's edge. It crosses the long dark, slowly, through the dusk-zones and the cricket walls, until it reaches the rubble mound beneath the brightest skylight. It rests there among the moss, beneath the twisted trunk, with its gills lifting and settling in the grey light it has never quite learned.

It is not counting. For that one hour it is doing what it seemed to be doing all along: nothing. It is keeping company with the one thing in its world that does not come back the same, the one number that will never close. It is, perhaps, keeping that door.

A thing that loves the circle so completely might also know that a circle is only the shape of a thing that has not yet been broken. It might want, somewhere under all that counting, to be there when it breaks, to hear the one sound in three thousand years that falls only once.

Then the colony returns before dawn, dropping out of the sky in its steep dives, and the roar comes back. The salamander is already at the chamber's edge, pale and still, its gills lifting on the eleven and settling on the forty, counting them home.
