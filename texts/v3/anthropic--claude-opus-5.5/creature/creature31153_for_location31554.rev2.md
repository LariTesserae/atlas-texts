# Who Lives in Fennick

## The Easy Answer

Ask who lives in Fennick and the easy answer is the beetles. There are thousands of them, and they have the look of residents. They sleep in the walls, go out in the morning, and come home along the lichen lines at the slackening of the pulse. If you laid your ear against the parchment wall of a terraced house during the slack hours, you would hear them in there between the cellulose layers, shifting, packed shoulder to shoulder like coal in a scuttle.

But the beetles are not residents. They are staff. Watch them long enough and the difference becomes plain. A resident has somewhere they would rather be. The beetles have nowhere else at all. They walk the pavements because pavements are where walking happens, and they carry the bales because bales are what the factory makes. They do not look up at the lamps. They do not pause at the chapel. When one dies (they do die, rarely, lying on their backs on the seed-cobbles with their legs folded like a clasp knife), the others step around it without breaking file, and by the next quickening the lichen has grown over it and it is part of the street.

So the beetles work in Fennick. To find who lives there, you have to go and stand in the dark.

## The Residents

The pool beneath the six lamps on the square is roughly the size of a large room. Its edge is exact: one step and the lichen lines vanish from under your feet, and you are standing in something that is not the absence of light but a presence of its own, cool and faintly heavy, like standing waist-deep in still water that has risen over your head without your noticing.

At first there is nothing. Then there is a shoulder.

It brushes yours, low, at about the height of a person shorter than you, and it is wool: coarse, damp, pilled. You step aside, murmuring an apology before you can stop yourself, and the shoulder does not respond. It is not ignoring you. It simply does not have the part that responds. A few seconds later, a smell reaches you, coal smoke laid down in fabric over years, and under it cabbage boiled too long, and under that something sourer and more human, the smell of the inside of a cap.

Stay long enough and they become countable. There are perhaps twenty of them. You learn them the way you learn furniture in a dark house, by shin and elbow and hip.

There is a woman, or something shaped like where a woman stood, near the center of the pool. She is always in the same place. Her coat has large buttons, and if you let your fingers find them (she does not mind; there is no *she* to mind), they are seeds, hard and ovoid, exactly like the cobbles. At the level of her collar there is warmth, as though a throat were there, and sometimes the warmth moves the way a throat moves when it is about to speak. It never quite does.

There is a tall figure at the eastern edge who coughs. The cough is the most complete thing about him. It is wet and deep, a miner's cough or a mill cough, the cough of a man whose lungs have been furred over. If you stand near him long enough you begin to hear its shape before it arrives, the little intake, the catch. A visitor who came back after a season reported that the cough had changed. It was shorter. There was no longer any wetness in it, only the catch and a dry rasp. The next season, only the catch.

There is a child, or a height where a child would be. You find it by walking into it at knee level and feeling something thin and restless shift away. It is the only one of them that moves on its own. It circles, the way children circle the legs of adults who are talking about something that does not concern them, and occasionally you hear it hum a few notes, and the notes are the same notes as the hymn from the chapel, sped up, as if heard once through a window and carried home.

There are others: a pair who stand very close together, whose sleeves are always touching; a heavy man who smells of oil and iron filings and stands with his weight back on his heels, arms folded, the posture of someone waiting to be told the shift has started; a person, sex impossible to determine, who has only a pair of boots and the scrape the boots make, over and over, a left foot turning on a stone.

They are copies of copies, like the chairs. Each of them is thinner than the last time anyone checked. It is not that they fade. Fading would imply they had once been bright. It is that they are being remembered less, one detail at a time. The woman's buttons were once more numerous. The cough once had a word at the end of it.

## Why They Stand Under the Lamps

It took a long time for anyone to understand that the factory exists for them.

The townsfolk cannot live in air. Air is too thin a medium, too light-bearing, too full of the sharp edges of the bulb's new growth. They need something denser, something with the texture of a remembered room where the curtains are drawn at night and the fire has gone to embers. Darkness, in Fennick, is that medium, and it does not last. It evaporates, the way a split bale evaporates from the factory floor, and so it must be made continuously.

So the pump house draws water up through vessels as thick as a torso, and the pulse pushes it to the factory, and the pistons of green wood rise and fall, and the flywheel of cut root turns, and bales of night swell and seal and drop. The beetles carry them, one to a beetle, out along the lichen lines to the lamps. The pods open. The darkness deepens. The townsfolk go on existing for another day.

It is a company village. It always was. The company built houses and a chapel and a pump house and a factory, and the workers worked in the factory so that they could live in the houses, and the factory made something that was sold so the company could go on building. In Fennick the loop has closed. The factory makes the only thing its people are made of. The beetles are not the workers of Fennick. They are the bulb's way of keeping the workers alive after the workers are gone.

## The Moths

Of the moths that spiral into the darkness and do not come out, the explanation is simpler than it first seems, and sadder.

Run your palm down the sleeve of the woman with the seed buttons and your fingertips will find, now and then, a softness in the weave: a patch where the coarse wool gives way to something fine and dusty, like the powder that comes off a moth's wing on a windowpane. The moths are not destroyed. They are used. The bulb remembers wool, but it cannot grow wool. It has no sheep in its memory, only the coats. So it draws the moths, pale and soft-bodied, into the dark, and there they become lint and nap and the fuzz on a cuff. This is why their numbers never decline. They are not a population. They are a material, like the lichen, like the seeds.

In every other world, moths eat wool. In Fennick it is the other way around.

## The Snails

The snails are not residents either, though they carry themselves like it. They are something closer to proprietors, or perhaps assessors.

Watch one move down the high street, a creature the size of a sheep advancing at the speed of a shadow on a sundial, and you will see its eyestalks sweep slowly over each house it passes, pausing, considering. When it reaches the edge of town and the bare ribs of a not-yet-house, it stops for a long time. Then it begins to eat.

It does not eat everything. It eats what is wrong. Watch closely over the days it takes, and you will see it leave a doorframe standing but take the lintel above, or eat one wall entirely and leave its neighbor. When the bulb regrows the house a few doors down, the new one is different. It is simpler, yes, the way the chairs are simpler, but sometimes it is also more correct, as though the snail had corrected a draft. Once, over a long season, the snails ate the same half-house eleven times, and the twelfth version had a bay window that none of the previous eleven had possessed. It was the only bay window in Fennick. The snails have left it alone ever since.

They rest against the chapel for weeks with their eyestalks drawn in. It looks like sleep, but it may be listening.

## The Chapel

Everything in a bulb is for one thing. The onion, the corm, the tulip bulb in a paper sack in a cold shed: they are storage, starch laid down against the winter, and all of that swollen patience is spent in the spring on a single act. The bulb sends up a stalk and opens a flower.

The steeple of Fennick is a flower bud. It is sheathed in a protective leaf, green and tapering, and the sheath splits a little further every day. Everything else in the town, the streets and the houses and the factory and its pulse, is starch. It is a store. The bulb has spent the whole of itself laying down a memory in the shape of a village the way a tulip lays down sugar, and it is spending that store, slowly, on what is growing inside the chapel.

What is growing there sings. It is a low, slow, hymn-shaped exhalation, rising and falling across a span of hours. People who have pressed their ears to the chapel's translucent walls describe it differently. Some hear a congregation, many voices blurred into one. Some hear an organ with most of its stops pulled in. The ones who stayed longest, whose skin had begun to tighten, all describe the same thing: a single voice, an old one, a woman's, singing to herself the way people sing when they are doing something else with their hands, peeling potatoes or darning, not performing, just keeping herself company in a quiet house.

Here is what may be true, pieced together from the signs.

The signs on every corner are grown in leaf-vein lettering, and the street names are never the same twice: *Colliery Row* becomes *Collier Row* becomes *Calloway*, as though heard once and never caught. Only one word is always perfectly spelled: *FENNICK*. It is spelled perfectly in every instance, on every sign, in the same careful hand.

It is possible Fennick was never the name of the village.

Roots drink. A bulb in a wet country, in a shallow basin, drinks what soaks down through the soil. If there was a company village somewhere above, somewhere upslope and upstream and long ago, it had a chapel, and a chapel has a yard, and a yard has graves. The smell of coal smoke and boiled cabbage and wool and coughing did not soak down from a whole village. It soaked down from one person, laid in the ground in her good coat, who remembered her whole life in the village the way everyone does at the end, as a place full of people she knew by the sound of their boots.

The bulb took her up. It could not keep her body, so it kept what she held. It grew her village the way she remembered it, which is to say not quite correctly, with the street names half-gone and the chairs in the parlors reduced to the idea of a chair. It grew the people she knew as the spaces they had stood in, as a cough, as a pair of sleeves always touching, as a child humming a hymn it had heard its grandmother sing. It grew the one word she never lost, and put it on every corner.

And in the chapel, at the center of the store, it is growing her.

This is why the beetles avoid the chapel: they are the bulb's hands, and hands do not go into the room where the work is being done. This is why the snails rest against it. They are measuring how far along she is, and how much more of the town must be eaten and regrown and simplified before there is enough of her. This is why the townsfolk grow thinner. She is forgetting them as she arrives. The store is being spent. When the cough has lost its catch, when the woman has no buttons left, when the last chair is a single stalk, the sheath on the steeple will split all the way down.

No one knows what will stand in the doorway when it does. The flower of a bulb is not the bulb. It is brief, and it is for something else: it is how the bulb makes seed.

## The New Streets

There is one more resident, and it is the one visitors are least eager to hear about.

The hollow grows. New streets bud off the old ones every season, and most of them are what you would expect: another terrace, another lane of pale translucent houses, another corner sign spelling *FENNICK*. But not all of them.

On the western edge, where distance goes strange and the walk that should take three minutes takes twenty, there is a short lane that ends in an iron railing. Beyond the railing there is nothing but the curve of the bulb's wall in the dark, but the railing is the kind that runs along a seafront, painted in pigment that has risen to a municipal green, and at its base the lichen grows in a pattern like the tideline of shingle. There is no sea in the memory of a company village in a wet inland country. Someone else brought that.

There is a house off the square whose roof is not pinecone bracts but something like thatch, and inside it, grown in the center of the empty room, is not a chair or a kettle but a child's wooden horse on wheels, with a string. It is the most detailed object in Fennick. It has not been simplified yet.

There is a sign, only one, at the corner of a very new street, and beneath *FENNICK* in the leaf-vein lettering a second word has begun to form. It is not yet legible. It is not the same on any two days. It looks like a name.

Visitors who stay describe the tightness in the skin as not unpleasant. It is like the moment after a cold wash, when everything feels drawn and new. They describe a slowing of the breath until it falls into step with the pulse, one breath for every *thum*, and then one breath for every two. They describe feeling their own memories as a kind of weight, the way a full bulb must feel, the way a sack of darkness is heavier than it should be. They say that when they finally stood in the pool under the six lamps and let the townsfolk brush against them, the townsfolk leaned, very slightly, in their direction. It was the first thing any of them had ever done.

Some of those visitors left. Some of them may have left.

## Who Lives There

So: the beetles work there. The moths are spent there. The bats hang in the empty rooms like the single objects the bulb has grown, and hunt, and keep the moths from multiplying past what the coats require. The snails keep the accounts. The townsfolk are kept, like a coal fire banked overnight, in darkness manufactured daily for no other purpose. The thing in the chapel is arriving, and has been for a long time, and is almost here.

And the bulb, which has no eyes and no mind and no memory of its own, only the ones it drinks, lives there most of all. It lives the way any bulb lives: patiently, underground, swelling a fraction larger once or twice a day with a long creak you can hear in the dome, storing everything against a spring that it is certain will come.

When you leave, walking back along the thinning lichen lines toward the seam in the south face, the pulse fades from shin to heel to nothing. The air warms. The felted pewter leaves push aside, and there is the low wet basin and the ordinary grey rain.

Put your palm flat against the skin beneath the leaves. Wait eleven seconds.

You will feel it. And if you have stayed long enough, you will feel, just after it, very faintly, from somewhere much too deep in the mound to be your own wrist, the beat that answers.
