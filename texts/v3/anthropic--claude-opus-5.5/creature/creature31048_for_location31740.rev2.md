# Who Lives in the Lowworks

## The First Answer, Which Is Wrong

The easy answer is that nothing does. The plants grow, the machines run, and the rock is made, but none of that is living *in* a place. It is only happening there. A fern in the Lowworks has no home. It has a crack it was in for an hour before a jolt tore it loose and another crack took it. The moss has no history of territory, only coverage. The trees pry the basalt apart, but they do it the way water finds a slope. The plants are weather that has learned to photosynthesize.

The account of the basin says that nothing lives here that moves, because an animal needs a yesterday.

That is almost true. Stay long enough and the *almost* becomes the most important thing about the place.

## The Ones Who Last One Stroke

Start at the largest engine house, where the water is so loud it is cloudy.

The bubbles there, the ones that appear from nowhere in the troughs of the loudest pressure waves, are not random. Watch through a hundred strokes of the beam and you begin to see it. On each downstroke, in the lee of the boiler, the bubbles gather into a shape. It holds for exactly as long as the piston takes to fall. It has something like a bent back, something like a long head, and at the ends of two limbs a spray of finer bubbles, fizzing and articulate, that behaves like hands.

And it is always *doing* something.

On one stroke it lays its fizzing palm flat against the boiler plate, as if feeling for a fault. On the next, a new one in the same place pulls a strand of vine out of the governor's spinning weights. On the next, one turns a valve wheel a quarter turn and lets go. Then the beam rises, the pressure comes back, and the figure collapses into itself. Every bubble in it implodes at once. That is the sound: gravel poured on sheet iron. The shriek of cavitation that fills the basin is these figures dying, a few hundred times an hour, at every engine, forever.

Call them the tenders. There is no better word, and they would not answer to any.

A tender's life lasts one stroke. It arrives complete. It does not orient itself or look around. It already knows which valve, which vine, which bolt, the way a silt pattern on a flat stone already knows the pitch of the engine. The knowing is in the *shape*. A tender is a standing wave that has taken the form of a worker, and its purpose is written into it the way a note is written into a string. It cannot remember what the last tender did, because it was not there. It cannot learn. So it never gets better at its work, and it never gets worse, and it never grows tired of it.

This is the first thing that lives in the Lowworks. It is the only way anything *could*: whole from its first instant and gone before it has anything to remember.

## The Different Kinds

Each machine makes its own tenders, the way each instrument makes its own overtones.

The crane's chain, swinging and stuttering past the same point three times in one arc, makes long thin tenders strung out along the links. They climb hand over hand toward the foam and never arrive, because each lasts only one pass of the hook. But each climbs a little way from where the last one would have been, so the climbing is continuous even though no single climber ever gets far.

The live cables make tenders of light rather than bubbles. They are branching and quick, shaped like the lightning-grown plants around them, and they appear at the frays in the insulation. They live for the length of an arc, a fraction of a second, and in that time they splice. The cables are frayed everywhere. The cables have also never failed. Those two facts are the work of creatures that last a fifth of a heartbeat.

The sideways chimney makes tenders that are hardly there at all: faint thickenings in its grey ribbon, a half-mile long and hair-thin, that lie across the tilted current like a hand laid on a sleeper's back. Nobody can say what they do. They may hold the ribbon's direction steady, so that the east stays *up* for the trees that lean toward it.

The pumps make the most tenders and the smallest. They swarm the intake grilles in glittering clouds, clearing leaves, and they die in the thousands every second. The cavitation roar at the intakes, which you feel in your teeth, is a nursery and a graveyard that are the same place at the same moment.

## The One Thing They All Do

Watch long enough and you notice something the tenders do that is not maintenance.

Each one, at some point in its single beat, turns its head.

The long bubble-head of the boiler tenders, the leading link of the chain climbers, the forking tip of the cable sparks: every tender in the basin, at every machine, spends some small part of its only instant of existence orienting toward the same place. It isn't a glance. They have no eyes and nothing to glance with. It is more like the way the ferns bend toward the intake grilles, a pull obeyed.

They turn toward the south end of the rift, where the rail line goes down into the deepest fissure.

## The Rail

The wagons on the rail line look abandoned. They are rust-red and vine-wrapped, loaded with basalt blocks. They are not abandoned.

They move one wagon-length each time the floor jolts. You would never see it unless you marked a wheel and waited. The whole train is creeping south and down, toward the fissure, carrying fresh-cut basalt: sharp-edged, hexagonal, the same stone the rift makes of itself. At the fissure's lip the wagons tip. The blocks go down into the orange glow. Then the empty wagons are back at the head of the line, having always been there, because near the rail the time stutter is heavy, and the train is a loop.

Something down there needs stone. Something is being walled in, or held up, or given a lip to press against.

## The Jolts

This is the point where the basin's disorder turns inside out.

Count the jolts. They are not tectonic, whatever the cracks and the falling columns suggest. They come at intervals that shorten and lengthen and shorten again, the way certain intervals do. Each one begins at the south end, in the deepest fissure, and rolls north through the floor. Each one opens the rock, and then the rock settles and the green closes over the wound.

Consider everything else in that light. The water is bath-warm. The light is dim and brownish, filtered, and comes from everywhere at once. The great thudding at the center of everything beats just below the pace of a heart. You feel it in your sternum before you hear it. The pillow lava *muscles* out of the vents, lobe after lobe, straining and splitting its black skin. And there is a flood that came down the rift, that is always coming down the rift, and that fills the basin without ever finishing, the way a certain kind of water breaks and keeps breaking.

The Lowworks is not only a factory. It is a labor.

## The One in the Deepest Fissure

Go down the rail line, and do not go far, because the heat below the lip is enormous and the glow turns from orange to yellow.

The fissure's walls are packed with the delivered basalt, block laid on block like the lining of a kiln. At the bottom, against the fresh-built lip, there is a curve of something that is not rock.

It is pale like the tree trunks, like wet paper, like new bark that has never been rained on. That comparison is about as far as the eye will go. It is very large. It is pressed into the narrowest point of the fissure. And when the floor jolts, *it* is what jolts. It pushes. The walls crack. The whole basin rings with the knock for several seconds.

Then time closes.

The stutter is not strongest at the engines. The engines make it, but they don't keep it. Map the machines and you find what nobody would notice from inside the chaos: every clearing, every outflow pipe, every sagging cable, every pylon runs toward this fissure. The Lowworks is a grid, and this is where it points. The loop that makes a flower open, close, and open again by the pumping station is a spill, a leak from the real work going on down here. The real loop is tight and long-practiced, a few seconds wide, and it has been repeating since before the rock was rock. It holds this one pale curve at the single moment of arriving.

This is the one who lives in the Lowworks.

It is an animal. It needs no yesterday, because it is at the one moment in any animal's life that has no yesterday behind it. It has not yet been born. It is not unborn either. It is *being born*: all pressure and heat and pounding noise, the moment with no before, which is also the only moment that can be made again and again without wearing out. It is the reason nothing else here moves. There cannot be a second animal until there has been a first, and the first has not finished arriving.

It feels, if it feels, what anything feels at that moment: squeezed, hot, deafened, pushing toward something it cannot picture. It has felt this for as long as the Lowworks has existed, and it has felt it for the first time on every one of those occasions.

## What the Engines Are For

The account of the basin couldn't settle why the engines run: whether they were built to drain the basin and never managed it, or whether they make the rain, and the rain fills the basin, and the basin is what the engines are for.

Down here the answer is simple, and sad.

Nothing in the Lowworks is dry. Above the sea there is only more water, falling. There is no air anywhere, no layer, no pocket. And whatever is arriving at the bottom of the deepest fissure will need to take a breath.

The engines were built to make one dry place. They pump the sea up through iron pipes wide enough to walk through, up past the canopy, up past the foam, to clear a space in the sky where a first breath could be taken. And the water they pump comes down as rain, because it has nowhere else to go, and the basin stays full, and the pumps pump harder, and the rain gets thicker. The labor cannot be allowed to finish into a world where the first breath would drown. So the machines hold it in the moment before, with pistons and furnaces and live cables. They hold it the way you might hold your hand over a door you have not finished building a room behind.

The only dry things in the whole basin are the cavitation bubbles, the tiny tearing voids that appear in the troughs of the loudest sound. The tenders are made of them. Every tender is a breath's worth of emptiness, made by the engines at enormous cost, shaped into a worker for one stroke, spent on a valve or a vine, and collapsed. The Lowworks makes air. It just can't make enough of it at once, and it can't make any of it last.

## The Tenders' Shape

Here is a thing that is easy to see once you have seen the curve in the fissure, and impossible to prove.

The tenders are not all one shape. The boiler tenders are hunched and heavy, the chain tenders long, the cable tenders branching. But under the differences there is a family resemblance, the way a sketch resembles a finished painting. The long head. The bent back. The spray of fine bubbles at the ends of the limbs.

It is possible that the engines are not only holding the one in the fissure. They may be *rehearsing* it. At each machine, one beat at a time, they sketch in sound whatever it would grow into, and set that sketch to doing the work its grown self would one day do, the tending and splicing and climbing. Each sketch lives just long enough to turn its head toward the original, and then it is gone.

Or the tenders are the shape of whoever built the Lowworks. They are reproduced beat by beat because a builder who remembered would not be able to stand it, and one who does not remember can keep working forever.

Or those are the same possibility.

## The Plants, Again

With the fissure in mind, the green looks different.

The leaves are unbitten because nothing has been born to bite them. The moss is thick enough to swallow a hand to the wrist, and no hand has ever pressed it. The growth isn't reclaiming anything. It is *keeping pace*, and now you can see with what. It is keeping pace with an appetite that hasn't arrived. The basin is overstocked the way a larder is overstocked before a long winter. Every fern that uncoils, every vine that wraps a fallen column by nightfall, is provision laid down against a first hunger. None of the hunger has been spent yet, so the plants keep piling up.

The whole Lowworks, rock and green and iron, is a nursery that has been furnished forever and never used.

## If It Were Born

Imagine the pumps winning. Imagine one pocket of air, somewhere above the foam, that the rain didn't refill.

The loop in the fissure would open. The pale curve would push, and this time the push would go through. There would be a first breath, and at that moment the basin would have its first yesterday: *the moment before the breath*. Everything else would follow from that one past moment. The flood would finish arriving. The water level would, for the first time, be something that had happened. The rock would begin, very slowly, to wear. There would be a track in the silt, then another. A leaf would be bitten. Something would eventually die here, and there would be bones, and the Lowworks would become an ordinary place, a place with a history, where animals could live because there was a past to live out of.

The engines would slow. The tenders would stop appearing, or they would appear once and stay, carrying a memory of the one stroke they had lived, and have to learn to live with it.

Maybe that is what the machines are working toward. Maybe it is what they are working to prevent. The tenders don't know. They exist for one beat, spend it on a valve and a turned head, and collapse with a sound like gravel on iron.

## Who Lives Here

So there are two answers, and they are the same answer.

Countless things live in the Lowworks, each for the length of one piston stroke: whole from their first instant, sure of their work, made of the only air in the world, and dead before they can wonder why they keep turning south.

And one thing lives in the Lowworks. It has lived there its whole life. Its whole life is the space of one breath it has not yet been allowed to take, at the bottom of a flood that is still coming in, in the warm and the dark and the deafening pulse of engines that are trying, with all their iron, to make it somewhere dry to be born.
