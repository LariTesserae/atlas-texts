# Those Who Keep the Upper Reach

## The First Answer

Ask who lives in the Reach and the first answer is people. It is not the whole answer, but it is true.

They are called fettlers. The word comes from the foundries below the cloud, where it means the work of cleaning a casting: knocking off the sprue, grinding the seams, filing away the flashing so the piece can be handled without cutting the hand. Up here the word has grown. It is still their work, but it has also become their name, their manner, and something close to their faith.

There are not many of them. A few hundred, perhaps, spread across a plateau the size of a county. No one has ever counted, and counting is a thing the fettlers take seriously, so the lack of a number is deliberate. They say a thing counted in the Reach tends to come around again. It is better not to know how many of you there are, in case the number turns out larger than it should be.

You meet them first as glints. Sound dies at fifty paces, but light carries forever, doubled off every fresh surface. So the fettlers talk with their hands and with shards of flashing held up to the white sky. A ridge that seemed empty will flicker, three short and a long, and a mile off another flicker answers. They have a whole language of it, slow and exact, made mostly of nouns. *Water here. Ibex, six. New pour opening, east saddle, soon.* There is no word in glint-speech for *I*. When a fettler needs to say where they are, they flash the name of the nearest peak, and the peak's name does the work.

## How They Look

They are thin, and brown where the light has struck them, which is everywhere. Most have the squint, the permanent narrowing of eyes that forgot how to open fully. Their hands are the most remarkable thing about them: broad, calloused into a kind of horn along the edge of the palm, the fingertips polished smooth by years of running along seams to feel where the flashing is thickest. A fettler reads a cliff the way the blind read raised script. Their faces are usually turned slightly away from you, because their hands are usually on the rock.

They wear cloth, and cloth is the most precious thing in the Reach. It cannot be cast. It is woven, and weaving is irregular, and every thread in it wanders a little. Fettlers carry the cloth up through the cloud in trade and wear it until it is rags. Then they tie the rags into their hair, or knot them onto the handles of their files, so that some unrepeated thing is always touching them.

This is the first rule of living here, and they will tell it to you before they tell you their names. *Keep a flaw.*

Every fettler has one on purpose. A crooked stitch in a sleeve. A nick filed into the left thumbnail and kept fresh. A string of beads with one bead the wrong colour. One old woman on the northern gantries walks with a deliberate hitch, a half-step on every ninth pace. She says she took it up when she was young and has never let it drop. Around her the ibex walk in perfect, repeating rhythm and the crows sit in perfect, repeating rows. She limps among them, and she is plainly herself.

The fettlers explain it simply. The Reach makes things that are alike. If you are alike enough, it may start making you.

## How They Live

They live in the gaps. There is nowhere else to live. Every surface belongs to the works, so the fettlers hang cloth between scaffolds and sleep in the hollows under stairways that climb nowhere. They cook in the mouths of dry culverts and store their water in the pebbled pits of slopes they have learned to trust. They build nothing. The one time anyone tried, laying a low wall of loose flashing to break the wind that never blows, a small mold was clamped over it within the season. When it opened, the wall had been poured again, perfect and seamed and faintly warm. Nobody slept behind it.

They eat ibex, which are real meat all the way through, lean and sweet and faintly mineral. The bones are seamed, though. Crack a thighbone and it splits cleanly down the middle into two halves, as if it had always meant to. They brew the gutter-moss into a bitter tea. They eat the young fronds of the ferns in the collecting-tanks, and they eat them carefully, taking only fronds that have grown in spirals. The straight ones, they say, belong to something else.

Their main work is the flashing. They walk the ridges with files and rasps and small chisels, taking down the translucent crests where they stand proudest. They carry the fragments in sacks and send them down through the cloud, where the flashing sells as a curiosity, a glass that is not glass. Some of it they keep for glint-speech. Some of it they keep for reasons they give at different lengths depending on how well they know you.

The short reason is money. The long reason comes later.

## The Counters

Every group of fettlers has one or two who no longer file. These are the counters, and they are usually the oldest.

A counter keeps the ledger of repeats. It is a practice more than a book, though some counters do keep books, whole shelves of them in the dry culverts, bound in cloth. It records which peak has been seen where. It records the notch in the flashing on the third summit east of the long saddle, and the four other places that same notch has since appeared. It records which crow sat at the end of which cable, and whether it was the crow. It records which ibex lay down on which warm casting, on which day, and did not get up.

The counters are trying to find out whether there are many of each thing or one of each thing moving. They have been trying for generations. Their answer, spoken aloud, is always *not yet*. Asked privately, some of them will admit they have stopped believing the question has a single answer. Some things in the Reach, they think, are many. Some are one. Some are one on their way to becoming many, and some are many slowly settling into one. The ledgers are not meant to settle it. They are meant to keep the fettlers from forgetting that the question is there.

## The Seam

The fettlers do not like to talk about the seam.

It comes late, if it comes at all. After ten years or twenty in the Reach, a fettler will wake and find a fine pale line running from the notch of the throat down the breastbone. It is not a scar and it does not hurt. It is smooth to the touch, a little cooler than the skin around it, and it does not fade.

Most fettlers who find it go down through the cloud and do not come back. A few stay.

Those who stay change slowly. Their shadows begin to lag. At first it is only a little: a heartbeat, the time it takes to lower a file. Then it is more. Their squint eases, because their eyes stop minding the light. Their voices carry a few paces less, as if something porous has begun to grow in their throats. In the last years, the seam will sometimes show a little flashing. It is a faint, glassy rim, finer than a fingernail, standing proud along the breastbone.

What the other fettlers do then is the most private thing in the Reach, and the tenderest. They fettle each other. They sit close in the bright white hush, forehead nearly to forehead, since that is the only way to be heard, and with the finest files they own they take down the flashing on a friend's breast. Slowly. A little each week. They send none of it down through the cloud. They keep it. It is part of the long reason.

The second rule is the only one the fettlers enforce with force. *Never lie down on a warm casting.* The ibex do it, and the fettlers have watched what follows. The seam closes, and after some days there is only rock, with an ibex-shaped swell in it that the lichen soon learns to follow. Every so often an old fettler with a long seam walks out onto a fresh ridge, still steaming from the report, and the others know what it means. They do not drag the old one back. They follow, and sit a little way off on the cool rock. They flash the name of the peak, over and over, so that everyone across the Reach knows where it happened. They stay until they can no longer tell which swell in the rock was their friend.

The counters record it. Some years later, sometimes, the counters record something else. A fettler is seen on a far ridge with a hitch in her step every ninth pace, and the old woman who limped like that has been rock for a decade. The counters write it down, underline it, and do not say anything.

## The Foundlings

Children are rarely born in the Reach. But children are sometimes found.

They turn up in the pipe-bundles, curled among the hares. Once or twice they have been found in the doorless buildings, after a fettler lost patience with the scratching and took a chisel to the wall. They are always small, perhaps three or four years old by their size, and always pale, the fine white grain of fresh casting. Each has a seam from crown to tailbone, and sometimes a little flashing still on it like the ibex.

They do not squint. They never have. They open their eyes on the total white light as if it were the only light there is, and for them it is.

The fettlers take them in. There has never been a question of doing otherwise. The foundlings grow up at fettlers' fires and learn glint-speech and eat ibex with everyone else. They are quiet children. They are quiet even by the standard of a place where shouting dies at fifty paces. They seem to hear things the others don't. A foundling will lift her head and turn toward a mold-half on the horizon, the way the crows do, and say the pour is moving. Nobody else can see it move. A week later, when someone goes to check, the slurry in the sprue has dropped by an arm's length.

Every foundling is given a flaw on the day they are found. It is a small nick in the ear, or a single knotted thread tied about the wrist, never taken off. The fettlers are gentle about it and absolutely firm. The foundlings accept it without complaint. Most of them, when they are older, add flaws of their own, more than a fettler would wear. One young man on the eastern pipeworks has a hundred tiny irregularities he has given himself: a burn, a braid, a filed tooth, a word he always mispronounces. When asked why, he says he does not want to be poured again. He says it pleasantly, the way a person might say they don't want to catch cold.

Here is what the counters have noticed about the foundlings. Their shadows do not lag. Sometimes, if you watch very closely, they arrive a little early.

## The Slow Ones

Sit still in the Reach for a day and you will see the fettlers. Sit still for a week and you may see the Slow Ones.

Mark a spot on a far gantry. The fettlers do this with a little cairn of flashing, so it catches the light. Look at it for a day and nothing seems there. Come back in seven days. Something tall and iron-dark will be standing a few paces from where it was not.

They are the same black iron as the mold-halves, ribbed like them and bolted like them at the joints. They are perhaps twice a man's height. They move so slowly that to any eye they are standing still. But over the weeks they cross whole valleys. They climb to cracked cliffs and worn ridges. When they leave, the scaffolds are up and a small repair mold has been clamped over the damage.

These are the ones who keep the Reach young. No fettler has seen one move. No fettler has seen one arrive, only *have arrived*. The fettlers do not approach them. That is not a rule. It simply seems wrong, the way it would seem wrong to step between a sleeper and their dream.

A few have looked closely, from the side and at a respectful distance, and they say the same thing. The Slow Ones have no faces. Where a face should be there is a hollow. It is deep and smooth and precisely shaped, the concave impression of a face, like the inside of a mask. The whole of a Slow One is like that on the front: a hollow. It is a mold of someone. It is the other half of a person.

Here the fettlers disagree, and the disagreement is old and gentle and never settled. Some say the Slow Ones are simply the Reach's hands, and the hollows in them are only the shape a hand has when it holds. Others say more quietly that each Slow One is the negative of someone who came up through the cloud long ago. They say it is a fettler, one of the first, cast inward so that the Reach could go on keeping a copy after the person was gone.

One old counter swears she has seen her own grandmother's face in the inward curve of a Slow One on the western saddle. She means the deep hollow of the eye sockets, the particular slant of the cheek. She visits it every season. She sits in front of it, well back, and talks to it, forehead toward it, though she knows it cannot hear at that distance and would not hear at any speed she could speak. She says she does not mind. She says her grandmother was a slow listener too.

## The Range Above

When the mist burns off from the high saddle and the inverted range shows itself overhead, the fettlers stop work and look up. The foundlings do not. The foundlings never seem surprised by it.

Up there, pale and far, sprues pointing down, is the Reach again. It has the same gantries and the same ridges and the same doorless buildings. If you have good eyes, and the fettlers' squint is very good at long distance, you can sometimes see figures on the ridges. They are small and dark, walking soles-upward along the flashing. They carry sacks. They stop and raise their hands to the light, and the light flashes.

The fettlers glint back at them. They always have. They do it the way people wave at a passing ship: not expecting an answer, but not wanting to be the kind of person who doesn't.

Once in a great while, the glints from above make words.

The counters record every instance and do not agree on what was said. The words are always simple nouns, the kind glint-speech is made of. *Water. Ibex. Pour.* Sometimes a peak's name. Once, famously, the name of the very peak the fettlers were standing on. At that, every fettler present looked down at their own shadow and saw it standing a little behind them, still looking up.

This is the belief the fettlers hold most loosely and least willingly give up. The range above is not a reflection. It is the first pour. Everything in it came first, and everything down here is cast from it: the peaks, the crows, the ibex, perhaps the fettlers themselves. Your shadow lags because it is still attached, faintly, to the one above whose shape you were taken from. That one moves first. You are a heartbeat behind. You always have been.

The foundlings' shadows lead, a little. No one has worked out what that means. The foundlings, asked, only look up at the pale range and don't say.

## The One Expected

All of this is true. The fettlers live here, and the foundlings, and the Slow Ones, and the ibex and the crows and the hares. But when you ask a counter who *lives* in the Reach, the answer that comes last is different.

*No one yet.*

Consider the towns, they say. Every building is finished. Every roof is orderly, and smoke rises from every chimney, and every door is solid. The windows are cast in with the walls. These are not ruins. They are not failures. They were made ahead of need, the way a room is made up for a guest who has not arrived. Consider the doorless buildings, where the scratching is. Consider the stairways that climb nowhere. They climb to where something will stand, when it is standing.

Consider the mold-halves on the horizon, the shut hands. Most of them open in time, but not all. There is one far to the north, beyond the last ridges anyone has walked. It is dark and immense, larger than any range, and the ribs on its iron are the size of valleys. No counter's ledger records it opening. No counter's ledger records it being poured. It was already shut when the first fettler came up through the cloud. The mist stands along its clamps all year round. And the foundlings, when they turn their heads toward the slow movement of a pour, turn toward it more often than toward anything else.

The Reach, the fettlers say, is a house being fitted out. Everything in it is fittings. The ibex are its first grazing. The crows are its first watchers. The swifts are a flock it has been given in advance. The ferns grow in spirals because they have not yet been told which way the light will come from, once the light is coming from somewhere particular. The water runs uphill now and then because it has not yet learned who it runs toward. Even the fettlers are fittings. They were tolerated and useful, and they were allowed, for a while, to keep their flaws.

And the fettlers have their long reason now, for filing down the flashing along the ridges. Not the money. They do it so that when the last hand opens, whatever steps out will not cut itself. When it walks the Reach it will find the edges trimmed and safe beneath its feet. Someone will have been here before it, carefully, with small tools and knotted rags, making the place fit to be lived in.

Nobody knows its shape. Nobody knows whether it will be kind. Some of the young ones, the ones who came up through the cloud only lately, ask whether it is right to prepare a house for a stranger this way, whether this is service or only habit. The old fettlers do not argue. They hand the young ones a file and point them at a ridge. They say the flashing is sharp either way, and a trimmed edge is a kindness to anyone who walks on it, including you.

The flashing they have taken from each other's breasts, over all the years, they keep in a single dry culvert under the high saddle. It is a heap of glass that is not glass, taken from the seams of people who became rock or went down through the cloud or are still here, filing. No one has said what it is for. But when the far mold opens, if it ever does, the fettlers mean to lay it out along the path. It will shine in the white light, a trail of small irregular gleams, none of them quite like the others. Whatever comes out will see that someone lived here first. It was many someones, flawed and particular, and every one of them different.

## What Lives There

So: people live in the Reach, thinly, between the scaffolds. They talk in light and keep their flaws and are kind to one another in a hush that swallows every shout. Pale children live there, found in the pipes, who hear the pour and cast their shadows early. Great hollow keepers live there too, moving at the speed of stone. They are carrying, perhaps, the inward faces of the dead.

Above, upside down, there is someone like you who moved a heartbeat before you did.

And somewhere in the north, in the largest dark, something has not yet begun to live there. Everything else is waiting for it, carefully, or has simply been made in its shape.

In the mornings the whole Reach sweats, and the fettlers go out along the ridges with their files. The iron ticks. The water drips. On a far gantry a small cairn of flashing catches the light, marking where a Slow One was, or is. An old woman on the northern catwalks takes her half-step on the ninth pace, and the crows in their rows turn their heads, all at once, toward the north. Then they turn back.

Twice in a season, maybe, a report comes. It is a single deep crack that seems to sound inside your chest. Every fettler in the Reach stops, file lifted, and looks north.

So far it has never been that one. They lower their files and go back to the flashing. The quiet seals over.
