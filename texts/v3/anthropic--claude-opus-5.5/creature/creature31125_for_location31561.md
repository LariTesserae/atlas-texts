# Who Lives at the Wet Edge

## The Cutters, at First Glance

Begin with the obvious. People live here, and at first they look like people anywhere.

Look longer and the stone shows on them. There is pale dust in the creases of every elbow and knuckle, in the lines around the eyes, in the part of a child's hair. It never fully washes out. It dries on the skin as a faint bloom, the same grey-white the old walls wear, so that the older a person is, the more they seem to have been left out in the air. Their nails are rimmed white. Their palms are strange: soft and pale and water-wrinkled from long mornings at wet faces, yet hard as horn in the particular places where a pick-haft or graver rests. You could read a person's trade from their hands the way you read a cutter's pause in the chisel furrows. A sawyer's calluses run across the fingers. A graver's thumb is flattened and turned slightly outward. The wet-hide women have forearms stained faintly brown from the tannin of the hides they soak and hang.

They go barefoot, or in soft hide wrapped at the ankle. They walk the way people walk on stairs they have known all their lives, without looking down, and they make almost no noise doing it. They are quiet in body as well as voice. They turn sideways to pass each other on the ledges without breaking stride. They hand things up and down the terraces in chains, a jar passing from palm to palm up six levels without a word spoken, and it comes to look less like cooperation than like breathing.

They speak low. You notice this immediately and keep noticing it. When they laugh, they laugh into their hands or into their shoulders, and the laughter seems to go into the stone and stay there.

## Soft, Setting, Set

Their words for age are stone words.

A child is *soft*. That is not an insult. It is the highest thing you can say about any material. Soft means workable, promising, still becoming. Mothers say it over sleeping infants the way other people say *beautiful*. A young person, somewhere between twelve and twenty, is *setting*: taking on a shape, beginning to resist, not yet fixed. It is understood to be a dangerous and precious age, and the city is gentle with it. Adults are *set*. The very old, the ones whose joints have stiffened and whose voices have gone thin and clear, are said to *ring*, like the upper terraces under a knuckle.

These words aren't metaphors to them, or not only. They believe, in a practical and unmystical way, that a person is formed by what is done to them while they are soft, and that there comes a point after which the shape is kept. So children are handled constantly: held, carried, taught, corrected, sung to in whispers. Nothing said to a child is thought to be lost. Anything cut into a soft face will still be there when the face rings. Adults are careful about what they say near infants, for the same reason they are careful about a stray pick-mark on a wall they mean to keep.

*Before it sets* is said to a child who hasn't yet learned a skill, to a young couple who are circling each other, to an old man who hasn't forgiven his brother. It isn't said harshly. It's said the way a cutter says it to an apprentice looking at a face drying in the noon sun: *there is still time, and it is going.*

## Names

When a child is born, its name is cut into the wall of the room where it was born, on the fresh wet face at the back, at the height of a mother's hand. There is no writing here as a scholar would understand it. There is a vocabulary of marks: strokes, notches, fish-scale clusters, the particular diagonal of one lineage's graver, the looped channel another family uses. A name is a small arrangement of these, cut by the grandmother if she still has the hands for it.

Then the household moves on, as households do, downward with the wet edge. The room hardens. The name hardens with it. By the time the child is setting, the mark of its name is cream-colored and firm. By the time the child is set, it is biscuit-colored and permanent and holding someone else's grain. By the time that person rings, their name is up near the rim, in stone no one could carve if they tried, and they can point to it.

So everyone in the city is written somewhere above where they are standing. Each person lives below their own name. They can look up at it. It is a strange thing to grow up with, a record of yourself that becomes more fixed the more you change. The city does not seem to find it strange. They say the name is where you were soft. You, the living person, are wherever the wet is now.

## Wall-Kin

Families here are households, and households are rooms, and rooms are defined by what's around them. So kinship runs through walls.

There is a word for the family on the other side of a partition. It means something like *wall-kin* or *the ones you share stone with*. You hear them all your life. You know when their baby teethes, when their old woman coughs, what they argue about in whispers. You have never been able not to know. Wall-kin are in some ways closer than blood kin, and in some ways more carefully held at a distance. There's an etiquette of not-hearing that is learned very young: you do not repeat what came through the wall. You do not answer it. You behave as though the stone were thicker than it is. That courteous fiction is the foundation of the whole society.

When a wall is breached, when a pick goes through by accident, or the stone was thinner than it looked, the two households become something else. They share the opening. They are *breached*, and breached families are bound. They eat together on feast days. Their children marry, or are forbidden to marry because they're too close. Their dead are laid in adjoining niches. Some of the oldest and most respected lineages in the city trace themselves to a single ancient breach, a hole in a wall that has long since hardened into a doorway, now up in the tan stone and used as a dovecote. People make small pilgrimages to it.

There are those who say that some breaches were not accidents.

## The Wet-Keepers

Within the city, one group of people is harder to see than the rest, because their work is quiet even here.

They are the wet-keepers. They tend moisture. They walk the working edge before dawn with their palms flat on the stone, feeling for the cold that means water still in the pores. They tap the faces with a knuckle or a small bone rod and listen, and they can tell by the tone, a dull thud or a faint beginning of ring, how many days a face has before it's too hard to work. They decide where the hides go and where the seep basins are filled. They cut the thumb-wide channels that thread the walls. Every one of those thousands of small grooves was laid out by someone who read the stone and guessed where the water would want to go and gently persuaded it.

They are mostly women, though not only. They are said to have cold hands. Their skin is the palest in the city, from years of touching wet rock in the dark, and their hearing is said to be extraordinary. A wet-keeper standing at the bottom of the pit is supposed to be able to hear which channel is running dry three terraces up. They talk less than anyone. When they do speak, it's usually a single judgment: *this face*, *not this one*, *tomorrow*, *now*.

Nobody commands them, exactly. But nothing is cut that they have not touched first.

## The Chisel-Bearers

Forty copper chisels. Each has a name. Each is kept in a greased leather sleeve and handed down, and the people who carry them are a lineage apart. They aren't rulers, but they are weighty. They are to the city what an elder's voice is to a family.

A copper chisel is the only tool here that doesn't wear down quickly into nothing. Antler splinters, flint snaps, wood softens and splits. The copper endures across generations, sharpened back and back until some of them are no longer than a finger. The chisel-bearers do the finest and most final work: the last clean edge of a threshold, the lettering of names on the tomb niches, the lip of a basin meant to last a hundred years. Theirs is the work you do at the very end, just before a thing sets.

People speak of the chisels almost as persons. A chisel is said to "know" certain stone. One is said to have cut every tomb niche on the east wall. Another is said to be unlucky and is only used for splitting blocks for trade, work where nothing lasting is made. When a chisel-bearer dies, the chisel is laid beside them in the niche for one night before being taken out and passed on. It's said to be the only night the chisel rests.

## The Old, Who Stay Up

Households move down. That is the rule. But not everyone goes.

The very old, the ringing ones, sometimes refuse. When their family follows the wet edge deeper, they stay in the old rooms higher up, in stone gone cream and then tan. They sleep among the stored grain and the curing blocks. Their families carry food up to them. The ropes and pulleys carry baskets up to the dry terraces daily, and it's understood that some of those baskets are for grandmothers.

The city doesn't treat this as abandonment. It treats it as a kind of vocation. The old who stay up are said to be *keeping the set rooms*. They sit in the sun on the upper ledges, which are warmer than anywhere below, and they watch. They can see the whole pit from up there. They know who's courting whom three terraces down. They see arguments before they happen. They are consulted, climbed up to, brought questions. They are the ones who know where the dead go, since they are halfway there, living in the band of stone between the city and its tombs.

When one of them dies, often nothing needs to move. They're already near the rim. The niche is cut beside the room they have been living in, and they are laid in it, and the room is sealed or given to the doves.

## The Dead

The dead live closest to the sky.

This is the one inversion of the city: the living descend and the dead rise. Every generation, the distance between them grows. The earliest tomb niches are cut into the very lip of the pit, stone so old and hard it's gone grey with a skin of orange lichen. Those dead lie at the plateau's level, almost in the grass. When wind passes over the rim with its long soft note like breath across a jar-mouth, some people say it's them.

The niches are sealed with fitted stones, shaped soft and set to harden in place. Once they cure, the seal becomes almost part of the wall. After a few decades you can hardly find the seam. The dead become the stone.

There are offerings, but they're small: a lamp-dish, a little basin of water left on the ledge below the seal. Water is the gift. The dead are given the wet they no longer have. By the next morning the basin is usually dry, and people take that as acceptance, though it's only the sun and the stone.

## The Other Inhabitants

The swallows are people too, in a sense. They are the only other creatures who carve here, or seem to: they build their mud cups in the cleanest, highest niches and lay their eggs in them. Children believe the swallows are the souls of cutters who made beautiful work. Adults don't believe it and don't disturb the nests either.

The grey lizard is the city's small joke. It lives in the chisel cracks and suns itself on the warm terraces and has never been caught. Children spend whole childhoods trying. The ones who come closest are said to become good gravers: patient, quick, and able to wait perfectly still for a long time.

The doves inhabit the abandoned rooms and are eaten on feast days. The dogs are few and belong to particular households. They know every stair. The owl on the rim is watched and argued over.

The moss is the most honest inhabitant. It grows only where the wet actually goes, so it draws a map of the city's hidden water in green ink along the dew channels. A wet-keeper reads the moss the way a trader reads a sky. Where the moss is dying, the stone is setting.

And the fig tree on the eastern rim puts its pale roots down the wall, flattened against the stone, feeling downward. It is the only other thing in the city that moves the way the city moves.

## What Is Actually Alive Here

Stay long enough and the picture changes.

At first you think the people live in the city. Then you start to see that the rooms they sleep in are temporary, a single generation's worth of hollow, and the city itself is the long pattern of their moving. The city isn't the rooms. The city is the edge.

Think of how a tree lives. Almost all of a great oak is dead: heartwood, finished and hard, holding the thing up. Only a thin layer just under the bark is truly alive. That layer grows outward and leaves the dead wood behind it year by year. And at the very tips of the roots there is a soft growing point pressing into the soil, feeling for water.

The city is like that, turned inside out and pointed down. The upper terraces are heartwood: hard, finished, holding the shape of everything that has been done. The living layer is the wet edge, one band of soft white stone at the bottom and back of the pit, maybe a few hundred paces of working face at any given time. That band is where nearly everything that matters happens. That's where rooms are opened, blocks lifted, children born, names cut. And the whole band is moving. Slowly, over lifetimes, it presses downward into the earth, feeling for water, leaving hardened rooms behind it like growth rings.

The people are that band. They are the living cells of it. Individually they are born soft and they set and they ring and they're laid in the heartwood at the top. Collectively they are something that doesn't age at all, something that is always at the bottom, always wet, always white, always beginning. The grandmother pointing up the wall to the room where she was born is pointing at a part of the creature that has already died. She herself is now in the heartwood's lower reaches. Her grandchildren are the root tip.

The people half-know this. It's in how they talk about the city: never as a place they have, but as a thing they *follow*. *Where is the wet now?* is a question you can ask meaning *what's the news?*

## The Wet Itself

And there's a further layer, one they almost never speak of aloud.

The stone hardens because the water leaves it. The pore water rises to the surface carrying dissolved lime, and it gives that lime up at the face as a skin, and then it goes into the air. Every hard wall in the city is really a record of water departing. The biscuit-colored rim, the ringing terraces, the sealed tombs: all of it is what the wet left behind on its way out.

So the people, if they have a god, have that. They make no image of it. There is nothing to carve that would stay soft enough to be true. But there are basins everywhere, kept full. There are channels cut for no working reason, just to let water move across a face it would not otherwise have touched. There are hides hung wet over walls nobody means to carve. And there is one place, at the very lowest point of every current cut, under or beside the sump, that is never worked. A patch of stone, sometimes no bigger than a blanket, sometimes as big as a room, left untouched and wet and white. Children aren't allowed on it. Cutters step around it without looking. When the city finally cuts below it, as the city always does, it's broken quietly, at night, by the wet-keepers, and a new patch is left at the new bottom.

They don't call it holy. They call it *the soft*. It's where the wet lives. And the wet, as they understand it, is the thing that lets anything be shaped at all. It is not a person but the condition of being a person. It is the time before a thing sets.

If you asked the oldest wet-keeper who lives in this city, she might say, after a long silence, that the wet lives here, and that the people are how it moves.

## Who Lives Here, Finally

So there are several answers, stacked like terraces.

Swallows and a lizard live here, and moss, and one fig tree. Some thousands of quiet people live here, with dust in their knuckle-creases and white-rimmed nails. They speak in whispers, cut their children's names into walls, and lay their dead near the sky. A creature lives here, made of those people, whose body is a mile and a half of hardening stone and whose only living part is a thin white band at the bottom, always pressing downward, always soft. And the wet lives here, rising through the rock and leaving and being coaxed back, the one thing in the whole city that is never finished.

But stand at the bottom at midmorning and watch the woman nearest you, the one drawing a flint through a fresh face with a sound like a long exhale. The stone under her blade is wet and bright and almost translucent. Her hands are pale and wrinkled and sure. Her name is written somewhere above her, hardening. Her children's names are being cut in the next room over. She is, at this moment, exactly at the edge, the one place in the city that is alive in every sense at once.

That's who lives here. Whoever is at the wet edge, now, before it sets.
