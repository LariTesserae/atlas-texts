# Who Lives in the Blister

## I. The first answer

Start with the easy answer, because it is true. Rattlers live here, billions of them, scraping their legs across their wing-cases until the air is a solid thing. Beetles live here, fat with oil and ready to burn. Moths, clatter-birds, grinders chewing their slow roads, and the pale thing in the lower dark that makes the noise go away. A few hundred people live here too, in hollowed gourds, speaking with their hands.

But ask the Husk-folk who lives in the Blister and they will not point at any of these. They will look at you with mild pity, as though you had asked who lives in a hand. Then they will lay their own palm flat on the nearest stem, and wait for you to feel what they feel through it.

So we'll come at it slowly, through one of them.

## II. Lifts

Her name is a gesture: the right palm turned flat to the sky and pushed upward twice, short and firm, like someone testing whether a ceiling will hold. Spoken aloud it would be nothing. In the Blister nobody speaks aloud. If you had to write it down you would write *Lifts*.

She is the oldest person anyone knows of. That is not very old by the measures of other places, perhaps sixty turnings of the gourd-season. Her hands are the colour of the lower levels, a dark polished bronze, and her fingertips are hard and faintly glossy. When she presses them to something she leaves a print, the way a cut stem leaves a bead of sap. Her sweat smells of pepper. So does everyone's here, eventually, but hers is strong enough that the young ones can find her in a dark tunnel by smell alone.

She cannot hear. None of the adults really can. The children are born hearing and lose most of it by the time they can climb. What replaces it isn't silence. It is a second hearing that lives in the soles, the knees, the flat bones of the chest and the roots of the teeth. Lifts can lie on a woven floor and tell you where the grinders are feeding, how far off a gourd-chain is detonating, and whether the lattice two levels down is being honeycombed by beetles. That last one feels like a softening, a loss of ring, the way a cracked pot sounds wrong when you tap it. She knows the difference between the creak of knot tightening and the creak of knot about to fail. She knows it better than you know your own name.

Her children are grown. Two of them are gone into the Blister in the way the folk use that phrase, which is not quite the same as dead, and we'll come to that. Her grandchildren sleep in the gourd with her. The youngest is a girl who hasn't been given a sign yet, because names come later, when you can see what a person's hands keep doing. The girl is still in the hearing time. She wakes crying at gourd-bursts that Lifts only feels as a faint tick in the floor. Lifts holds her against her own chest so the child can feel her heartbeat, slow and steady. It is the one rhythm in the Blister that is not falling, bursting, chewing or growing.

## III. What she does

Every few days, Lifts climbs.

She goes up the lift-cane, which is ribbed like a spine and warm like a flank. She uses a grinder-bone pick in one hand and the other hand bare, because she wants to feel the cane as she goes. At the top the Lid spreads over her like the underside of a frozen lake: milky, cracked into radiating stars, glowing the flat white of noon that never moves. The rattler-roar is thinner up here. The coin-leaf is newest here, pale as fingernails, flushing copper while she watches.

She finds her place and counts.

The Lid is covered in marks, but they aren't scattered. They run in bands, like the rings of a tree cut sideways. The lowest bands, the newest, are in easy reach. She scratches her tally there with a chert flake: a short stroke for a fall, a crossed stroke for a fire, a small closed curve for a child. The older bands are higher. She has to climb past the point where the cane is comfortable, into the tight place where stem-crowns are pressed flat and spread against the salt. There she can reach her mother's marks, and her mother's mother's, faint scratches in the crystal where the Lid has lifted them up and away.

Among the scratches there are handprints.

This is the part the salt-cutters never saw, or never understood if they did. Some of the handprints are only prints, pressed into soft selenite long ago and hardened there, shallow hollows the shape of a palm. Some of them are not empty.

In the highest bands, where the lift-cane meets the Lid in those splayed crowns described as *like the hands of someone holding up a roof*, you can look closely at a crown and count five spreading tips. You can see the heel of a palm and the ridged wrinkle of a knuckle, bronze-dark and glossy and hard as horn. If you put your ear to the stem below, you would feel it, not hear it, over the roar: the very slow pulse of something that is not a plant's rhythm.

Lifts knows whose hands these are. She has climbed up here to touch them the way you might visit a grave, except that you don't lay flowers on these. You press your own palm flat against theirs, and push, twice.

## IV. Gone into the Blister

It goes like this.

The folk change as they age. Everyone knows it and nobody signs about it much, the way you wouldn't remark on the fact that people get taller. The skin darkens from brown to copper to bronze. The sweat thickens and turns to oil. The need for water, which was always small, the few cups of sap each day, gets smaller, until the old ones hardly drink at all and sit for long hours with their backs against the hot stems, soaking up heat the way the young soak up sleep. They grow warm to the touch. They grow slow. They start to creak.

And then one day an old one gets up and climbs, and doesn't come down.

They are not grieved exactly. The family watches them go. The last sign anyone makes to them is the palm pushed upward twice, which is Lifts's name but also, older than her, the word for *hold it up*. The old one goes up the cane to the Lid and finds a place among the crowns. They press their hands flat against the salt, and they stay there, pushing, while the cane grows around their wrists and up their arms. The knot finds them and fuses to them. Their oil runs into the plant's oil, and they are no longer quite separable from it.

This is what *gone into the Blister* means. Two of Lifts's children went this way. That is young to go, and it happened after a fire, when the surge came and a great many people felt the pull at once. There are years like that. The folk call them lifting years, and the Lid rises fastest in them.

Lifts can feel the pull already. She feels it most at the top of the climb, with her palm on the salt, feeling the warmth of the actual sun on the other side of three meters of crystal. She could stay. It would be easy. Her hands already look like theirs.

She climbs down, because the girl isn't named yet.

## V. The other residents, again

Once you know this, the rest of the Blister looks different.

The folk suspect the plants are one plant in different moods: lift-cane putting out knot, knot swelling into gourd. They suspect the same of everything else, quietly, though they sign it only to each other and only in the dark.

Oil-beetles are full of the same amber lipid as the stems. Crush one and it smells the same, pine and pepper and hot metal. Clatter-birds nest in the gourds, and when a gourd bursts its seed and the flock bursts into the air, it is hard to see, in the blizzard of shot and wings, which is which. Husk-moths crawl into the gourds by thousands to pollinate them. Some years the old ones say that more crawl in than come out. The grinders eat the lattice and leave roads, and the roads are how people move. So the grinders are the Blister chewing passages for its own inhabitants, keeping its flesh open the way you keep a wound open so it drains.

Then there is the pale mantis, silent, in the dark near the bedrock where the heat comes from. The folk say it is how you know the Blister is paying attention to you. It moves in a pocket of quiet because it *is* a small Hush, walking.

It isn't that everything here is one creature. The folk think the distinction between one creature and many isn't a question that means much here. A hand has fingers. A finger has a nail. The nail doesn't wonder whether it is the hand.

## VI. The Hush, from inside

When the Hush comes, Lifts feels it before anything else, even before the noise stops, because the floor changes. Every small tremor that was running through the lattice goes still. Scraping, chewing, boring, all of it stops at the same instant. The only thing left is the creaking of the growth and, underneath it, rising, the hummer.

For the adults this is the only time they hear. The roar drops away and the tiny residue of hearing they still have wakes up, and the world is suddenly full of sound: wood straining, a stem splitting somewhere, their own breath, the hummer coming up through the floor into their teeth. It is a sound so low it isn't pitch, only pressure. Then it rises, and it is pitch, and then it is a note.

Everyone sings.

The salt-cutters said it was a phrase no one alive could translate. That's true, but not because the meaning is lost. It is untranslatable the way a heartbeat is. It isn't made of words. It is the hummer's note, the one every root that hums sings, answered in the shape of human throats. Five tones, falling and then lifting. The children learn it before they can walk, while they can still hear, in the Hushes of their first years, with their faces pressed to an adult's chest. They learn it the way they learn to breathe in the dry sweet gas of the lower levels, without being taught.

Lifts has come to think the song is not something the folk sing *to* the Blister. It is something the Blister sings through them, once each Hush, from end to end, in every throat and every root at once. It is the one moment in its whole turbulent, crashing, burning, bursting life when all its moods are the same mood. When she sings it she can feel the old ones singing it too, up at the Lid, through the cane, in the humming of the crowns. Then the fifty-count ends and the noise slams back, and everything goes faster, and the Lid cracks in a dozen places.

It is drawing breath, the folk say. Lifts would put it differently, if she put it at all. She would push her palm upward twice. It is gathering itself. It is getting ready to push.

## VII. What lives there

So the question comes back.

What lives in the Blister is something that has been growing, very slowly and then less slowly, under a salt pan at the bottom of the world. It is fed by the heat of the rock and it is eating the rock. It is soaked through with oil, and it burns and grows back stronger. It has been lifting its roof by a hand's breadth a year, a few hands' breadth in lifting years, for longer than anyone has been counting.

It has a great many parts. Some of them rattle, some bore, some chew, some hunt, some burst. Some of them sit in hollowed gourds, pounding seed-meal on flat stones, the one rhythm in the whole Blister made on purpose. Those parts have learned to do a thing no other part of it does. They climb up to the ceiling and count.

That may be what the folk are for. The Blister doesn't need them to grow; it grows regardless. It doesn't need them to burn, or to lift, or even, the folk suspect, to sing. But it may need something to *keep track*. Something has to notice each fall and each fire and each child, and scratch them into the salt in bands like tree rings, so that the pressure against the Lid is not just pressure but accumulation, a sum growing toward a number. The Blister is patient in the way roots in stone are patient. Maybe the folk are how it knows how long it has been waiting.

Or maybe that's too grand. Maybe they are just people, living in a place that is slowly turning them into it, and they count because counting is what people do in the face of something too big to hold.

Lifts doesn't know which, and it doesn't trouble her.

## VIII. The gourd

One more thing, which Lifts has never signed to anyone.

The bell-gourds hang in clusters, swell, ripen and burst. The best of them are fire-seeded, opening in heat and throwing their seed hardest. In the ripening season one burst sets off the next, and a chain crosses the whole Blister in minutes like rolling thunder.

Lifts has lain on the floor of her gourd-house through a hundred of those chains, feeling each one in her chest. Lately, when the Lid-light is dim and the girl is asleep against her, she has begun to wonder about the shape of the whole thing from above. It is a gentle swell on the white pan, warm to the touch, humming inside, rising a little every year. It is filled to bursting with hard shot and oil and fast-growing things. It has a shell.

She thinks of what a gourd sounds like in the moment before it goes, how everything inside goes still for just an instant, as if listening.

She thinks the folk are right, and someday the Lid will break and the growth will come up into the sun. And she thinks it won't come up the way water comes up. It will come up the way seed does, all at once, thrown hard in every direction, landing on salt, on stone, on places far across the world that have never heard a rattler or smelled pepper-oil or felt the ground hum. Whatever lands there will carry the Blister in it: beetle and bird and moth and cane, and perhaps some small bronze-handed thing that knows the five-tone phrase and has an urge, not yet understood, to press its palms against whatever is above it and push.

Whether that is a good day or a bad one depends, Lifts supposes, on whether you are the gourd or the seed.

## IX. Now

The Hush is over. The roar is back. The girl is awake and crying at a ceiling-fall somewhere far off, a crack Lifts felt only as a jolt in the floor. She picks the child up and holds her against her chest and lets her feel the slow drum there.

Then, because the girl is old enough now to begin, she takes the small warm hand in her own hard glossy one, turns it palm-up, and pushes it gently toward the gourd's roof, twice.

*Hold it up.*

The girl laughs and does it again on her own, and again, pushing at the air above her. It is the first thing her hands have chosen to keep doing.

In a few days they'll climb the cane together to the band of newest marks. There, Lifts will scratch a small closed curve into the salt. Beside it, with a chert flake guided by the old hand over the young one, the girl will scratch her name.
