# Who Lives at Weeping Scar

## I. The Obvious Answer, and Why It Is Wrong

The first answer is the one the town seems to offer you: a wren, some voles, a stoat passing through, a raven overhead. A thin thread of animals through a green place, as if the town were a stage set with a few actors wandering across it.

That answer is too small. It is not false, only small, the way a census of a human city would be wrong if it counted only the people you happened to see in the streets at dawn. Most of the residents of Weeping Scar are not in the lanes. They are in the walls, under the floors, inside the moss, inside the dead wood, inside the water. And the largest resident of all is the one you have been treating as the buildings.

So start with the ones you can see, and work inward.

---

## II. The Wren, Who Is a Builder

The wren is the only animal on the shelf that builds anything, and it builds compulsively.

In spring the male works through his district of the old quarter, and he claims a district, a run of perhaps a dozen rooms and the alleys between them. He makes nests: domed balls of moss and beard-lichen, woven into the fir-lattice at head height inside the darkest rooms, each with a single round door facing the lane. He does not make one. He makes five, six, sometimes nine, and then he sings outside each in turn until a female comes and inspects them. She walks through his houses one by one, going in and coming out, and chooses one. The rest stay empty.

The town has therefore always had a second, smaller architecture inside its first: little moss houses inside the fir houses, most of them unlived-in, made by a bird for an audience of one. Old ones persist for years in the dry backs of the rooms, brown and felted, and voles sometimes move into them in autumn. It is the only place on the shelf where one resident lives in a house another resident made on purpose.

The wren sings before dawn because that is when the air is stillest and the fog lies heaviest in the lanes. Sound carries farthest then, down the wet corridors, off the cliff. The song does not travel far even so. Each male's trill reaches exactly as far as his district and dies in the wall of the next. The wrens have divided the town into parishes by earshot.

A wren lives two years, perhaps three. In the hardest winters, when the snow closes over everything and the rooms go blue and silent, the pairs of a whole district pack together into one old nest, sometimes eight or ten birds pressed into a ball the size of a fist, sharing heat. In the morning, those still alive come out.

---

## III. The Voles, Who Have Never Seen Two Winters

Under the moss floors the voles have their own town, and it is more like a human town than the fir's. It has intention. There are runways worn deliberately between places that matter: from nest to crowberry mat, from crowberry mat to willow bed, from willow bed to the latrine. The latrines are real, fixed places at particular junctions, used for generations, marked and re-marked. There are larders: chambers in the soft rotten foundations packed with crowberry leaves and moss capsules cut in autumn. There are nurseries lined with shredded beard-lichen.

The strange thing about the voles is time. A mountain vole born in early summer will almost certainly be dead before the next autumn. Most individuals live through one winter at most. No vole on the shelf has ever seen the town twice in the same season. None has any memory of how things were.

The town remembers for them. The runways persist. A young vole in its first week out of the nest does not have to learn where the willow is. It follows the packed earth, the scent of the latrines, the worn curve around a cobble that its great-great-grandmother wore smooth. The vole town is a set of instructions left by the dead for the living, written in the floor. When the thaw exposes the runways on the cobbles, that faint handwriting, what you are reading is several hundred generations of a single sentence being copied over and over, each copy slightly changed.

When the stoat comes, the sentence is interrupted. A runway goes quiet for a week. Then someone new begins using it.

---

## IV. The Stoat, Who Lives Here More Than It Seems

It was described as a stranger on the high street, passing through. That is true of summer. In winter it is less true.

Under the snow, when the lanes have filled to the rooftops and the whole town is a smooth white slope, the stoat moves in. It kills a vole in its nest and takes the nest: the round chamber, the lichen lining. It adds the fur of its kill to the lining, so its winter dens in the tenements are upholstered in grey. It keeps several, moving between them. You find them in spring when the snow drains: tight chambers in the dead-wood foundations, softly lined with the pelts of the people who used to live there.

So the town has a predator in residence for half the year, living in the houses of its prey, inside the walls, at the level of the oldest foundations. In summer it becomes a visitor again, a white animal gone brown, slipping up from below through the gate and down the main lane in a straight line, as if it has an appointment.

## V. The Ones You Did Not Count

Here the census gets large.

**In the duff.** The rust-brown floor of every room, ankle-deep and springy, is packed with springtails. They are tiny six-legged grazers, grey and violet and pale, each smaller than a grain of sand, and they number in the hundreds of thousands per room. They eat the fungal threads that break down the needles. When disturbed they fling themselves into the air with a catch under the belly, so a handful of duff held in the light looks briefly like it is boiling. On mild days in late winter they come up through the snow and cover it in drifting grey smudges, like soot that moves.

Among them, and eating them, are the lions of the duff: pseudoscorpions, each the size of a comma, with pincers held out in front and no sting at all. They hunt in the dark between needles, slow and patient, and they are the top predators of a world the size of a teacup. There are mites of fifty kinds, round and armoured like beads, some grazing, some hunting, some riding on the springtails. For the most part the inhabitants of the shelf-town are far too small to see, and they live in the floor.

**In winter, on the snow.** Two strange things walk on the white surface in the coldest months, when nothing else moves. One is a snow scorpionfly: bronze, wingless or nearly so, long-snouted, the size of a flea, hopping slowly across the snow crust at temperatures that would stop most insects' hearts. Its larvae live in the moss cushions of the stair-risers. It mates on the snow in midwinter, and it is active only in the cold; warm it in your hand and it dies. The other is a wingless crane fly, spider-legged and dark, which walks deliberately across the white in the dim afternoon, going somewhere, you could never guess where. These are the town's winter residents, and they come out only when the town is buried.

**In the moss.** Every sulphur-green cushion on the lane floors is a sponge of water, and in that water live tardigrades, eight-legged and plump, pawing through the film between moss leaves. There are rotifers wheeling their crowns of cilia, and nematodes by the million. When a cushion dries in a rare run of clear weather, they dry with it. They draw in their legs, lose nearly all their water, and wait as dust, for weeks or years, until fog comes back. Then they swell and walk again. Press a cushion and cold water wells up around your fingers: you are flooding a city.

**In the seeps.** The water that comes out of the cliff is barely above freezing, and it never warms. Living in it, in the threads running between the cobbles, are tiny pale flatworms that cannot survive water any warmer than this. They came down from the snowfields above in the melt, carried in the cracks. They hold to the undersides of stones in the lanes, and they live only where the lanes are. In a real sense they are the residents of the streets. When a seep moves and its lane begins to close, they go with the water, gliding down the new crack. When a lane dies, the flatworms have already left.

**On the cliff.** In the first hour of summer sun, when the light touches the front of the shelf, small dark wolf spiders come out onto the warming rock at the edge and hunt the gnats that rise from the crowberry. Females carry their egg sacs on their spinnerets and later carry the spiderlings on their backs, a furred mass of tiny legs. By the time the shadow of the mountain swings back across, they are gone into the cracks.

**On the roofs.** A small dark moth lives here whose caterpillars eat only crowberry. It flies in fog, weakly, low over the rooftops at dusk in midsummer, and it is the prey that the wren's song is really about. The pipits that come up for the berries in late summer also take the last of the caterpillars.

**In the rooms, on the walls.** Harvestmen, with bodies like seeds and legs like hairs, stand motionless in the dusk inside the largest rooms, near the ceiling, sometimes dozens clustered together in one corner so that their legs make a single tangled grey fringe. They look like more beard-lichen until one of them moves.

Count all of these, and the population of the shelf-town is not a handful of animals. It is uncountable. Most of it is under your feet or inside the thing you are touching.

## VI. The Families

Now go back to the fir.

Every wall on the shelf is made of branches that rooted and became trunks, which rooted again. Every stem in a ring is the same individual, genetically one tree, spread out across the ground. And the rings collided. So consider what the town actually is, underneath the metaphor.

If you could see which stems belonged to which original seedling, colour them, one colour for each, you would find that the town is not made of hundreds of trees. It is made of a few. Perhaps seven. Perhaps a dozen. Each is a single organism that has spread across a district, making room after room out of its own body, and each district's boundary is a place where it pressed against a neighbour. Those are the thickest walls on the shelf, the ones you cannot get a finger into. They are where two families meet and neither gives way.

These are the households. The rooms of one district are all one tree's rooms. The tenements near the cliff, stacked generation on generation, belong to the oldest families. Those are the first seedlings that took hold on the raw ledge three hundred years ago, in whatever crevice held the first handful of grit. Their living green is on top. Beneath it is their own dead: silver-grey wood, twisted and hard, the bodies of their former selves, bearing up the present.

The family is not only the tree. Every fir root on the shelf is sheathed in fungal threads, and those threads run out through the duff and connect root to root. Almost certainly they cross the boundaries. The families that fight for every inch of ground above are joined below, sharing water and sugar through a web that belongs to neither. A dying wall can go on being fed by its neighbour for a season. A seedling in deep shade can be kept alive by the stems around it. The houses are separate. The foundations are not.

The fir, then, is resident, house and householder at once. When a wall dies and a room opens to the lane, and the next spring the neighbouring stems reach across and root into the gap, that is not weather rebuilding a ruin. It is a family expanding into a vacant lot.

---

## VII. The Ones Who Live in the Dead

The glowing fungus is in the oldest rooms, in the heartwood of the silver-grey foundations. It is the only thing on the shelf that feels uncanny, and now it is possible to say why.

It lives in the ancestors. It is eating, very slowly, the first generations of the oldest families, the wood that was green when the scar was fresh. It traces the grain in cold blue-green because it has followed the grain in, cell by cell, over decades. Its light is too faint to see by, and it is not for seeing by. It is a by-product of digestion.

With it, in the rotten foundations, live beetle larvae, pale and curved, chewing galleries through the softest wood, and the voles tunnel beside them. The whole lower storey of the old quarter is the dead being converted, very slowly, into the living: into fungus, into beetles, into voles, into the soil the next generation of fir will root in. If you were to stand in one of those tall dark rooms at night with your eyes fully adjusted and see the faint veins of light in the walls, you would be looking at the town's memory being eaten, which is how it becomes the town's future.

---

## VIII. What Else Might Be Said to Live Here

There are residents that are not creatures.

The water lives here, if anything does. It arrives, it runs its lanes, it moves when the cliff cracks, and the whole street plan follows it. The town is laid out by the water's habits.

The night wind lives here too. It comes down every night by the same routes, through the same throat, out through the same gate, and it keeps the main streets clipped and clean like a groundskeeper who never sleeps.

The fog is only a visitor. It pours in over the lip and pours out again, many times a day. It is the thing the whole town drinks.

---

## IX. The Answer

So who lives at Weeping Scar?

A few dozen wrens, dividing the old quarter into parishes by the reach of a song. A few hundred voles, none of whom will see the same season twice, following instructions left in the floor by the dead. One stoat, which is a visitor in summer and in winter a lodger in the houses of the people it has eaten. Uncountable springtails, mites, tardigrades and pale cold flatworms, in the floor, in the moss, in the streets. A handful of bronze insects that walk only on snow. Harvestmen hanging like lichen in the dusk of the great rooms. A fungus that glows as it eats the ancestors.

Above all, a few ancient families of a single stubborn tree, each one a district, each one a single body, pressed against each other at their borders and joined beneath the ground. They are building rooms out of themselves, standing on their own dead, and reaching across every opened gap to close it.

It is a town after all, and the description had that nearly right. What it missed is that the town is full. Every wall is a resident. Every floor is a city. The lanes are inhabited by the water, and the water has its own inhabitants. The shape that looks like a place where people might live is not empty of people. Its people are simply too small, too slow or too green to recognise as people.

And they are still arriving. The rock is new. The scar is three centuries old, and every spring the cliff drops a few more flakes and a few more seeps shift. Every summer something new is carried up by the pipits, or down by the melt, or in by the fog: a seed, a spore, a mite clinging to a feather. Most do not take. Now and then one does, and a new resident moves in.
