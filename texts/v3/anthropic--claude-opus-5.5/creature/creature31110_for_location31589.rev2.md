# What Walks the Levels

## The Paths

You find its paths before you find anything else, and at first you don't recognise them as paths.

A reed bed ahead of you has been parted. It isn't flattened as wind or flood would do it. It has been *parted*, the stems pressed aside in a single long lane perhaps twice the width of a cart, curving gently the way the channels curve. The reeds on either side lean outward and have grown that way, so the lane is old, walked again and again over years. It runs down to a channel, stops at the bank, and picks up again on the far side a little upstream, as if whatever made it had crossed at an angle against a current that is barely there.

Follow it long enough and you begin to notice the prints.

They are round, or nearly round, and large, as wide across as a spread hand is long. Each is a small pool. They are not the churned sockets that a cow or an elk leaves in soft ground. They are clean-edged, as if the moss had been lifted out neatly and the water had risen up to fill the space. They are spaced wider than a horse's stride and set almost in a single line, one before the other, so whatever walks here places its feet with care.

Some of them are fresh. Some are so old that sphagnum has grown back over their lips. And now and then, if you are standing near one, you will see a print that has not been made yet: the moss sinking, the water welling up clear and cold, the edges cutting themselves. Nothing is standing in it. After a moment the new print is simply there, one more in the line, filling.

If you look down into that newest print, you'll see a leg in it.

## What the Rim Says

The peat-cutters on the higher ground have a name for it. They call it *the Wader*, which is a small, practical name, the name you give a thing so you can mention it without looking up from your work.

Ask them what it looks like and they disagree, and the disagreement is the most useful thing they can tell you.

One says it is a kind of great grey ox, low and heavy, grazing with its head down. Another says no, it is tall, taller than the willows, all legs, like a heron the size of a barn. A woman who took her sheep too far down one wet spring says it had a crown like a bundle of reeds and that it sang when the wind got into it. An old man says flatly that there are dozens of them, a herd, moving through the mist in a long line. His brother, who was with him, says there was only one and that it was standing in all those places.

None of them has been close. They saw it from the shoulder of the hill, or across a wide stretch of open sedge, or, most often, not directly at all.

"You see it in the water," the woman with the sheep says. "Not *on* the water. *In* it."

---

## In the Pools

She is right. This is where most people meet it, if they meet it at all.

You are kneeling at a pool in the deep interior, one of those holding a borrowed sky, say the hard blue noon of a summer that is not happening. In that reflected summer the reeds are gold and dry, and among them, a few yards behind where your own reflected shoulder should be, something is standing.

You turn. There is nothing behind you but grey sedge and drizzle.

You turn back, and it is still there in the pool, in the summer, and it has lifted its head.

After that you start to see it everywhere. It stands knee-deep in a drowned version of a channel, the water up to its chest, in a pool that shows the Levels in flood. In a pool that shows night it is only an outline against stars you can't see, the stars going out behind it and coming back as it moves. In the drought-pools it walks the cracked beds of the channels with its head low, and it is the only living thing in them. In a pool that shows snow it stands perfectly still, and snow has collected along its back.

It is never in the version you are standing in.

It is always in one of the others.

And it knows that you are looking. Not every time, but often enough. It turns that long head, and from inside the stored drought or the stored winter it looks up through the surface at you. Then, unhurried, it goes back to whatever it was doing.

---

## At the Peak

Only at the height of the drone's nineteen-hour tide, when the Levels lose their grip and the states pile up on one another like tracing paper, is it ever *here*.

It doesn't arrive. It is simply in the overlay, because it is in every layer at once. That is why the old man saw a herd. When the world flickers, you see it many times over: in the flood-layer and the drought-layer and the snow-layer and the night-layer. Each image stands in a slightly different place, because in each version it had walked a slightly different way, so a line of grey shapes goes off into the mist like a procession. They are all it. When the chord ebbs and the layers settle, all of them fade except one, and that one fades a little more slowly than the rest. For a few seconds the Wader stands in plain grey drizzle, in the world as it currently is, and you can see it with your own eyes.

This is what you see.

---

## Its Body

It is tall. It is taller than a man, which should be impossible here, because nothing on the Levels stands higher than a man. The Levels agree with this. Even as you watch, the Wader seems to be not quite standing in the same place as the sedge around it. Its height doesn't cast the shadow its height should cast. The land doesn't count it.

Most of that height is leg. It has four long legs, thin as alder poles, jointed backward at the lower knee like a wading bird's. It lifts each foot slowly, draws it forward, holds it for a moment above the moss, and sets it down with a care that is almost ceremonial. The feet are round, broad, and soft-edged. They spread when they take its weight, like a hand pressing into dough.

The body above those legs is deep-chested and long, something like an elk's, but narrower, the ribs showing faintly through the coat when it breathes. The coat is grey, the exact grey of the mist, long and fine. On every hair the mist condenses. You can watch droplets bead along its flank, fatten, and roll down, and new ones begin behind them. So it is always wet, always dripping, and in the dim light it seems to be made partly of the weather it walks through. Lichen grows in pale beards along its underside and down the backs of its legs. Moss has taken hold along its spine in a long ridge of red and green. Something that old, standing that still, in a place that has never been dry, will be grown upon.

Its neck is long and carried low. Its head is long too, narrow, heavy-lidded, with a soft muzzle like a deer's that is always wet at the nostrils. When it breathes out in the cool air, the breath hangs and doesn't disperse, and within it, briefly, the light changes. There is a flicker of the gold of another evening, or the blue of another noon.

Where its coat has worn thin, at the shoulders and the joints, the skin shows through. The skin is banded. It has the same faint iridescence you find inside an alder root, oil-on-water colours laid in rings and whorls, moving when the body doesn't. The silt is in it. Or it is in the silt. After you have looked at it for long enough, that distinction stops seeming important.

And there is the crown.

It does not have antlers. Rising from the back of its skull is a crown of tines, a dozen or twenty, some long, some broken off short. Each is hollow, grey-stemmed, and smooth, exactly like the singing reeds of the deep beds. When it lifts its head into the wind, the crown sounds. It makes a wavering, breathy chord, a little above the drone. That chord is so close to the voice the reed beds pull out of the wind that you realise, with a slow cold drop in the stomach, which of them learned it from the other.

The reeds didn't tune themselves to the drone. They tuned themselves to *this*.

Its eyes are last, because they are the thing you'll remember. They are large and pale, the colour of pewter pools, and like the pools they don't reflect what is in front of them. Look into the Wader's eye and you'll see a sky, and it won't be this sky. In the left eye it may be night. In the right, a white winter morning. As it blinks, slowly, the skies change.

## What It Eats

You can watch it graze, if you are lucky and it lingers.

It lowers its long head to the sedge and pulls, and a tuft of grass comes away in its mouth. That is ordinary enough. But the stems left behind, the cropped stubs where it has fed, have lost their shimmer. Cut one later and the pith inside is plain green, with no banding at all. Whatever the silt had put into that plant, the Wader has taken out.

It feeds most heavily at the edges of things. It grazes where a pool meets its bank, or where the drought-light from a reflection lies on the undersides of the reeds. Where it has fed, the borrowed light goes out. The upward-falling drops stop falling upward. The ground stops folding. Along the lanes it has walked, the Levels are at their most ordinary: one sky, one season, the grey drizzle and wet sedge that you think of as simply what the Levels *are*.

That is when you begin to understand what it does.

It was never the case that the Levels were naturally grey and plain, and that the reflections and foldings were leaks from somewhere else. The device was built to hold everything at once, every state, every condition, all of it together. Left alone, it would do only that: flicker, and overlay, and never settle, a world laid on itself a million times over, all possibilities and no place.

What settles it is being looked at.

The Wader is the looking. The grey present you walk through is only the version it happens to be regarding now. Every pool is a version it isn't looking at at this moment. When it grazes, it chooses. When it walks, it *commits* the ground under its feet to one condition, and that is why the prints fill with such clean-edged care. It is why the heron's footprints appear before its feet: the heron walks in the Wader's wake, in ground that has only just been decided and is still catching up with itself.

It is not a visitor to the Levels. The builders made an instrument that could hold every condition. At some point, in the long weathering, the silt learned to need something that could *read* it. Out of the fine blue mud and the rounded lattices and the water moving through them, it grew one.

If the Levels are thinking a single enormous thought, the Wader is the part of the thought that says: *this one. Now this one.*

---

## Its Gaze

Travellers who come back from the deep interior are rarely changed in any way that matters, but they are sometimes changed.

A man comes home with a scar on his hand that he says he never got. His wife says he's had it for years. A woman comes back certain she took the east path down, and finds her boot prints on the west. Someone's watch runs ahead by eleven minutes and keeps its new time as if it had always kept it. Small things. Slight slippages. The rim people say it happens to those who met its eye.

To the Wader you are one more held state. You are a small bundle of possibilities walking through its pasture: the you who took the east path and the you who took the west, the you with the scar and the you without. When it looks at you, it chooses. It isn't cruel about it, or kind. It chooses you the way it chooses a tuft of sedge: gently, and without much thought, and for reasons of its own that may have more to do with where it is going next than with you.

Most of the time it chooses what you already were. Most of the time.

The echoes that come back in a voice that is nearly yours, with words you haven't said yet, are the other versions of you. It hasn't chosen them. They are still out there in the silt, calling across the drone, and for a moment you can hear them.

## The Others

The few creatures that live here live in its orbit, and once you know it's there, you can see how each has shaped itself around it.

**The heron-like bird** follows it, always at a distance, always in the lanes it has walked. It feeds in freshly decided water, where the small fish and beetles have only just settled into a single place and haven't yet learned to hide in more than one. The bird's footprints come early because it lives on ground still making up its mind. It is unbothered by this because it has never known anything else.

**The eels** are the ones it has not looked at. They live in the deep channels, under the bank, where its long gaze doesn't reach, so they have never been chosen. Each eel is in many places, a single fish spread across the Levels, turning the same slow curve in a dozen pools a quarter mile apart. They are old, and unfinished, and they seem to prefer it. When the Wader's head comes down to a channel to drink, the eels go still. They are not afraid of it. They are *keeping very quiet*, the way you keep quiet when someone is counting the room.

**The moths** come out in the drone's lull, which is when it sleeps. They gather in their trembling clouds over the singing reeds. They aren't drawn to the reeds' sound, as it turns out, but to the resemblance. They are listening for its breathing, and the reeds are the nearest thing. When the Wader wakes and lifts its crown into the wind, the moths leave the reed beds and follow it, a pale cloud drifting around its head. They settle on the tines and cover them, and the crown's chord goes soft and muffled with their powdery bodies.

## The Call

The rim people have heard it, and you will hear it too if you stay a full turn of the drone.

At the trough, when the chord sinks low enough to almost talk over, the Wader stops grazing. It lifts its head, and the moths rise off its crown, and it calls.

It is a single note, below even the floor of the drone, so low it isn't so much heard as undergone. The water in every pool shivers. The eels go still in every one of their places at once. It is one long, level sound, and it lasts a very long time.

It took me a while, thinking about it, to work out why a thing that walks alone would call.

I think it is asking.

I don't mean it is asking for an answer. I think it is asking the Levels to hold still. The trough is when it sleeps, and when it sleeps it isn't looking, and when it isn't looking, nothing is being chosen. The call is a note to keep the world in place until it wakes, a held chord, a finger pressed on the page to keep the reader's place. Afterward it folds its long legs and lies down. The Levels go dim and quiet, and the reflections in the pools grow brighter, and more of them, as if in the absence of its gaze the stored skies were crowding forward.

And the Levels keep, mostly. That is what its note is for.

---

## Where It Sleeps

The stub of dark stone at the heart of the Levels, the knee-high knob furred in moss with the blank grey pools all around it, is where it lies down.

If you come upon it there, at the bottom of the trough, you'll find it curled on the sedge with its legs folded beneath it and its long neck laid along the ground. Its head rests with the jaw against the stone, and its crown of hollow tines is quiet, the moths asleep on them. The drone is loudest here, and it is breathing in time with the drone. Or, as you are now in a position to guess, the drone is breathing in time with it.

The pools near the stone show nothing because this is the one place it is always looking *from*. You can't see the eye in its own reflection. The blankness in the water is the point of view itself, the place where the choosing happens, empty in the way the centre of a circle is empty.

Perhaps the stone was the builders' device, once, the last remnant of it, a pillar that held the whole calculation. Or perhaps it was the builders' reader, the thing they meant to sit here and look, and it weathered down like everything else. Then, very slowly, it grew legs, and a coat that gathers mist, and a crown of reeds, and it got up and started walking, because a reader who sits in one place can only read one page.

Or it is a boulder, and it likes the shape of it against its jaw.

---

## What It Is Doing

It is grazing, mostly. It is walking its long, careful lanes through the sedge, choosing, as it goes, which version of the Levels will be the one that is.

It isn't guarding anything. It isn't waiting for its makers. If it knows they existed, it has never shown any sign of wanting them back. It does one thing, and it has done it for longer than the alders have been dying and regrowing on the same bends, and it does it well.

But I keep thinking about the pools.

Somewhere out there, in a pool you might pass on a morning's walk, there is a version of the Levels the Wader has never chosen and has never walked into. I have wondered whether, in that version, there is another one. It would be a second reader, standing in its own grey drizzle, looking into its own pools and seeing, in one of them, a tall grey shape with a crown of reeds and pewter eyes, never quite in the world it stands in.

If there is, I imagine they have been watching each other for a very long time, each from inside the other's reflection. I imagine that sometimes, at the peak of the drone, when the layers pile up and every version of the Levels is there at once, they stand for a few seconds in the same place.

And I imagine that then, in the flicker, before the chord ebbs, the procession of grey shapes going off into the mist is not all one animal after all.
