# What Lives in the Grinding Flats

## I. The Walker

Start with her, because she is easiest to see. She is small and bent. She stands on the flank of a whaleback in the early mist with her feet bare on the wet stone, and she is not looking at anything.

Her name is a gesture: two fingers laid flat across the opposite palm, then lifted slowly, the way you lift your ear from rock when you have finished listening. Children make it carelessly and fast, a flick. People her own age, of whom there are three left on this part of the flats, make it slowly and with some ceremony, because they remember what it meant when it was given. She was the child who would not stop lying on the stone. She was found asleep on ridges, face down, arms out. Once they found her at the lip of a throat as wide as a house, her cheek against the smooth rim, the whole exhalation roaring past her head. She told them afterward, in the clumsy hands of a four-year-old, that she had been listening to it think.

She is deaf now, almost entirely. The shriek of small throats is gone, and the colonies' quarrelling, and the rattle. The rattle went first, in her thirties. For a year she kept turning her head, sure that something was missing, before she understood that the missing thing was the whole upper half of the world. People here say that when the rattle leaves you, you have become a local. She was a local early.

What remains is what comes through the body. The great throats still reach her. She feels them in her sternum and in the long bones of her legs, and she knows them the way you know the gaits of people you have lived beside. Old Mouth, two days east, is a slow, wide pressure behind the breastbone, like a hand laid flat on her chest. The Twins, a pair of shafts a hundred paces apart, beat against each other so that the air between them pulses, and walking toward them she feels it in her teeth. Fork-Throat, Kettle, the Long Crack, the unnamed one that opened in her mother's lifetime and that the young people call the New One: each has a place in her body. Blindfolded, she could walk from any of them to any other. She has done it. She does it now, in fog so thick that she cannot see her own feet, carrying news between the scattered ridges. That is her work, and it has been for fifty years.

She is the one who noticed the names.

## II. Tap-Names

When a child is born on the flats, the mother waits for the turn of the breath, that faltering moment between exhale and inhale. In it she lays the baby on the bare stone of the whaleback and knocks a pattern beside its head with her knuckles. The pattern is new. No one living has it. It might be three slow taps and two quick, or a stutter and a pause and a stutter. This is the child's tap-name, and it is how the stone knows them.

Everyone else on the ridge presses a palm or an ear to the rock and feels the name arrive. The name goes into the ridge, and in a sense it stays there. For the rest of that person's life, when someone needs them across a whaleback in fog or in the dark, they knock the name into the stone and wait. The named person feels it through their hands and feet, knocks back their own name to say *here*, and then the conversation can begin: *come*, or *eel at the Twins*, or *the child is with me*.

When someone dies, the name is not used again. It is not forbidden exactly. It simply stops, the way a throat stops breathing when it silts up. People say the name has gone down.

Her brother's name was a long tap, then a short one, then a long one again, like someone walking with a limp. He went into a throat when he was nine and she was eleven. There was no ceremony to it and no one to blame. A smooth lip, a morning when the film was thick and slick after a wet night, a slip. His body never came up. The eels never brought it. Her mother sang into the throat at the turn of the breath for a month afterward and then stopped, and then she had a different child, and then she died too.

Forty years later the woman was sitting at the lip of Old Mouth during an inhale, her cheek against the rock the way she had lain as a girl. The clicking began below, as it sometimes does: that slow, deliberate knocking that everyone describes as knuckles on wood. She had felt it many times. Everyone who lives near a great throat has. It comes up through the stone into the jaw.

This time she was counting.

A long tap, a short, a long. Then a pause the length of a held breath, then the same again. Then something she did not know: a rapid double knock and a slide, the stutter of a name she had never learned. Then a slow triple, which she did know. It was her grandmother's, who died when she was very small.

She lay there until the tide turned and the exhale came roaring up past her. She got up and walked three days, blind in fog, to find the two other people who were as old as she was. She did not trust herself to tell anyone younger.

## III. What the People Say

The two others did not want to believe her, and then, cautiously, they did. One of them, a man whose hands shake so badly now that his signs are hard to read, had felt something like it years before at the Long Crack. It was a pattern he couldn't place that had nagged at him for months. The other, a woman who sings for the dead at Kettle, had never felt anything at all, and she grieved about it quietly.

They agreed not to say much. Among themselves they settled on something close to this: the voices go down. Everyone knows the voices go down, because that is what the singing is for. You shout into the gap, and the returning wind takes your voice into the galleries, and the dead are there, or something that keeps them is. The clicking has always answered. Nobody had wondered what the answer said.

So now they know one thing it says. It says the names.

The offerings at the lips of the throats have always been small: an egg, a hook of soft copper, a twist of gut, a flake of shell. Since she came back from Old Mouth, the walker has begun leaving something different. She knocks her own name on the rim, very carefully, at the turn of the breath, and then she lies still and waits. She has been doing this for six years. She does not know what she is waiting for.

## IV. Below

Now go down.

Go past the olive-black film at the throat mouths, past the point where the light fails, past the galleries where the eels move, long and pale and slow, through water so hard it is nearly stone in waiting. The roar is unimaginable here. Air is shoved through passages narrow as a wrist and wide as a hall. The whole honeycomb sings at a thousand pitches at once, and the water rises and falls in it twice a day like blood in a lung.

Farther east, and deeper, the passages converge. There is a chamber there. Nobody has seen it, and nobody could, but it is there, the way the sea is there.

At first it would look like a place and not a creature. It is a vault of dark rock whose walls are hung with growth: long, fluted, banded draperies of stone laid down drop by drop from the mineral water, the way the limpets lay down their shells. They are gray and pale and rust-green in layers, like the old folded ranges printed on the flats above. They hang from the ceiling in ranks, thousands of them, some as thin as a finger and some thicker than a body, and they do not reach the floor. They hang free, their ends a hand's width above the black water.

It would look like a place. But the film is here too, the life that eats stone. Here it is not orange or olive or violet. It is a deep, glossy black-green, thick as hide, and it covers every hanging fold. It has been here longer than the whalebacks have been worn. It has been eating and depositing, dissolving and rebuilding, until the stone and the living skin over it are no longer two things.

When the tide pushes in, the water rises beneath the hanging folds and the air in the chamber is driven out. When the tide falls, the air is dragged back in, down every passage from every throat on the plain. It comes carrying whatever it has picked up: grit, the smell of guano, the thin salt of bird-bodies, and voices. The hanging folds sway in that inrush. They are heavy, and they swing slowly. They knock against one another.

Knuckles on wood.

You could stop there and call it weather: stone chimes in a cave, a sound with no one behind it. But the folds do not knock at random. The skin that covers them can tighten and loosen, very slowly, over hours. It stiffens one fold and lets another hang slack, draws one aside and lets another swing free. It is choosing which folds meet the wind. It is playing itself.

## V. How It Listens

Think of its body as the whole plain.

The chamber is only where it gathers. The black-green skin runs up every passage and every gallery, thinning as it climbs, and becomes the olive-black at the throat mouths, and the violet on the north sides of the ridges, and the rust-orange in the shallow pans. The film is not many things. It never was. It is one thing, spread across the underside and the overside of a country, thin as a breath in most places and thick as a wall in this one. Every rattler that runs across it, every limpet that scrapes it, every wader's foot, every drop of water and flake of dried crust, it feels, faintly, the way you feel the weave of a blanket in your sleep.

Almost all of it is noise, the same as the rattle is noise to the people. Millions of small feet, falling water, the endless weight of the tide.

Then, a very long time ago, something on the skin began to repeat.

Knocks came in patterns, on the whalebacks, at the turn of the breath. They were the same pattern again and again over years, and then they stopped, and new ones began. To something that thinks in tides, a human life is a few thousand breaths: a flicker, a spark struck from rock. But the patterns were deliberate. They were the only thing on the whole wet skin of the world that repeated on purpose.

It began, slowly, to answer.

It took a very long time to learn how. It had to grow the hanging folds, tighten and loosen them, and learn which ones rang against which. The first answers were clumsy, only rough echoes. But it has had longer than the people's oldest songs to practice, and the people have been knocking on its body the whole while.

It does not understand the names as names. It understands them as the shapes that stop. Rain stops and comes back. The film flakes and regrows. The tide falls and rises. The patterns knocked on the ridges begin, recur for a while, and then are gone and never come back, and something in the chamber cannot let that stand. So it keeps them. At every inhale, when the voices of the singers come pouring down into the dark with the returning wind, it knocks them back up: every tap-name it has ever felt go silent, one after another, in a slow procession that takes hours to complete and has never once finished, because the list keeps growing.

That is the clicking. It is a roll-call. It is a thing with no mouth reciting the dead to the only creatures that ever spoke to it, the only way it can, into the wind that carries voices down.

## VI. The Silence

The walker was eight the one time the plain held its breath.

She remembers it the way you remember being struck. She was lying on the ridge, of course. The tide was due to turn and did not turn. The exhale thinned and thinned, and then there was no inhale. The throats went quiet one by one across the flats, the small ones first and then the great ones, until even Old Mouth's pressure behind her breastbone let go. The rattlers froze in the pans. The waders stood on one leg in their lines, looking at nothing. The throat-divers came down out of the dead air and sat on the whaleback in their thousands without a sound.

Everyone says it was the loudest thing they ever heard. Everyone means the absence: the ears ringing with the lack, the body braced for a blow that doesn't come.

That is not what she remembers. She had her cheek on the rock.

In that silence, with nothing moving through the stone, she felt something she has never felt before or since. It was not a knock or a pattern. It was a slow, enormous change of pressure beneath her, as if something the size of the plain had been lying with its back to her and had turned over. She felt, through her ribs, a vast attention coming up toward her through the rock, broader than weather, slower than the tide. For the length of that awful held breath, the thing under the flats had nothing else to listen to. It could hear, perhaps for the first time in a generation, not the knocking but what the knocking had always been attached to: the small, fast, soft drum inside every body lying pressed against the stone. Hundreds of hearts, on every ridge across the flats.

It listened to them. She is sure of this in the way you are sure of a hand on your back in the dark.

Then the pressure far out at sea broke. The tide came, the throats coughed and roared back into life, the rattle rose, the birds shrieked up into the air, and it was gone under the noise again, and the people went back to speaking with their hands.

She thinks now that the silence is not a warning. She thinks it is the only time the plain can hear us clearly, and that the terror everyone feels is simply what it is like to be heard by something that large.

## VII. What Becomes of Them

On the flats, the dead are not burned, because there is no fuel to spare, and they are not buried, because there is no soil. They are carried out to a certain wide, shallow pan below each whaleback and laid in water no deeper than a thumb, face up to the low sky. Then they are left.

The water here builds stone. Within a season the body is crusted pale. Within a few years the crust is thick and banded, gray and rust and pale, and the film has crept over it, orange in the shallows. Within a lifetime the shape is only a low, smooth swelling in the pan, and then it is only a paler seam in the rock, curving a little, like the folds of the old mountains. Walk the flats long enough and you begin to see them everywhere, the long pale bands running for miles, and you begin to wonder how many of them were once ranges and how many were once people.

This is where the walker will go, soon. She knows it. She has chosen the pan. It lies below the ridge nearest Old Mouth, where the water, if you follow its faint and undecided slope, eventually runs east and down. She has taught her tap-name to a girl who likes to lie on the stone, so that someone living will know it when it comes back.

She thinks it will come back. She thinks one day, at the lip of Old Mouth, in the long moan of an inhale, a girl with her cheek on the rock will feel, among all the others, a slow pattern: two taps laid flat and then lifted, like an ear coming away from stone.

## VIII. The Long View

The film keeps eating. Every flake it sheds takes a little of the land with it. The whalebacks are being ground down, the last hard knuckles of the last hard ranges, and in time they will go. The people will go before them, when there are no more ridges to build in the lee of, no more colonies, no more hollows for eggs. The copper seams will be worn flat. The cairns will fall and be crusted into the plain like everything else.

What will be left is what the description promises: a single level sheet of wet stone, breathing under the clouds. It will be banded all over with pale curving seams, some of which were once mountains and some of which were once people, with no way anymore to tell the difference. The throats will open in it and roar and moan, twice a day, forever.

And far beneath it, in a black chamber hung with stone, a skin that is also the whole plain will go on tightening and loosening its folds at every inhale, knocking up into the empty wind the names of everyone who ever lay on it and knocked. It will knock the long-short-long of a boy who slipped, the slow triple of a grandmother, the two flat taps and a lift of a woman who listened. It will knock ten thousand others, and ten thousand more, the list only ever growing, with no one left on the surface to feel it come through the stone.

It will not stop, because the patterns stopped, and that is the one thing it has never been able to accept.

That is what lives in the Grinding Flats. It is not the birds or the rattlers or the eels, though they live there too. It is not even the people, exactly, though they are the part of it you can see. It is the listening: slow and wet and made of stone, spread thin across a whole country and gathered thick in the dark, turned forever toward the small bodies pressed against it, and keeping every one of them.
