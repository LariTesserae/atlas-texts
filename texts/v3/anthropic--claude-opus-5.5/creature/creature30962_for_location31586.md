# Who Lives in the Knot

## The one you hear first

Start with the sound at the end, the shell blade cutting somewhere up the spiral. Start with whoever is holding it.

Say it is a woman, though in the dark that matters less than it would above. Her name is two presses and a drag, laid between the shoulder blades with the heel of the hand. It means something like *the one who comes back along the rope*. She was given it as a child, when she got lost in the eastern quarter for a day and a half and found her way out by counting knots.

She is not old, but she is older than most. Her hands are large, as the fen-people say all Knot hands become. The palms are callused into something like hoof. The fingertips have stayed so soft they can read a rope-knot through a mitten of mud. Faint pale lines run along the insides of her forearms, the backs of her knees, and the long muscle of her neck. Every tenant who has lived here long carries them. They are rootlet-tracks, where the root found her in her sleep and was pulled away before it could settle. In the dark no one sees them. Everyone can feel them. A lover's fingers find them and trace them the way you would trace the veins in a leaf.

She walks her stretch of the spiral every waking shift: tier nine to tier fourteen, the western arm, forty-one rooms. Six of them have ever been slept in during her lifetime. She cuts all forty-one. She puts her face into each doorway and breathes out slowly to see how the room answers. A healthy room takes the breath and gives back a faint cool pull from somewhere deeper. A closing room holds the breath against her face like a hand.

She is not afraid of dying in the Knot. She has already chosen which lower room she would like, when the time comes, and has told three people by touch. What she fears is simpler. She fears being the one who forgets a room, the one who walks past a doorway on a tired day and doesn't feel how narrow it has become. She fears the room going out behind her like a held breath, and someone else, a season later, tying the knot for it on the long rope.

That is one of the tenants. There are fifty-nine others, and they are like her and not like her, in the ordinary ways people are.

## The tenants, more closely

They are shaped by the place, the way any people are.

Their skin twitches. It happens constantly and without their noticing: small ripples along the arms and shoulders that shed springtails and mites the way a horse's flank sheds flies. Children born in the Knot learn it before they learn to walk. A visitor from outside is recognizable for months because he holds still, and the grain of small bodies settles on him and begins to move in.

They breathe downward. It is a habit so deep it has become posture. Their chins tuck and they exhale into their own chests, into the hollow of the collarbone, into the cloth of their clothing. Breath is what the root wants, and a tenant spends it the way a traveler in winter spends warmth: close to the body, never flung. Only children and the dying breathe out freely. When a tenant does it, others take it as a sign of great trust, or great grief, or both.

They hear in textures. The hiss that visitors describe as one sound, like snow on snow, is to a tenant a field of distinctions. There is the fine dry sift of amphipods on vertical wood and the softer patter of the same animals on a ceiling. There is the particular silence of harvestmen gripping tight because something large is moving in the next room. Tenants can tell who is walking three tiers up by how the hiss parts ahead of them.

The children who are born here have never seen light. When they are old enough, which the tenants reckon by the size of the hand and not the count of years, they are taken out once through the flooded passage. They go up into the fen on a clear night, so the sky will not be too much. Most of them come back down. They say, to the extent they can say it, that up there everything is too far away. The air doesn't hold you. Nothing is keeping anything open. It all just stays, and that is frightening.

## But

But the tenants would not say that they are what lives in the Knot.

Asked, if you could ask in the slow touch-language, pressing questions into someone's back, they would give you the word that means both *home* and *the work of keeping home open*. They would say they are the work. They would say the Knot keeps them, not the other way around. They are warm, and they breathe, and the root grows toward them. They cut the root back. In that pushing and cutting, a shape is held open that would not otherwise exist. They are not the inhabitant, they would say. They are the breath going in and out.

So look past them.

## The root

There are three roots you can name, and one you can't.

Alder is the bulk of it: dark, fibrous, quick-growing, bitter-smelling when cut. Most of the daily work is alder. Bog myrtle threads through it, finer and aromatic, and a room cut mostly through myrtle smells faintly of resin and warm leaves, a smell no one in the Knot has any other reason to know. The children born below call it *the up smell* without knowing why.

The fourth root is the pale one.

It runs mostly through the lower tiers, near the water, around the rooms where the old and ill are given. It is colder to the touch than the others. Its surface is smooth as a tooth and gives back no grain of small bodies, because the amphipods don't graze it and the mites don't cross it. It grows slowly, much more slowly than the alder. When it does reach for a sleeper, it doesn't come as a hair-fine rootlet that pulls away easily. It comes as a single pale thread, and the thread doesn't want to leave. Tenants who have been found by it carry a different mark: a small cold spot on the skin that never quite warms, even in the heaps of winter sleeping.

No one knows how old it is. The fen-people above have no word for it. The tenants' word for it is the same as their word for the long rope of lost rooms.

The foxfire at the bottom of the spiral, that faint thinning of the dark, glows only in pale root. It glows only where the pale root has been dead a long time, and it is dead only where it wrapped a given room and was then, generations later, cut back into.

## The rooms

Look past the root, too.

Walk into one of the empty rooms, one of the three hundred and sixty that no one sleeps in. The cutter has just been through. The walls are raw and sharp-edged and bleeding cold sap. Moths lie dead in the corners in soft drifts. Your breath-mist hangs where you leave it. Nothing is here.

Stay a while. Stay long enough to stop listening for the tenant behind you and the eels below, and even for the hiss.

The room has a shape. It is not only the shape the blade made. The blade followed the grain the root gave it, and the root grew along the draughts, and the draughts ran along the shape of every other room. This room is where it is and as big as it is because four hundred others are where they are. Its air moves the way it does because of a passage six tiers down. If that passage closed, this room would feel it. The draught would falter and the pull at the back of the room would go slack, and a tenant putting her face in the doorway would feel the breath held against her like a hand.

The room has a name. Every room does. The names are not labels. The tenants' word for *room* is also their word for *reason*, and they mean it. This room is here because of that one. That one is here because of the stair. The stair is here because it has always been here. The whole is here because the tenants believe, and have never been proven wrong, that a simple hollow closes and a complex one holds.

So the room is not empty. It is full of a reason. And the reason is not its own. It belongs to the whole.

Stay longer. After many days in the dark, the tenants say, you begin to feel it: the city as a single held thing, a pattern of absences in a living mass, and each absence leaning very slightly against all the others. It is the way a word leans against the words around it, or the way one knot in a net takes a little of the strain from every other knot.

That, the tenants say, if they are pressed, is closer to what lives in the Knot. It is the shape. It is the argument itself, four hundred reasons long, made out of nothing, held in place by sixty people with shell blades and a root that hesitates at complexity. It has no body. Its body is where the body isn't. It has no voice, only draughts, the slow change of a room's breath, and the way a closing stair makes the whole spiral feel faintly wrong under your feet. It is not alive the way a shrew is alive. But it is kept, and it keeps, and it is always nearly gone, and it has never gone. They do not know what else to call that.

## The rooms no one cut

Here is the part the tenants do not tell visitors, and do not often tell each other.

Sometimes, walking the spiral, a cutter finds a doorway that should not be there.

It happens rarely at first. You notice it the way you notice a stair has one more step than you remembered. A cutter's hand, running along a wall she has cut for twenty years, falls into an opening. It is a passage, clean-sided, the right height, the right width, breathing properly, with a faint cool pull from farther in. Behind it lies a room.

There is no knot for it on any rope.

There are no blade marks. The walls are not raw and sharp the way cut walls are. They are smooth, as though the root had simply never grown there, as though it had drawn back and held back and left the space. The room smells of nothing: not green, not vinegar, not the warm sweetness of a room gone wrong. It is cold. Where the walls are pale root, which they almost always are, they give back no grain of small bodies at all.

The first time it happened in living memory, the finder assumed she had forgotten the room. That was the thing she feared most, so she told no one and quietly cut it, and named it, and tied a knot for it. She added a scratch of new blade marks over the smooth wall so it would be hers. Then it happened to someone else, and again, and it began to be spoken of. The one who comes back along the rope has found two. Both were in the lower tiers, both near the given rooms, both along seams of pale root that had once, generations back, been cut open where the dead were laid.

The tenants have argued about this in the only way they argue, which is slowly and by touch, over whole winters.

Some say the rooms are old ones, lost before the ropes were kept, reopening on their own. Some say it is the water, dissolving softwood in some seam they can't reach. Some say nothing at all, and cut the new rooms, and knot them, and go on.

But a few say this, pressing it carefully into each other's backs in the heaped sleep of midwinter, with the ice sealed over the entrance and the hiss nearly gone:

The pale root has taken breath for longer than anyone has kept ropes. It has taken the old and the ill, one at a time, in lower rooms, with withy screens across the doors. It has grown through them slowly, and it does not let go. Those people spent their whole lives in the argument. They walked it every day. They held the shape of four hundred rooms in their hands and their feet and their slow downward breath.

Maybe the root has learned it.

Maybe the root that grows toward breath, and hesitates at shape, has taken in enough of the ones who made the shape that it has begun, very slowly, to make the shape itself. Maybe it grows toward the city now. Maybe it draws back where a room ought to be, as if it too understood that the hollow must stay hollow. Maybe the argument has found its way into the thing it was always argued against.

## So, what lives there

Let it be all of these, one inside the other.

The swarm lives there, the amphipods and harvestmen and eels, the frogs with stopped hearts, the shrews with needle voices. They are the city's weather, its dust, its constant faint hiss.

The tenants live there, sixty of them, twitching and breathing downward, reading ropes in the dark with their soft enormous fingertips. They are the work. They are the breath going in and out.

The root lives there, alder and myrtle and the pale one without a name. It is the body, and the mouth, and the patient, attentive pressure of the living world closing around anything warm.

And between them lives the shape. It is four hundred rooms, most of them empty, every one of them a reason. It is a pattern made of absence, older than any wall in it and younger than all of them, recut every day and never quite the same. For as long as anyone has kept ropes, it has been held open from outside, by hands. It has been kept rather than keeping itself.

Now, in the lowest tiers, in the cold smooth seams of the pale root, near the rooms where the dead were laid, it may be starting to hold itself open from within.

It is not finished. It may never be. The tenants still cut every room, including the ones they didn't make. They still tie the knots. They say, if you ask, that nothing has changed.

But if you swam in through the black water and came up gasping, and stood in the Knot long enough to stop hearing your own heart, you might notice two sounds from up the spiral rather than one.

The first is a shell blade, soft and patient, cutting root.

The second is lower, somewhere near the water, where the foxfire makes its faint thinning of the dark. It is a sound like a knuckle cracking, which the tenants have always said is the root growing.

Lately, a few of them have started to say it is the root drawing back.
