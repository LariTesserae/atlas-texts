# Those Who Live in the Undercrown

## I. The Threaders, as They First Appear

You meet them before you see them. A line of cord runs taut beside you, strung between strands where no strand-tissue grew it, twisted in a spiral too regular to be the plant's own. Then a knot. Then, a long way off in the flat white glare, a small dark crouch on the underside of a bridge that turns out to be a person.

They are pale. That is the first thing. It is not the pallor of sickness. It is the look of a people who have never been shadowed. Their skin has the faint, even, milky tone of the walls, and their hair, which they wear long and bound in cord, runs from ash to near-white. Their eyes are the startling part. Having grown up in a light with no direction, they have irises of a very light gray, almost silver, and they narrow them constantly. When a threader looks at you, you feel squinted at from inside a snowfield.

They are small, narrow, and long-limbed, with hands that look too large for their bodies. The fingers are long and thick at the knuckle, and the calluses run in bands where cord has passed through them ten thousand times. Their feet are bare more often than not, even in the cold, because a bare foot reads a strand better than a wrapped one. The soles are thick as hide and gray with the scar of frost.

They wear layers of beaten fiber, felted and pale, and over them capes of husk-plate stitched in overlapping scales. The capes clatter softly when they move. It is one of the few sounds in the Undercrown that is always near, and a threader listening in the silence can tell by that clatter who is coming and how tired they are.

They move in groups of eight or twelve or twenty, roped together much of the time, strung out along the strands like beads on a line. When they rest, they hang. They sleep in their husk-pod shelters slung beneath the bridges, rocking with the breathing winds, and when the wind is gusting the whole pod swings in slow arcs over a drop that has no bottom anyone has seen.

They do not fear the drop. That is not quite right either. They have a relationship with it the way sailors do with deep water. They call it *the Open*, and they speak of it as a place, not an absence. People go into the Open. Sometimes they come back.

## II. Breath-Reckoning

Threaders do not count days. There is no night here, no sunrise, only the slow swelling and slackening of the light as clouds cross the far-off stalk-tips. What they count is breaths.

An inhale is a *draw*: the long sinking wind, the cold coming down, dust and pollen and snow falling through the columns. An exhale is a *give*: the push back upward, the membranes bowing, the rigging of the strands leaning all one way. A draw and a give together make a *breath*, and a breath is four days, or five, or seven. It varies, and the variation is what they watch.

A child is so many breaths old. A journey is so many breaths long. A person who has lived a thousand breaths is old. There are songs, long ones, whose verses are meant to be sung one per breath, so that finishing the song is a kind of calendar. Some of these songs take years to finish, and every group sings them a little differently, and the difference is how you know who someone's grandmothers were.

When breaths come irregular and the storms rise, threaders say the Undercrown is *dreaming*. They tie themselves down and wait it out in the swinging pods, and they do not travel, and they tell stories, because it is bad luck to be silent while the hollow dreams.

## III. The Coldreaders

Every group has one or two whose work is to feel the cold.

They are called by a word that means something like *the one who is touched first*. Their gift is partly training and partly temperament. They are the people who notice the chill on the back of the neck a moment before anyone else. They go barefoot always. They sleep at the edge of the pod, nearest the wall, with one hand pressed against the tissue.

A coldreader can tell by the quality of the chill what kind of growth is coming. A sharp, needling cold that raises frost on the eyelashes is a tip moving fast: a new strand, dangerous, best stepped back from. A broad, soaking cold that seems to rise through the soles is a wall thickening, a chamber about to close. Follow it the wrong way and you are sealed in. A cold that comes and goes, breathing, is a membrane thinning. Wait a few breaths and a new hollow will open, and the first to enter it will find the richest moth-swarms and the freshest tissue.

Coldreaders live hard. The cold gets into them. Their fingers and toes whiten and stiffen and are often lost by middle age, and the best of them are recognizable by their missing digits the way old sailors are recognized by rope-scars. It is an honor. A coldreader with no toes left is listened to above all others.

They are the threaders' map, since there are no maps. They are, in a way, the threaders' priests.

## IV. The Warm Inversion

This needs explaining, because it is the center of how threaders think.

In the Undercrown, cold is life. Cold means the plant is growing, and growth means food, water, passage, the future. Warmth means stillness, dead tissue, resorption, the end.

And threaders are warm.

They know it. It is the oldest thing they know about themselves. Every threader carries a small, stubborn heat inside a world where heat means death. Their breath fogs white. Their bodies soften frost where they sleep. When a threader dies, the corpse stays warm a little while, and then it is cold like everything else. Death, for them, is when you finally match the world.

So they have an ambivalent reverence for warmth. It is theirs, it is precious, and it is a little shameful, like a smell. They cup it. They keep it inside the pods. The lamp, the small orange flame of longwalker fat, is the most sacred object a group owns, and it is also treated with a kind of wary tenderness, like a sick child or a live coal carried in the hands. The lamp is never allowed to go out. It is also never allowed outside the pod. To carry fire into the Open is a curse, the worst they have.

They greet each other, when groups meet on the strands, by pressing palms and saying a phrase that means roughly *you are still warm*. It is both a blessing and a gentle joke. It means *you are alive*. It also means *you are not yet part of this*.

## V. The Truth Beneath the Inversion

For a long time, if you traveled with them, you would believe the obvious thing: the threaders follow the growth, because the growth feeds them.

Watch longer.

Watch where the new strands go. Watch the coldreader sleeping with her hand against the wall, and watch the frost bloom there, under her palm, over the course of a breath. Watch how the tissue around a threader shelter thickens faster than tissue anywhere else on the bridge. Watch how a strand, groping out into the Open with its frost-mist trailing, bends a few degrees toward the orange glow of a pod lashed on a far wall, as if it smells the lamp.

The plant grows by drawing heat. It is starving for heat. Down here, with the sun's warmth left behind on the surface, there is almost nothing to draw from: thin cold air, cold tissue, the faint warmth of sap. And through this vast cold hollow move small, bright, stubborn engines of heat on two legs, carrying fire.

The threaders follow the growth. The growth also follows the threaders.

The old coldreaders know this, or half-know it. They do not say it plainly. They say *the Undercrown loves us*, and the younger threaders take it for poetry. It is not poetry. When a group stays too long in one place, it is not only that the ground grows unstable beneath them. It is that the ground grows *toward* them, and around them, reaching for their heat, and a group that stays a whole season will find its pods furred with frost and its cords grown into the tissue and the walls thickening in close, closing, closing, the way a hand closes around a coal.

That is why they keep moving. They are not nomads because the land is unreliable. They are nomads because they are loved by something that would eat them.

## VI. The Dead

Threaders do not keep their dead.

When someone dies, the group washes the body with sap-water from a wound-pool and wraps it in cord, knotted with the person's name and their breath-count and the verses of the long songs they knew. Then they carry the body to the end of a young strand, one still growing, still frosted, still reaching out into the Open. They lay it at the base of the tip, lash it lightly, and leave.

They do not look back. That is the rule.

But you can look back. And if you do, you will see the strand change. The frost thickens around the body in the first breath. The tip, which was wavering, straightens. Within a few days the strand has grown past the body and around it, and the body is inside the strand, a pale shape under pale tissue, and the strand has surged forward farther than it would have otherwise, sometimes all the way across to a far wall where it roots and becomes a bridge.

Threaders have a saying: *the strongest bridges are built on the dead.* It is literally true. The greatest bridges in the Undercrown, the ones thick as rivers, the ones longwalkers travel, the ones that last years instead of seasons, often have a threader somewhere at the root of them, the last warmth of a body spent in one long reach across the void.

This is the archive. This is why old knots appear in cut tissue, and old knives, and pieces of shelters. The plant takes in warmth, and what comes with warmth, it keeps. It carries the dead slowly through itself, strand to wall to strand, and when tissue splits and something ancient is revealed, it is often a person first. The cords around them are knotted with a name no one living knows, in a knot-code a few generations out of date, with the verses of songs that are still sung, a little differently.

Threaders who find such a body do not grieve. They read the knots aloud, sing a verse, and let the tissue close again. They consider it a visit.

## VII. The Frost-Moths, Closer

The moths are not simple.

At a distance they are a white swirl at the growing tips. Close up, each is the size of a thumbnail, with four wings of such fine, frost-feathered scale that they seem made of rime. They do not fly so much as hang, riding the mist that growth exhales. What they eat is the mist itself. The chilled air around a growing tip is thick with something the plant breathes out as it works, a sugary vapor that condenses on their feathered wings, and they lick it off each other.

They have no warmth at all. A frost-moth in the hand is as cold as the strand, and if you close your fingers around it, it dies of your heat in a few seconds and goes limp and wet.

Threaders catch them in fine nets and eat them by the handful, raw and crackling, sweet with the plant's breath. They are the staple food. Children learn to net them before they learn to tie most knots.

But threaders also watch them for another reason. Moths follow the cold. Where the cold goes, the moths go, and so a spiral of moths is the plant's intention made visible. When the moths all drift one way in a long, slow river through the Open, the old ones say the Undercrown is *turning its head*, and they turn to look where it is looking, and sometimes, far off, they see the dark shapes.

## VIII. The Longwalkers, Closer

Longwalkers are warm.

This is the thing that makes them strange. They are the only large warm creatures besides the threaders themselves. Their bodies are great pale drums of fat and slow gut, slung high on legs that fold in five places and end in splayed, fibrous pads that cling to strand-tissue the way the plant clings to itself. They graze the soft outer rind of the bridges, scraping with broad keratin plates, and they are always warm, warm enough that their bellies steam in the cold air, warm enough that frost never forms on their backs.

Where a longwalker has walked, the bridge thickens behind it.

Threaders hunt them only rarely, when a group is hungry or a lamp needs fat. A single longwalker keeps a lamp lit for a hundred breaths. They take it with ceremony. They ask forgiveness not of the animal but of the bridge.

The question of where longwalkers go when a bridge falls has an answer, though few threaders have seen it. They go with the bridge. When a strand tears loose and begins its long tumble into the Open, the longwalker does not leap. It folds its legs, all five joints of each, tight against the body, and grips the falling strand, and rides it down. The fall takes minutes. The landing, somewhere far below where no one has ever gone, must be catastrophic.

Some threaders believe the longwalkers survive the landing. Some believe they do not, and that this is the point. A great warm body, driven deep into the lower tissue by the force of the fall, would be a feast of heat for the plant, a sudden surge of growth somewhere in the depths of the Undercrown. Perhaps the longwalkers are the plant's way of carrying warmth downward. Perhaps it has bred them for it.

The threaders call a falling longwalker *the long gift*, and when they see one they stop and watch in silence until it is gone.

## IX. The Dark Things

And now the dark shapes in the Open.

They are the only dark things in the entire hollow. They drift far from any strand, riding the breathing winds, and they never come close. They are large. From a distance it is hard to say how large, because there is nothing to measure them against in the empty white, but a threader who once saw one pass near a bridge said it was longer than her whole group roped end to end.

They are dark because darkness drinks light.

Everywhere in the Undercrown, the pale tissue scatters the light and gives back nothing, stays cold. The dark things take the light in. They soak it up from every side at once, the shadowless glow pouring into them from all directions, and they turn it to heat. They are, in the only way possible down here, warm. Warm and warm and warmer, riding the winds, gathering the light that the plant itself cannot use.

They are seeds.

Or they are something the plant makes that is between a seed and an animal. The threaders argue about this. The coldreaders do not. A coldreader who has stood on a bridge while a dark one drifted past will tell you that as it passed, the strand beneath her feet went cold, suddenly, brutally, cold enough to crack, and that the frost bloomed on the tissue facing the dark thing, a whole long flank of the bridge furring white in a moment, reaching, reaching, like a sleeper turning toward a fire.

Each dark thing is a hoard of heat. Each is a promise of growth. When one finally settles somewhere, and the threaders say this happens only once or twice in a lifetime, it lands against a wall and the wall *erupts*. A whole region of the Undercrown blooms around it in a single breath: strands flung out in every direction, chambers ballooning open, frost in blizzards, moths in clouds so thick the light dims. The dark thing is consumed, drawn down to cold nothing, and in its place there is a new country.

Threaders call this a *kindling*. Groups will travel for years toward one when word of it passes along the knot-lines. A new-bloomed region is rich beyond anything: fresh wound-pools everywhere, moths enough to eat without netting, walls soft and new. Those who reach a kindling feast for a season. Then they leave, fast, before the new country's hunger turns on them.

## X. The Carvers

Which leaves the strangest remains: the flat carved plates of unfamiliar material, the spirals of what might be writing.

The threaders have a story about who made them.

Long ago, the story goes, there were people who came *down*. They came from the surface, from the cold plain with the glassy reeds, and they came down the stalks, down the long throats of light, lowered on cords that were not plant-fiber, carrying things that were not husk. They were dark, the story says. Not dark like the seeds, but darker than threaders, skin that had known a single sun in a single direction, skin that had shadows. They brought fire, a great deal of it. They built warm places. They stayed still.

They were loved very much.

The story does not say what happened to them. It does not have to. Threaders know what happens to warm things that stay still in the Undercrown. The carved plates circulate through the tissue, surfacing and sinking again over centuries, and some of them are surely chips of whatever the carvers built, and some of them are surely things the carvers carried, and some of the spiral writings, when a strand splits open and reveals them, run around and around a pale shape inside the tissue that is the size of a person and has been there a very long time.

Some threaders believe they are the descendants of the carvers: that a few survived, and learned to move, and paled, and became threaders over a thousand generations of breath. This is the more common belief among coldreaders. It explains, they say, why threaders still have so much fire in them for so cold a world. It explains why they are loved.

A threader who finds a carved plate ties it to the lamp-pod as a charm. When she does, she presses her palm to it and says *you are still warm*, and she means it as a joke, and a blessing, and an apology.

## XI. What Actually Lives There

So what lives in the Undercrown?

The plant lives there, of course, or rather is there. It is the hollow and the light and the weather. It is the only permanent resident, and even it is never the same twice. It is old in the way a river is old: nothing in it older than a few decades, the whole of it older than memory.

The moths live there, cold creatures of cold breath, the plant's exhalation given wings.

The longwalkers live there, warm drums carrying heat along the bridges and, at the end, down into the depths.

The dark seeds live there, or pass through it, drinking light and turning it into the promise of new country.

And the threaders live there, small and pale and stubbornly warm, moving and moving and moving, feeding the thing that feeds them. They are loved and fled from in the same gesture. They are carried after death into bridges that will hold their children. They keep a single flame lit in a husk pod over a bottomless drop, never carrying it out into the Open, knowing it is precisely what the whole vast bright cold hollow is reaching for.

If you stand with them on a strand in the middle of all that white, the coldreader will take your hand, feel the heat in it, and smile with her few remaining teeth. She will point at the frost blooming on the tissue at your feet, where you have been standing too long. *See*, she'll say. *It knows you're here.*

Then she will tug the cord and the group will move on, their husk-capes clattering softly in the silence. Behind them, where they rested, the strand will already be thickening, reaching out into the Open in the direction they have gone.
