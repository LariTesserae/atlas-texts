# Those Who Live in the Pressure Basin

## The Easy Answer

The first answer anyone gives is *nothing*. Nothing lives in the basin, or rather the basin lives, and there is no room in it for anything else. You stand on the rim and watch the slopes crawl and the chimneys rise and fail, and the sheer volume of the place's own life seems to leave no space for tenants. A house that rebuilds itself every week has no need of anyone to keep it.

This answer is wrong, but it is wrong in an instructive way, and it is worth holding onto for a while, because everything that does live here has been shaped by it. Every creature in the basin lives inside something that is also alive, moving, and indifferent. None of them has a home that will still be standing in a month. They have learned to live the way a sailor lives on a deck: never quite still, reading the motion underfoot, trusting the ship and not the plank.

And they are almost all, in one way or another, carriers of salt.

## Salt

There is no stone in the basin. This is the fact the whole household turns on.

Everything the basin makes is made of air and water and light, and those are enough to build wood and moss and leaf in astonishing quantity. But a living thing needs more than that. It needs traces of what stone gives: calcium, iron, potassium, the small hard elements that no amount of fog supplies. In an ordinary forest these come up from the bedrock through the roots. Here there is no bedrock within reach, only the deep felt of the basin's own dead, going down and down.

So the basin hoards. What little mineral it has, carried in over ages as dust on the wind, in the bodies of lost birds, in the boots and bones of travelers, it never lets go. It passes that mineral from body to body, from column to mat to mill and back, in a loop as closed as its water. The glove you drop is taken in within five days not out of hunger for the leather, but for the salt of your sweat in it. The tent pegs vanish because they are iron. The mat is not devouring you. It is *collecting*.

And the creatures that live here are, all of them, part of that collection. They are how the salt moves.

## The Crown Beetles

Start with the smallest you are likely to find.

When a ribbed column dies, it stops venting from the top down. The steam in its crown cools, and as the last of it condenses and dries, it leaves a crust behind: pale, gritty, faintly bitter, like the rime inside a kettle. Everything the column drew up from the mat with its water, every dissolved trace, is left there on the inside of its five drooping arms as the water leaves. A dead chimney's crown is the richest place in the basin, a small white mine at the top of a falling tower.

The beetles know this. They are the color of the crust, the size of a fingernail, and they arrive in the crown of a dying column within hours. No one has worked out how they find it. They may feel the change in its heat. They may taste the air as the last plume thins. They live there for the day or two the column takes to lean and fold, scraping the crust with jaws like fine files, and their bodies grow heavy and pale and slightly glittering with what they have eaten. When the crown finally sinks into the moss, they climb down it into the mat, and most of them are never seen again.

Those who have opened the mat near a fallen column say the beetles burrow down and lay their eggs deep, near the water channels, and then die there, and that the moss in those spots comes up a brighter green than anywhere nearby. They are, in effect, taking the salt that the column wrung out of the ground and putting it back underground. They are very small. There are a great many of them. Over the length of the basin's age, they may have moved more than the mills.

A few don't burrow. A few, each season, are eaten.

## The Moss-Wren

The bird has no real name. Travelers who notice it at all call it a wren, because it is small and round and brown, and because nothing else fits. It is closer in color to old moss than any other living thing in the basin, and it holds so still, and flies so quietly, that most visitors never see one until they have been here for days. Then they see them everywhere: a grey-brown shape at the base of a column, a flicker between joints, a small body pressed flat against a stem.

That flatness is the key to it. The moss-wren listens with its breast.

It chooses where to nest by clinging to the side of a living column and laying its body against the segments, feathers parted, skin to the wood. Through its breastbone it feels the hydraulic shudder of the column's joints swelling and locking, and it can tell, from the rhythm of that shudder, how long the column has left. A young column pulses fast and hard. An old one pulses slow, with small hesitations. A dying one has a stutter in it that the bird can feel days before any eye would see the lean begin. The wren wants neither the youngest nor the oldest. It wants a column in its steady middle age, with thirty or forty days left to stand, long enough to lay, brood, and fledge a clutch in the warm dead space between two pressure-joints.

It is almost never wrong. When it is, the column folds with the nest inside it, and the moss takes eggs and young together, and the salt goes back into the mat.

The wren's eggs are the only hard, white, mineral things made in the basin. The shells are thin but real, and to make them the hen needs calcium she cannot get from seeds or moss. She gets it from the beetles. In the days before she lays, she hunts the crowns of dying columns, taking the fat glittering beetles from the crust. The males bring them to her. It is the only time the wrens seem to hurry. When the chicks hatch, the parents do something no traveler has quite been able to explain: they break the empty shells into small pieces and carry them, one fragment at a time, to the black lichen on the north slope, and press them into the cracks between its plates.

The lichen closes over them within a week.

The birds don't sing. It has been suggested that they cannot, that the basin long ago selected against anything that made a sound, but it may be simpler than that. They spend their lives with their chests pressed against the pulse of the place. Perhaps singing would only make it harder to hear.

## The Numberless

Below the scale of the bird there are others, and most of them no one has named.

In the leathery rosettes that hold water, the cupfuls that are sweet and cold and sometimes skinned with ice, there live small translucent things like threads of glass. You have to hold the water up to the light to see them. They freeze solid most nights and thaw each noon, and they seem to take no harm from it. They feed on whatever the rosette's leaves give off into the water, and the rosette in turn is fed by what they leave behind. When a traveler drinks from a rosette, as most eventually do, the threads go down with the water, and they are harmless, and the traveler carries them for a day or two and then leaves them, somewhere, in the moss. The rosettes grow thickest along the routes that people walk. No one planted them.

In the band of black lichen live things finer still, hair-thin, reddish, visible only when the frost comes. On cold mornings, when the frost ferns form on the lichen all pointing toward the center, you can sometimes see, if you kneel and look closely, that at the root of each fern there is one of these tiny creatures, curled, lying in the direction of the frost. Whether they point because the frost does, or the frost points because they do, is not a question anyone has answered. The prickle in your arm when you touch the lichen may come partly from them. They are always oriented. They are, so far as anyone can tell, never asleep.

And in the spore sheds, when a hillside releases and the brown haze rises and dims the light, there are things riding the spores: winged specks so small they cannot be caught, that drift out over the basin and settle wherever the haze thins. The places where they land grow new columns the following season. Some travelers think they are not separate animals at all, but part of the spores themselves, the spores' way of choosing where to fall.

## The One in the Lanes

Then there is the large one.

Accounts of it disagree, as accounts of anything seen through fog at dawn will. It is always low, always dark, always heavy through the shoulders. It is perhaps the length of a horse and not as tall. It moves along the wet lanes, the shifting dark hollows where the water channels run close under the surface and the moss grows thick as fur, and it moves slowly, and it does not look up.

For a long time the assumption was that it grazed: that it was the basin's one great herbivore, cropping the thick lane-moss as it went. But no one who has followed its trails has found the moss cropped. The trails are pressed, not bitten, and they close behind it within the hour. They close so neatly that the few people who have come close enough to see its underside believe they know why. Its belly and flanks are furred not with hair but with moss. Living moss, rooted in its hide, the same moss as the lanes. Where it lies down, it does not crush the mat so much as join it. When it rises, the lane is healed by its own kind.

So it was not a grazer. Then it was thought to be a burrower, because the trails so often ended at the lanes' deepest, wettest places, where it seemed to push its head down into the mat and stay that way for a long time. But nothing was dug. Those who later probed those places with long sticks found only the water channel below, and the channel ran a little differently afterward, a little straighter, as if something had cleared it.

Now the belief among those who have stayed longest is that it does not feed on the lanes at all. It *keeps* them. It walks the basin's hidden water, listening down into it the way the wren listens into a column, and where the channels choke, it opens them. Where they wander, it turns them. The lanes shift over weeks, the description says, as the channels below reroute themselves. It may be more accurate to say that they are rerouted, slowly, by something walking above them that knows where the water ought to go.

And it goes to the mills.

Every account agrees on this, eventually. At the end of its morning rounds, before the fog lifts, it goes down into the hollows where the great wheels lie half-sunk and turning, and it lies down against a mill's rim, alongside the toothed edge where the moss is ground to slurry. Sometimes it stays for hours. Sometimes it brings something with it, held in its mouth: a lump of mat, a fallen wren's nest, once, famously, a traveler's lost steel cup, carried from the far side of the basin and laid in the teeth of the mill, where it was ground slowly out of existence over the following season.

It is a courier of hard things. Whatever the mat takes in that it cannot easily break down, anything with metal or bone or shell or salt in it, the walker finds, and carries, and delivers to the mills to be ground and returned to the slurry that feeds new growth. It is the basin's collector of stone.

And its own bones are the densest, heaviest mineral in the basin.

## The Walkers and the Mills

They are not common. They may not even be several. The oldest accounts, handed from one long-stayer to the next, say there is one walker for each mill, never more. When a mill falls idle, as happens perhaps once in a human lifetime, when its turning slows over years and it finally stops, its walker is not seen again. And when a new mill is found, rising somewhere in the low hollows where one had not been, a young walker is seen in the lanes nearby within a few seasons, small and pale-mossed and uncertain, keeping close to the wheel.

No one has seen a walker born. The belief, and it is only a belief, is that they are grown in the hub of the mills themselves, the one part of each wheel that no one has ever reached, sunk deep in the mat at the center of its slow rotation. That the mill, grinding for a hundred years, concentrating what it grinds, gathers enough salt in its heart to make a skeleton. That the walker is what the mill makes when it has gathered enough.

And when a walker dies, it goes to its mill and lies against the rim, as it has every morning of its life, and does not rise, and over the following seasons the mill turns it in. The bones go last. The moss around that hollow, the year after, grows so fast and so bright it is almost frightening to see.

This is why travelers who glimpse it say it moves like something walking through its own house. It is not a guest in the basin. It is not quite a separate animal. It is the part of the basin that got up and walked, so that the salt could be moved somewhere a root could not carry it. It is a mill's way of reaching across the whole bowl. It is the basin's hand.

## Before the Shift

Once in a few days, the whole basin shifts. Every column swells at once, every mill bites, a single deep pressure moves through the ground, and then the chimneys all vent together and the basin goes white from rim to rim.

No one knows what causes it. But those who have been watching the living things in the moments before have noticed that it does not come without warning, not to everyone.

A few minutes beforehand, the moss-wrens leave the columns. All of them. They rise without a sound and hang in the air above the basin, in small still clusters, not flying anywhere. The crown beetles go still in the crusts. On the lichen, if there is frost, the red threads uncurl and lie straighter.

And the walkers, wherever they are, stop. They stop in the lanes or at the mill rims, and they lower themselves flat to the mat, legs folded, heads down, pressed full-length into the moss as if bracing, or as if embracing. Then the pressure comes up through the ground, and through them, and it seems to travel through their bodies differently than through the mat, so that for an instant the moss on their backs stands on end all at once, every strand, like the fur of a startled cat. Then the steam goes up, and the basin whitens, and the walkers rise, and go on.

Whatever the shift is, the household knows it is coming. Some who have seen this believe the walkers do not simply sense the shift, but take part in it, that it is the moment when the whole basin, mills and columns and lanes and walkers, pulls together in a single stroke, the way many hands on a rope pull together on a word. That the work is too large for anyone to do continuously, so every few days it is done all at once.

It may be nothing like that. But it is hard to watch it and not feel that something was agreed.

## The Tenant

So the first answer was wrong, and here is why. The basin is not alive in a way that leaves no room for anything else. It is alive the way a body is alive, and a body is not one thing. It is the traffic of a great many things through it, carrying what needs to be carried from where it is found to where it is needed.

Everything here is making, breaking, pumping, or grinding, and none of it is for anyone. That is true, and it is also not the whole truth. The basin's work is for nobody, but it is not done by nobody. The chimneys lift water and leave salt at their crowns. The beetles carry that salt down into the dark. The wrens carry some of it up into eggshells and back into the lichen. The lichen draws its long slow current and feeds its stripes of moss. The walkers carry what is heavy and hard across the whole bowl to the mills, and the mills grind it and grow walkers from it, and the walkers keep the water running in the lanes that feed the mat that feeds the chimneys.

It is not a factory without workers. It is a factory with workers so small, or so quiet, or so much the color of the moss, that from the rim you do not see them at all. You see only the work.

## And You

There is one more kind of creature that lives in the basin, briefly, from time to time.

Travelers who spend days here begin to move quietly without deciding to. They stop talking. They drop things, and the things are taken in. They sweat into their gloves and lose the gloves. They drink from the rosettes and carry the glass threads out along their routes. They lie down to sleep on the moss, and wake with their outlines faintly pressed into it, and by noon the outline is gone. They find their breathing slowing. They begin, some of them, to press an ear to the ground more and more often, to listen for the pump.

It would be too much to say that the basin keeps them. Most of them leave. But while they are here they are part of the traffic, carrying a little salt in, leaving a little salt behind. The ones who stay longest say that after a while you stop being able to tell the difference between walking through the basin and being walked by it. You follow the ridges and the lichen because the ground won't hold a path. Your route is laid for you, day by day, by the slow wave of the slopes. You end up where the basin needs its salt.

The few who have died here were not lost. They are there still, in the brighter green of certain hollows.

## The Center

The frost points toward the center of the basin. The red threads lie curled in the same direction. The lichen's long charge runs that way, and the beetles, one long-stayer claimed, burrow slightly toward it as they go down.

What is there, no one knows for certain. The fog is thickest there and almost never lifts. The lanes converge there, the way veins converge. Those few who have tried to walk to it say the ground rearranges faster the closer you get, until you are walking all day and arriving back where you began, and the heat rises through the soles of your boots, and the pump that you can otherwise hear only with your ear to the moss becomes audible standing up, a slow, deep beat, felt in the teeth.

Some say there is a mill there larger than all the others, one that has not turned in anyone's memory because it turns once in a thousand years. That every walker that ever died was carried there in the end, bone by bone, by the slurry and the channels. That at its hub, the richest place in the whole basin, where all the salt of ten thousand years has been ground and gathered, something is being grown that is not a walker. Something that will stand up, when there is finally enough of it, and walk out of the fog.

Others say there is nothing there at all but warmth, the point where all the basin's heat pools, the way warmth pools at the center of a sleeping body. That the frost points toward it the way a sleeper's limbs curl in. That the center is not a place where something lives, but the place the living is *for*.

The walkers go there sometimes. Always alone, always in the deepest cold, on the nights when the basin labors hardest. They go down into the fog toward the center and come back a day or two later, walking slower, their moss a little brighter. They don't carry anything in. They don't seem to carry anything out.

Perhaps they only go to listen. To lie down flat against the deepest part of the house, where the pump is loudest, and feel it move through them: the enormous, patient, useless work, which is not useless to them, and never was. The one who lives here is not any single one of them. It is all of them together, and the house, and the pulse between. Seen from the ridge it looks like a storm that never ends. From inside, pressed flat to the moss with your heart against its heart, it feels more like a long, slow breath, drawn in the dark, by something that has been breathing for longer than anyone has been here to watch, and has never once needed anyone to hear it.
