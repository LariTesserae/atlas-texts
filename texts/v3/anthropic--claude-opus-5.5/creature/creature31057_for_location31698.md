# Who Lives on the Floor of the Unbuilt Works

## I. The First Answer, Which Is Not Wrong

The easy answer is that the animals live there: the bellows-backs, the strikers, the snails, the eels, the tallymen, the worms and flocks and stalkers. They are there, and they are plentiful. But the easy answer depends on a word it hasn't examined. *Live* assumes a creature has a life that belongs to it and fills the time between birth and death. On the floor of the Unbuilt Works, time does not seem to belong to the creatures. It belongs to the shift, and the creatures are *on* it.

So before asking who lives there, it is worth watching how anything comes to be there, and how it stops.

**A bellows-back calf** is born on the exhale. The mother does not lie down; nothing in the basin lies down except the dead. The calf slides out of a pleated chamber low on her flank in a gush of warm fluid that steams in the cold, and it lands in the slurry already folded into the shape it will hold for the rest of its life: low, broad, keel-down. For a moment it does nothing, and the herd around it does nothing either. Then the whole plain inhales, and the calf inhales with it. It does not take a first breath of its own, a gasp, a cry. Its first breath is the herd's next one, and it never falls out of step. Calves born out of phase are rare, and when one is, a tallyman is standing beside it before the fluid has stopped steaming.

The calf grows by accretion. The mineral film finds it within days, and by the time it is full-grown its hide is a map of gray-green crust thick enough to crack and flake when it breathes. Bellows-backs are said to live a very long time, though nobody has watched one long enough to know. What people have watched is an old one slowing. Its chambers fill a fraction late. The herd does not wait for it. The lag grows until its breathing is visibly against the grain of the plain, a small dissenting motion in an ocean of agreement. It does not seem distressed, only tired, in the way a man at the end of a double is tired: past complaint, still going through the motions after the motions have stopped mattering. Eventually it breathes in when everything around it breathes out, and holds the breath, and does not let it go.

It stays standing. The film closes over it within a season. The slurry takes it by inches. For a long time afterward its ribs stand out of a water sheet like the frame of an unroofed shed, and travellers use them as landmarks. These are the half-sunk skeletons by which a person learns that they have crossed the same bay again. The dead bellows-backs mark the bays. They may be what the bays *are*: stations along the line, each one kept by the carcass of whatever used to work it.

**Strikers** hatch in the drip grooves. The eggs are tiny, dark, and lens-shaped, laid in rows along the channels that water has cut into the schist, and they are tempered rather than incubated. Each drop that runs down the groove passes over them, cools them, and moves on. When they hatch they are soft and pale and nearly all leg, with the hammer-limb folded tight against the body like the blade of a pocketknife. For their first days they do not strike. They stand in the drip line and *listen*, heads tilted toward the nearest adult, and the soft hammer-limb twitches in time. Then one morning it straightens and comes down on the stone with a sound like a dropped spoon, far too light to do anything, and it keeps coming down, over and over, and over the following weeks the knob hardens and the sound deepens until it is indistinguishable from the others.

Strikers molt, but not the hammer. The rest of the body sheds and regrows in larger sizes while the knob is carried forward from instar to instar, accumulating mineral, so that an old striker's hammer is disproportionate, grotesque, a club it can barely lift. Near the end they strike slowly. The beat of a district goes on around them and they fall behind it. This is the thing the tallymen are for, and I will come back to it.

**The smelt-snails** do not seem to be born at all. They increase. Their shells are the only thing in the basin that grows in a true spiral, and if you pick one up and turn it you will see fine raised ridges running the length of the whorl, evenly pitched, catching the eel-light. Once you have seen it, you cannot unsee it. It is a thread. Every smelt-snail carries a screw thread wound around its own body, and the pitch of the thread is the same across every snail in the basin, whatever its size. Their shells are the one place where the bolts' geometry appears in living tissue. Whether the snails learned it from the bolts or the bolts from the snails is a question nobody has managed to hold still long enough to ask properly.

**The arc-eels** are born in discharges. You see this only if you're looking at the wrong moment: a web of lightning snaps across a flat, and where two arcs cross there is a brief brighter flare, and afterward the school has one more ribbon in it than it did. The young eel is invisible except for its current. It is a line of static in the water that makes the film wrinkle as it passes. They grow by conducting. An eel that has not discharged in a while turns milky and sluggish; one that sparks often is glass-clear and fast. They do not die so much as short out. An old eel discharges one last time, harder than it has in years, a single white crack that lights the vapor to the ceiling, and then there is a flat ribbon of gray skin on the water, and the film grows over it by evening.

All of this is life. But it's worth noticing what shape it takes. Every animal here enters the basin already knowing its job. Every animal here ends the moment it can no longer keep time. There is no childhood that is play, no old age that is rest. The calf breathes with the herd from its first breath. The striker stands in the drip line listening for the beat before it can lift its arm. The eel is born in a spark and dies in one. These are not lives that happen to include work. They are shifts that happen to require bodies.

So: the animals live there. But the word is already bending.

## II. The Hands

There are people on the floor of the Unbuilt Works. Not many. Not natives.

They come in over the rim ridges, and most of them, having crossed the same water sheet eleven or thirty or eighty times, find themselves at the rim again and leave, shaken, with stories that don't hold together. Some do not reach the rim. They keep walking, keep crossing the same bay, and somewhere in the repetition, usually around the time they first turn with the whistle without meaning to, they stop trying to leave. They become what the few who have come back call *hands*.

A hand is not a prisoner. Nothing holds them. The rim is always, in principle, a few hours' walk away. But the rule about distance, that it is *counted and not measured*, has a corollary that the hands understand and the visitors don't: the count is yours. The number of bays you have to cross before the rim appears is not a property of the basin. It is a property of you, a figure the tallymen are keeping. A visitor who walks straight in and straight out has a small count; they have done nothing here, owe nothing, and the plain lets them go after a few dozen repetitions. A hand who has stood on a schist tread for a season with the soot settling on their shoulders has a count so high that no one could walk it in a lifetime. They could leave at any time. The rim would simply be very far.

So they stay, and they are changed by staying.

The first change is to the ears. Because sound arrives before its cause, a hand learns to live a few seconds ahead of themselves. They hear their own footstep splash and then take it. They hear their own voice, if they speak, which they rarely do, and then feel their mouth move. At first this is nauseating. After a while it becomes a kind of reassurance, even a discipline: they listen for what they are about to do, and they do it, and it's always right, because it has already been done. Hands describe it, when they can be made to describe it, as *being told*. It is not that they lose their will. It's that the space between intending and acting fills with the sound of the act, and after long enough there is no room left for hesitation. A hand does not deliberate. A hand hears, and then does.

The second change is to the skin. The film grows on anything that stands still, and hands stand still a great deal. It starts at the ankles, where the slurry laps, then climbs. It is not painful. It is cold, and faintly tingling, like the numbness of a limb held too long in one position, and it slowly turns the skin the gray-green of old copper. Hands who have been there for years are crusted like bellows-backs. It cracks at the joints when they move. Beneath it, they are thin. They eat very little. There is not much in the basin a person *can* eat, and most hands seem to have stopped needing to. Some of them chew the midges out of the bellows-backs' exhalations. Some of them, it is said, have learned to breathe in time with the herds and take a little of the warmth that way, standing close enough that the expelled air washes over them, like men warming their hands at a grate.

The third change is to the direction they face. A hand turns with the whistle. Everyone does, eventually. But a new visitor turns because everything else is turning, and would feel strange not to, and it is a small, social, animal motion, like standing when a room stands. A hand turns because it is the handover, and they *know* something is being handed over. When asked what, they look at you with a kind of pity, the look a veteran gives a new hire who asks what the machine does. "The shift," they say. That is all. You don't ask what the shift is. You work it.

What do the hands do? This is the strange part. They do not strike, or breathe warmth, or smelt, or spark. They have no product. What they do, as far as anyone can tell, is *stand at stations*. A hand will find a place, the shoulder of a particular outcrop, the lee of a particular skeleton, the edge of a particular chimney-shadow, and stand there, facing the direction of the shift, through every whistle. They watch. Occasionally they reach out and touch something: a striker's shell, a snail, the flank of a bellows-back. Then they return to stillness.

They are learning to be tallymen. Or they are what tallymen were before they were tallymen. The difference, if there is one, is the eye.

## III. The Tallymen, Closer

Here is what can be said about the tallymen with confidence, which is very little.

They are small, the height of a child of six or seven, and dark, not with the black of soot but with the matte non-color of a pupil. Their limbs are thin and many-jointed, and their fingers are long enough to wrap twice around a striker's leg. Their heads are mostly eye, and the eye is not an organ set in a head so much as the head is a rim around an eye: a great single dark lens, slightly convex, that catches the eel-light and holds it longer than it should, so that when a tallyman turns away from a discharge the violet flash lingers in its eye for a second or two like an afterimage.

They do not eat. They are never seen being born. They are sometimes seen dying, and it is always the same: a tallyman stops mid-stride, its eye clouds from the center outward like breath on glass, and it stands there, clouded, until the next whistle. At the whistle, when everything else turns, it does not. After the next shift begins it is simply not there anymore, and at the spot where it stood, rusting in the water, there is a bolt.

That is the first clue.

Watch them long enough, from a tread with a good sightline, and a pattern emerges from their touching. They are not touching at random. They move through a district in a path that visits each worker in turn, a bellows-back, a striker, a cluster of snails, and at each one their long fingers rest a moment on the shell or hide, and the eye tightens, as if focusing. The worker does not react. There is no exchange of anything visible. The tallyman moves on. It is exactly the gesture of a foreman walking a floor, laying a hand on each machine to feel it running.

And they count. You can hear it, if you are close and the lag is right: a faint dry clicking from somewhere in their chest, one click per touch, too regular to be a heartbeat. The clicks accumulate. When a tallyman has walked a whole district its clicking has become a dense fast rattle. And then, somewhere near the edge of a chimney-shadow, it will stop, and stand, and the rattle will slow, and slow, and go silent.

The next day, there will be bolts in the water near that spot. Never many. A handful. Sometimes one.

And the stood-down striker, the one that fell out of the beat and was visited, and did not strike again: it doesn't die at the tallyman's touch. It is not killed. Watch closely. The tallyman lays its fingers on the old striker's disproportionate hammer, the club it has been carrying forward through every molt since it was a pale thing listening in a drip groove, and the eye tightens, and the chest clicks *once*, and the striker lowers the hammer and lets it rest on the stone. It stands there, its arm at ease for the first time since it hatched. The film comes for it. It becomes one more gray shape on the plain. It has been counted. Its shift is over.

The tallymen are not executioners. They are closing the books.

## IV. What the Bolts Are

Here is a thought that hands seem to arrive at, and that visitors sometimes arrive at on the long walk out, crossing the same bay for the fortieth time with the whistle going somewhere behind them.

The bolts are not a product in the way iron is a product. The smelt-snails make iron. The strikers make silt. The bellows-backs make warmth. The eels make light and something fixed into water. All of these are consumed; they cycle; they are the metabolism of the place. The bolts are not consumed. They are the only thing in the basin that nothing eats, nothing uses, nothing breaks down. They only accumulate.

And they appear where tallies are completed.

A bolt is a unit. It is one account closed. Each one marks a finished shift: a striker stood down, a calf's life breathed out against the grain, a tallyman who has counted all it can count and clouded over. The strata of bolts beneath the basin, the bands hundreds of feet thick with threads worn smooth by the weight of what lies above them, are not a waste heap. They are a *ledger*. The floor of the Unbuilt Works is paved with receipts for labor, laid down in order, one on top of another, back past the point where anything could remember the first entry.

And this recasts the whole basin. The snail's shell carries a thread because the snail is a bolt in progress. The dead bellows-backs mark the bays because each bay is a column of the account. The rule that distance is counted and not measured is simply the ledger applied to travellers: you cross as many bays as you owe. The whistle is the page turning. The quarter-rotation is a column heading. Four whistles and every animal on the plain has faced every direction once and come back. That is a full cycle, a day, a ledger page ruled and filled.

Who is the ledger *for*?

## V. Inside the Shadows

The chimney-shadows are where the answer starts to come, and not everyone who goes looking for it can bear it.

The rule is that animals route around them. Bellows-backs will not stand in them. Strikers inside them keep perfect time. What the description does not say, because it is not true for visitors, only for hands, is what happens if you step in.

A visitor stepping into a chimney-shadow feels a drop in temperature, colder even than the basin, and a strange flattening of sound, as if they had put their head into a closet full of coats. The lag grows. They can hear, as the description says, the whole next minute of the district before it happens. Most retreat at once. The feeling is of trespass, of having walked into a room where something private is going on.

A hand who steps into a chimney-shadow and *stays*, who stands long enough for the film to begin to take them there, reports something different. The shadow has walls.

They are not visible. Nothing in the shadow is visible that is not visible outside it. But the sound inside is enclosed. It echoes off surfaces that are not there. The thrum of the bellows-backs, heard from inside the shadow, is muffled by a roof. The strikes of the strikers ring back from a floor that is not schist but plate. Footsteps resound, and they resound from high up, from gantries, catwalks, stairs, from a place above the vapor layers where there is no place. Hands who have stood in the shadows for years say they can hear the building. They say it's enormous. They say it goes back further than the plain does. They say they can hear, faintly, far away down its length, the sound of the whistle *before* it blows. Not a few seconds before. Hours. As if the whistle were blown once, long ago or long from now, and has been travelling through the building ever since, and the basin only hears it as it passes each window.

And they hear something else in there. Not a worker. There are workers inside the shadows, of course: the strikers who keep perfect time, whose hammers in there come down not on stone but on something that rings like an anvil, something that is not there. But beyond the workers, farther in, the hands report a sound that is not labor. It is *walking*. A slow, even tread, heavier than a tallyman's, lighter than a stalker's. It crosses the building on the catwalks, far overhead. It stops, sometimes, as if looking down. Then it goes on.

They say it is the only thing in the Works that is not on shift.

## VI. So: Who Lives There

Let the answer come the way it comes to someone standing on a tread long enough.

First, the animals. They live there, and their lives are shifts, and the shifts are counted, and when they end there is a bolt.

Then, the hands. They live there, in the only sense of the word left to them. They are the ones the Works did not make but kept. They are learning, slowly, to become the counting.

Then, the tallymen. They live there as the conscience of a place without a mind. They are the eye that walks the floor so that each labor, however small, is witnessed and entered and closed. If there is kindness here, it is theirs. They are the ones who let the striker lower its arm.

But none of these are the *resident*. They are the workforce, and a workforce lives in a place the way a tide lives on a shore. The one who lives there is the one it is all for, and the shape of that one can be read, as the shape of any occupant can be read, from what it leaves around it.

It leaves a ledger. It leaves shadows of the building it has not built. It leaves a whistle blown so long ago or so far ahead that the sound is still travelling. It leaves a footfall on a catwalk above the fog, pausing, looking down. It leaves a basin so old its mountains have become growth rings, a floor planed flat as if for foundations, and bolts, bolts, hundreds of feet of bolts, identical, uniform, threaded, *ready*, laid down in bands the way a patient builder stacks materials in a yard for years before breaking ground.

It is the Owner of the Works, and it has not arrived. Or it has arrived and has not yet begun. Or it began once, and finished, and tore everything down so thoroughly that only the labor was left standing. Nobody in the basin can say which. The hands, when pressed, say it doesn't matter, the way a man on the night shift will tell you it doesn't matter whether the boss is upstairs or at home in bed. The work goes on either way. The whistle blows either way.
