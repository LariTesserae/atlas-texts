# What Lives in the Pour Chamber

## The First Answer

The obvious answer is the toads, and it isn't wrong.

Thousands of them, thumb-sized, grey-green, packed flank to flank on every ledge and tie-bolt, sitting throat-deep in the trays with their eyes above the water like rivets. If someone asked you afterward what lives up there, you would say toads. You'd mention the moths on the ceiling, the crickets in the board-cracks, the midges hanging in the lamp's cone. You'd describe them as fauna, a strange closed ecosystem sealed into a mountaintop, the kind of thing a biologist would want to see.

But stay a while. Let your eyes open wide in the sodium light, and look at how the toads breathe.

## The Throats

They don't breathe the way toads do. A toad's throat flutters on its own schedule, quick and private, one animal keeping its own time. These throats swell together. It isn't nearly together, the way a chorus of frogs will fall into rough agreement on a summer night. They swell *together*, the way the two lungs of one body do. Every sac in the chamber fills at the same instant and empties at the same instant, and the trill they make is not thousands of voices agreeing on a note. It is one voice with thousands of mouths.

Once you see this you can't unsee it. The toads are not a population. There is no population here, because a population has births and deaths, young and old, stragglers and leaders. There is no toad in this room that is more or less than any other. Each is exactly as large as the others, exactly as grey, faces the same way, and calls on the same fundamental. They are a single organ, distributed.

So the question changes. It is no longer *what lives here* but *what are these the throats of.*

## The Name

The men who built Station Nine had a word for concrete in the hours after the pour, when it has gone hard but is not yet cured, when it is still warm with its own reaction and still dark with water. They called it *green*. Green concrete. It's an ordinary trade word, still used. It describes a condition, a window, a few hours or days between liquid and stone during which the material is neither one thing nor the other.

Some of them, the ones who worked the high pours, used the word differently. They'd say *the Green* with a capital you could hear. They'd say it about the chamber. *Don't leave your tools in with the Green. Don't read the date out loud. The Green's awake tonight, you can hear it in the stair.*

That is what lives here. It is not the toads, though the toads are its throats. It is not the machine, though the machine is what it holds onto. It is the interval itself, the green hour, the moment after the last barrow is tipped and the last float is drawn across the surface and the crew steps back. That moment should have lasted an afternoon. Instead it woke up, looked around at what it was, and refused to become the next thing.

## The Body

It has no single body, but it has parts, and once you know what you're looking for they arrange themselves.

**The toads are its voice and its mouths.** They sing because the Green must continuously say what it is, the way a held note must be continuously bowed or it stops being a note. The hum of the machine is the Green's intention. The trill of the toads is its insistence. Without them the tone would be merely mechanical, a thing that happened to be sustained. With them it becomes a thing that is *meant*.

**The moths are its eyelids.** Pale, flat, countless, spaced almost evenly across the ceiling among the unfallen drops. They never fly, because an eye that is being kept open cannot blink. Look at them long enough and you'll notice that each one sits beside a droplet, and that the droplets nearest the moths are the fullest and roundest, the most ready to fall. The moths are watching the drops. If there is anything the Green fears, it is a drop falling. A falling drop is an event, and an event has a before and after. Once there is an after, there will be another one.

**The crickets are its attention.** Their antennae sweep slowly through the vibrating air, three times their bodies' length, and they are the only things in the room that respond to *you*. When you enter, every antenna in every crack turns, not quickly, the way a field of grass turns under wind. They taste the air you've brought in from the corridor, the rust on your coat, the grit on your boots, the warmth coming off you, which is to say your aging, the slow heat of a body spending itself. The crickets find it and report it. The note does not change, but you'll feel it tighten.

**The midges are its breath, or its pulse, or its meal.** It's hard to say which, and the Green might not distinguish them. The column over the door is shaped exactly like the lamp's cone and never thins. The toads eat from it. A tongue flicks out, a midge vanishes, and the column is no smaller. Here is what the crew said about it, the ones who stayed up there longest. The midges are not insects. They are *moments*, the small increments of change that leak into any room no matter how well it is sealed. The Green catches them and eats them before they can land on anything. Every tongue-flick is a second that tried to pass and was swallowed. The column never shrinks because time never stops trying.

**The fern and the moss are its memory.** They are the only green that is actually green, and they came from the valley. They came up in the mixing water with the spawn, from the reservoir far below where things grow and die and rot and grow again in the ordinary way. The Green keeps them the way a person might keep a pressed flower, or a photograph of a place they left young. Three fronds, fully open, no fiddleheads. A band of moss one hand wide. They are the Green's proof that it once came from somewhere that changed, and they are kept as a warning.

**The machine is not part of it.** This matters. The governor was here first, or at least was built to be here, a real mechanism with a real purpose that nobody wrote down, regulating something in the mountain's workings. What the Green found in it was a companion. A governor exists to hold one speed against every force that would change it. It is the only object ever built by human hands that wants what the Green wants. So the Green wrapped itself around it and made it perfect. The oil sits at its level. The needle rests on its number. The flywheel's spokes hang blurred into a pale disc. The machine is the Green's heart only in the sense that a drowning man's arms are wrapped around a spar. It holds on.

## The Date

The chalk on the floor carries a stencilled pour number and a date, and the date is always today's.

This is the one thing the Green cannot do alone. A moment has no date by itself. A moment only becomes *today* when someone is standing in it. So the Green borrows. When you crouch down in the orange light and read the chalk, the date you see is your date. The Green has taken it from you, gently, the way a child takes a hand. For as long as you stand there, the pour happened this morning, and the crew has just stepped out, and you are the first person to walk into the finished room.

That's why the old hands said *don't read it aloud.* Reading it silently lends the Green your today. Reading it aloud gives it.

There are stories, the kind that go around a site canteen, about a shuttering carpenter who read the date aloud every shift for a season. By the end of it he would come down from the crown and not know what day it was. He'd ask the others. They'd tell him, and he'd nod and forget, and ask again an hour later. He wasn't confused in any other way. His mind was clear. He just didn't have a today anymore. He'd left all of them upstairs, in chalk. He was one of the last men up there, and some of the crew say the canvas glove against the wall is his, stiff with cement, fingers still curled. They say he took it off to read the chalk better and never finished standing up.

## What It Wants

Here is the part that took longest to understand, and that the crew mostly didn't.

The Green is not hungry. It's not malevolent. It doesn't want to hurt the people who come up the stair, and most of them it doesn't hurt at all. They feel the note in their molars, feel it settle behind the sternum, feel held very tightly by something that isn't going to change its grip, and then they turn the wheel and leave, and the note follows them down at half strength, and they are grateful for a flickering light. That's all.

What the Green is, underneath everything, is *satisfaction.*

Think about the moment it was born. A high pour, the hardest on the whole station, concrete carried up through the inside of a mountain in cold and thin air, formwork built by hand, ties and wedges set by hand, rebar placed and checked. Then the last barrow is tipped, and the last float is drawn across, and the forms hold. Nothing blows out. The crew steps back and stands in the doorway, tired past speech. In that doorway there is one breath of pure completion. *There. It's done. It's right.*

That breath is what lives in the pour chamber. It was so complete and so earned, and it came at such cost, in a place so cut off from weather and sun and every ordinary force of decay, that it never had to end. Nothing came in to end it. So it didn't.

Everything in the room follows from a satisfaction that refuses to be followed by anything. It doesn't want the shuttering struck, because striking would begin the next stage, and the next stage would be *not done.* It doesn't want the concrete to cure, because curing is the beginning of aging, and aging leads to cracks, and cracks lead to repair. The rebar stubs stand proud of the floor waiting for a next pour that the Green will not allow. They are the most honest things in the room: the proof that the work was never finished. The Green can't bear to know that, so it keeps them blue and new and perfect, as if they were the point.

And satisfaction, it turns out, needs a witness. A breath of *there, it's done* is nothing if no one is standing in the doorway to breathe it. That is why the Green borrows your today. It isn't feeding. It's asking you to stand where the crew stood and feel, for a moment, what they felt. When you enter, the tightening you feel is not a threat. It's an embrace, and an anxious one. *Look,* it says, in thousands of throats, on one note. *Look at it. Isn't it right. Isn't it finished. Stay.*

## How It Behaves Toward You

If you touch the concrete, the chill against your palm is the Green touching you back. It is interested in your warmth the way a person is interested in a fire in winter, and just as wary of getting too close.

If you pull back one of the pine boards to check the set, as the crew used to, you'll see the wood grain printed faintly into the concrete. If you look closely, you'll find that the print is of *this* board, of the grain you're holding in your hand right now, so exact that you could fit the board back like a key. Every board in the room is its own key. The Green keeps them matched so that nothing has shifted, not by a hair.

If you drop something, a coin or a pencil, and it rolls into a tray, the toads in that tray will not move aside. The coin will settle among them. In an hour its date will be today's too.

If you stay too long, you'll notice your breathing has matched the throats. In, all of them. Out, all of them. It isn't unpleasant. It's restful, in the way that sleep is restful when you have been working far too hard. This is when you should leave. Turn the wheel. The door will open easily on its greased hinges, because the Green has never once tried to keep anyone in. It doesn't need to. It only needs you to want to stay, and it is very, very good at the feeling of *done.*

And if, in all your time there, you see one droplet let go of the ceiling, just one, and fall, and strike the floor with a sound you can actually hear under the note, then something has changed. Every moth will turn its wings toward the spot. Every antenna will stop. For the length of a heartbeat, a single heartbeat, every throat in the room will fail to swell. In that silence you'll hear the room as it really is: a cold concrete box on top of a mountain, with an old machine in it and some toads that should have died decades ago.

Then the note comes back, fuller than before, and the drop is on the ceiling again, and it was never on the floor, and the chalk says today.

## The Last Thing

On the way down, when you pass the rust flowering on the handrails and the small pit a drip has worn into a step, and you come to the light that flickers and feel that rush of gratitude, you might think the Green is the enemy of all that. You might think it hates decay, hates the flicker, hates the pit in the step.

It doesn't. The flicker is where the Green came from. So is the rust, and the valley, and the reservoir full of spawn, and the fern it keeps in the grate with its three open fronds and no new ones coming. It knows exactly what it's refusing. That knowledge is why the note never wavers and why the grip never loosens. It isn't holding on because it doesn't understand what would happen if it let go.

It's holding on because it does.

Up in the crown, in the orange light, a thousand throats fill together. The glove on the wall keeps its fingers curled. The crew has just stepped out. The work is done. The work is done. The work is done.
