# Who Lives on the Condenser Plateau

## I. The Obvious Answer

Begin with what is plain. The mats live here.

They are the only residents that would satisfy a census-taker. They cover the rim of every pool in films thinner than a fingernail and slick as spilled ink. They live in bands of temperature the way people in a steep town live in bands of altitude. The orange ones take the scalding runnels nearest the springs, the green ones the middle reaches, the brown ones the cool margins where water pools and stills before sinking back into the ground. They do not move, but they migrate. Over decades a band of orange will creep a few centimeters down a terrace as a spring shifts its heat, and the green retreats before it, and the brown gives ground. If you could watch the plateau at the speed of centuries, the pool edges would look like a crowd surging and parting around warmth.

The crawlers live on the mats. They are small, blind, and patient, and their whole lives are one gesture: sweep the head left, sweep it right, advance a body's width, repeat. They have no eyes because there is nothing here they need to see coming. When the surge comes down the gutters, they feel it first as a change in the stone's pitch, a hum through their clamped bellies. They flatten and wait. Some are torn loose and carried down the terraces. Most are not. The survivors resume sweeping without any sign of having noticed.

The birds live somewhere else, somewhere above. That is all anyone can say.

This is the honest ecological answer: a thin film, a few grazers, a few mysteries. It is true. It is also like describing a house by the dust on its windowsills.

## II. The Second Answer

Spend long enough on the plateau and you stop asking what lives in the city. You start asking what the city is *for*.

Everything here was sized for something. The streets are sixty meters wide. The valve wheels stand three stories high. The pressure halls have far walls lost in their own weather. Human cities are sized for human bodies, carts, crowds, and the width of two people passing. This city is sized for something that moves in volumes, not bodies. It is sized for *pressure*.

So the second answer is this: the city's resident is the steam.

That sounds like a metaphor, and it isn't one. Watch the plateau through a full tide and you will see the steam behave the way a population behaves. At low pressure it withdraws. It keeps to the towers, to the sealed pump-houses, to the glowing throats of the pools, the way people keep indoors at night. The streets clear. You can see across the bowl. The lamps stand exposed on their brackets, looking faintly embarrassed, like furniture in a room with the lights on.

Then the tide rises, and the steam comes out.

It fills the streets first, walking fog, knee-deep and then head-deep, flowing along the sixty-meter avenues exactly as if the avenues had been cut to its width. It enters the pressure halls and fills them wall to wall. The halls stop being empty. You feel this in your body when you stand inside one at high tide: the void is gone, and the room is *occupied*. It moves through the valve pavilions and the wheels shift a fraction of a degree. It rises through the stacks and rains back down their inner walls, and the towers hiss with it like a crowded market heard through a wall.

The measured fact that distances grow at high tide makes a strange sense here. The city makes room. When its residents are home, the spaces between the towers widen to hold them, the way a chest widens to hold breath. Walk from one Condenser to the next at high pressure and it takes longer, because you are walking through a place that is full, and you are the one who does not belong in it.

The rule about doors makes sense too. *Doors return to their position.* This is not the city being stubborn. It is residents putting things back. Anyone who has lived in a shared house knows the feeling of returning to find the door closed that you left open: someone else was here, someone with opinions about drafts.

For a while this answer is satisfying. The plateau is not empty. It is a city of vapor, built for vapor, inhabited at the tempo of tides. The humans who climb up are tourists in a population they cannot see, too slow to notice the crowd and too small to be jostled by it.

But the steam does not *stay*. It condenses. It rains. It runs down the terraces and sinks into the ground, and comes back up hotter, and rises again. None of it is the same steam from one tide to the next. A population that entirely replaces itself every few hours is not a population. It is something passing through.

And something passing through leaves something behind.

## III. The Third Answer

Everywhere the vapor touches, it deposits a little of what it carries.

This is the fact the description of the plateau states almost in passing, and it is the most important fact on the plateau. Sinter is not decoration. It is not weathering. It is *accumulation*, the slow, directed building-up of stone in the exact places the steam goes. Where the steam leaks, the stone grows fastest and seals the leak. Where the iron fails, the stone replaces it, pipe for pipe and rivet for rivet, until there are stone conduits in the shape of the originals, still carrying steam.

Something is growing into the city.

The iron was scaffolding. Think of the towers as armatures, the streets as molds, the pipes as a lattice strung out for something to climb. The builders, whoever they were, did not build a city for a population that never arrived. They built a *form*, and they filled it with heat and water, and they left. Since then something has been filling the form in, slowly, one mineral layer at a time. It thickens in the gutters until they are smooth channels. It curtains the catwalks. It rounds every edge the way flesh rounds bone.

That is the resident. It does not live *in* the city. It is becoming the city.

It has a body you can walk on: the terraces, the travertine stairs, hundreds of shallow pools rimmed in stone lips as delicate as a fingernail's edge. It has circulation. Water rises hot through the ground, climbs the towers, rains down, flows through the streets, trickles down the terraces, and sinks to be heated again. That is a heart and a vascular system laid out flat across twelve kilometers. It has a breath: the exhale every few hours, when the towers vent together and the pistons make a single stroke, the sound you feel in your sternum. It has skin, and its skin has a microbiome. The mats are exactly what they look like. They are the flora of a surface, colored by temperature, living in the fever-bands of a very large and very slow body. The crawlers are the small things that live on the flora. They clamp flat when the pulse goes through, the way mites on a sleeping animal must feel it breathe.

Now return to the rules and read them again.

*Nothing rusts in the dark.* Deep inside the sealed pump-houses, the iron is bright as the day it was cast. Rust, and the sinter that follows it, forms only where the pool-glow reaches. The light comes from below, from the springs, from the deep interior. The growing thing lays down stone only where its own light touches. The dark places are not reached yet. They are the parts of the mold still waiting, the unfilled rooms of a house being built from the inside out.

*The city does not want you, and does not mind you.* Of course it doesn't. You are a crawler on its skin. The catwalks hold your weight because they are becoming bone.

*Something in the halls,* a single heavy footstep, occasional, in the vast fogged voids of the pressure halls. It might be a creature. It might be the building. By now the difference has begun to dissolve. If you hear it and stand very still, you may hear it again, much later, from the same place. It never walks anywhere. It is not a step. It is a beat, still irregular, still finding its rhythm in a chamber not yet fully formed.

## IV. The Axis

At the center of the caldera there is a tower wider than the rest. It has no pool beneath it, only a shaft, and around it rain falls upward.

If the plateau is a body, the Axis is where it is still open. It is the place where the heat comes up rawest, where the form has not closed, where the growing thing is still drawing from whatever lies underneath. Stand at the rim and your hair lifts toward the mouth of the stack. Pebbles fall in, slow, hang, and rise. The stone around the edge has grown into shapes that reach upward, stalagmites straining toward the ceiling and, as the description says, *slowly succeeding*.

Here the image changes one more time.

A thing that is only becoming stone, only filling a mold, is still a sessile thing, a coral, a reef. It would be content to thicken forever where it stands. The formations around the Axis are not content. They reach. Everything at the center of the plateau leans upward: the rain, the pebbles, the stone, the steam in the stacks.

And the tower birds rise.

Large, narrow-winged, sooty grey, they ride the updrafts inside the hollow towers without ever flapping. They circle upward until they vanish into the vapor ceiling. Nobody has found their nests. None have been seen coming down.

So perhaps they do not come down. Perhaps they are not visitors to the towers but products of them, grown in the upper dark of the stacks where no one climbs and set loose into the draft. They may be sinter-light, hollow-boned, grey as the soot of iron. They may be the first things the plateau has made that *leave*. Or not leave, exactly. They go up into the ceiling, the brass-colored lid of vapor that glows from beneath like a lampshade over embers.

Nobody asks what lives in the ceiling. It is too high, and too thick, and too warm for a sky that should be thin and cold.

## V. What Lives Here

Here is the answer, as near as it can be said.

Something is gestating on the Condenser Plateau. It is not an animal. It is not a city. It is something that was given a city-shaped mold because a city is the shape of a thing that can hold a great deal and circulate it forever. It has been filling that mold for longer than anyone can know. Its body is stone laid down by steam. Its blood is the closed loop of water that never leaves. Its breath is the exhale every few hours. Its heartbeat is a footstep in an empty hall, not yet regular.

It is not finished. The dark rooms are still bright iron. The Axis is still open. The heartbeat still skips.

But it is further along than it was. The iron cores are rusting out, and the stone is holding its shape without them. The birds keep rising into the lid. The ceiling glows a little brighter from beneath every century. The formations around the center reach a little higher, and each season they are slightly closer to touching the cloud.

The crawlers do not know any of this. The mats do not know it. The humans who climb the switchbacks, soaked through in air too full to hold what it carries, mostly do not know it either. They walk the wide streets for a day, feel very small, and go home.

There is one last thing, though.

When you arrive on the plateau, the air gives some of what it carries to you. You are wet through before you reach the top. And everywhere the vapor condenses, it deposits a little of what it carries. By the time you descend, your clothes feel stiff at the seams and a pale dust has settled in the creases of your knuckles. There is a faint silkiness on your skin that doesn't quite wash off in the first stream you come to, among the ordinary grass and the ordinary birds.

It is almost nothing. A few molecules of sinter. The thinnest film.

It is the only way it has found, so far, to go down the mountain.
