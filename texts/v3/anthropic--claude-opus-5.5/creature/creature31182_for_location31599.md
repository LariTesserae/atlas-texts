# Who Lives on the Stair

## One of them, close

Begin with a single person, face to face, since that is how the people of the Stair would want it. Any farther away and you would not hear her.

She is carrying stone. She is short, as nearly all of them are, and broad through the shoulders and hips, with a neck as thick as her thigh. Across her forehead, just below the hairline, runs a hard pale ridge of callus where the goat-hair sling has sat every day since she was nine. Every adult has this ridge. It is the first thing a child reaches up to touch on a parent's face, and when two people of the Stair greet each other closely they press these ridges together, brow to brow, for the length of a breath.

Her skin is dark from the glare, dark everywhere except in the folds. When she squints the creases at the corners of her eyes open into fine white lines, like cracks in glazed clay, and her face looks briefly drawn in chalk. She squints almost all the time. Her visor hangs at her throat, a curved slip of bone with a slit cut through it, and she pulls it up over her eyes when she turns toward the snowfields.

She walks leaning forward, even when she is carrying nothing. Her whole body is angled into a slope, and it stays angled on a level floor, on the flat rock of the headwall, or asleep. The people of the Stair lie on their felts curled a little uphill. If one of them ever went down the long valleys to the flat country and stood in a field, she would feel herself falling forward, with nothing to climb.

Her hands are cracked across the knuckles and very strong. The pads of her fingers are almost smooth, because stone has worn the whorls away. The people do not know each other by fingertips. They know each other by palms: the particular broad heat of a hand laid on the forearm, where the voice will come.

Her boots are soft. They are layered felt over goat-hide, with no hard sole, so that she can feel the lane through them. She knows when a stone under her foot has shifted since yesterday. She knows the difference between ice that will hold and ice with water moving under it. Her feet do half her seeing.

She has a name, but you will not hear it unless you are very close to her for a long time. What the town knows her by is a flash: two long, one short, thrown off the snow. That is her name as it crosses the lanes, and it is not a secret. Everyone can see it. The spoken name is something else. It is a soft, vowel-heavy sound, mostly breath, murmured against a forearm, and it belongs to a handful of people: her mother, the man she sleeps beside, the two children who have not yet learned to flash well. When she dies it will be spoken one last time into the drip-trough under her house's north wall, and then no one will say it again.

## How they speak

Their spoken language is almost all vowels and hums. Consonants are useless here, because a hard *k* or *t* dies in the air before it crosses the space between two faces. What carries, when anything does, is the low buzz of a voice in the chest, and what receives it is the skin. When two people of the Stair talk they hold each other's forearms, or rest a palm flat on the other's sternum, and they feel the words more than hear them. Lovers talk throat to throat. Mothers talk to infants with their lips against the soft top of the skull.

So speech is intimate by nature. You cannot speak to a crowd. You cannot speak to someone you are unwilling to touch. An argument conducted aloud is a strange, close, embracing thing, two people gripping each other's arms and humming furiously into each other's collarbones. The people of the Stair say that anger spoken aloud rarely survives the holding.

The public language is light.

A child's first mirror is a scrap of mica the size of a thumbnail, bound to her wrist with a hair cord. She wets it with her mouth and waves it, and the adults around her answer with bright little stabs off the snow. She learns to flash before she learns to hum a word. By six she can send a message the length of the town. By twelve she can do it without looking, holding her mirror low at her hip and tilting it by feel while her other hand steadies a stone on her back.

The grammar of light has registers, and they depend on what you throw the light off.

**Snow-flash** is common speech. It is bright and wide and meant for anyone. Warnings go by snow-flash, and so do the day's news, the calling of children, the naming of hands needed on a wall.

**Sky-flash** is angled straight up off the deep violet overhead and brought down again. It is narrow and precise and harder to catch. The headwall families use it, and people use it to speak to the headwall families. It is the register of respect, of formal asking, of old business between hearths.

**Water-flash** is thrown off the surface of a drip-trough or a pool in a lane-hole. It is dim and trembling and does not carry far, and it is for the person standing at the next doorway, or in the next window. It is how you tell someone something you would rather the whole town did not see. Courtship is conducted almost entirely in water-flash. A young man crouched by his family's trough at noon, tilting the water with one finger to send a shivering thread of light across the lane, is doing something everyone can see him doing and no one else can read.

There are rudenesses in the language too. Flashing directly into someone's eyes is mockery, or a threat, depending on how long you hold it. Withholding light is worse. To turn your mirror to its dull side when someone flashes you is to refuse them entirely. During mourning, people carry their mirrors wrapped in felt for as long as it takes the dead person's hearthstone to reach the lip. For those days they are silent in the only way that counts here, and the town gives them space. Their lanes go dim in the middle of the bright noon.

This means every person of the Stair lives two lives, a day life and a night life.

The day self is public, bright, and exact. It is a flash-name and a stream of signals going out across the whole tongue, and it is almost never alone. By day everyone can see what everyone says.

The night self lives in the dark houses by the fat lamps, and it is made of murmurs and skin. It is only ever with the four or six or ten people it loves. By night nothing can be seen, and everything must be touched.

People from elsewhere, the few who have climbed up to the cirque, have said that the Stair folk seem cold and bright by day and are soft and unguarded after dark, almost like different people. The Stair folk would not understand the complaint. Of course they are different. The sun is gone.

## What they own

The people of the Stair do not own stones.

This follows from everything else, and it shapes them more deeply than anything. No stone stays anyone's for long. The block you set in your uphill wall this morning will be at the downhill wall within half a year, and then you will lift it and carry it back up. If you stop, it will go past your house, past your neighbors, down into the drifting quarter and over the lip. Stones are borrowed from the ground. The ground wants them back.

What a family owns is a position. It holds a place on the slope and the name of a lane, and it holds the right to be *there*, which it pays for every hour with its backs. A household is a location that is kept. Wealth is height, and height is labor, and so the rich of the Stair are the hardest-working people in it. The headwall families, whose houses stand where the ground is fastest, keep a dozen carriers moving from dawn to dusk. Their grandmothers carry, and so do their children. It would be unthinkable, in the Stair, for the honored to rest. Resting is what drifting is.

Time is measured the same way. A **crossing** is the time it takes a stone set against the headwall to ride all the way down to the lip, roughly half a year. People count their ages in crossings. A child of ten crossings is five years old. A grandmother of a hundred and forty crossings has seen, in theory, every stone in the town pass beneath her seventy times. When the old people want to say something is ancient, they say *it has outlasted the whole town twice over*. The town they mean is the physical town, the matter of it. By that measure almost everything outlasts it.

Only one kind of thing stays in a family: the **lichen-stones**.

A few blocks in every house carry lichen colonies older than anyone's memory. There are orange rosettes, crusts of chartreuse, and black dots like soot, and some of them have spread across the stone's face for twenty generations. These stones are carried with more care than any others. They are never set in a place where a goat will scrape them, and never laid face-down. Every family knows its lichen-stones by sight and touch the way it knows its grandmothers' faces, and in a sense it *is* that knowledge: this orange ring, this black bloom shaped like a hand.

A newborn is laid on the family's oldest lichen-stone within its first day, skin against the crust, for as long as she will lie there without crying. When two people marry, each carries a lichen-stone from their family's house to the new house they will keep together. They set the two stones side by side in the same course, touching, so the colonies may, over many lifetimes, grow into each other. Some houses in the Stair have walls where six or seven family lichens have merged into one continuous mottled skin, and the people who live there can trace their ancestry across the stone with a fingertip.

A family drifts only when it has lost its hands. If it loses its lichen-stones, it is gone.

## Going down

The people of the Stair die the way the stones do: slowly, in public, and in one direction.

When someone is very old, or very sick, or simply finished, they may stop carrying. There is a phrase for this in murmur-speech that means something like *to go down with the stone*. It is not despair. It is closer to a decision about the shape of the end. The person's household stops rebuilding their room, or builds them a small separate house of their own, one they will not maintain. Then, over the following weeks and months, it carries them away.

A person going down sits in their doorway most days. They face uphill, the way everyone does, watching the town they are slowly leaving. Their mirror lies in their lap, dull side up. They have stopped speaking in light. They will only murmur now, to whoever comes to hold their arms.

And people come. That is the custom and the whole purpose of it. Every day the grandchildren walk down to visit, and every day the walk is a little longer. The house has passed three lanes, then five. It has left the quarter where the family has always lived and entered quarters where the old one knows no one. The neighbors there bring goat-milk and sit in the doorway too, and learn the dying person's spoken name, which they never knew before. In the last weeks of going down, people often become more loved than they have been in years. The whole town between their old house and their new position turns into a road their family walks every day.

Sometimes they change their minds. A woman who has gone down twenty lanes may stand up one morning, pick up a stone, and begin to carry. When that happens the lanes open around her. Her family comes down to help her climb, and in a few weeks she is home. There is no shame in it. The ground goes down, we go up. Anyone may choose to go up again.

Most do not. They go on down into the loose quarter by the lip, among the houses of the dead and the departed, where no one carries and every wall is leaning. When they die there, the family closes the door and walks back up the slope. The body stays inside. Over the next days the house comes apart, one stone at a time, and each stone tips over the lip into the milk-green tarn. The last to go is usually the hearthstone, heavy and flat, and families mark the day.

*My grandmother's hearthstone went over in the thaw.*

A few people go down to the lip to watch. They stand at the rubble edge and look into the tarn, which is opaque, pale, and the color of a lichen, and they wait for the soft *plock*. Then they unwrap their mirrors and walk home, flashing again. Most families do not watch at all. They have walked down to that house every day for months. They have already said everything, arm in arm.

## Ask them who lives here

All of this is the people, and they are the ones you see from the snowfield rim: the crosshatched lines of carriers, the thousand small flashes, the anthill.

But if you pressed your forehead to one of them and asked, in murmur-speech, *who lives on the Stair*, she would not say *we do*.

She would say it in a way that is hard to translate. The nearest version is something like *the one who keeps the words*. Or *the cold under*. Or she would not say it aloud at all. She would take your hand and press it flat against the ground, against the wet weeping stone of the lane, and hold it there.

This is the thing they believe. They do not quite call it a god, because they would not know what a god was. It is simply the resident: the ice.

Somewhere beneath the tongue, beneath the voids and the dark meltwater and the pikas' tunnels, lies the core of old ice that makes the rock glacier move. No one has seen it. Every child who has pried up a slab has seen water well into the hole within a breath, and understood that there is something cold down there, always, that the stones are sweating off. The ice is older than the lichens and older than the lane-names. It is older than the first carriers, who, the old people say, came up to the cirque following the pikas and found a ground that walked and decided to live on it anyway.

The ice moves the town, and the people know this plainly. But they believe something else about it too, and it comes from the silence.

Sound dies in the Stair. A shout does not cross a lane. Everyone who lives here knows this the way they know the cold, and anyone from elsewhere can give the reasons: the thin air, and the voids, all those millions of wet dark spaces between the stones that drink sound like moss. The people of the Stair do not dispute any of that. But they ask the question anyone would ask about something being drunk: *where does it go?*

It goes down. That is their answer. Every word ever spoken aloud in the Stair has sunk through the gaps in the rubble, into the cold water, and down to the ice. The ice has taken it and kept it. That is what the ice does. It listens. It has always listened. It is listening now, under your boots, to everything. The silence of the Stair is not an absence. It is the attention of something very large and very cold, taking in every sound as it is made.

So the people of the Stair murmur. Partly it is necessity, and partly it is courtesy. You speak close so that your words reach the one you meant them for before they sink. You speak softly so as not to throw your voice down into the dark carelessly. Children are taught never to speak into a lane-hole, the fresh wet hole where a slab has been lifted and water is welling up, because anything said into that hole goes straight down. It is too direct, like shouting into someone's ear.

Lovers do it anyway. It is the most private thing two people of the Stair can do: kneel together over a newly opened hole at dusk, when the lanes are empty, and say something into the water. Neither of them will hear it. Only the ice will. But it will be kept.
