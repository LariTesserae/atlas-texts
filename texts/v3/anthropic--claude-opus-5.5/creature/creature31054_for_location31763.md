# Who Lives in the Knot

The first answer is *no one*. The first answer is always no one, in a room like this. The men are gone, the lamps are gone, and the catwalk ends in air. Whatever keeps the city warm keeps it warm without a pulse.

The second answer is *everything*: the crickets, the moths, the fungus, the root, the fern. The room is full of living things, and you could list them the way the tags list the city.

But a list is not the same as an answer, and when you look closer, most of the Knot's living things are only passing through.

The crickets come and go with the cool air over the sump. They are born, they leap at the knock, they die, and others come, and none of them lasts long enough to learn the room. The moths are visitors who made a mistake. The stone keeps them, but only as dust patterns under the mineral skin. The fern is an accident of a crack and forty minutes of grey. The fungus is a process more than a creature, a slow fire eating canvas. And the root, which seems most like an inhabitant, is not really here at all. It is the fingertip of something that lives above, in the wind and traffic and weather. It reaches down the way you might reach into a drawer in the dark. Its body, its crown and its leaves and its seasons, is on a street somewhere. The root is a visitor too, just a very slow one.

Only one thing lives in the Knot in the full sense: born to it, bound to it, knowing it from the inside. It lives at the bottom, where the oldest iron goes down into the water.

---

## The Animal

She is about the length of a forearm. She is pale in the way the lagging is pale, a soft and faintly yellowed white, and her skin is thin enough that with a light you could see the dim shapes inside her: the dark thread of the gut, the slow pulse of a heart the size of a lentil. Her body is long and eel-like, and her legs are small and oddly delicate, with three fingers in front and two toes behind, as if she were an old sketch of an animal and nobody ever finished her. Her head is flattened, blunt, faintly spade-shaped.

Behind her head, on either side, are her gills: three feathery tufts on each side, pink as the inside of a lip, flushed with blood. Apart from the glow behind the sight glass, they are the only red in the Knot, and the only red that moves. In still water they barely stir. When the water is poor she fans them slowly, and they open like small hands.

Where her eyes should be there are two specks under the skin, like grains of pepper pressed into wax.

She has lived here, as far as anyone could reckon if anyone were reckoning, for about as long as the room has been quiet. She may be more than a hundred years old. Animals like her live slowly. Her heart takes its time. She can go years without eating. Her body barely spends itself, the way the flowstone barely grows. She and the room share that scale of time. Everything else lives faster.

---

## The Light She Remembers

She was not always blind.

Her kind hatch with eyes. For a season, as larvae, they can see. The eyes are small and simple, but they work. As the animal grows, the skin closes over them and they sink and fade. In total dark there is nothing for them to do.

She hatched while the men still came.

It is almost certain that the first thing she ever saw was lamplight: a swinging yellow glow above the sump, broken into pieces by the grating, sliding across the water as someone walked the catwalk. She could not have known it was a lamp. She would not have known what a man was. But for a few months of her life the world had a top, and the top was sometimes gold, and it moved in a rhythm that was not the room's rhythm. It was the rhythm of shifts and footsteps and a man stopping to lean over a valve and give it one more turn.

Then her skin grew over her eyes, and the lamps stopped coming, and she never learned which ended first.

She keeps whatever an animal like her can keep of that. It is not a memory in words. It is a disposition. When something warm passes over the water, she rises toward it instead of away.

## What the Water Tells Her

To an eye, the Knot is almost black. To her it has never been dark.

Water carries everything, and she lives in water. Along her flanks run the pits of her lateral line, pressure-sensing organs that feel every stir and shiver in the sump. Scattered across her snout are other organs, finer ones, the ampullae, which feel electricity: the minute fields that living muscle makes, and the fields of other things too.

The Knot is full of those other things.

Put copper and iron into the same warm, mineral-laden water and you have made a battery. The room is a stack of batteries. Every era of pipe that meets the sump is a different metal. Riveted wrought iron sits beside cast iron, galvanised steel beside copper. The runoff from every stratum drips down and joins them. Each metal holds its own faint electrical tension against the others. Where they meet in the water, current flows. It is slight and ceaseless, and it is the same slow current that eats the pipes from inside.

She hears it.

Not hears, exactly, but it is the nearest word. To her the sump is not silent water with some pipes standing in it. It is a chord. The wrought iron has a low, broad potential, like the lowest string of a bass. The cast iron sits close beside it, slightly off, a faint beating between them. The copper, where its drip-straws feed the water, is sharp and bright, a high thin note. The newer steel is something else again. She knows the room by these tensions the way you know a house in the dark by the smell of each room. She could not read the tags, but she has never needed to. She knows *Asylum East* and *Municipal Baths No. 2* as voices.

She knows, too, which pipes are warm and which are cold. The live lines bring heat down into the water where they plunge. A dead line's stub goes cool by degrees over years, and its note changes as the chemistry around it changes. When the men capped a line, it did not go silent for her. It went strange. It slowly changed key.

In a sense she has heard the city change above her. A district demolished, a building gone, a school become a car park: she knew none of that. What she felt was a slow shifting in the chord at the bottom of the room.

## Her Day

She has no day, but she has the room's cycle.

Through the high-load hours the water is warmest and the chord is loudest, with current running strong through the hot lines. She stays low in the angle between two wrought-iron mains, where they go down into the silt. Over the decades her resting has pressed a hollow there, and the mineral fall has built up a faint rim around it, a shallow cradle shaped exactly like her. Rust floc drifts around her there, soft as ash. She lies in it, gills barely moving, for hours or weeks.

Toward the small hours the load falls. The chord softens. Then the rain begins.

To her the rain is a new sky. The water's surface, which she knows as a ceiling that is sometimes still, becomes a field of small arrivals. Each drop that lands is a tiny pressure blow, and each has its own signature, because each fell from a different height, off a different lip, carrying a different metal. Drops off the copper are sharp-flavoured and electrically bright. Drops off the iron are dull. Drops off the lagging are soft and alkaline. Some carry faint traces of the fungus. The sump level rises by a fingernail's width. Fresh water, carrying a little oxygen, spreads down through the column, and she opens her gills to it.

She lifts her head in the rain hours. If she has a pleasure, this is it.

---

## The Knock

Then, perhaps once every quarter hour, the knock.

To her it is not a sound. It is the whole world flinching. The pressure wave comes down the old iron and into the water, and her lateral line takes it all at once, flank to flank. The chord in the sump stutters. Silt lifts. For a moment the room's electrical voices jumble as metal shivers against metal.

Above, on the grating, the crickets leap.

Most land again on iron. Now and then, though, one lands wrong, on the lip of the sump or clear into the water, and kicks.

A cricket's kicking legs have a field. To her it is the brightest thing in the world: small, frantic, alive, not metal. She turns toward it with the slow certainty of something that has done this a thousand times. She does not hurry, because she does not need to. The cricket is in her world now, and in her world she is the only thing that knows where everything is.

This is how she eats: perhaps a few times a year, sometimes less. The knock prunes the flowstone straws, startles the crickets, and feeds the salamander. It is the room's heartbeat, and to her it is also a dinner bell that rings a hundred times for every meal.

She eats other things too: small blind shrimp in the silt, pale and flicking, worms, whatever settles. But the crickets are her feast, and they come to her on the knock.

## The Thing That Fell

Once, a long time ago, something much larger came into the water.

She felt it before it arrived: a scrabbling, a body's field far too big, coming along the catwalk overhead. Then came a fall, a heavy entry, and water that thrashed. For some time a creature many times her size struggled at the edge of the sump, in the narrow gap between the oldest pipes where the brick drops sheer and there is nothing to climb. It had four toes, and it had come out along the catwalk's paste of dust toward the broken end.

Its field was hot, much hotter than hers, and loud with fear. She stayed low. It weakened. Its field faltered and flickered. Then, over a night, it went out.

In the months after, the shrimp came to it in numbers she had never felt. She ate well that year. Then the body was gone into the silt, and the mineral fall began its slow work on what remained. Somewhere in the sump now are small bones wearing a skin of calcite and iron, the way everything here does eventually. She passes over them without attention. They are part of the chord now, a faint lime note.

It is the largest thing that has happened in her life, apart from the lamps going away.

---

## Alone, or Not

She is probably alone.

Probably. At the far end of the sump, below the waterline, there is an outflow: a culvert that carries the overflow away into some deeper drain that no map remembers either. Water comes and goes through it very slowly. And a few times in her long life she has felt something in the outflow that was not metal and not prey. It was a field shaped like her own, long, slow and cool, lingering at the edge of her world.

Once, decades back, one of those came all the way in. For a season there were two of them in the sump. Afterward she laid eggs, a small clutch pressed one by one to the underside of an old flange, pale beads in the dark. Some of them hatched. For a few months the sump held small moving sparks with eyes. Then they drifted, as the young do, and went out through the culvert into the deeper water.

So there may be others, down there in the lower drains, in water that no one has seen. They would be her children, grown now, blind now, each of them keeping in its body some faint disposition toward light it saw once and cannot name. It is even possible that the fields she has felt in the outflow since then were theirs, coming back to the edge of the room they were born in, and turning away.

She does not go into the culvert. The Knot is the place she knows. Beyond it, the chord ends.

---

## The Root

There is one thing in her world that is changing toward her.

For years, perhaps twenty or thirty, there has been a new voice in the chord. It is very faint, fainter than any metal, and it does not come from the water. It comes from above. It is not steady like a pipe's tension. It pulses faintly with the room's cycle, rising in the rain hours and falling in the dry. It is alive. It is the only living thing she has ever sensed that is neither prey nor her own kind nor the frightened heat of the thing that fell.

It is the root.

It is still far above her, spreading its net over the upper lines and drinking the condensate. But it is growing down, a few centimetres a year, and every year its field is a little stronger at the surface of the water. She could not tell you this. She could not tell you anything. But her cradle between the iron mains is no longer where it was. Over the past decade, so slowly that a watcher would never catch her at it, she has shifted her resting place. She has moved a hand's width at a time across the silt toward the spot beneath where the root is coming.

It may be nothing more than the oxygen. Water under the root is a little richer in the rain hours. It may be the drips, which fall there with a softer, plant-sweet taste. Or it may be the old disposition of an animal that once saw a gold light moving overhead and has leaned toward any warm thing above the water ever since.

---

## What She Is

She does not tend the Knot. She has never turned a valve and could not. She does not seal the leaks or lay the flowstone or hold up the lines. The room does all of that itself.
