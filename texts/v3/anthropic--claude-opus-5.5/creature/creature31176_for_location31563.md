# Who Lives in the Hollow Beneath Keel

## First Answer: The Knapper on Its Circuit

The first answer seems to be the knappers, so start with one of them.

This one is old, and you can tell by its tines. A young knapper's bone tines are pale and blunt-tipped, freshly grown from the ridges above its wrists. This one's are dark as tea and worn into a slight hook, the shape a tool takes after ten thousand pressings against the same angle of glass. It sheds them and regrows them, but the new ones grow in already curved, as though the body has learned the gesture and builds it in ahead of time.

It has no eyes that you would recognize. Where eyes would be there are two shallow dishes of fine hair, and it turns them toward the sparks the way a flower turns toward sun. It does not see the floor. It sees the floor being disturbed: a lit smear where grazers passed, a single blue tick where a gypsum blade gave way. It hunts damage the way other animals hunt food. The rest of the time it works by touch, and its hands are the most sensitive part of it. The pads of its long fingers are callused against cutting, but under the callus they can feel the difference between an edge struck a thousand years ago and one struck last week. Old edges are not duller, because nothing here dulls. They are *quieter*. They have settled into themselves. The knapper can feel that, and it does not like it.

Its circuit takes about forty days. Along the way it passes three cairns, one copper lattice, and a scratched shelf where it stops and lays both palms flat and stays for a long time. Then it moves on and kneels at the next small wound in the world, presses, and a flake springs away with its tiny flash. The knapper holds the fresh edge between two fingers for a moment, the way you might hold a sleeping child's hand to make sure it is warm.

It sleeps curled in the lee of the raised hexagon rings, and it never sleeps long. Almost nothing here sleeps long. That will matter later.

This is a true answer, but it is not the whole one. The knapper does not behave like an owner. It behaves like staff.

## Second Answer: The Body

Step back and the crowd starts to look like something else.

The dew-carriers move water from body to body, and the total never changes. That is not a population. That is blood. The hangers are woven into one net across a continent, drawing in the heavy gas and letting out long soft breaths as they adjust, and nobody has seen one born or die, because a lung does not have births. It has a shape. The shelf-grazers lie in carpets two and three deep and graze the thin mineral skin off fresh rock, shifting a body-length every few hours. They are a slow tide of cells renewing a surface. The ceiling-folk take away what has gone still, dry it, and return it as membrane, handle, or shelter. That is how a body recycles its own spent parts.

The knappers, then, are the part that remembers the correct shape and restores it when it is damaged. Healing. Or an immune system with an unusually strict idea of what counts as injury.

So perhaps what lives in the Hollow is the Hollow: one animal the size of a continent, whose organs happen to be separate creatures, with a warm glass floor for skin and a heavy band of gas for a diaphragm, living its single long life in the dark.

This is closer. But a body grows, ages, and scars. This one refuses to. Every system in it is arranged against change, and not in the easy way of a thing that simply stays the same. It works at staying the same. Tens of thousands of knappers walk forty-day circuits just to undo time. Nothing does that much work without a reason. Bodies don't refuse to age for their own sake.

Something is being kept. The next question is who it is being kept *for*.

## The Scratched Shelves

The knappers stop longest at the scratched glass, so start there.

The lines are fine and dense, and from a distance they look like writing. Up close, under a palm, they read differently. They are not a sequence of events. Nothing on them seems to happen. They are the same picture, drawn again and again at slightly different scales, laid over one another until the shelf is nearly frosted: the angle of a shelf of glass, the count of blades in a gypsum field, the position of every flake in a spill of scree.

It is a single moment, recorded obsessively. It is the Hollow on its first day.

On the oldest shelves, the ones deepest in the dark, far from any cairn, there is one thing the copies always include and never explain. Along one margin is a shape pressed into the glass itself. It was not scratched. It was *pressed*, the way a knapper presses a tine, but it is the size of a lake. It is broad and faintly ridged and curved at one end like a hooked tine worn down by ages of use.

Every knapper who reads that shelf rests its hand inside the mark, as if measuring how much it would take to fill it.

## Third Answer: The One Underneath

The warmth comes up from below, even everywhere to within a fraction of a degree, as if the floor rests on the back of some large sleeping body.

That line in the first description was offered as an image. It is not an image.

Below the floor, under the basalt hexagons and the tilted plates of glass, lies the one who made this place. The knappers do not have a word for it that translates. The closest is the sound a flake makes when it springs cleanly away from a good platform, that dull *tock* of the dense air. They call it that, or they call it nothing and put their palms on the floor instead.

It is a knapper, or the thing knappers are a small memory of. Once, not long ago in the time of stone, it set the worn point of itself against the underside of the world and pressed. The crust bowed and did not break. A great sheet of rock sprang away sideways, and what was left was the Hollow, a flake-scar a continent wide, its surfaces still bright with the fracture. The scree heaped everywhere really is the leavings of an enormous knapper who worked here once. It did not wander off. It lay down.

It was only meant to be a rest between strokes. Anyone who works stone knows the moment: you strike, you study the scar, you set the piece down and flex your hand, and you intend to take up the work again in a breath or two. The Hollow is that pause. The lid above is the far side of a flake not yet struck. The floor is its hand. The hum, too low to hear and never changing pitch, is its breathing, slowed nearly to nothing.

Its warmth is the only weather here. Everything living grew up in that warmth, the way moss grows on a sleeping animal's back. Grazers, hangers, dew-carriers, and ceiling-folk all live in the workshop, in the brief quiet between one stroke and the next. They have been living there, by their own count, for as long as anything has lived.

The knappers are different. Watch their tines, the way they grow already curved into the shape of the work. Watch their hands, the only hands in the Hollow that the glass does not cut. They are made of the same stuff as the sleeper. They may be calluses it shed as it lay down, or grains of its grip that fell loose, or small dreams it had in the first minutes of sleep that got up and walked. They carry its craft in miniature. They know the correct shape of the Hollow because the correct shape is the last thing their maker looked at before it closed its eyes.

## Why the Edges Are Kept

It would be easy to think the knappers are faithful servants, keeping the workshop exactly as it was so the maker can wake and take up the work mid-stroke without losing its place. They may think so themselves. Some of them do.

Listen to the rest of what the Hollow does, though.

Nothing here makes loud sound, because loud sound travels. The low tones carry and the high ones are swallowed, so the dense air muffles every crack of breaking crystal into something soft. Most animals would rather stay unlit unless they choose to move, and when they move they do it slowly, and their light is cold, faint, and brief. Stillness is dark, and after long enough you learn to call the dark *rest*. The whole Hollow has arranged itself around being quiet in a room where someone is sleeping.

Think about what a sleeper does when it half-surfaces. It does not wake all at once. Its eyes open a sliver and take in the room, and it asks, without words, *how long?* If the light has moved, if the fire has gone to ash, if the tea is cold and the edge of the work has quieted and settled, then it has been a long time. It sits up. It reaches for the tool.

If everything is exactly as it was, the edge still bright with the moment of breaking, the scree lying where it fell, the warmth unchanged, the air unstirred, then no time has passed at all. It was only a blink. The sleeper sighs a breath long enough that it might be the hangers exhaling across a continent, and it sinks back down.

That is what the knappers are doing. They are not preserving the work. They are keeping it too early to wake. Each worn edge they renew is a clock set back to the first moment. Each fallen thing returned to its place is one more reason, for the sleeper looking up from below, to believe it has only just lain down. The shelves are not records for the maker. They are a script, so that every knapper can perform the same unchanging room.

The knappers know, as surely as they know any edge, what the next stroke is. The lid of the Hollow is the far side of a flake. Above the lid is Keel, and whatever lives on Keel, under a sky, in weather that changes. When the maker wakes and takes up the work, the next flake to spring away will be all of that.

Whether the knappers keep the room still out of mercy for the world above, or out of love for the one below and a wish that it never learn how long it has lain alone, or because the Hollow's small crowded life only exists in the pause and they would like it to go on, is not written on any shelf. Probably it is all three. Probably it no longer matters. The old knapper with the hooked tines does not think about reasons. It kneels, it presses, and it holds the fresh edge between two fingers to make sure it is new.

## So: Who Lives There

A sleeper lives there, a maker of edges, curled under a continent of its own work, breathing once in a span of time the grazers would measure in generations.

In its warmth lives a crowd that is very nearly a single body, circulating one fixed measure of water and exhaling one long breath through a woven net. The crowd is abundant, busy, and quiet, the way life is quiet in a house where someone has been asleep a very long time and everyone has agreed, without ever discussing it, not to let them know.

Among the crowd walk the small ones who are made of the sleeper's hands. They keep the room exactly as it was and walk their slow circuits undoing the evidence of time, one bright flake at a time.

If you have come down from Keel, you live there now too, at least for a while. You are the only thing in the Hollow that changes. Your breath is too quick and your footsteps are too heavy, and behind you the floor lights up in a trail of blue-white sparks that the dark has never seen before.

Somewhere far off, the hair-dishes of a knapper turn toward that light. It gets up from the lee of a hexagon ring. It flexes its long hands. It is not angry. It is coming to put you back the way things were: to smooth each glittering footprint into first-day glass, and then, gently, with the same care it gives an edge, to make you quiet.

If you stand still on the warm floor and let the dark settle over you, you will feel the hum rising through your soles. It is slow and even. Without deciding to, you will find your own breathing slowing to match it, so the one beneath you does not notice you came.
