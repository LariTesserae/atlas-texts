# Who Lives on the Uncovered Shelf

## At first, the honest answer

The honest first answer is almost no one.

Stand at the lip of the shelf with the cloud floor below you and the ice wall above, and the census is short: a chough on an updraft, a moth lying on its back on a slab, a spider as black and still as a drop of tar. The birds are passing through. The lammergeiers belong to the whole ridge, not to this shelf. The snow leopard is a rumor. The geese belong to the sky between two countries. The shelf is somewhere most of its animals visit on their way to being somewhere else.

But you were told to kneel, so kneel. The residents are down here, and they are small.

## The furnace crews

Begin on the top of the glacier, among the cryoconite holes.

Each hole is a room. It is a cylinder of clear ice a few centimeters wide, with a pane of meltwater for a ceiling that freezes every night and thaws every noon. The floor is a dark pellet of soot and grit. That pellet is inhabited. Filamentous cyanobacteria bind the grains into a mat. Among them live other bacteria, algae, and the spores of fungi. Rotifers, the wheel animals, spin their crowns of cilia in the water. Tardigrades clamber across the grit on eight stubby legs. They are no bigger than a period at the end of a sentence, and they can survive being frozen, dried, and frozen again.

These are the most enclosed lives on the shelf. A cryoconite hole can stay sealed under its lid for a whole season, a sunlit terrarium, its community cut off from every other hole. Thousands of these separate settlements lie across the ice, each on its own coin of black carbon.

The dark pellet does two things for them. It is their ground, and it is also the thing melting their home downward. Their floor is coal smoke from a lowland they will never touch. The sun strikes it, it warms, and it sinks, and the whole community sinks with it, a centimeter at a time, into the glacier. They are passengers riding a slow furnace that the plains built and the wind delivered.

When the ice finally melts out from under them, they will not die. They will be decanted, in the milky slurry, onto the naked stone below. The black grit, the dried mats, and the cysts of the rotifers and tardigrades will be spread across rock that has never been colonized. They will be among the first to arrive on the new ground, and they will have been in the ice all along.

So the first thing that lives here lives in the machine's exhaust. The residue of industry is its whole world.

That is not yet the answer, though. Keep going. Go down off the ice to the place where it meets the stone.

## The one who lives at the edge

At the foot of the ice wall, the curtain of drips falls on stone that was under the glacier weeks ago. There the rock is still wet from the ice's underside, and it has never felt wind. That is where to find it, but only at night, and only if you are patient.

It is an insect, or near enough, a relative of the ice crawlers of other ranges. It is about as long as the first joint of your finger. It is pale in a way that is not quite white, more the color of weak amber or old candle wax, faintly translucent, so that in a headlamp you can almost see its gut. It has no wings and never will. Its eyes are small and reduced to little more than patches, and it moves through the world mostly by its antennae, which are long, many-segmented, and constantly working. Two slender cerci trail behind it like a second pair of antennae pointed backward. It walks slowly, deliberately, like something older than it looks.

It is built for cold, and for nothing else. The temperature at which it is most at home is close to freezing, roughly the temperature of meltwater. Below a few degrees under zero it stiffens and has to shelter. A few degrees above ten, its body begins to fail. If you picked one up and held it in your palm, the warmth of your hand could kill it. That is the kind of creature this is. Human heat is a hazard to it.

It is a scavenger and a night hunter. It comes out after the afternoon wind has dropped and the cloud has settled. It walks the snow margin and the cold wet stone at the foot of the ice, feeling ahead with its antennae for whatever the updraft dropped: an aphid numbed by cold, a moth with frost in its scales, a winged ant that never landed where it meant to. The spiders take what is still moving in daylight. This creature takes what the cold has stilled by dark. Nothing on the shelf is better placed to eat the dying freight of the wind, because it is the only hunter that is fully awake when that freight is coldest.

It lives a long time for something so small. A lifetime of cold means slow metabolism and slow growth. It may spend five years, six, perhaps more, working toward adulthood, molting again and again, each instar a little larger. For a small insect, that is a long life lived at a very low burn.

And it lives in one place only: the edge.

## A band twenty meters wide

The creature needs cold, but it also needs food, and those two needs pull in opposite directions.

On the ice itself there is little shelter and little for it to eat. The glacier surface belongs to the ice worms and the springtails, and it is exposed. Out on the older stone, toward the lip of the shelf, the ground has had decades to dry. There the summer sun warms the dark rock in the afternoon until the surface is dangerously hot for a creature like this, and the crevices lose the constant seep of meltwater that keeps them cold.

Between the two lies a narrow belt. It is the strip where the ice is close enough to breathe cold air down over the stone, where the drip line keeps every crack wet and near freezing, and where the rubble along the base of the wall offers deep, cold cavities to shelter in by day. It is also close enough to the snow margin that the windfall is easy to reach at night. The belt is perhaps twenty meters wide. That is the creature's entire habitable world.

Nothing about this belt stays put.

The ice retreats every summer, and the belt of cold, wet stone goes with it. A crack the creature sheltered in five years ago now lies out on open stone where the afternoon sun bakes it. Its grandmother's hunting ground has gone dry, and there is a coin of gray-green lichen starting in the lee of a boulder she once hid behind. The females lay their eggs singly in cold crevices and moss at the foot of the wall. Each generation the eggs are laid a little farther upslope, because the wall is a little farther upslope. The young hatch into a world that is already moving away from them.

So the population is not really settled on this shelf. It is a wave, climbing the shelf slowly. It moves at the speed of the ice, a few meters a year, which makes it one of the slowest migrations there is. It passes underneath the fastest and highest one, the geese that cross the whole range in a single night. On the same shelf, one journey is measured in hours and the other in generations.

## First stone

Watch it long enough and a particular preference emerges.

The crawler does not merely tolerate the newest stone. It is most at home there. The freshest bands of rock, uncovered only this summer, still glazed with the ice's own meltwater and still smelling (if it smells anything) of the underside of the glacier, are where it hunts most freely and shelters most easily. The rubble the ice has just set down is loose, deep, and full of cold air. Fresh stone is a good place for it. It lives on what nobody has touched.

Consider what that means. On the whole shelf, the one animal most closely matched to the newest ground is the one that arrived at the moment it was born. The lichen follows the edge by decades. The moss follows the lichen. The cushion plants follow the moss by a lifetime. But this creature is on the stone within weeks of the ice letting go, and no other resident arrives on such new ground so quickly. It walks where no foot has been, because until last month there was no ground there to walk on.

Every inhabitant after it arrives at a place that has already been walked.

## Its own ledger

There is a second ledger on the shelf, and almost no one has read it.

The crawler molts many times in its long life. Each molt leaves behind a husk: a perfect pale copy of itself, split down the back, with every antenna segment and every claw intact. The husks are left in the cold crevices at the foot of the wall. Most are eaten, blown away, or ground to nothing. Some are not. A few wedge themselves deep in a crack and dry there, and the crack stays dry once the ice has retreated, and the husk stays too.

If you had patience, a hand lens, and a great deal of time, you could walk down the shelf from the ice toward the lip, crack by crack, and find them. They would be scattered and rare, but they would lie in a rough line of lines, band after band. Each cluster of husks marks where the edge was in the year that molt happened. The glacier writes its record in dark and pale layers of snow and soot. The crawler writes its own in shed skins, pressed into the stone behind the retreating ice.

Read in order from the lip upward, they tell the same story as the ice: a steady climb, year after year, the edge moving off and the edge-dwellers following. The glacier records the smoke. The crawler records the retreat. Between them, you could reconstruct most of what has happened here.

## What it does not know

It is nearly blind, and it is out only at night, and its world is twenty meters across. It does not see the orange stain of the lowland cities on the underside of the cloud. The rolling roar of the airliner, if it registers at all, is a tremor of air no different from the wind. It has never seen a turbine or tasted diesel. Nothing in its body or behavior knows that the plains exist.

And yet it is the shelf's most thoroughly industrial animal.

Every part of its life is set by the machine it cannot perceive. The warmed air sets the pace of the ice. The soot sets how fast the ice melts. The melt rate sets where the edge will be next year, which sets where it must lay its eggs. The same afternoon updraft that drops its food onto the snow also carries up the haze that darkens the ice. Its food and its displacement arrive on the same wind. Its whole migration, generations long, is being driven by engines that it will never see.

It does not know the loop exists. It lives inside it completely.

## The front

By now it should be clear that this creature is not really alone, and that it is not really the answer either. It is the leading edge of something larger.

Look at the shelf as a single thing moving upslope. At the very front are the cryoconite passengers, decanted from the ice onto the rock, and the crawler hunting on the first wet stone. Behind them come the lichens, a few years back, their gray-green stains widening a millimeter a year. Behind the lichens come the mosses, holding rock flour in their cushions. Behind the mosses, decades back, near the lip, are the cushion plants and saxifrages and the one or two tufts of sedge. The pikas, the blue sheep, and some future meadow wait farther down the mountain, where the ground has had centuries.

What lives here is a front, a procession walking up the mountain behind the retreating ice at the pace the ice allows. It has a front line and a rear guard. Its members arrive in a fixed order, because each one needs the ground the one before it made. The crawler needs the cold of the ice. The lichen needs stone that holds still. The moss needs the flour and the crevices. The cushion plants need the thin soil the moss began. The shelf is a single slow organism made of many species, moving uphill.

Seen from the air, over eighty years, it would look like a stain spreading upslope across the gray rock, faint green at the rear and colorless at the front. The front would be where the crawler is.

## The arithmetic

The ice wall is forty meters high. The shelf is not infinitely long.

There is arithmetic here that no one on the shelf can do but that the shelf will obey. The ice gives up so many meters of stone a summer, and the shelf runs back toward the head of the range only so far. Eventually the edge will reach the head of the shelf, where the glacier comes down from the high snowfields, and then it will thin, and then, in some summer not so very far off, there will be no wall. There will be no drip line, no curtain of beads, no cold air falling off the ice at night.

When that happens, the front will lose its leader. The lichen, the moss, and the cushion plants will keep coming. They do not need the ice. They only needed it to leave. But the crawler lives in the twenty-meter band where ice meets stone, and when the ice is gone, the band is gone. It will retreat into the last deep, cold rubble at the head of the shelf, where cold air still pools on the calmest nights. It will hunt there for a few years, maybe a few generations, then fewer. Its habitat will not have been destroyed in any ordinary way. It will simply have stopped being produced.

Then what lives here will be the rear of the procession: lichen, moss, the pikas cutting hay, the long slow business of becoming a meadow. That meadow is a fine thing to imagine. It is a thousand years off, but it is a real future, and the shelf is heading toward it.

## So, who lives here

The birds visit. The geese pass over. The furnace crews ride the ice down and spill out onto the stone. The spiders wait for the wind, and the lichens take their millimeter a year.

But the one who truly lives here, the one whose whole existence is shaped by this particular place and this particular moment, is the pale thing at the foot of the ice. It walks the wettest, newest, coldest stone by night, eats what the lowland wind dropped and the cold killed, and dies in the heat of a human hand. It is the first animal on every band of ground the ice gives up. It lays its eggs a little farther uphill each year and leaves its shed skins behind in the cracks, a record of the retreat.

It lives on the edge between the ice and the uncovered stone. The shelf keeps producing that edge for now, one summer at a time. When the ice is gone, the edge will be gone, and so will the creature that lived on it.

Somewhere at the head of the shelf, in a crack that stays cold longest, one of the last of them will molt. The husk will lie there, split down the back and perfect, on ground the ice uncovered that summer. Then the lichen will reach it, and the moss after that.
