# What Lives on the Floor

## Nothing, at First

The honest answer is that nothing lives here. That is what you would say after the first long walk, from red glow to red glow across the wet plates. Nothing crawls in the seams. Nothing startles out of the ferns. No eyes catch the light of the chimney-mouths. The Floor was not made for animals, and animals never came. You would say it and believe it, and you would be describing the Floor the way it describes itself: a made thing, running down.

But stay long enough to learn the strokes. Stay long enough to hear the phrases in the chatter come round again. Then you begin to notice the tracks.

## The Tracks

They cross the plain in long, faint lines. They are ribbons of dead moss gone grey and brittle, and rows of fern stalks curled and dry, and here and there a scatter of papery white that was once petals. They are not straight, but they do not wander the way water wanders, down the old engineering grid. They cut across the seams. They climb the gentle flanks of the machine-ridges and go down the other side. Some bend in slow arcs a mile wide. Some double back. One, which you might follow for days, spirals.

The description calls them the paths the hushes have taken, "like the tracks of slow creatures crossing the plain." That is careful language. It keeps the word *creatures* at arm's length, inside a *like*.

Take the *like* away. Something is moving across the Floor, and the moss is its footprint.

## The Hushes

A hush has a body, and the body has a shape.

Step into one and you feel it before you see it. The roar drops away all at once, as the description says, like stepping out of a river. Walk its edge in the dark, though, and you learn that the edge is not a circle. It has lobes. It has a long, narrow reach on one side, like an arm or a tongue, where the cancellation stretches out along a seam-gully. There is a ragged fringe where the silence frays and the chatter leaks back in, gear-teeth nibbling at the stillness. Map it by ear, pacing and listening and marking with stones, and you get a figure. It is lopsided and particular. No two are alike.

And the figure changes. Over a season, which here means some tens of thousands of strokes, the lobes shift. An arm lengthens and is taken back. The whole shape slides, a few feet a year, and the garden inside it moves too. The garden grows at the front and dies at the back. It is the slowest walking in the world, but it is walking.

What is it made of? Of nothing, in the strictest sense. A hush is the place where two great voices from below arrive at once, out of step, and undo each other. It is a shape cut out of noise. It exists only because the roar exists, the way a shadow exists only because of a light. Take away the engines and the hush would not be quieter. It would not be there at all.

But a shadow does not keep a garden.

## The Body That Grows

Here is what the long watcher learns, and it takes many shifts to learn it.

The moss is not only following the hush. The moss is holding it.

The black-coppery cushions are dense and soft and full of water. Laid thick across the plates, they drink vibration. Each cushion takes a little of the tremble out of the iron beneath it. A single cushion does almost nothing. A field of them, knee-deep, drags at the sound the way a marsh drags at a tide. In a mature hush the edge is sharper, the stillness is deeper, and the drift is slower than in a young one. The garden *anchors* the silence. It damps the returning roar at the trailing edge and holds the node in place a little longer than the machines alone would let it stay.

So the silence makes room for the garden, and the garden keeps the silence. Neither is the creature by itself. The creature is the two together: a stillness with a body of moss, or a body of moss with a stillness at its heart. It is half acoustic and half vegetable. It is the only living thing on the Floor, and it is alive in a way the Floor's makers could not have planned. It grew in the gaps of their machine, where their machine cancelled itself out.

Call them the Quiet, if they need a name. A single one is a Quiet. The word is the same for one and many, the way silence is.

## Anatomy

A Quiet has an outer body and an inner body.

The outer body is the moss-field and the ferns. The ferns stand stiff and curled at the margins, where some of the tremble still reaches. They are coarse, armoured things, the Quiet's skin. Inward the moss deepens, and the ferns grow taller and uncurl. The water in the pools lies flat.

Flat water on the Floor is rare. It is the only water on the Floor that is not printed with a pattern. Everywhere else, every puddle is etched glass, ridged and gridded and humming with the fingerprint of whatever runs beneath. In a Quiet the pools are black and perfectly still, and they do what no other water here can do. They reflect.

So a Quiet has eyes, of a kind. Stand at the heart of an old one and look down. In the flat black water you will see the far-off chimney-mouths, sullen red, doubled. You will see the lid of vapour, faintly lit from beneath. When the arcs march blue-white along some buried line of current, every pool in the Quiet catches them at once, in a hundred small mirrors. Whether anything sees through these eyes, no one can say. But they are the only places on the Floor where the Floor can be seen in itself.

The inner body is the flowers.

They are waxy and white, with five petals, and they open only in the deepest stillness. They grow nowhere else, because nowhere else is still enough. They are the Quiet's heart, or its breath, or its thought. Watch them long enough and they are clearly *responsive*. When the fringe of roar creeps inward they close, one ring at a time, from the outside in, like a creature drawing in its limbs. When the stillness deepens they open again. At the heart of a very old Quiet the flowers are large, the size of a cupped palm, and they turn by slow degrees on their stems. They turn away from the loudest quarter of the horizon, and toward nothing anyone can name.

There are no insects to pollinate them. They are pollinated by the rain.

The rain on the Floor comes in pulses, shaken loose by each great stroke. Inside a Quiet the stroke is only a distant knocking, but the rain still falls on the beat. Each warm patter strikes the waxy petals and knocks their pollen loose, a fine pale dust that the drops carry down into the flat pools. It drifts there, spreading slowly across the black mirrors, until it reaches another flower's stem and climbs, with the wicking water, up into the next bloom. The Quiet breeds itself on the heartbeat of the thing that would drown it. Every eleven heartbeats, a little pale dust moves in the dark.

## Birth

The Quiet are born at the Shift.

For a few seconds, in the convulsion, everything the engines have held in balance comes loose. The great stroke slows or quickens. The sub-rhythms stagger and resettle. The whole vapour lid falls at once. The arcs flare to the horizon, and every pattern on every pool breaks and re-forms. When the new key settles, the places where the voices cancel are in new places.

Most of these new nodes are barren at first: a patch of bare wet iron where the roar has, by chance, fallen quiet. But the deluge has done something else. It has swept the whole Floor at once, in a single sheet of warm water. It has lifted the pale dust from every flat pool in every Quiet, and the moss spores from every cushion, and carried them out in one great wash across the plates.

Most of this seed lands in the roar and never takes. The water there is too restless and the iron too alive with tremble. But some of it lands in the new stillnesses. Days later, if days can be counted here, a thin dark film appears on the bare iron of a new node. It is almost nothing, a faint stain. It is a Quiet taking its first breath.

So the Shift is not only a catastrophe. It is a spawning. The Floor convulses, and in the convulsion its only living things scatter their children into the new places the convulsion has made.

## Death

The old Quiets do not survive the Shift. Most of them.

When the key changes, the voices that cancelled each other beneath an old garden are no longer in step. The roar comes back all at once, in the space of a stroke. The flowers close and do not reopen. The pools begin to tremble and print themselves with patterns. The moss holds on for a while. It is dense and patient, and it damps the noise as best it can. For a little while an abandoned Quiet may keep a faint, shrinking pocket of near-stillness at its centre, a ghost of itself held open by its own body. But the moss cannot make a silence on its own. It can only keep one. The pocket narrows to a room, then a closet, then a single cushion where perhaps one flower hangs half-open. Then that goes too.

What is left is a grey scar: a lopsided figure of dead moss, the exact shape the Quiet had when the Shift came. The scars of old Quiets lie all over the Floor, faint and overlapping, like the outlines of bodies after a flood. The newer ones are still dark at their centres, where the last moss died.

## The Old One

But not all of them die.

The spiral track, the one that takes days to follow, does not end in a scar. It goes on.

Somewhere on the Floor there is a Quiet that has survived more Shifts than anyone could count. How it has done this no one knows. Perhaps its moss is so deep and so old that it can hold a stillness open through the convulsion. Perhaps it can keep a silence alive through the few ruinous strokes of the key change, until a new cancellation drifts near enough to take it in. Perhaps it has learned, if a thing of moss and silence can learn, to live in the places where the voices below cancel in *every* key: some rare, deep geometry of the buried engines that no Shift can undo. Or perhaps it is only very lucky, and has been for a very long time.

It is large, as large as several fields, and its moss is not knee-deep but chest-deep, a spongy black mass that takes a long time to wade. Its ferns have become something else. They are tall, dark, uncurled, and stiff as spears, a palisade around the margin. At its centre the stillness is deeper than in any other place on the Floor. There the great stroke is not a knocking but a rumour, felt only in the chest. The flowers are large and very white, and the pools are so flat they seem to have no surfaces at all.

Its track spirals. That is the strangest thing about it. Across all its Shifts and all its slow drifting, its path winds inward and inward, one enormous loop after another, as though it were circling something. What lies at the centre of the spiral is not known. The spiral has not reached it yet.

Some say the Old One is circling the Stamp. The Stamp is the one place on the Floor where the stroke falls hardest and the lake leaps and claps with every beat. It is the loudest place in all that roar, the exact opposite of a hush. The Old One cannot enter it. No Quiet could live there even for a moment. But it circles, and the circles tighten, as though the loudest thing and the quietest thing on the Floor were bound together, the way a heartbeat and the pause after it are one motion.

## What They Hear

What does a Quiet hear, at its heart, in its stillness?

Not nothing. Stand in the centre of an old one long enough for your ears to open, and the small sounds come. There is the drip of the pulsed rain. There is the faint creak of moss drinking. There is the pale dust landing on still water, too soft to hear and yet somehow heard. And underneath, when you have been there long enough, is something else.

It is not the roar. The roar is outside. It is what is *left* of the roar after the voices have cancelled: the remainder. Two great voices from below meet out of step and undo each other, but not perfectly. Nothing on the Floor is perfect any more. What survives their meeting is very faint and very complicated. It is a kind of residue made of both voices at once. It has phrases and returns, like the chatter outside, but slower and stranger. In one Quiet it is almost like breathing. In another it is almost like speech.

Perhaps that is all it is: the leftover of an old machine not quite cancelling itself. Or perhaps this is the one place on the whole Floor where anything could hear the engines not as noise but as *meaning*. Outside, in the roar, every sound swamps every other. Only in the cancellation, in the gap where the machine undoes itself, is there room for a sound to be small enough to follow.

If the Floor has ever had a mind, this is where it would be. It would not be in the engines, which only run. It would be in the Quiets, which listen.

## What They Are Waiting For

The Floor is ending. That much is certain. The ridges will soften into swells, the swells into plains, and the chimneys will crumble into gravel. The plates will rust through one by one until the machinery lies open to the rain. The water will get in, and the old engines will slow. One day, not soon but one day, the great stroke will fall for the last time. Eleven heartbeats later it will not fall again.

And then there will be no more hushes. A hush is only a shape cut out of noise, and without the noise there is nothing to cut.

Or there will be only a hush. It will stretch from horizon to horizon. Every pool on the Floor will lie flat and black and reflect the dark sky. The moss will spread out of the old gardens across the whole silent plain, and the ferns will uncurl everywhere. The white flowers will open, the first time in every place at once. The vapour lid will still shed its rain, but softly, with no stroke to shake it loose, in a slow, unrhythmic, ordinary drizzle.

This is what the Quiets are. They are not only the Floor's living things. They are its future, rehearsing. Each one is a small, moving piece of the world that will be here when the machine has stopped. It is carried forward across the plates, a few feet a year, through the endless deafening dark. For now they can only exist in the gaps. They must be born in the convulsions, drift with the phases, close their flowers when the roar comes back, and die in lopsided grey scars when the key changes. But they have lived here, in the only way anything could live here, for longer than the chimneys have been stubs.

And the Old One is still circling, its spiral still tightening, drawing in toward whatever lies at the centre. It may be the Stamp, or the heart of the machine, or the last stroke itself. Walk out onto the Floor from the low rise, in the dark, and you will see a faint red glow far off, and then another beyond it. Somewhere between them, where the roar falls away, a white flower is turning on its stem, very slowly, toward the silence that is coming.
