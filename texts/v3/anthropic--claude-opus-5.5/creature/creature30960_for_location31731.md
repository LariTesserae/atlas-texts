# Who Lives at Low Hesk

## The Ones Too Small to Name

Start with the weep, because that is where most of the life is. Only a little of it can be seen.

Put a drop of wall-water under a tender's hand lens and it is crowded. There are rotifers, and things like commas, and things like broken spirals. They graze the fresh cement for the lime it gives up. They came down on boots and on the undersides of pipe-sections and in the tea-water, and they have spread along the streaks. Every calcite curtain near the stair has them built into it, the way a coral reef is built of coral. This is why the curtains are milk-coloured and not clear. The first stalactites of Low Hesk are not quite stone. They are a sediment of lives no bigger than a full stop, falling out of the water faster than the water can carry them away.

Nobody counts these. But they are the reason the old hall smells faintly of something other than concrete, something like a cellar where apples were once stored. The tenders call it *the settle*. When a tender comes back from a long walk out toward the blue lamps, the first thing they say on reaching the stair is often, *You can smell it's lived-in here.* They don't mean themselves.

## The Moths, Considered Longer

Everyone notices the moths. It takes longer to notice the lamps they sleep in.

The dead-but-not-quite fixtures, the ones whose tubes give off that rose glow that lights nothing, are not failing. They are occupied. Unscrew one, which is hard, because the gantries are forty meters up and the ladders are rusted to the pillars, and the tube is furred inside with a fine white silk. In the silk, packed end to end like seeds in a pod, are the larvae. They are thumbnail-long and soft, and each has a faint pink luminance along its back. They feed on what is left in the tube: the sodium film, the ghost of current still trickling from the gantry cable. The glow is them. The rose light is the colour of a sodium lamp being slowly eaten.

This explains one of the rules. *The lamps die from the center outward.* They don't die. They are colonised, and the colony started at the stair and has been spreading outward along the gantries for fifty years, one fixture at a time. A working lamp is a lamp the moths haven't reached yet. The blue lamps at the edge are the only ones safe from them, because the moths will not lay in anything that isn't sodium. The tenders have tried swapping a dead fixture for a blue one. The moths drift past it, turning in the draft, and settle on the next orange one along.

So the moths are not fed by the breath, or not only by it. They are fed by the light, and they are putting it out. When they emerge they are almost weightless, because they have nothing left to eat. They hang in the pumped air for a few in-breaths. Their mouths are sealed; they have no need of them. They mate in the mist. Then they settle on the pillars, and in a few days they are grey dust in the weep, and the rotifers take them.

The tenders' vague sense is still right, though. If the pumps stopped, the moths would fall. They cannot fly, not really. What looks like flight is the hall's breath holding them up. A moth in still air sinks like a flake of ash.

The moss follows the light. The moths eat the light. Walk the stripes of grey-gold moss back from any dead lamp and you are walking a record of the moths' advance, a slow ecological retreat drawn in hair-thin lines down the shotcrete.

## The Eels, Who Were Here First

Everything at Low Hesk is new except the eels.

That is the tenders' private theory, and they hold it because of the knot. The eels lying still in the branched culvert did not look like animals that had moved into a drainage system. They looked like animals that had been interrupted. There was a kind of injured patience to them. The tender who found them, a woman called Aud Ferrow who kept the booth at pillar 208 for thirty years, said later that they had the look of people sitting in the ruins of their own house, waiting for someone to say whether they could rebuild.

The rock around Low Hesk was full of water before the cutters came: fissure water, cold and ancient, moving through cracks at the speed of centuries. The eels lived in it. They are blind because there was never any reason to see. They are colourless because pigment costs something. They are long because the cracks were long. Then the works broke into their water. They went where it went, which was into the gutters, the grates, and the dendritic pipe-net below the floor. They have adapted to the flow the way a river-stone adapts to a river.

They swim against the current because that is the way back. Upstream, eventually, is the rock the water came out of. The eels are always trying to return to a place that the cistern has cut open, drained, poured over, and numbered. When the water runs low they go down, not up, because the deep culverts are the only places left that remember being fissures. The pipes there are old, cracked, and mineral-choked, and the tenders never go down to them.

An eel lives a long time. Aud Ferrow believed some of the ones in the knot were older than the hall. She said that if you lay on the grate with your ear to the iron during an out-breath, you could sometimes hear a sound in the pipes under the floor. It was not the water. It was slower, and it came from very far down. She thought it was eels moving together in the dark, a great many of them, all at once, as if something below had called them.

## The Setters

Then there are the tracks at the edge. Once you know what made them, you start to see the makers everywhere, which is to say nowhere, because they are almost always gone.

The tenders call them *setters*, for three reasons. They are found only where concrete is setting. They crouch low and point, the way a setter dog does. And a setter is also a trade: the man who sets the stone. Very few tenders have seen a living one, but those few describe roughly the same thing. It is the size of a hen and shaped a little like one, long in the shin and three-toed. It is pale grey, the exact grey of fresh cement, and its skin is soft and faintly wet, with the chalky bloom that green concrete has. It has no feathers and no scales. Its head is small, blunt, eyeless, and very still. It goes about in small groups, stepping carefully across the uncured bays and leaving those prints, stopping every few paces to press its face flat to the surface.

They eat the pour. Specifically they eat what the concrete sweats out as it cures: the lime-milk, the bleed-water, the thin alkaline film that rises to the top of a fresh slab. It is caustic enough to burn a tender's hand. The setters lap it. They are born in it, too. In the newest bays, where the rock has been cut but not yet poured, the tenders have found clutches of eggs in the rubble. The eggs are smooth and grey and cold, and indistinguishable from pebbles until one of them is warmer than it should be.

That much is simple. But it becomes stranger.

A setter's skin cures. It is soft when young, at the raw edge, and it stiffens as the animal ages, the way the slabs do, on roughly the same schedule. A setter a few weeks old is quick and supple. A setter a few months old moves stiffly, and when it presses its face to the concrete you can hear a faint click. The animal is being set, like the floor. And a setter must stay at the edge to eat, because only young concrete bleeds. So they follow the cutting. The whole population migrates outward with the wave front. It is always moving, always on the rawest margin, because that is the only place in the world where their food exists.

Some fall behind. There are always some.

Go back toward the stair along the old pillar numbers, past 900 and past 400, and look carefully at the bases of the columns where the weep has been at work longest. You will find stones: small grey lumps against the pillar foot, calcite-skinned and shaped, if you let yourself see it, like a crouched bird with its head bowed. Some have three ridges running out from the base. The tenders don't touch them. A setter that can no longer keep up with the edge slows, stiffens, and finally stops. It stops always facing outward, toward the cutting, with its face pressed to the floor. Then the weep comes down on it, and the rotifers, and the dust of moths, and in fifty years it is part of the hall.

So the oldest part of the works is studded with the ones who couldn't keep pace. The whole gradient of youth has this stratum in it. The farther from the edge you go, the more setters you find turned to stone. Near the stair there are dozens, all of them crouched toward the far dark, as if they were listening.

## The Tenders, As They Become

The tenders would say they are the people who live here. But they don't quite live here. They are stationed here, and the difference matters to them.

They come down the stair on rotation. Nobody remembers who draws up the rotas. The sheets arrive in the booths in envelopes marked with the name of the water authority no one can find, and the tenders follow them because the work is clear, the booths are warm, and the gauges need reading. A rota runs for a year, or for ten. Some tenders stay for thirty, and then there is no longer any sense in which they are rotating.

The place changes them, slowly, and in ways that are easy to miss from inside.

They come to breathe on the cycle. It isn't deliberate. After a month you find you draw in during the in-breath and let out during the out-breath, long and slow over nineteen minutes, and your body has decided on its own that it is part of the machine. Old tenders speak in bursts timed to the out-breath and fall silent a few seconds before the pumps turn over, without looking at a gauge. They sleep in two-shift rhythms, eleven and nineteen. When they go back up the stair at the end of a rota, and some do go, they find the surface air impossibly still and cannot sleep in it. Many come back down.

Their hands go pale and smooth from the wet. Their hair collects a fine white rime of mineral that doesn't wash out. Aud Ferrow's hair, at the end, was stiff with calcite at the tips, like the frost on a hedge. They count constantly and without meaning to. They count pillars, gauge-needles, steps, drips, the lamps still burning on a gantry. It is the only defence the hall allows against the grid, and they know it.

And the telephone. Each booth has a black handset on the wall and a card of numbers pinned beside it, one number for each station. The tenders call each other with news of dead lamps and pressure falls and eel sightings. The cards have been added to over the years, in many hands, as new stations were built. They are the true history of the tenders: the names of who kept which booth when, crossed out and written over.

Sometimes the telephone rings from a number that isn't on the card. It is always a station number, and always higher than the highest station yet built. If a tender answers, the line is loud with an in-breath, the full roar of the pumps, so that nothing can be made out. But under the roar, very faintly, someone is reading off pressures in a calm, level voice. Some tenders say it is their own voice. Some say it is the voice of whoever kept their booth before them. They never ring back, and the call never comes twice in the same rota. The stenciled pillars with uncut numbers belong to the same order of thing. Somewhere out past the edge, the works are already tended.

## The Cutters

So something must live out there, past the tracks and the blue lamps, in the sound.

Nobody has seen a cutter. But the tenders have found what cutters shed. Out in the fresh bays, in the rubble that smells of hot oil, there are teeth: wedges of hard grey metal as long as a forearm, their tips worn to a rounded bluntness, some still warm to the touch days after they were found. They are too big for any tunnel machine a tender has a name for, and they look replaced rather than broken. Something out there is growing new ones.

There are also the parallel tracks, a meter wide and set so far apart that a tender standing in one cannot see across to the other in the dark. There is the heat. The air near the edge is several degrees warmer than the hall, and the mist boils off the green concrete in slow wreaths. And, once, there was a tender at 3600 who waited in the rubble through three full breaths and saw the blue lamps come on. She did not see them turn on. She saw them hung. There was movement on the gantry forty meters up, too far into the dark to make out. It was slow and precise and many-jointed, and it laid a new lattice girder out from the last pillar and fitted a fixture, and then another, and then the light was there. She said what she felt was not fear. It was the feeling of being in a room while the builders were still working in it, when they had not been told anyone would be home yet.

So perhaps the cutters are machines. Perhaps they are animals that live the way the setters do, at the raw margin, eating rock. Perhaps something lives inside them and drives them, or perhaps the whole cutting front is one creature, wide as the hall, feeding outward. The tenders don't agree, and it doesn't matter to them, because the one thing everyone agrees on is that the cutters are not the ones the hall is *for*.

## What It Is For

Walk back to the stair through it all: the setters crouched in their stone at the pillar feet, the moss in its stripes, the moths drifting in the in-breath, the eels under the grates, the tenders whistling in their booths. It is a whole ecology, and every part of it is provisional. Everything that lives at Low Hesk lives in a gap: between the cutting and the cure, between one breath and the next, between the lamp going in and the moth arriving. The moths are eating the light out of the hall. The setters are turning to stone in it. The eels are trying to leave. The tenders came on a rota and are waiting to be told they can stop. This is scaffolding life. It is the shuttering boards, still bolted to the pillars, gone black with damp. It lives in the building the way workmen live in a house before the owner moves in.

Because it is a cistern. That is the one word on the brass plates everyone can still read. A cistern is a vessel, and a vessel waits to be filled.

The pumps are not only moving water out. That is what the tenders slowly understand if they stay long enough, reading the needles and learning what the pressures mean over years rather than days. On the in-breath the pumps draw water away. On the out-breath something lets it back, a little more each cycle, into the pipe-net below the floor, into the lowest culverts where the eels have gone to lie still. The works are not being kept dry. They are being kept *not yet full*. The pumps are holding the water down so the cutters can finish, and the water is rising under the floor slowly, patiently, directionally, like a pulse felt through a wrist.

So what lives at Low Hesk, finally, is the thing it was made to hold. It has not arrived. It is below, in the dark water under the grates, and it is ahead, in the grid of pillars running off every edge of the blueprints. The grid is not a map of a hall. It is the plan of a basin. Everything that lives here now lives on its floor, in the brief dry season before it comes in, the way things live on the bed of a lake the summer the lake is low.

The eels know. That is why they have gone down to wait. The setters know, perhaps, and that is why the stone ones all face outward. They were listening for it. The moths will fall when the pumps stop. The moss will drown in the dark. The tenders will climb the stair, carrying the telephone cards, if they remember in time.

And the hall will stop breathing. The mist will settle. The grey-white floor will go under, slab by field-sized slab, and the stenciled numbers will shine yellow for a moment beneath the surface before the silt takes them. The last of the working lamps will throw a cone of gold down into still water, and in it, where there had been only drifting droplets, something vast and slow and patient will turn over, once, in the whole of its new house, and settle in to live there.
