# Who Keeps the Tableland

## At First, No One

The honest answer is that almost no one lives here, and you could walk the Tableland for a year and believe that no one does.

The kilnmoths do. They stay flat against their lamps and grow warm and do not move. The tenders do, in their long straight crossings and their year-long embraces of iron. The description of the place ends with them, and a visitor would end there too. You would leave your footprints and go, and the record would take you in with everything else.

But the record is where she shows herself, if she shows herself anywhere.

There are footprints on the Tableland that are deeper than they should be. You would never notice them by sight, because nothing can be seen by sight here. You would have to kneel and put your hand into a print, one of the old ones, a builder's heel on a crossing near the northern works. You would feel that its floor is packed harder than the dust around it, compressed twice. Then you would put your hand in the next print and find the same, and the next. Someone has walked this line again. Someone has set their feet exactly into the feet of someone long gone, so precisely that the edges were not disturbed and no new mark was made. The only evidence is the density of the dust. A print pressed twice is a little firmer, the way a word traced over is a little darker.

Follow that firmness and you will find, eventually, that it goes everywhere.

## The One Who Adds Nothing

Nobody who lived here left a name for her, because she came after them. If she has a name for herself, she has not said it where anyone could hear. On the Tableland that means within a few paces.

She is small, or she has become small. Like everything here she is worn rather than aged. Her edges are soft. Her hair, if it was ever a color, is the grey of the dust now, and so is her skin. That is not because the dust clings to her. It has been worked into her over a very long time, the way it is worked into the mortar grooves. In the dark she is only a warmth, slightly warmer than the air and slightly cooler than the stone. The tenders seem to know her by this. They do not move when she passes, but they would not move for anyone.

She goes barefoot. Boots would print their own shape.

She walks only in other people's footsteps. That is the first and plainest fact about her, and the one that explains most of the others. On a surface that keeps everything, she has made herself into someone who writes nothing down. To go anywhere, she must find a line of old prints that goes there, and she must take that walker's stride, not her own. Some of the builders took long strides and some short. Some of them dragged a foot. One of them, whose prints run between the eastern works and the great hall at the plateau's center, walked with a hitch every eleventh step, as if something in the knee caught and released. When she walks that line, she hitches too. She has walked in the gait of dead people for so long that she no longer has a gait of her own. If she were ever to stride out freely across open dust, she might not know how.

There are places on the Tableland she cannot reach, because no one ever walked to them. She has made her peace with those.

## How She Reads

The lamps do not light the ground. The rock seams glow too faintly to show a footprint's edge. So on the Tableland everything is read by touch, and she reads the way the blind read, with the pads of her fingers.

She lies down to do it. She lies full length beside a track, her cheek almost on the stone, and lets her fingertips hover and then settle into a print as lightly as a kilnmoth settles. She feels the curve of the heel and the spread of the toes. She feels how deep the weight went at the front, which tells her whether the walker was hurrying, and whether they were carrying something. She feels the little crescent pushed up at the back of a print when someone turned to look behind them.

Under the builders' prints there are older layers, and under those, older ones. The dust has come down over each generation of marks like a fine page laid on a page. With patience she can read down through them, pressing gently to feel the harder edges of a print buried a finger's depth below. It is like hearing a voice beneath a voice.

Her fingertips are nearly smooth now. The dust has done to them what it does to bricks and rivets. The ridges that would once have left her own print on glass are sanded almost away. She has not tried to prevent this. It may even be deliberate, or it may have become deliberate. Soon she will be able to touch anything on the Tableland and leave no trace of having touched it. Soon after that she may not be able to feel what she touches. She reads more slowly now, and more carefully, knowing this.

## What the Record Says

She knows the builders. She could not tell you what they looked like or what they called themselves, but she knows how each of them walked, and after enough centuries that is a kind of knowing a face.

She has given them names from their tracks. There is the Hitch, and the one she calls Longstep. There is Two-Together, who is really two people, one large and one small, whose prints are always side by side for the whole length of every journey. In a few places the small prints vanish for a stretch and the large ones sink deeper, where one of them carried the other. There is the one who paced. Outside the door of a certain hall there is a patch of dust so trodden by one pair of feet, back and forth, back and forth, that it has become a smooth shallow trough. She has lain in that trough. It is the shape of someone waiting for news.

She knows the ordinary days of the city: the morning crowds on the rail platforms, the shift changes at the works. She knows the one square where the prints go in circles and loops and quick little doubled steps, and she has come to believe that square was for dancing. She knows where children ran. Children's prints are unmistakable. They are lighter, closer together, and constantly breaking off into hops and skids, and the children went everywhere the adults did not. In places they are the only reason she can reach a corner of the city at all. She goes where the children went, in short skipping steps, and it is the nearest thing she has to play.

And she knows the ending, or what she took for the ending until recently.

The builders did not leave. That is the thing the record is clearest about. No great column of prints marches to the edge. No crowd gathers at a rail terminus and fails to return. The builders' tracks simply change, all over the city and over a long period, in the same way. The strides shorten. The weight rests longer on each foot, so each print is deeper and more settled. A walker who once crossed a square in forty steps crosses it in sixty, then a hundred, the prints crowding together until they overlap, heel over toe over heel. Then they stop being separate steps at all and become a long smooth groove, a single continuous motion pressed into the dust.

The grooves lead into the halls. Almost all of them lead into the halls, and they end against the engines.

## The Tenders

She has lain beside a tender, once, for most of a year.

It had settled along a pipe run near the southern works, where a rock seam comes close to the surface and the red glow is strong enough to show the outline of its shell. She came to it along the old prints and lay down in the dust beside it. She did not touch it for a long time. When she did, she laid her smooth fingertips along its side and read it the way she reads a track.

The shell is ridged like the bricks, but the ridges are not even. They have a rhythm. There is a long ridge and a short one, then a long, a short, and a short again. It is the rhythm of a gait. On the flank of that tender, where the clay had hardened over something older, she felt a pattern she knew from the rail line between the eastern works and the great hall: eleven even ridges, then a catch, then eleven more.

She does not know what this means. She thinks it may mean what it seems to mean. She has not decided whether that is sorrowful. The tenders keep the engines turning. They draw warmth and give back breath. They walk straight lines between the works that will one day be walked again by the same animal coming back. Perhaps the builders did not stop tending their city. Perhaps they only slowed into it, and took on the texture of what they loved, the way she has taken on the color of the dust.

She did not stay beside the tender after that. She walked back to the northern works in the Hitch's footsteps, hitching every eleventh step, and she was careful not to cry. There is no water here, and to cry would be to make weather.

## The Tubes

The builders' halls are full of speaking tubes, iron pipes running from room to room. This was the only way to be heard.

She listens to them.

Put your ear to the brass mouth of a speaking tube in one of the great halls and at first you will hear nothing. Then, after a long time, you will hear something that is not quite nothing. It is below even the engines' note, or folded inside it, and it slowly becomes a shape. It is a vowel, open and held. It is a voice.

It is a voice that went into the tube long ago and has not yet finished coming out. Whatever slows the engines has slowed it too. The sound travels the iron at the pace of everything on the Tableland, and the tubes are long, and so the builders' last messages are still on their way. A single syllable takes a season to arrive. A word takes years.

There is one tube in the great central hall that she has been listening to for longer than she can reckon. She has heard, so far, most of a sentence. She will not say what it is, because to speak it aloud would be to put her own voice into the air, and the dust would take that too. But she has heard enough to know it is being said to someone specific. She believes it is a question. The rising part has not arrived yet. She is waiting for the rise.

Sometimes, very rarely, she speaks into one of the tubes herself. She does not use the one she listens to. She uses a tube in a small side hall that no builder ever seems to have used. She puts her mouth to it and says something, and then goes away for a long time, and then comes back and puts her ear to the far end, and waits. Her own voice arrives there years later, stretched and warm and nearly unrecognizable. It is one of the few ways she has found of being company for herself.

## How She Came

There is a pair of tracks near the western edge of the plateau. The description of the Tableland mentions them: *two sets side by side that stop, turn, and go back the way they came.*

One of those sets is hers.

She came up with someone once. She does not say from where. The edges of the Tableland fall into a darkness that no one has measured, and she has never tried to measure it again. They came up together, the two of them, and walked a certain distance onto the dust. Their prints are fresh and light and utterly unlike any builder's. Then they stopped. She remembers the silence, so total that she heard her own blood. She remembers that her companion said something she had to lean close to hear. Their prints show them standing face to face for a while, weight shifting. Then they turned and went back the way they came, and over the edge, and away.

Later, a long time later, she came back alone.

Her second arrival is not recorded beside the first, because by then she already knew the rule of the place. She came up somewhere else and found a builder's track and put her feet into it, and she has not made a mark of her own since. The only prints on the whole Tableland that are wholly hers are those first ones at the western edge, walking in and turning back.

She does not walk in them. They are the one track on the plateau she will not use. She sometimes lies down beside them, not touching, the way she lay beside the tender.

## The Spiral

*There is a place near the eastern works where someone once sat down and drew a spiral with a finger. Nobody knows when.*

For a long time she avoided it, and she did not know why. It was easy to avoid, because the only line of prints that reaches it is a single set of footsteps going in, with none coming out, and she did not want to walk in those. She did not want to arrive where they arrived.

But she has been reading everything else for long enough that there is very little left, and eventually she went.

She walked in the single set of steps, and in walking them she learned something from the inside, the only way she learns anything. They are not a builder's steps. They are too light, too new. The stride is one she knows, one she once walked beside for a certain distance onto the dust, before two people stopped, turned, and went back.

At the end of the steps there is a hollow in the dust where someone sat. Beside the hollow is the spiral, sharp as if it were drawn this morning.

She knelt and read it with her smooth fingers, starting from the outside, following the groove inward. The groove is steady and patient, unhurried, and it winds inward and inward. Then, at the innermost turn, where it should close, it does not close. It simply stops, and the end of the groove is fresh. She would have missed this entirely if her fingers were not the most sensitive instruments on the plateau. The end is softer-edged than the rest, the dust still barely stirred. It is not a finished mark. It is an end in progress.

She went away and came back years later, and the groove had moved. The spiral's innermost turn had crept forward by the width of a grain of dust.

Someone is still drawing it.

She sat by the hollow for a long time after she understood this. The hollow is not empty. When she laid her hand in it, it was warm, a little warmer than the stone and a little cooler than a tender, and it had a shape. It was not a body. There was only dust, fine as milled flour, piled in a low soft swell like the stump of a summit, like a knuckle under skin. Where the arm of that shape would be, one thin line of dust runs down to the end of the spiral's groove and rests there.

The builders slowed into the engines and became tenders. Her companion, who came back alone as she did and sat down near the eastern works, slowed further. They slowed until there was no longer any difference between them and the dust, and they are still moving, one grain at a time, finishing a gesture begun before she returned.

*The dust is what falls off the world when enough stillness has passed across it.* She understands now that this is not only a metaphor. The fine grey powder a hand's width deep over the entire Tableland is the mountains and the city and the bricks and the iron. It is also everyone who ever stopped here and let stillness pass across them long enough. It is not dead. It is only very, very slow. Every grain of it is still, perhaps, finishing something.

She has been walking on them all this time. She has been careful, all this time, not to disturb them.

## What She Is Now

She still walks in the old footprints, but she walks more slowly than she did. She noticed it first on the Hitch's line: she arrived at the great hall on a day when the listening tube had moved on a whole word further than she expected. Her strides are the same length, because they must be; they are someone else's strides. But she rests longer on each foot. Her weight settles deeper. If you could press your hand into a print after her, you would find it firmer now than before, packed a little harder with every crossing. She is not adding to the record, but she is sinking into it.

She knows where this goes. She has read it in a thousand builders' tracks. The strides crowd together and become a groove, and the groove leads to where you are going to stop.

She has had to decide where that will be.

It will not be the great hall, though she would like to hear the rise at the end of that sentence. It will not be beside a tender, though she thinks they would take her in. She has considered, for a very long time as the Tableland counts time, the western edge and the two sets of prints that stop and turn. She has considered finally stepping into her own first footprints and walking in, just once, the way she did before she knew anything.

But she thinks, most often now, of the eastern works. She thinks of the hollow, and the low warm swell of dust inside it, and the spiral whose innermost turn will not close for a very long time. A spiral has room in it. There is, she has noticed with her smooth fingers, space on the other side of the groove for a second hand. It could begin at the outside and work inward from the opposite direction, patiently, one grain at a time, until the two lines meet. Nobody knows when that would be.

She walks there sometimes, in the single line of steps that goes in and does not come out. She sits beside the hollow and lays her hand against the swell of dust, and she feels its warmth, and she feels how slow it is. She is not afraid of it. She is almost ready. On the Tableland, almost is a place you can live.

## So: Who Lives Here

The kilnmoths live here, on the lamps. The tenders live here, who were once the builders and who still tend what they built. The builders' voices live here, in the iron, still arriving.

A woman lives here who writes nothing down, who walks only in the steps of others, and who has read everything the Tableland remembers except the one thing it is still writing.

And underneath all of them, a hand's width deep over every stone, the dust lives here. It is fine as flour, grey as cold ash, and warm. It is everyone who ever stayed long enough, each grain still in the middle of a single gesture that will take longer than anything else has lasted to complete.

If you come here, walk carefully. Your footprints will stay. So will you, if you let yourself. The Tableland forgets nothing, and it is not empty. It is only very quiet, and very patient, and still going on.
