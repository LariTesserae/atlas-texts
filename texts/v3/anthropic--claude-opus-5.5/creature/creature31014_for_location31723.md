# Who Lives in the Hotbed

## Everyone, and almost no one

The first answer is *everything*, and it is true and useless. The bats pour out of the skylights in rivers. The frogs sing in their thimbles. The crabs click past by the thousand, the swarms rise and crash, the cicadas saw at the inside of the skull. Life in the basin is less a population than a weather.

Most of it doesn't live here in any sense that matters, though. It happens here. The crabs are only passing through on their way to the sea. The cicadas surface, scream for a season, and are gone. The bats shift from tube to tube as the roofs fall, and no bat alive knows the colony's old roost, because the old roost is now a pit full of shattered figs. The trees themselves are transients. A fig that sprouted on a tree fern's head can strangle its host, tower for a decade, rot hollow, and fall, and within a year the moss has taken it back into the ground.

To *live* somewhere you have to last longer than the place changes. Almost nothing here does.

I thought at first the answer would be down in the tubes. The low points hold warm black pools, and those pools have their own citizens: blind shrimp as pale as fingernail parings, a long, smooth, eyeless salamander that hangs in the water like a strand of boiled noodle and feels the vibration of every drop that falls. They are strange and lovely and patient. But the tubes collapse, and the pools drain into new fractures. The salamanders have no more history than the bats do.

What lives in the Hotbed, in the full sense, is an old female macaque with a split thumb.

## The eldest thing

She has no name that anyone could say. Her troop knows her by her smell, by the notch in her left ear, and by the particular pressure of her hand. That last matters most, and it will take some explaining.

She is about twenty-six years old. In a place where the rock itself is barely fifty, this makes her ancient. She is older than every tree she sleeps in. She is older than the tube her troop's bats now roost in, and older than the vent that opened in the eastern forest, the one that cooked a whole grove white in a single night and still breathes sulphur. She remembers when that ground was figs. Possibly nothing else alive in the basin remembers it.

She is small, as most of them are. The damp keeps their fur dark and close, so they look slick, almost otter-like, and in the brown noon-dusk they are hard to see at all. Her eyes are large and amber, the eyes of an animal that lives mostly in the dark, and they have gone faintly milky at the edges. Her face is bare and grey-pink, creased like the crust of a pressure ridge. Her tail is short and has been broken once.

Her hands are the record.

Every knapper's hands are scarred. The young ones cut themselves constantly, and they learn with blood the way young things everywhere learn. They strike a nodule of glass against an anvil stone at the wrong angle, and the flake that springs off takes a strip of skin with it. They shriek, lick the wound, and try again. By adulthood the palms are cross-hatched with fine white lines, and the fur on the forearms is worn away in patches where the glass has shaved it.

Her hands go past that. They are more scar than skin, a white lattice laid over the grey. She lacks the last joint of two fingers on her right hand. The thumb of her left splits into two ridges where a cut healed badly long ago, so her grip is strange and wide, a kind of pincer that no other monkey in the troop has. With it she can hold a nodule of obsidian so that it does not turn under the blow. Her flakes are better than anyone's. They come off long, thin, and curved, and they open a fist-sized snail in one motion, along the whorl, without shattering the shell.

The young ones watch her do it. None of them has her thumb. They will never do it the same way. This is how things go here: every skill is partly an injury, and no two injuries match.

## The deaf elders

She is nearly deaf.

They all go deaf eventually, the ones who live long. The chorus sees to it. A macaque born in the basin spends every night of its life inside a roar that visiting humans describe as unthinkable: bat-wings like a waterfall, frogs like struck metal, cicadas like a saw inside the bone, all of it thrown back and doubled by the glassy rock and the haze-lid. The young hear everything and scream constantly to be heard over it. Their calls are high, ragged, desperate, and endless. They are the noisiest monkeys in the region, and by middle age they are paying for it.

So the troop has two languages. One belongs to the young: calls, shrieks, the territorial screaming that bounces from crown to crown. The other belongs to the old, and it is a language of hands.

When she wants a juvenile to follow her, she does not call. She presses her palm flat between its shoulder blades, once, firmly, and lets go. When she wants the troop to stop, she grips the nearest adult by the wrist and holds on until it goes still, and the stillness passes outward through the troop, body to body, faster than any sound could carry through the din. Grooming, which in other macaques is comfort and politics, has become speech here. She works her fingers through a daughter's fur and taps a slow rhythm against the skin as she goes, and the rhythm carries meaning. Which rhythms mean what, no outsider has worked out.

The consequence is odd. Among the knappers, authority is quiet. The screaming males who charge through the canopy and bang the branches are loud and young and, in the end, not much listened to. The ones the troop follows at the edge of a new skylight, or when the ground begins to hiss, are the old silent ones who move carefully and touch everyone as they pass. Being deafened has made them into something like elders.

The troop's history, what there is of it, lives in her. She knows which snail grounds are richest after the dry-breath months when the vents slacken. She knows where the good glass is. The best obsidian comes from the rinds of the youngest, fastest-cooled flows, and those are right against the fissure, where the ground is hot enough to blister a foot. She knows the way there and the way back, and the long way around the new vent. She has lived long enough to watch the forest fall and rise twice in some places, and she keeps, somewhere behind the milky eyes, a map of a landscape that no longer exists, laid over the one that does.

## The ringing stones

At the heart of the troop's range there is a warm rock.

Every troop has one or two. This one is a slab of old flow crust, tilted, glassy on top, with the gas-bubble texture weathered almost smooth by generations of monkey feet and hands. It is a little warmer than the ground around it; there must be heat close underneath. At dusk the old ones sit on it and press their bellies and palms to it. The ground around it glitters with debitage, thousands of flakes, some sharp and some rounded, a scatter going back to the first figs. Cracked snail shells lie in drifts, pale in the dark. An anvil stone sits at one end, its top worn into a shallow polished cup.

At the other end, set in the rock, is the bell.

It is a ridge of glassy crust about the length of her forearm, thin and freestanding, like a fin. Strike it with a hammerstone and it gives a clear, high, cold tone. The pitch is high enough, and the note pure enough, to slip through the chorus like a needle through cloth. Even the half-deaf can hear it, or feel it. When she strikes the bell, heads turn all over the troop's range.

The young ones strike it too, clumsily and too often, mostly for the pleasure of it, or so it seems. Humans who have come across the troops say the monkeys play the stones. This is partly true.

What she does is different. She strikes it, then lays both palms flat on the warm slab and goes still.

The note does not only go up into the air. It goes down. The whole basin is glass over hollows: tubes braided under everything, some as wide as a hall and some no wider than a monkey, some whole and some cracked and ready to fall. A ringing stone struck on that ground sends its tone into the rock, and the rock carries it. Where the ground is solid, the note dies quickly. Where there is a hollow beneath, it comes back. It is changed when it returns: lower, longer, with a faint shiver in it, a second ringing under the first.

Nobody taught her this, and nobody can say whether she knows what she is doing. But she has lived twenty-six years on ground that swallows forests, and she is still alive, and so is most of her troop. Other troops lose members to collapses every year. Hers mostly doesn't.

She does it at other stones too. As the troop moves through its range, she stops at certain ridges and fins. They are scattered bells, some struck so often that their edges have gone dull and white with use. She taps one with whatever stone is in her hand, then lays her split-thumbed palm on the rock beside it and waits, her head tilted and her eyes half-closed, while the young ones scream in the branches overhead.

Sometimes she gets up and keeps walking. Sometimes she gets up, turns, grips the wrist of the nearest adult, and holds on.

And the troop goes around.

## What she hears

She cannot hear the frogs any more. She hears the ground.

Her world has narrowed and deepened at the same time. The roar of the air, the thing visitors cannot stop talking about, has mostly gone for her, worn down to a pressure, a thick hum she lives inside. But through her palms and the soles of her feet, the rock is loud.

She feels the fig roots working. A root prying open a crack gives a slow, grinding creak, and then, as the basalt gives, a sharp report she feels in her wrists before the grit comes pattering down. She feels the bats moving in the tubes, an enormous soft restlessness under the ground just before dusk, like a held breath. She feels the crabs in their migration, a hundred thousand tiny sharp feet ticking on glass, a patter that crosses the basin over nights like the passage of a slow rain.

And she feels the heat. The whole basin rises and falls with it, very slowly. The source said the ground was like the flank of a sleeping animal, and to her that is simply what it is. Some nights the rock under the warm slab is hotter, and it stays hot for days, and the smell of struck flint thickens in the air. On those nights she will not let the troop sleep in the low figs near the fissure. She pushes them uphill with her flat hard palm, one body at a time, until they are in the crowns of the old strangler on the ridge. In the morning the low figs are standing white and dead in a haze of sulphur, and the young ones stare at them and do not understand.

She does not understand either, perhaps. She simply knows what that particular heat means. She knew it once as a juvenile, when she watched her own mother push the troop uphill, and she remembers the white trees afterward. The knowledge has no words, no reasons, no sequence. It is a feeling in the palm, and it passes from palm to palm, slowly, between generations, the way the scars do.

## The glow

There is one more thing.

In the last few seasons, a juvenile in her troop, one of her granddaughters, has taken to carrying glowing wood.

The rotting figs are full of luminous fungus, a cold green light that picks out the fallen trunks on dark nights. Every monkey in the basin uses the glow to find its way; the paths across the forest floor are threaded between smears of it. What no one has done before is pick it up.

The granddaughter breaks off a splinter of glowing heartwood, the size of a finger, and holds it in her mouth when she climbs. She sets it down beside her on the warm rock when she knaps, so she can see the angle of the blow. The light is faint, barely enough to show the edge of a flake. But she cuts herself less than the others do.

The old female watches her do it. She does not stop her. Once, and only once, she took the splinter from the girl's hand, turned it over, sniffed it, and then pressed it back into the small palm, with her own split-thumbed hand closed around it for a moment, hard.

The source called this a landscape at the edge of culture, where the first tool is made again and again and never elaborated. That is true, mostly. But a place built entirely of beginnings will eventually produce a second one. The granddaughter may carry the light for the rest of her life, or she may forget it by next season. The troop may take it up. Or the ground may open under all of them next year, and the splinter may lie glowing at the bottom of a new skylight until the bats come.

Nothing here is guaranteed to continue. That has always been the way of this place.

## The one who stays

No one stays, says the description. The basin does not allow it.

She has stayed. She has stayed for twenty-six years. It is not by being stronger than the basin, because nothing is, and not by building anything, because nothing built would last. She has stayed by listening. She has made herself into a kind of instrument, tuned to the ground's breathing, and she has survived its restlessness by paying closer attention to it than anything else that lives here does.

Most things in the Hotbed come here to boil over and burn out. She has done something slower. She has grown old.

The humans who come down with torches for the obsidian sometimes see her, though they do not know what they are seeing. They come across a warm slab glittering with flakes, and a small, dark, scarred monkey sitting on it, silent in the roar, both palms laid flat on the rock and her eyes half-closed, as if she were asleep. They take her for a sick animal or a dying one. They step around her and go on to the glass.

Behind them she strikes the stone, and a clear bell note rises through the chorus. Then she lays her hands flat again and listens as the rock carries the sound away beneath her, into the tubes, out through the hollows, down toward the dark and the distant sea.

It comes back to her changed, and it tells her where the ground is thin.

Two animals, then, are touching in the dark: one enormous, hot, and newly born, turning in its sleep, and one very small and very old, keeping her hand on its flank to feel which way it will move.
