# Who Lives in Cantle

## I. The ones who announce themselves

Start with the frogs, because they insist on it.

The bulls in the gutters of the orphan street are the loudest thing in Cantle. Observers call them the aldermen, half as a joke. Each one claims a length of gutter, perhaps four paces, and sits at its downstream end with its throat working. They are the color of old bronze gone green, heavy and wet, and their eyes sit so high on their heads that they can watch the whole sky while their bodies stay submerged. Their boom is not quite a call. It is closer to a measurement. A bull booms, listens, shifts a little, and booms again, and if you sit near one long enough you realize it is sounding the street. The street reroutes during surges, sometimes curving where it ran straight, sometimes breaking into two runs with a pool between. The aldermen find its edges by echo. A gutter is the best territory in the basin, cool, channeled, rich in drowned moths, and a bull who loses the street in a surge will boom through the fog for hours until he finds a stretch of cobbles again.

What the aldermen really do for Cantle, though, is fall silent.

A surge begins as a change in the clock. The hum under the mat tightens and quickens, too low for most people to notice until their teeth ache. The frogs notice. A minute or two before the Sward begins to roll, every frog in the basin stops at once, and the silence is so sudden and so total that you can hear the pores clicking, the conduits hissing, your own blood. Every observer learns this within a day or two. When the frogs stop, you get off the lawns, away from the pools, and onto the highest firm lignin you can reach, and you hold on. When the frogs start again, one by one and then in a wave, the surge is ending.

The smaller frogs live differently. The emerald ones on the windows have a problem no other frog on earth has: their ponds are temporary on a schedule. They lay eggs in the finger-deep water of the window-ledge gutters, and those gutters exist only as long as the building does, a few days at most, sometimes less. The frogs that bred slowly are long gone, their eggs dissolved into the hot bubbling mat around collapsing footings. The ones that remain are a single lineage, or several, pressed into shape by the churn. Their eggs hatch within hours. Their tadpoles are tiny, fierce, and nearly transparent, and they eat constantly. They grow legs in two days. By the time a hall's roofline begins to darken and soften, the tadpoles in its window-gutters have become froglets the size of rice grains, and they climb out and scatter across the wet lignin like thrown seed, looking for the next rising building.

Their generations are measured in houses. An old emerald frog, if there is such a thing, has lived through perhaps two hundred buildings. Observers have noticed that they gather at the edges of blisters, the barn-sized humps where new buildings are about to split open, and wait there. When the ribs push up and the membranes stretch and harden, the frogs are already climbing. They are usually at the windows before the windows are clear.

## II. The ones who never touch the ground

The swifts have solved the churn by refusing it.

No swift has ever been seen standing on the Sward. They sleep on the wing or cling inside the belfries, and they build their nests the way swifts have always built them, from their own saliva, in the scalloped cup-niches the newest towers now provide. But a tower lives perhaps a day and a half. A swift's egg needs longer than that. So the swifts of Cantle have learned to choose, and they choose by sound.

A rising tower is loud. The ribs lock with cracks like gunshots, and as the conduits fill, the whole shaft rings faintly, like a struck glass. Observers who have recorded these sounds say the pitch of the ringing varies. Some towers ring high and some ring low, and the low-ringing towers stand longer. The swifts know this. When a tower begins to rise they circle it, dozens of them, screaming, dipping in to listen at the half-formed openings. If the pitch is wrong they scatter. If it is right they commit, and the first nests are already being glued into the niches before the belfry has finished uncurling.

Even so, many broods do not make it. The swifts have an answer for that, too. When a tower begins to die, the lignin darkening at the top and the belfry sagging, the parents do something no other swift does. They pull the nest free. A saliva nest is a hard, light cup, and a pair of swifts working together can lift one, eggs and all, and carry it clumsily across the open lawn to another tower. They drop some. The herons have learned to stand beneath the dying towers and wait.

Their fledglings never learn to land. They learn to rise. A swift chick's first flight is timed to the cresting of a new tower. It clings to the tip of the belfry as the shaft pushes skyward, and at the top of the growth, when the tower gives its last shudder and locks, the chick is flung off into warm air a hundred feet up. Observers have watched the adults carry chicks to a rising tower and set them at the peak on purpose. It is the only way a swift in Cantle can learn to fly: the town throws it.

## III. The ones who remember

Along the inner curve of the root-levee, where the Sward thins and the warm bark of the ring rises like the lip of a bowl, the crabs have built their shanty-town.

At first it looks like wreckage: heaps of door-frames, stair-treads, cornices, and window-sashes, stacked and leaned and wedged against the root in low tangled structures, with crabs scuttling in and out of the gaps. The crabs are broad and pale, the color of old bone, with one claw grown enormous and one delicate for eating. They are not tidy. They build by dragging whatever they can pry loose from a softening building across the lawn, often a hundred paces or more, and jamming it into the heap at the levee. Then they live in the gaps.

But the levee is the one edge of Cantle the Sward does not reach. The mat stops a pace or two short of the root, as if even the Sward respects its own walls. Whatever the crabs carry there is never taken back. It dries and hardens, going from wet cedar to a pale silvery grey, and it stays.

It stays for a very long time.

This is the discovery that has kept a handful of scholars coming back to Cantle for their whole lives. The crab-town is an archive. It is the only place in the basin where time piles up instead of going round. Dig into the heap, carefully, because the crabs defend it and their big claw can break a finger, and you are digging backward through the town's memory. Near the surface are rounded doorways, blank lintels, and plain swellings where capitals should be, just like the buildings standing on the lawn today. A little deeper, the doorframes are squarer. Deeper still, the corners are sharp. Below that, the scholars have found a column-head with carved leaves, eight of them curling outward, each leaf veined. They have found stair-treads with a groove along the front edge, the kind of groove that keeps a foot from slipping. They have found a window-sash with a latch, and the latch is sized for a hand with a thumb.

And they have found lettering. Only fragments, because the crabs drag pieces, not whole lintels, but some of the fragments are sharp and deep, cut into the lignin as crisply as if the Sward had used a chisel. Nobody can read them. The script matches nothing known. But it is clearly script: repeated shapes, spacing, what might be a word break. One fragment, a scholar's favorite, holds six characters and then a broken edge. On the lawn, over the doors of the halls, the same place on the lintel carries a single rounded glyph. It is possible, though nobody can prove it, that the glyph is those six characters, spoken a million times until they wore down into a single sound.

The crabs do not care. They like the older pieces for their crevices, because sharp detail makes good hiding places. The deepest layers of the heap are the most crowded, full of tiny pale juveniles tucked into the curls of carved leaves. Without meaning anything by it, and without knowing it, the crabs have become the town's memory. They are the librarians of a library they live in for the corners.

## IV. The ones who clean

Everything the crabs do not steal, the eels finish.

A dissolving house is a feast. The lignin goes soft and spongy, the Sward around the footing heats and bubbles as it draws the material back down, and the water steams and smells of fermenting apple. Into that steam go the eels, long bronze bodies moving through water too hot for anything else, feeding on the sloughing material before the mat can take it. They are big, some longer than a person is tall, and they move slowly and deliberately, with a heavy, patient way of turning their heads.

They are very old. An observer once marked a large eel with a notch of pale dye along the dorsal fin and saw it again, unmistakably, eleven years later, a little larger. No building in Cantle lasts longer than a few days. The eels have outlived tens of thousands of them. They know the pools between the buildings the way a person knows the rooms of a childhood house. The pools move, but the eels seem to know where they will move to.

In the newest halls, in the lowest rooms where the floors have become shallow pools with smooth ramps leading in, the eels now rest. They come in from the lawn-pools by channels the Sward has begun to leave open, lie in the dim warm water, and sleep. Of all the town's inhabitants, the eels may be the first in an unknown span of ages to use a room for exactly the purpose it was grown for. The Sward seems to know this. The eel-rooms are becoming more common with each season, and more carefully made: their ramps are gentler, their water deeper, and small overhangs of lignin cast a shade that the eels plainly prefer.

## V. The ones who keep still

There is a rule in Cantle: keep moving. The Sward builds around stillness.

Some things have learned to break the rule on purpose.

At dusk, when the air cools slightly and the mist begins to lift off the pools, small pale salamanders come up out of the Sward and climb into the doorways of the standing buildings. They are the color of the conduits, milky and faintly veined, and so soft-skinned they look half-dissolved. The scholars call them lintel-newts. Each one finds a doorway and settles into the angle where the frame meets the wall, tucks its tail round, and stops.

In the night, the Sward grows over them.

It happens slowly, the way it happens to a sleeping person. A lip of lignin rises around the newt and folds over it, sealing it into a pocket in the wall. By morning there is only a small smooth bulge in the doorframe, like a knot in wood, and inside it the newt is living. The pocket is warm and damp. One of the pale conduits runs beside it, and at intervals the conduit weeps a bead of clear sweet fluid through the wall of the pocket, and the newt drinks. The newt lives there, fed and sheltered and entirely enclosed, for the whole remaining life of the building. When the building's cycle ends and the lignin darkens and softens, the pocket opens, and the newt climbs out into the steam, somewhat fatter, and goes looking for another doorway.

They are not the only ones. Snails do it too, and a kind of flat grey beetle, and seeds. The Sward is full of lodgers sealed into its walls, a whole hidden population living inside the architecture. The building treats them the way it was built to treat anything that stays: as residents to be housed and fed.

A few people have been enclosed, mostly by accident, and have come out to report on it. They speak of it with a strange reverence. Inside the wall, they say, the clock is very loud: not a hum but a pulse, slow and steady, close to a heartbeat. It is warm. It is not dark, because the lignin glows faintly amber with the light outside. And at some point, always, a bead of sweet water forms on the wall by your mouth. You drink it, and you understand that the building knows you are there. It thinks you live here. It is doing what it can.

## VI. The ones who come to the lamps

The flower-lamps along the orphan street glow butter-yellow at night, fed by the conduits, and they draw moths in clouds.

This used to be an accident. It is not an accident anymore. The lamps are changing. The newest ones are deeper, their petals curved inward into a throat, with dusted pollen at the rim. The moths go in for the light and come out gold-powdered, and they carry the dust to the next lamp. The street has begun, very slowly, to pollinate itself, and the lamps have begun to set a seed. Those seeds end up in the cobbles, in the husks that pave the street, and the finches eat them, and the Sward grows more.

When the lamps open all at once at dusk, with that sound like a crowd drawing breath, it is for the moths now. Nobody else is coming down the street to need the light.

## VII. The ones who study

The people who study Cantle live in it too, briefly and badly.

They walk. That is most of what they do. The veterans have a gait you can recognize in any city in the world afterward: a loose-kneed, rolling, slightly splay-footed walk, built for ground that dips like a hammock and wells up water at every step. They sleep in short stretches, strung up in hammocks from the high galleries of buildings they judge to be young, and they wake themselves every hour by habit to check whether the frogs are still singing. They learn the ringing of rising towers, like the swifts. They learn to read a roofline for the first darkening. They carry no tents, because a tent is stillness, and they leave nothing on the ground they want to see again.

Every season some of them decide to stay. Few manage it. The churn wears on a mind. Nothing is ever where you left it, and after a few months many observers describe a kind of vertigo, a sense that they themselves are being worn down, copied from one day to the next a little imperfectly, rounding off into some simpler version of themselves. Some find this terrifying. A few find it peaceful and never quite leave. There is said to be an old woman who has lived in Cantle for twenty years, sleeping in walls by choice and letting herself be enclosed and released, enclosed and released, drinking the sweet water. Nobody has seen her in some time. Nobody is sure whether she left, or whether she is sleeping in a doorframe somewhere, a small smooth bulge in the lignin, waiting for a building to end.

## VIII. The one who builds

And beneath all of them, the Sward.

It is easy to think of the Sward as the place, the ground, the substrate on which the real inhabitants live. That is a mistake. The Sward is the largest inhabitant of Cantle by an unthinkable margin. It is alive in every fiber of the basin, and it is doing something, all the time, with enormous effort and enormous patience.

The clock is its thinking. Nobody knows what it thinks. The scholars who have spent their lives with their palms flat on the warm bark of the root-levee say the clock is not a single rhythm but many, layered: a slow tide of pressure under a faster flutter, and under that faster pulses still, too fine to feel except as a buzz. Some of them believe it is counting.

Counting what? Residents, perhaps. The town was made to be shaped around its population, to grow more of what was used, to keep its people housed and repaired. To do that it would need to know who was there. It would need a census. And when every frog stops singing at the start of a surge, when every newt climbs into its doorway at dusk, when the swifts scream around a rising tower and the eels slide into their pools, the Sward is feeling all of it. The weight on its pads. The warmth in its walls. The small mouths drinking from its conduits. It is taking the count. It has never once found the count to be zero.

Perhaps that is why it has never stopped.

## IX. The one it builds for

Here is the strangest inhabitant of Cantle. It has never been seen, because it does not exist.

Every building in the town is made for someone. Look at the buildings closely enough and you can see who.

The doorways are tall and narrow, so the resident is tall and narrow, long-necked, long-legged, carrying its head high.
