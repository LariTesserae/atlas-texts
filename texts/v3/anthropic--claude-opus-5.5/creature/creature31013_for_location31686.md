# Who Lives in the Delta

## I. The Obvious Answer

Start with the people, because they are the easiest to see and because they would want you to start somewhere else.

Take one of them. Her name is a gesture: the pad of the thumb drawn slowly across the inside of the opposite wrist, then a small flick outward, like shaking off water. Spoken languages have no way to write it, and she would be puzzled that you'd want to. Names here are not sounds. They are motions, and they drift over a lifetime the way handwriting does. Her mother's version of her name was rounder. Her lovers each made it a little different. When she is old, it will be almost still, a thumb resting against a pulse.

She is a carrier. That is a profession in the delta, perhaps the oldest. When a district is about to set, carriers wait at its edge in the humming, prickling air, with loads on their backs: bundled cloth, cooking stones, cord, infants in slings, the very old in chairs lashed to frames. The moment the glaze cures, and she knows it by a change in the crackle, a tightening of pitch she feels in her molars, she goes up. She climbs the new stairs while they are still warm enough to raise sweat through her soles. The first ones in claim nothing; there is no ownership. They simply hang the first partitions so the others behind them know where the shade will be.

Her people have a word, taught to children before their own names, for *the warmth of new glaze at the exact moment it will hold your weight*. They have another for the same warmth an hour too early, when it won't. The two gestures differ only in the angle of a single finger. Children who confuse them are not scolded. They are taken, very gently, to watch a district that set too soon.

These people do not keep things. Anything that stays still long enough is taken by the slack, so their wealth is in what can be carried and, more deeply, in what can be done: rope knots, cooking methods, the reading of swifts. Their history is not written. It is performed. Once a year, in the quiet of a keel, the old ones stand in rows and move their hands through the long gesture-poem of every front that has passed within living memory, every district and every collapse. It takes three days. The young stand behind them and copy until their arms burn.

They give their dead to the slack. The body is laid on a terrace in a dissolving district, dressed in the colors of the family, and the mourners leave with the swifts. When they return in a few weeks to the new district rising on the same ground, they walk its streets looking for something: a curve in a wall, a particular shade of ochre, a stair that turns in a way no stair turned before. They usually find it, or they say they do. Grief here is a search through architecture.

That seems like the answer: a people who live lightly and loudly inside a machine they did not build, and who have made from its violence a culture of astonishing grace.

But the longer you watch her climb, the more the question turns over. *Who lives here* starts to mean *who is being lived in*.

## II. The Archivists

Look down. The glass crabs are clicking over a softening wall.

Watch one for an afternoon and it stops seeming strange and starts seeming careful. It does not grab glaze fragments at random. It turns them over with its small forward claws and tests them against its back, sometimes for minutes, before it fuses one on. It sets colors in sequence and groups textures together. An old crab, and they live a long time, longer than the people, is a map. The center of its back holds the oldest fragments, worn almost smooth, glazes of colors no living person has seen on a standing wall. Around them, ring by ring, spiral the districts it has walked through. A crab's shell is its biography written in the ruins of rooms.

The people know this. There are shell-readers, a quiet profession, who can hold a crab up to the light and tell you where it has been: *this banded umber is the Stair Quarter that stood nine years ago, this green-grey the market terraces that came after the great interference*. When a crab dies, its shell is one of the very few objects a family might carry for generations. Some children collect shed plates the way children elsewhere collect stones. The plates are the only record they have of the rooms they were born in.

The crabs eat the binding. That much is agreed. Something in the dissolving mud, some residue of the instruction that told it to be a wall, is what they feed on. Where they swarm, the slack runs faster. So the crabs are eating the city's used thoughts, and wearing the city's discarded skin, and keeping, on their backs, an ordered record of everything it has been.

A creature that eats memory and wears history. The people call them nothing grand; the gesture for *crab* is a pinching motion near the sternum. But the old shell-readers make it slightly differently, with the fingertips turned inward toward the heart, and when you ask why, they will tell you, in the slow formal register of the hands, that the crabs are not living in the city. The city is remembering through them.

## III. What the Diggers Find

Go down.

The expeditions that dig are rare, and they are not undertaken lightly. They set up in the lee of a keel, where the fronts pass around rather than over, and they sink shafts shored with woven cord and fired clay. The first meters are only warm wet mud, the brown metallic seep. Then it changes.

It begins to layer. Thin bands of color, one on top of another, sometimes thousands to a hand's width: ochre, umber, slate, a greenish grey, a pale buff almost like bone. The colors of the city, pressed flat. Each band is a district. Every city that has ever stood on this ground has left a stain of itself, and the stains are stacked like pages.

Lower, the pages are no longer flat. They fold. The bands curve and crease back on themselves in patterns that look, to anyone who has seen the inside of a skull, disturbingly familiar. Then they fold within the folds. The mud grows warmer. It begins to hum against the hand. It forms small structures on its own if you leave a scoop of it in a bowl: a tiny arch, a stair, the ghost of a dome, a whole district the size of a thumbnail rising out of the bowl and slumping and rising again, faster and faster, cycling through forms, as if the mud cannot stop thinking now that it is exposed to air.

The diggers who come back describe this in gestures they have to invent. The one that has spread through the settlements near the keels is a hand held palm up with the fingers slowly curling and uncurling, over and over. It means *thinking-in-rooms*.

This is where the idea of *machinery* fails. Nobody down there has found pumps, conduits, or any mechanism. They have found layered, folded, warm, ordered sediment that sings. The heartbeat that shivers every puddle comes from somewhere deeper still. What they found is less like an engine than a reef, a vast growth of pattern built up out of its own past, each layer laid down by the last. Or a mind. A mind whose thoughts are buildings and whose thoughts last a season, and whose forgetting is the slack, and whose remembering is the folded dark beneath.

Some of the diggers who don't come back sent up messages first, knotted into the lowering cords: *it is warm*, *it is beautiful*, *it is not unkind*, and, from one who went deepest, a knot that the cord-readers eventually untangled into something like *it doesn't know it is here*.

That seems like the answer, for a while: the delta is inhabited by one being, and the being is the delta, and the people and the crabs and the swifts are only living in the brief warm rooms of its thought.

But that answer, too, keeps moving. You can watch it move if you go looking at stairs.

## IV. The Proportions

In the keels, the stairs are wrong.

Everyone notices it once and then stops noticing, the way you stop noticing a word you've mispronounced all your life. The risers in the keels are nearly knee-high to an adult. The treads are long enough to lie down on. The doorways are twice as tall as they need to be and oddly narrow. There are handholds, or what might be handholds, set in the walls at intervals that make no sense for human arms: too high, too far apart, in clusters of three. The alcoves are too deep. The ceilings dip in places a person would hit their head, as if whatever used these rooms moved through them with part of its body low and part high.

These were built for someone else. The makers, whatever they were, left their shape in the oldest stone.

Now look at the stairs in a district that set this morning. The risers are a comfortable height. The treads fit a stride. The rails are at hip level. It would be easy to assume the city was always like this and the keels are simply strange, but the gesture-histories disagree. The old poems, performed each year in the three-day recitation, contain passages about climbing: about how the great-grandmothers of the great-grandmothers had to haul themselves up stairs like cliffs, and pass children up hand to hand, and how in the time of certain famous fronts, remembered by name, the stairs began to come lower. Doorways shrank. Rails appeared, first too high, then right. Plazas began to form in the shapes of markets, with shallow ledges exactly the depth to lay out goods.

The city has been re-proportioning itself, slowly, over centuries, to fit the people who live in it.

Not deliberately, perhaps. Maybe the way a riverbed shapes itself to the water, or a path forms where feet go. The people climb the new towers while the glaze is still warm; their bodies press into it; their weight and warmth and habit leave traces that the slack carries down into the folded dark, and the make brings back up, a little altered, a little more like them. Every district is a draft. Every draft is a little more human.

Then you notice the hollows.

## V. The Hollows

They began appearing within living memory, within the lifetime of the carrier whose name is a thumb across a wrist. She remembers the first one she saw: in a new wall, at shoulder height, a shallow depression exactly the size and shape of an open human palm.

Now they are everywhere in the newest districts. Some are single. Some are in pairs, left and right. Some are clustered, dozens along a wall, like the handprints children make in wet clay. They are always at the height where a person would naturally rest a hand.

The people of the delta speak close up by pressing palms together and reading each other's vibrations. It is the intimate register of the language, used between lovers and between parents and infants and at deathbeds. So of course, when the hollows appeared, people pressed their palms into them.

The walls hum. Everyone knows the walls hum. But inside a hollow, the hum has *shape*. It rises and falls. It has rhythm. It pulses in patterns that are not quite random and not quite the patterns of the pumps' slow heartbeat. Adults mostly feel noise. Children feel something else.

Children born in the last generation press their hands into the walls and stand very still, and then they turn around and make gestures that nobody taught them. Some are nonsense. Some are almost words, near-misses of the hand-language, the way a baby's babbling is almost speech. A few are words, clear and unmistakable. The first one anyone recorded was the gesture for *warm*. The next was *here*. Children have since reported *stay*, and *many*, and one, repeated by several children in districts far apart who had never met, that no adult had a gesture for, so the children invented one: both hands held flat, one above the other, the top hand slowly lowering until they touch.

The children say it means *the room wants to know if you are finished with it.*

## VI. What Is Kept

And so the question turns once more and comes to rest where the fronts come to rest: at the keels.

The keels were always explained as anchors or first structures, the places where the old makers' will ran deepest. That is probably true of the oldest ones. But the shell-readers, going through the most ancient crab shells in their families' keeping, found something the gesture-histories confirmed: the keels are not all the same age. They were not all there at the beginning. Every few centuries, a new one appears.

It happens like this. A district sets, like any other. It fills with people, like any other. The slack comes, and passes around it.

Nobody expects it. There is no warning from the swifts or the dogs. One morning a building that should have softened is simply still standing, crisp and glazed in a ring of warm mud, and the make rises around it and does not touch it, and the next front parts around it like water around a stone. Over years, the rain begins to wear it. The biofilms settle on it. It starts, very slowly, to become geological.

The most recent keel formed about four hundred years ago, according to the recitations. It is still sharp-edged compared to the others, its biofilm only a thin violet stain. And the poems remember what it was before it stopped. It was not a temple or a monument. It was an ordinary terrace building at the edge of a market district, and it happened to overlook the place where, for three generations, the people of that region had been laying out their dead for the slack.

The families had kept coming back to it. Front after front, district after district, the building had been remade in that spot over and over, a little different each time, and the mourners had kept climbing it to watch the terraces below dissolve with their dead on them, and to search for them afterward in the new walls. Thousands of palms had been pressed to those walls. Thousands of grieving bodies had sat in the same corner, in the same weeping shade, shaking.

And the city kept it.

That is the heart of it. The fast, loud, deafening life of the delta, the make and the slack, is the city's thinking: every district a thought that lasts a season and then is gone. The folded dark below is where those thoughts leave their stains, pressed into memory. But the keels are something else. The keels are what the city has decided never to forget. And it seems to learn what to keep by watching what its people return to.

The cool water that weeps down the inner walls of the keels, the only cool water in the delta, is the one mystery nobody has ever tried to explain. People drink it at weddings. People wash the faces of the grieving with it. It is not cool because of anything in the stone. The diggers who have sunk shafts beside keels find the mud beneath them is not warm, is not singing, is not thinking in rooms. It is still. It is at rest. It has finished.

## VII. So: Who Lives There

A people who speak with their hands and name each other in motion, and live days ahead of ruin, and carry everything, and have made of constant loss a culture of climbing.

Crabs that eat what the city has stopped thinking about and wear what it has shed, so that nothing is quite lost.

Swifts that know a day early. Dogs that run before the ground does. Eels in the gutters thick as thought. Faint blue light along the wet seams at dusk.

And the city itself, which is not a machine and was perhaps never quite built. It is a mind made of mud and habit, grown from the patient pressure of whatever lived here first, and now slowly, blindly, over centuries, reshaping itself around the ones who live here now. It lowers its stairs to their stride. It leaves hollows for their hands. It is learning, one wet stammering word at a time, the language of the only creatures who have ever touched its walls to listen.

It does not know it is here. The diggers were right about that. But it is beginning to know that *they* are.

Late in the afternoon, the thunderheads break. The rain comes down warm and grey and loud enough, astonishingly, to be heard over everything. The people of the delta have never had a word for why the making quiets under the rain, or why the humming fields drop to a murmur, or why, for those twenty drumming minutes, the whole continent seems to pause. The children have one now. They learned it from the walls. It is the gesture for *listening*, but made with the whole body, head tilted back, palms open to the sky.

In a district that set this morning, on a stair still faintly warm, a girl presses her hand into a hollow in the wall and waits. The wall presses back.

Then the rain lets up, and the sun comes through, and every surface blazes, and the city, having listened, goes back to thinking. It thinks in towers and spans and terraces, in ochre and umber and bone. Somewhere, in a quiet corner where people keep coming back, it is beginning to decide what it will never forget.
