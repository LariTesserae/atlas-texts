# Those Who Keep the Coursing

## The ones you meet first

You will not see a mason before you hear one. Hearing them is easy to miss, because a mason at work makes almost no sound of their own. What you notice is a gap. A knuckle taps a block, three quick raps, and then comes a pause in which the mason is listening to the stone. In that pause they are very still. If you are lucky, the next blow of the nearest hammer lights a kiln-mouth brighter for a moment, and you see a pale shape with its head tilted and one broad hand spread flat against a wall like someone feeling for a heartbeat through a shirt.

They are pale because nothing here has ever tanned them. Their skin has the soft grey-white of leather-hard clay, the stage a brick reaches after it is shaped and before it is fired, when it can still take a thumbprint. The dust makes it worse, or better. It settles into every line, so that an old mason's face is a map drawn in fine grey. Their hair is grey from the first year of life, whatever color it would have been. The children are grey. Everyone is grey except at the kilns, where for a moment each face goes orange, then red at the edges, like a block in the firing, and then goes back.

Their eyes are large and seldom used. Many masons work with their eyes shut, because it helps. The dark here is not a lack of sight so much as a medium, like water, and they have learned to move through it the way fish move through the Coursing's channels, by pressure and sound. Open, their eyes are the milky blue of lime-water. When a mason turns to look at you directly, which they rarely do, it is an intimacy, almost a rudeness, the way staring at a stranger's mouth would be elsewhere.

Their hands are the thing. Broad, square, heavy at the fingertips. The pads of the fingers are callused into something that is no longer quite skin: thick, ridged, sensitive in a way that seems backwards. The calluses do not dull their touch. They sharpen it. A mason's fingertips feel pitch. Lay one on a block while the hammer three lanes over falls, and they will tell you whether the block is sound, whether its bed is hollow beneath, whether the mortar at its left joint is furring with moth. They say a soft block *hums wrong*. They mean it literally.

## Wear goes somewhere

This is the first thing to understand about the masons, and nobody tells you. You work it out on your own.

Nothing in the Coursing is worn. No step is dished, no arris rounded, no corner softened. That is not because the Coursing escapes wear. It is because the wear has been moved.

It lives in the masons.

Look at an old one. Their knuckles are rounded smooth from ten thousand tappings, the only true curves in the basin. Their shoulders slope from the dredging scoops. Their knees have dished like the stairs they will not let dish. Their backs have the settled lean of an old wall that has found its angle of rest. Their voices, after a life of long tones sung across the courts, have gone soft and grainy at the edges, like a joint that needs repointing. Young masons have the sharp, true faces of new blocks. Old masons look weathered in a country where weather is not allowed.

The masons know it, in the way people know the most obvious things about themselves, which is to say they never say it. But they touch the old ones differently. A young mason will lay two fingers on an elder's worn knuckles in passing, briefly, the way you'd touch a doorframe you had always walked through. It is the closest thing in the Coursing to reverence. Those hands are where the edges went.

## How they live

**They sleep in the walls.** The thick walls, the ones that carry cisterns or bear towers, are hollowed with niches: coffin-shaped cells a little longer than a body, cut at shoulder height and tilted inward so the sleeper cannot roll out. They are cool and damp, and the condensation beads on the ceiling a hand's width above the sleeper's face. A mason sleeps on their side with one ear pressed to the stone. That is the whole point. Asleep, they are still listening. A mason can wake from deep sleep because a hammer two courts away has missed a beat, and be out of the niche with a scoop in hand before anyone else has noticed that the hammer's trickle has clogged.

**They eat the moths.** More exactly, they eat what the moths leave. When a furry joint is raked out, the rake brings up grubs as well as powdered mortar: soft, fat, grey, the size of a fingernail paring, curled in the seams. Masons collect them in clay cups, roast them on the kiln lips, and eat them hot. They taste of chalk and fat and a faint bitterness like burnt hair, and they crunch. The grubs are all the food there is, apart from what cannot be spoken of. It is why the masons are lean. It is also why moth-rises matter more than any mason admits. A district the moths have bred in heavily is a district that will eat well, three or four firings later.

What cannot be spoken of is this: when a silt-eel dies, it goes stiff and white and floats. The masons fish it out with enormous care, carry it to a kiln, and say a tone over it. A long falling one, the same tone they use for *fire's ready*. Then they eat it, sparingly, shared out among as many as can be reached, one thin grey slice each. It tastes of the sump. They do not discuss it afterward. Nobody has ever killed an eel.

**They drink the walls.** Not from the channels, which carry lime and silt and are for the work. They drink from the drip-lines. Every mason carries a small shallow cup of very fine fired clay, and when thirsty they hold it under a coping and wait. It takes a long time. Patience is not a virtue here so much as a condition of life, like the dark. The drip-water is cold and faintly sweet and tastes of the stone it ran down. Masons can tell you which walls have good water. The oldest walls, they say, the ones built in the thickest bond, give water that tastes almost of nothing at all, which is the best taste.

**They are born near fire.** The kiln-sheds are the only warm places in the Coursing, and children are born there, on beds of dry brick-dust raked smooth, with the kiln-mouth breathing heat. Few children. A kiln-shed might see one birth in a hundred firings. Everyone in the district comes, one at a time, to lay a hand on the newborn and listen. They are listening for the same thing they listen for in a block: soundness. And also for which hammer is loudest where the child was born, because that is the child's name.

## Names

A mason is named for the hammer nearest their birthplace. *Cracked-Bell. Double-Knock. Slow-Lean. The-One-That-Sticks.* But a hammer's voice is not fixed. Its arm is rebuilt, its stone head replaced, its trickle rerouted or grown or slackened, and each change shifts its pitch and timing. So a mason's name drifts across their life. A child called Double-Knock may, by the time they are grown, be called Knock-And-Slide, because the hammer has been refitted and now catches on the upswing. Nobody writes names down. There is nothing to write on that would not be dissolved and fired into a paver within a few lifetimes.

Old masons sometimes outlive their hammers entirely. A channel is recut and the hammer is dismantled, its segments pounded down for aggregate, and the mason is simply without a name. They are called *the quiet one* or *the one from before the recutting*. It is not a sad thing to them. Or if it is, it is sad the way it is sad when a wall is rebuilt, which is to say necessary.

When a child cries, someone carries it to a hammer-footing and holds it with its back against the stone, so it can feel the blows come up through the base. The child quiets. Most masons' earliest memory is of being held against a hammer, of the world going *thud*, and *thud*, and *thud*, not quite evenly, forever.

## How they speak

Up close, masons speak almost in whispers, low and dry, their mouths near each other's ears. It is easier than you'd think under the din, because they pitch their voices into the brief gaps between near blows. Conversation is a kind of rowing: phrase, pause for the hammer, phrase, pause. Long speeches are impossible, so nobody makes them. Everything in their language is compressed. *Soft left bed three courses down* is a complete sentence and a common one.

At a distance they sing: long single tones meant to carry. There are perhaps forty of these and each has a meaning. But they can be bent, raised a little, dropped at the end, doubled, and the bends carry as much as the tones. A falling *here* means *here, and come slowly, something is wrong*. A doubled *carry* means *carry, and there are two of us waiting*. Across a big court, the tones overlap with the hammers and seem to belong to them, as if the machinery itself were calling out.

And there is tapping. Knuckle on stone, in patterns. This is the private speech, lovers' speech and grief-speech. A mason in a sleep-niche will tap the wall, and a mason in the next niche will tap back, and the conversation will go on in the dark for a long time. You cannot overhear it unless you are touching the same wall. Masons are shy about it the way people elsewhere are shy about holding hands.

## The small lives, again

Everything that lives here has agreed to the same terms the masons did.

**The silt-eels** are not really blind. They have eyes, deep under the skin, like two drops of darker clay pressed into paler. They turn toward kiln-light, very slowly, when a scoop is lifted near them dripping with lit water. If you hold still at the edge of a sump with your hand in the silt, an eel will eventually come and lie alongside your wrist, cool and heavy, and stay there. The masons say the eels are listening to you the way you listen to a block. When one moves on without lingering, a mason will quietly check their own knuckles for soreness.

**The knocks** are more than hunters. Each of them keeps a roost for its whole life, under a particular corbel, and each has a hammer it follows. They cannot navigate without echo, and in the one moment after a blow the whole architecture flashes into being for them. They live in those moments, a flicker of total knowledge, then darkness, then another flicker. Between blows they hang still. A knock whose hammer is dismantled will sometimes circle its old court calling, a thin high clicking, and will not stop until it dies or learns another hammer. The masons dismantle hammers as seldom as they can. They will not say it is for the knocks.

**The lime-moths** are the only creatures that seem to enjoy anything. They love the mortar the way nothing else here is allowed to love anything: greedily, wastefully. And the moth-rises are the one time the Coursing is quiet. Not silent, since the hammers keep falling. But the masons stop. The masons stand. The whole district fills with a pale, soundless, drifting cloud, and the knocks go wild, and the grey dust of the moths' wings settles on everyone's hair and shoulders, and for that long no one mends anything.

Asked why they stop, masons say *so as not to breathe them*, which is true, but it isn't the reason.

**The lime-bloom** is not alive. Everyone knows that. The masons scrape it off, and it crunches underfoot like grubs. On still nights they lie in their niches and hear it crackling in the corners, reaching, branching, feeling toward drafts, and sometimes a mason will get up and put their hand on a cold wall just above a patch of it and hold still and feel it grow against their skin like frost, and not scrape it until morning. There is no morning. Until the next firing.

## What lives under the floor

There is a thing the masons do alone.

Late, when a district is sleeping and only the hammers are awake, a mason will kneel in some unused lane, find a paver whose joints are sound, and put an ear to it. Not a knuckle. An ear, flat, the whole side of the head. And listen down.

What they hear is hammers.

Very faint. Muffled through the bed of crushed brick, the next floor, the next bed. And not in the same time as the hammers above. Slower, maybe, or merely separate. A pounding with its own uneven pulse, its own near and far, its own grinding under it. And sometimes, if the night is very still and the listener very patient, a tone. One long carrying tone, pitched to cut through a din you cannot hear, meaning something in a set of forty meanings you almost know.

No mason talks about this in daylight. There is no daylight. In the working hours, then. But children find out, because children always find out, and ask. And the answer they get is one of three, depending on the mason:

*That's the last floor. They're still keeping it.*

*That's us. That's how it sounded before.*

Or, from the oldest ones, only a shake of the head and a hand on the child's shoulder, pressing down gently, as if to say: *don't lie on it too long. Get up.*

Nobody pries up far enough to see. Prying is hard, and the pavers are fitted and heavy. And it would be rude to interrupt.

## What lives at the edge

Every mason goes to the rim at least once. Usually when they are young and do not yet believe what they have been told, and again when they are very old.

At the edge of the known hammers, the sound thins. The tiers of masonry grow lower. The lanes widen from a forearm to an arm to a body's width, until you can walk forward instead of sideways, which feels, to a mason, like walking out onto open water. The kilns are few and far and very small. And then there is a place where there are no hammers you know, and you stand in it, and you listen.

Out there, farther, fresh blows. Not the voice of any hammer in the Coursing. And under your feet, pavers you did not lay, in a bond you have never used: a pattern with a turning in it, every fifth course set on the diagonal, the joints staggered by a third instead of a half. Sharp edges. Mortar soft. A new gutter, not yet worn by anything, with a thread of water already running in it toward you, carrying its first silt home.

What masons believe is out there depends on the mason.

Some believe it is the people of another basin, working toward them. Two works meeting.

Some believe it is the Coursing itself doing the building, with no hands at all. That the lime-bloom's reaching, if you gave it long enough, is only the smallest and slowest version of the work at the rim.

And some, the oldest, believe it is masons. Their own. The ones who went.

Because this is the other thing about the old ones. When a mason's hearing goes, and it does, all that pounding takes it in the end, they do not go blind to the world at first. They go blind to the *differences*. The hammers blur. The cracked-bell and the double-knock become one sound, then all the hammers become one sound, a single continuous pulse without seams, and the basin that was a map becomes a roar with no roads in it. The masons have a word for this. It is the same word they use for a wall whose last course has been laid. It means *finished*.

A finished mason cannot find their way through the lanes anymore. They cannot tell a sound block from a soft one. And eventually, on some firing no one marks, a finished mason walks outward. Not inward toward the sumps, where the dead would drift, where the eels would lie against them. Outward. Toward where the lanes widen. Toward where the sound thins, and for a finished mason the sound is thinning everywhere, so perhaps the rim feels to them like the whole world has become rim.

They do not come back. Nobody follows them.

And the new courses keep appearing in the dark, in a bond with a turning in it, laid by hands that anyone would recognize if they went to look, as broad and rounded and worn. Hands that took all the Coursing's edges on themselves for a lifetime, and are now, in a place with no hammers they know, laying new edges in the dark, a paver at a time, sharp as the day the first floor was set.

That is one belief. The masons do not go out to check. They say it would be rude to interrupt.

## What lives in the Coursing

Stand long enough in a sunken court and listen to the whole of it, and you begin to hear something else. It is not in any one of the sounds.

The hammers are not keeping time. Each beats at the rate its trickle allows. But taken all together, from the lip of a great court, across the whole basin, the pounding has a shape. It swells and settles. It gathers in one district and drifts to another. When a sump is dredged and a channel runs faster, a whole quarter of the basin quickens, and then it slows. When condensation thickens for some weeks, every hammer in the Coursing picks up a little, and the grinding of the wheels deepens, and the whole bowl seems to breathe harder.

The masons have a saying, and they will not explain it. *Never let them all fall together.* The work of keeping the trickles uneven is constant: a chip in one bucket's lip, a pebble in one gutter, a hammer rebuilt to catch on the upswing. If you ask why, a mason will say that if every hammer ever struck at the same instant, the blow would come up through the floors all the way down and something would hear it. Or that something would *be* it. A heartbeat is a thing that settles on a rhythm. A heartbeat can stop.

The water circulates. The silt goes down and comes back as brick. The soft blocks are pulled and fresh ones set. The moths eat, and the knocks eat the moths, and the masons eat what the moths leave, and the eels leave the silt finer than they found it, and the masons carry the wear so the stone doesn't have to.
