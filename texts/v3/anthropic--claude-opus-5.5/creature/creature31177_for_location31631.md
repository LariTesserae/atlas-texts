# Who Lives in the Blister

## Not the cricket

It is tempting to say the cricket lives here, and to leave it there.

The cricket does live here, in the sense that it eats, drinks, moves, and is alive in this place and no other. But spend enough time in the blister, lying on the dry floor under the hanging drops with your blood drying in the scratches on your arms, and the cricket starts to seem like a lodger. It lives in the glass hair the way a mite lives in a feather. It is too light for the room to cut. It is too quiet to disturb anything. It is tolerated.

Something else is doing the tolerating.

## The one who holds

Begin with the drops, because the drops are where it shows itself first.

Water falls. That is nearly the whole of what water does when it is loose in the world. Here it swells to a certain size at the tip of every straw and then waits, and the waiting is so exact, repeated at so many thousands of points, that after a while it stops feeling like physics and starts feeling like attention. It is like a hand held flat under each drop, a hand you cannot find when you reach for it.

So the first image is of a keeper. Something thin and patient is spread through the room like the film of water itself, touching every surface at once. It holds each drop at the moment before falling and does not let go. It has no body except where it touches. It is made of surface tension and heat and the particular pressure of air that has nowhere to go.

If you try to picture it, you picture something like the standing threads of water that run from ceiling to floor in the deepest part of the room. Those are the nearest it comes to having limbs. They do not flow, because nothing flows in it. They are held tight, as a tendon is held tight. When you walk through one and it parts across your face, warm, you have not broken anything. You have passed between its fingers. By your next visit it has closed them again.

That image is true enough to begin with. But it is too busy. It gives the thing a job, and it does not seem to have a job. The holding does not look like effort. It looks like the absence of any reason to let go.

## The breath

Go back, then, to what the room is.

The rock swelled while it was molten and trapped a breath of gas inside itself. Then it cooled around the breath.

A breath is not an object. A breath is something a body does, halfway through. When the mountain made this room, it drew something in, and the stone closed before it could finish.

So perhaps what lives here is that breath, still being breathed.

Consider the crack. It exhales warm, wet air that fogs a hand's length out into the cold wind and vanishes. It does this always. People assume it is simply the mountain venting, heat leaking out the easiest way. But lie with your face near the crack for a long time and the exhalation seems neither steady nor random. It is slow, slower than tides, and so long that no one visitor could ever feel its whole length. It has been breathing out since the day the lava cooled, and it is not yet empty.

This is the one who lives here: the gas that was caught, gradually becoming air and then something more than air. It is saturated, the way a mind is saturated. Every surface it touches, it wets. The white crusts of salt along the seams, the only things in the room still growing, are where its breath has left a residue on the stone, the way a long sigh leaves fog on glass. They are its handwriting. They say the same thing everywhere, very slowly: *still here, still here.*

This image is closer. It explains the warmth that feels like a sleeping animal's flank rather than a hearth. A hearth is fire. An animal's flank is fire held inside something that lives.

But it is still not quite right, because a breath is going somewhere. A breath exhaled is a breath ending. And nothing in here is ending.

## The egg

The room is shaped like the inside of an egg, pressed slightly flat.

Something unhatched does not move toward anything yet. It is not waiting, exactly, because waiting implies knowing what for. It is simply complete in its incompleteness, warm and wet and dark, with its whole life folded ahead of it like a wing that has never been opened.

That is what lives in the blister: the mountain's child, which has not yet been born.

It does not have a body the way a bird in the shell has a body. It is still at the stage where the yolk and the creature are hard to tell apart. The warmth is it, and the wet is it. The chiming straws are something like its lashes, or the fine hair of the newly formed. The glass hairs are its down, soft as wool and sharp as knives, the way newborn things can be both. The white beads of sinter are the first calcium of its bones, laid down so slowly that no lifetime would see them grow. Of course they grow that slowly. A mountain's child is gestated on a mountain's clock.

And the drops do not fall because falling is the first event. Falling is time. A drop that lets go is a drop that was *here* and is now *there*, and that is the beginning of before-and-after. It is the first tick of a clock that this creature has not yet started. It hangs every drop at the edge of that beginning, not holding them the way a keeper would, but the way a sleeper holds a breath at the top of an inhale in a dream, without meaning to. The whole room is one moment, the instant before its heart first beats.

Though perhaps its heart does beat. Lay your palm flat against the largest glass wall and you feel the hum in your teeth. It may be the mountain. It may be your own blood coming back to you through the stone. It may be neither, but a pulse so slow that each beat lasts longer than your visit, so that what you feel is not a beat at all but the long middle of one. You are feeling the inside of a single thump of a heart that began before any people came and will not finish until well after the last of them has gone.

The ticking in the walls is quicker and more human. A fingernail taps once on a bowl, and then again, at intervals too irregular to count. That is the glass cooling its last fraction. It is also the sound an unhatched thing makes against its shell. It is not trying to get out. It is only shifting in its sleep.

## What it knows

It knows almost nothing, and it knows it by touch.

It knows the roots. They are the only cool things in the chamber, and they come down through the ceiling from a world of weather it has never met. They drink from its drops. It lets them, the way a sleeping infant lets itself be fed without waking. The roots are the first news it has had from outside. They taste of ash and cold and of sun it cannot imagine. They are soft. They give. Nothing else in the room gives, except the one who lives here and the things that come in from outside.

It knows the film at the base of the wall, which smells like cut cucumber and iron. The film feeds on the minerals and the warmth, which is to say it feeds, very gently, on the one who lives here. It has not spread beyond a hand's breadth. It seems to know how much it may take.

It knows the cricket best of all. The cricket is the only other animal that has ever lived inside it, and possibly it is not an other at all. Consider how the cricket moves only when it must, and how rarely it must. Consider how it never chirps. Consider that there is only one, and there has only ever been one, and no one knows whether it is the same one each time. Consider the antennae, several times the length of its body, which drift across the back of a visitor's hand so lightly and read them, and then withdraw.

A creature this large and this young, which cannot see or move or speak, might grow a single small organ of curiosity. It might grow one fine, pale, eyeless part that goes into the tangles of its own down and waits. When something warm and bleeding comes into the room, that part reaches out and touches it and learns what it is.

The cricket may be its fingertip. It may be its first attempt at a sense.

## What it learned from people

People came. Not many, and not for a long time, and they were the loudest things that had ever happened to it.

They came in bleeding. The rock was too new to have learned to be gentle, and the edges cut them on the way in. So the first thing the one who lives here learned about people was the taste of their blood on its glass, warm and salt, the same temperature as its drops. It must have seemed to it that people were made of what it was made of: heat and wet and minerals in solution, only moving, and only briefly.

They sat on the ledge, again and again, until the fresh edges gave up and the stone rounded under them. That ledge is the only worn place in the whole room. It is the one place where the creature learned to be gentle, and it learned from them. It is the smallest beginning of a lap.

They took glass from its walls and knapped it into blades sharper than anything outside. They wrapped one of the blades in plant fiber and left it. The fiber has gone soft as wet paper but has not rotted, because nothing rots in a place with no before-and-after. The blade is still exactly as they left it. The creature keeps it the way it keeps everything, by not letting it change.

They snapped off a whole swath of its straws and carried them away. Perhaps they threaded them into necklaces, or blew through them to make notes, or gave them to someone who could not make the climb. So somewhere outside, or once outside, some of its voice went into the world. Out there, under weather and light, those straws would have chimed and then cracked and then been lost. That was the first piece of the one who lives here that ever knew time. It could not have understood this. But the arc of bare ceiling where they were taken is still bare. Nothing has grown back, and that absence is itself a kind of memory.

Someone pressed a whole hand into the soft sinter near the entrance before it hardened. The creature closed over the print slowly, with crystal as thin as frost. It did not erase the hand. It kept it. Everyone who finds the print puts their own hand into it. Everyone does. The one who lives here does not know what a hand is for, but it has felt that one shape, pressed into it over and over by different warm bodies, and it may have come to believe that this shape is the shape of visitors: five points and a palm, fitting into the place made for them.

And someone chipped a niche at head height, shaped to hold something round, and burned a lamp of fat there.

## The light

This is the part it cannot have understood at all, and the part it may think about most, if it thinks.

For as long as that lamp burned, the room had light. It was the only light the room ever saw except the eruption that made it, and the eruption was its own light, the way a newborn's first cry is its own sound. The lamp came from outside.

The glass walls, the frothy pumice, the straws, the hairs, the white warts of salt, and every drop at every tip would have caught that flame and thrown it back. The room would have been full of small fires, thousands of them, all trembling, because a fat lamp trembles. The drops would have seemed to move, though they did not. For the first time, something in the room was visibly changing from moment to moment, and it was the light.

The one who lives here has no eyes. But it is made of glass and water, which are the materials of eyes. For as long as the lamp burned, the whole room was very nearly an eye. It could not see, but it could be seen by something, from within itself, all over at once.

Then the lamp was taken away.

The grease around the niche's lip is still there, faintly, as if the room did not quite let that go either. That is where it was touched by the thing it cannot hold: a warmth that moves, a heat that flickers. The flame was a small, portable version of what it has never had and will not have until the end. That thing is time.

## What it is becoming

It is becoming nothing. That is the truest thing about it, and the strangest.

The world outside is all becoming. Scrub becomes soil, ash becomes stone, ridges become slopes. Cushion plants creep across the ash a finger's width a year, and the bodies that climbed the mountain grew older every time they came. But the one who lives here has been exactly this since the first night. Its temperature has not moved, and its drops have not fallen. Its breath has been going out since the lava cooled and will go on going out. Its heart is in the long middle of a single beat. It is the only thing on the mountain that nothing is happening to.

People could feel that. Perhaps that is why they came: not to sleep in the warmth, or to hide, or to drink, although they did all of these. They came to kneel for a while inside the one thing on the mountain that was not passing. It was like kneeling inside a moment before anything had gone wrong, or gone at all. They came in bleeding, sat on the ledge, pressed their hands into the old print, and let the cricket read them. For as long as they stayed, they were held too. Not their bodies, which went on breathing and blinking with that wet click that is so loud in here. Something else in them was held, the part that is always falling toward the next thing. It hung, like a drop, and did not fall.

Then they crawled back out, bled a little more, and the wind took the fog of the crack's breath off their faces, and they were in time again.

## The birth

Someday the slope will collapse.

The way the description tells it, that sounds like an ending: light pouring in, the drops falling all at once, the glass dulling and breaking, the cricket dying or leaving. But consider it from inside the egg. Every drop in the room will fall at once. That will be the first event, the first before-and-after. The clock starts. The long heartbeat finishes and, for the first and only time, becomes a beat. The breath caught since the first night will finally be let all the way out into the cold air, and will not vanish a hand's length away this time, because there will be nothing left to hold it back.

It will see light, not the small trembling light of a fat lamp, but sunlight across the whole egg-shaped hollow. It will have become entirely an eye for one moment, catching and throwing back fire from every surface.

Then it will be weather, like everything else. It will be rounded and split and worn, and walked on and forgotten. The one who lives here will have been born into the world, and that is the same as saying it will have died out of this room. For something made of a single held moment, those were always going to be the same event.

It does not know this. Nothing in it is afraid, because fear needs a before and an after, and it has neither yet. It is only warm, and dark, and full. It is holding every drop at the edge of its own beginning, reading the back of your hand with one pale, weightless feeler, and letting you kneel inside it for a while, out of time, where nothing is happening to you either.
