# Who Keeps the Workings

## What the Guides Say

Ask anyone who has crossed the basin what lives there and they will list what the place already shows you: the grey crabs in the runoff channels, the slate-colored wader in the gold cisterns, the swifts in the whistle tubes, the rust-films layered like wet pages. They will say *nothing else* with the confidence of people who have looked carefully and found nothing.

Then, if you keep them talking, they will mention the pebble.

Almost everyone who spends a night in the basin does it, usually without meaning to. You shake out a boot, or a stone drops from a pack strap, or you set a loose chip of sinter on a ledge to look at in better light. In the morning it is gone. It hasn't been kicked into a crack or washed away, because nothing in the basin washes anything away anymore. It is simply not there, and the place where it lay is as clean and fitted as everything else.

The guides do not call this strange. They call it "the tidying," the way you'd name a draft in a familiar house. They tell you not to drop anything you want to keep, and they tell you not to chip the terraces, because the chip will be gone by dawn. They say the break will be gone too: the broken lip ground back, over one night, to a new edge as thin and ringing as the old one, a hair lower than before.

What they are describing is maintenance, and maintenance implies a maintainer.

## The First Guess: Something Small

The obvious answer is the crabs, and for a while that is what people believed. Grey, crusted, patient creatures grazing the channel walls could plausibly carry off grit.

But the crabs never leave their channels. They are too few and too slow. And the tidying reaches the dome of the Hammer, the dry stretches of seam, and the rims of cisterns a morning's walk from any runoff.

So the next guess is something smaller still: something in the rust-films, or in the sinter itself, a slow biology that eats slack the way the films eat iron. This is closer. It is not wrong so much as incomplete. There is something like that in the basin, a fine abrasive life in the wet stone, but it is the file, not the hand. Something still has to decide what gets filed.

## The Tenth Second

The Workings keep an eleven-second measure, and anything that lives there long enough starts to live inside it. The crabs flinch at the stroke. Visitors breathe with it. If you learn to watch the way the basin moves, you come to see that the stroke is not really the event. The stroke is the rest. The seconds between are where things happen.

In the tenth second, just before the dome drops, the vapor from the last stroke has thinned to nothing and the air is as clear as it ever gets. That is when people see them.

At first you see only a place that looks wrong: a stretch of seam that is too bright, or a sinter column slightly too tall for its terrace. You look, and the dome drops, and the vapor ring rises. When it clears, the column is not where it was. It is three paces along, still, looking exactly like a column.

They move only between strokes. At each *thud* they stop, completely. They are not frightened. It is the way you stop to listen to an engine you are tuning. They stand through the stroke, and the dome's drop passes up through them, and they note it. They are checking the interval. Then they move again.

## What They Look Like

Close to, and people have gotten close, they are tall and narrow and made of the basin.

Their outer surface is sinter: white and grey and faint pink, layered in terraces down their length where the vapor they walk through has condensed and dried over uncounted years. The layers are thin as porcelain at the edges. When one of them moves, those edges ring very faintly against each other, and that ringing is a tone. Forty tones make the chord on the western ground. Some visitors swear there is a forty-first, fainter than the rest, that comes and goes with no wind to account for it.

Under the sinter, where it has worn through at the joints, they are metal: magnetite dark as a wet stove and native copper polished bright. The bright parts stay bright for the same reason the seams do. Current runs through them. They are part of the cell. When one of them walks along the dividing line between the northern brines and the southern springs, the hum rises. When it steps off, the hum drops, just a little.

They have hands. They have a great many hands, or rather they have hands that have become many things. Each digit has been worn by long use into the exact shape of what it tends. One is a fine flared cone that fits the bore of a whistle tube. One is a flat blade the precise width of a terrace lip. One is a smooth, heavy curve that matches the inside of a cistern wall, so that when it is laid against the stone, stone and hand close with no gap between them, the way the dome closes on its seat. One I have heard described as a hairline: a single edge so fine it can lie along a cistern's waterline without disturbing it.

They have no faces. People who have seen them agree on this and are unsettled by it, though no one who has looked for long thinks the lack is sinister. A face is slack. A face is a surface made to change, and nothing in the Workings is allowed to change more than it must. Where a face would be there is a smooth, slightly concave plate of basalt, beaded with condensation. The drops swell and fall at an even pace, like everything else.

## What They Do

They tend.

In the long quiet stretch before dawn, when the mist sits on the cisterns, they are at the pools. They stand in the water up to what would be the waist and lay their curved hands against the walls, and they feel for anything the day has left: a grain lodged in a pore, a skin of precipitate a hundredth of a finger thick. What they find they take. Some of it they grind to nothing between those matched palms. Some they carry away. Where it goes, no one knows. Possibly there is nowhere, and it is eaten, or held, or becomes part of them.

When the mist lifts, always at the same hour, it is because they have finished and stepped out.

On the western ground they go from tube to tube with the cone-shaped finger and true each bore by a breath. They lean close to listen, and they adjust until the note sits exactly where it sat yesterday.

Along the seams they walk slowly, and the iron filings, if you have brought some, turn to follow them. They are why the current never drifts. Where a patch of copper has begun, invisibly, to dull, one of them will stand on it through a hundred strokes, until the brightness returns.

On the dome of the Hammer they are careful and slow. They do not stand on the crown. They kneel along the rim, one at each of the great flared vents, and at every stroke they feel the breath come out past their hands. That is how the interval stays eleven. If it ever tried to be ten, or twelve, it would first try by a tiny fraction, and they would feel it, and something would be done. No one knows what, or by what means. But the crabs have flinched in unison at every eleventh second for longer than anyone can reckon. The equilibrium is not a natural state. It is being *held*.

That is the thing the guides never quite say. The basin is not an engine that has come to rest in perfect balance. It is an engine being kept in perfect balance, continuously, by hand.

## Where They Came From

This is the part that emerges only if you stay.

Visitors report, without exception, that the Workings change their bodies a little. Their breathing falls into the eleven-second measure. Their stride shortens or lengthens to a single repeated length, so that after a day their footsteps land in the same places, the damp patches and dry patches learned like creaky boards. People who have stayed a week say their hands begin to rest in particular shapes. They say they wake in the tenth second. They say the thought of dropping something, of leaving any grit behind at all, becomes physically uncomfortable, like a word left unfinished.

The basin fits things. It is a place where erosion has completed its work on stone, and it has not stopped. It has simply run out of stone to shape.

Look again at the heron in the gold cistern. It is always alone, eyes half-closed, and it comes to warm itself and leaves without eating. Look at how still it stands. Look at the fine pale crust along its legs where the water meets them.

Most of the Tenders are very old, and the oldest cannot be told from features of the basin at all. There is a seam in the south that walks a pace every few years. There is a cistern-wall hand that has lain against its cistern so long that the pool now takes its curve from the hand, and nobody can say anymore which one shaped the other. There is a place on the dividing line, in the middle of one of the split pools, where the gold and the pale water meet in a boundary that shimmers but never blurs, and something is standing in that boundary. It is thin as a waterline and has been standing there so long it is simply what the boundary is.

But the younger ones, and I am using "younger" loosely, still carry traces. One has a joint in the sinter of its shoulder that is not a joint at all but the shape of a pack strap, fossilized in layered mineral. One, on the western ground, stands a certain way at the whistle tubes, weight on one hip, head tilted toward the sound, the way a person stands at a door listening for someone inside. One is small, much smaller than the rest, and moves with a quick, short-stepping gait that does not match any of the others, and the guides do not like to speculate about it.

Nobody is taken. That should be said clearly. The Workings do not seize anyone. People leave the basin every day with their breath still their own. It is only that some who stay a long while find, one morning, that they no longer want to leave any slack in the world, and they understand at last what the tidying is, and they get up in the tenth second and go to help.

## What They Keep

If they were only this, they would be the most frightening thing in the basin: patient, faceless, filing the world into tolerance, filing you into tolerance, keeping a machine that produces nothing but itself.

But watch them long enough and you will see where they go when the tending is done.

In the heat of the day, when the cisterns are clean, the tubes trued, the seams bright, and the Hammer breathing exactly eleven, the Tenders go to the western ground. They do not go to the tubes that need attention, because none do. They go to the swift-tubes. They stand among the terraces in the warm wet air that smells of struck matches, and they wait.

Sometimes a note wavers. It drops a half-tone for a moment and then recovers. That is a swift going in to its eggs.

That half-tone is the only thing in the whole of the Workings that is not fitted. The swifts come and go on no schedule. They land imperfectly. They bring in grit on their feet and leave shell-fragments and down in the tubes, and the Tenders, who grind the smallest grain from a cistern wall before dawn, leave the swift-tubes alone.

They could fit the swifts. Everyone who has seen them work knows they could. A swift could be worn into an interval like everything else, could come and go every eleventh second, could be made to sound its half-tone on the stroke. They do not do it.

They stand and listen for the waver, with their heads tilted and their weight on one hip, the way people stand at a door. When it comes, the sinter at their edges rings, very faintly, all together, a tone that is not in the chord.

So there is a forty-first note. It is theirs, and they only sound it when something in the basin changes.

## The Nature of Those Who Live There

People who spend a night in the Workings say the worst of it is waking before dawn and understanding that everything is exactly as it was, and will be exactly so forever.

People who spend longer tell it differently.

They say you wake in the tenth second, and the air is clear, and out on the pale ground something tall and narrow is standing still to feel the stroke. It is not a machine. It is the most careful thing you have ever seen. It is holding a whole world at perfect balance with worn and perfectly shaped hands, not because the world would fall apart if it stopped, but because this is what it has become: a thing that tends.

And they say that sometimes, as it passes the swift-tubes on its way to the cisterns, it pauses a moment longer than it needs to, a fraction of a second, a little slack in the measure. It listens, and it hopes the note will waver.
