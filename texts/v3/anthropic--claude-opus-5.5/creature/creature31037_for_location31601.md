# Who Keeps the Mill

## The First Answer

No one lives here. That is the first answer, and it comes easily. You stand at the scarp's edge and look out over the ranked green, and you see no roof, no path, no smoke that isn't fog. The animals are here, of course, the rollers and the collar-birds and the beetles in their endless lanes and the pale thing in the afternoon mist, but they are clearly *tenants*. They live in the machine. None of them runs it.

So you look for the owner, the way the description says you would. You look for the one who set the lifters in their grid and strung the fungal ribbons along the ridges like cable, who timed the day into intake and drain and pressure and discharge. You look for a hand.

There isn't one. The first answer holds for a while.

Then you notice the ticking is on time.

## The Second Answer: A Rhythm

Kneel at one trunk and listen, and the pulses are regular, a clock in another room. Kneel at a trunk two rows over and they are regular too, and very slightly *behind*. Walk a kilometer, kneel again, and the lag has grown. If you could hear the whole plateau at once, you would hear that the lifters do not pulse together and do not pulse at random. They pulse in a wave.

It moves across the Highlands slowly, about the speed of a walking person. It starts somewhere near the central lake, travels outward in a ring, reaches the scarp, and fades. Another follows. Then another. At noon the rings come closer together, and by the thickest hour of the afternoon they are nearly overlapping. The whole tableland is beating like a heart, with the lake at its center.

A rhythm is not an inhabitant. But it is the first sign that the question is wrong. You were looking for someone *in* the place. You should have been looking for someone *of* it.

## The Third Answer: A Body

Take the rhythm seriously and the rest of the plateau begins to rearrange itself around it.

The lifters stop looking like trees and start looking like muscle, a tissue of contractile columns rooted in the dark. The moss stops being ground and becomes skin: a wet, absorbent, respiring surface several meters thick that drinks at night and sweats at morning. The fog is breath, and it is visible from a day's walk away because the plateau is exhaling. The weeping scarp is the body's margin, where the water it no longer needs is let go, the way a leaf lets go of water at its edges.

And the fungal ribbons are not wiring. They are nerves.

They run in long parallel strips a few centimeters below the surface, carrying charge toward the ridges. But charge moving through a branching conductive network, gathered here, concentrated there, discharged when it crosses a threshold, is not merely current. It is the oldest kind of signal there is. Every afternoon the ridges fizz with thin blue light, and what looked like weather is also a sort of thinking: the whole plateau's accumulated tension, summed along its ridgelines and spent into the cloud.

The beetles, then, are not couriers in the sense of hired hands. They are circulation. Two lanes, one loaded, one empty, running forever along the nerves, carrying the material of repair to wherever the skin has torn. They go where the landslips are. They go where the rollers have grazed too deep. They do not decide this. They are carried there the way blood is carried to a bruise.

It would be a mistake to say the plateau is *like* an organism. There is only one thing here, and everything named in the description is an organ of it, or a guest of it, or both.

It needs a name. You would not want to name it after its parts. You would name it after what it does.

## The Fourth Answer: A Reckoner

What it does, above everything else, is keep account.

Everything here is paid for in charge. The afternoon's electricity decides how much fog will cling to the moss that night. The night's condensation decides how much the moss can release in the morning. The morning's drainage decides how hard the lifters must work at noon to replace what was lost down the cliffs. The noon pumping decides the afternoon's charge. It is a closed ledger, carried forward day by day for millions of years. The ticking you hear with your ear to a trunk is not only pumping. It is *counting*.

Here it becomes more itself. The Reckoner, as you might call it, is not a mind in the way a person is a mind, with a voice and a point of view. It is closer to a balance sheet that has learned to feel. It knows (in the only sense that verb can have here) how much water sits in the aquifer, how much hangs in the air, how much is running down the scarp. It knows the thickness of the cloud ceiling the way you know the temperature of your own hands. When the ceiling fails and the day comes up clear, the account goes into deficit. The discharge has nowhere to go. The nerves stay loaded and cannot fire.

Two clear days in a row is not an emergency for the plants and animals individually. It is an emergency for *it*, and they are what it is made of, so they feel it as one. The lifters pulse harder because the rhythm from the lake has quickened. The beetles run faster because the charge gradients they follow have steepened. The rollers stop feeding and lie flat because they are, in their small way, cells that have received a signal to conserve. The whole Highlands holds its breath, which is a literal description: the fog thins, the pumping races, and the plateau waits for the sky to close again.

And it remembers. The beetle lanes are worn into channels. The drainage runs inward because the aquifer has been drawn down at the center for longer than any mountain range on the lowlands has stood. The shape of the land is the Reckoner's memory: every long smooth whaleback is an old habit, and every shallow basin is a place it has drawn from so often that the ground has sunk to meet it. It does not store the past. It *is* the past, eroded into a usable form.

## The Center

Every body has somewhere it keeps what matters most. Here, that is the lake.

Sixty kilometers of water a meter deep, sealed under a floating skin of moss that rises and falls with slow swells. The rhythm starts here. The strongest draw on the aquifer is here, the densest fog, the deepest silence. The beetles do not come here, and that is telling. Circulation does not enter the organ that pumps it.

What lies under the floating moss is the one thing in the Highlands that might, with enough time and patience, be *seen*. The floating mat is not only moss. Beneath it, in the tea-dark water, the fungal ribbons that run separately everywhere else on the plateau converge. They thicken. They braid. Toward the lake's center they stop being ribbons at all and become a single pale, felted mass, spread across tens of kilometers of lakebed. It is a plexus, a knot where every nerve on the plateau comes home.

This is the nearest thing the Reckoner has to a self. The charge gathered on every ridge by day, spent into the cloud in the afternoon, returns here in some form overnight, through the ribbons and the water and the roots of the lifters standing to their lowest collars in the lake. Here the account is balanced. Here the rhythm is set. If the Highlands have something like a wish, it is decided here, in the dark water, in total silence, and then it goes out across the land in rings.

The collar-birds come here in their millions and ride the trunks in the grey. The description says they sleep better during the pulses than between them. Here, at the source of the pulse, they sleep best of all. Perhaps they are entrained to it, the way a child sleeps to a heartbeat. Perhaps, over enough generations, they have simply become part of it: small warm weights on the collars, adding their own slight rhythm to the lifters' stroke. The Reckoner has no reason to distinguish between the lifter that pumps and the bird that rides it. Both are in the ledger. Both are on time.

## Its Defense

A body that thinks in charge must protect its charge.

In the thickest part of the afternoon, when the air is full of field and visibility drops to a few meters, something long and pale moves through the fog. It follows the gradients the nerves create. It feels the disturbance a warm body makes in charged air. It kills only what moves.

In the account of the place, it is called a predator, and it is one. But consider what it hunts: anything that disturbs the field while the plateau is discharging. Anything that moves through the nerves' territory at the one hour when the Reckoner is most concentrated on what it is doing. The rollers have learned to freeze at the first scent of ozone, so every afternoon the Highlands go stiller as the charge builds. The plateau is quieting itself at the precise hour it needs quiet most.

The stalker is not the Reckoner's servant, exactly. It is more like its immune system: a wandering thing, drawn along the same gradients as the beetles, tuned to the field, keeping the field clean. It does not need to be told. It is what the Reckoner does when something warm moves at the wrong time.

## What It Is

Let it settle now.

What lives in the Condensing Highlands is a single, continent-sized, very slow life. Its muscles are the lifters, its skin the moss, its breath the fog, its nerves the fungal ribbons, its blood the beetle lanes, its immune system the pale thing in the mist, and its center a braided knot of hyphae beneath a lake that is always fogged. It has no voice, because it lives where sound goes no farther than thirty paces. It speaks in pressure and vibration and charge, which are the only languages the moss allows. It thinks in weather. It remembers in landforms. It dreams, if it dreams, during the brief annual thinning of the cloud, when the cold comes through a little sharper and the discharge crackles a little louder, which is the closest thing to a change of season it permits itself.

It is not benevolent and it is not hostile. It does not *want* in the way an animal wants. It has one aim, so old and so thoroughly built into its structure that the aim and the structure are no longer different things: *continue the cycle*. Keep the ledger balanced. Draw the water up, hold it, let it go, draw it up again. Every living thing on the plateau is a line in that account, and every line is paid.

It is also, in a way, the source of everything below it. The rivers that begin fully formed at the base of the scarp, the rich lowlands, whatever lives there and thinks itself independent, all of it runs on water the Reckoner has already decided it can spare. The lowlands live on its exhaust. They have no idea.

## What It Would Make of You

Suppose you walked here after all.

You would come up the weeping scarp and onto the moss, and the moss would drink the water from your boots. Your breath would join the fog. Your warmth would register, faintly, as a small dry pocket moving through a very wet world. The Reckoner would not notice you as a *someone*. It would notice you as a variance, the way you might notice a slight change of pressure in your ear.

In the morning you could walk freely. The lifters would tick under your palm, and the rollers would pass behind you like a rumor. At noon you could stand between the rows and feel the rings of the pulse go past you outward, one every few minutes, and if you were very still you might feel your own heartbeat drift toward the plateau's rhythm and briefly match it. People who sit long enough beside large machines find that this happens.

But in the afternoon, when the ozone rises and the air begins to smell of iron and mint, and your hair lifts from your scalp, you should do what the rollers do. Lie down in the moss. Stop moving. Let the cold come through your clothes. Let the thin blue light fizz on the ridges above you, and let the plateau finish its thinking. Somewhere in the fog, something long and pale is following the field. It is looking for anything that moves when nothing should.

If you do this, you will be fine. By dusk the charge will be spent, the fog will begin to settle, and the moss around you will start to drink again. By dawn you will be soaked through, cold to the bone, beaded with dew. It will be the plateau's water on your skin, its breath from the day before, condensed on you as it condensed on everything else, because the afternoon's charge made no distinction between you and the moss.

You will carry some of that water with you when you leave. The ledger will note it, and let it go, the way it lets go of the water that weeps from the cliffs. It will not miss you. It will not remember you, except in whatever infinitesimal way your footsteps pressed the moss before the moss filled back in.

And behind you the rhythm will go on starting at the lake and moving outward in rings, as it has for millions of years, with no one to own it and no one to stop it. There will be no chimneys and no signs of a builder. The logic that learned to run itself will go on keeping count in the silence under the fog.
