# Who Lives in the Underleaf

## The First Answer

The first answer is the one the travelers give, and it isn't wrong. They describe the moths, the clear shrimp in the cups, and the pale walker on the high galleries. They list three kinds of creature, the way you'd list the birds in a wood.

Stay long enough and the list starts to feel like a mistake. It's like listing a person's eyes, hands, and voice and calling them three animals.

## The Moths

Start with the moths, because they are the easiest to find once you know how to look.

They sit on the undersides of the broad leaves with their wings flat and open. The wings are thinner than the leaves. Up close they aren't really wings. They're more like the leaves' second thought, a thinner layer that came loose. Their bodies are a short soft seam, and they have no mouths. Their eyes are two faint domes, and in each one the ambient glow shines back like it does in the hanging drops. They have no pupils. Nothing is behind the domes. The light goes in and doesn't come out.

When you disturb one, it lifts and loops and settles. If you watch where it settles, you'll notice it always lands somewhere that has changed. It might be a leaf you brushed with your shoulder, or a tendril you parted, or a stem your hand leaned on to steady yourself. The moth comes down on the place you touched and rests there, wings open, for a while.

Then it lifts off again, and the place you touched is the way it was before you touched it.

So the moths are not quite animals. They're the attention of the place, sent out in small pieces. They land on a disturbance the way your tongue goes to a chipped tooth. They don't mend anything. They notice, and the noticing is enough for the next part to happen.

## The Shrimp

The shrimp in the cup-pools are the same attention, turned toward you.

There is one in almost every cup, though you only see it when it moves. It hangs in the blood-warm water, a clear curl of body about the length of a finger joint, with a fan of fine legs that never stops stirring. You can see its insides: a thread of pale fluid running the length of it, the same fluid that moves in the columns. It has no color of its own. Its eyes are black, and nothing else in the Underleaf is black.

When you lean over its pool, it turns so its face is toward you. When you step to the left, it turns left. If you drink from its cup, it stays with your mouth as you lower it, and it watches you from inside the water you're drinking. It doesn't flee, and it doesn't get swallowed. It isn't clear it could be. Travelers who have tried to scoop one out say their cupped hands came up holding only water. The shrimp was still in the pool, turned toward them. The water level hadn't dropped.

Each cup is an eye, and the shrimp is its pupil. That's the simplest way to say it. The Underleaf has thousands of eyes, set at knee height and waist height all through the lower galleries. They sit open and wet under the unmoving light, and each one turns, very slightly, to follow whatever has come in from above.

The shrimp don't seem hostile. They watch the way an infant watches: completely and without judgment, as if you are the most interesting thing that has ever happened. For the Underleaf, you might be.

## The Walker

Then there's the walker. That's the right name, because walking is almost all anyone has seen it do.

It's the size of a deer, or a large dog, or both, depending on who is telling it. The size isn't fixed, because it isn't a body in the way yours is. It's a gathering of limbs. Its legs are the same pale jade-and-celery tissue as the stems. They are long and smooth and without joints, and they bend in one continuous curve like a tendril reaching. They have no feet. Where a limb touches a gallery it doesn't press down. It simply meets the gallery and becomes part of it for a moment, and then it separates and moves on. When the walker crosses a lattice of braided stems, you can't be sure afterward which strands were the lattice and which were the walker.

Its body is a slow bulb of the same translucent tissue, carried level. Inside it, the fluid moves faster than it does in the columns. It moves in eddies, like water just after a stone has dropped in, except the stone never lands. Some travelers say they saw a shape in the eddies, but none of them agree on what shape.

It has no head. A few people who came close say that isn't quite true. At the front of the bulb there's a place where the tissue thins almost to nothing and holds a pool. Something small and clear hangs in that pool and turns to face you.

It never hurries, and it never stops for long. It walks the high galleries in long, patient circuits that nobody has mapped, because they don't repeat. Where it passes, it touches things. It lays a limb along a stem for a moment, drags another through a curtain of filament, or passes its whole body under a broad leaf so the hanging drops tremble and then go still again.

It doesn't eat or rest, and it doesn't leave. That was the traveler's list, and it's accurate. But it's the wrong way round, because the walker is always doing something, and it's the most tiring thing in the world. It is keeping the next moment from arriving.

## What the Walker Does

Here is what you piece together if you stay.

The Underleaf is not untouched by time. Time comes down into it the way the light does, through the roof, from every direction at once, and it settles on everything. Every second a little of the future lands on the leaves. Drops begin to fall. Tendril tips begin to lengthen. Your scratch on a stem begins to be a scratch. The first frayed edge begins to exist.

The moths find these beginnings and settle on them. The cups watch. Then the walker comes. It never comes straight, never quickly, but it always comes, and it lays a limb across the place, and the beginning un-begins.

It doesn't repair the scratch. It unmakes the moment after the scratch, so the cut goes back into the moment it came from and stops there. The drop stays on the leaf. The tendril keeps its length. This is why nothing heals: there's never anything to heal. The travelers were right that the place doesn't resist damage so much as forget it. They just didn't know who was doing the forgetting.

The faint ticking inside the columns belongs to the walker too, or to what it's doing. It's the sound of a clock that is being held. The mechanism is still trying. The escapement catches and is caught, catches and is caught, as it has for as long as anyone knows. It's always the same second.

The single deep note across the hollow, the finger drawn around a glass rim, happens when something almost gets through. A drop actually falls, or a leaf actually tears, or a moth that has sat too long actually begins to be old. The walker takes it back, but the effort rings through every column at once. No one has found a schedule for the note because it doesn't have one. It's a flinch.

## Why

This is where the image stops being a creature and turns into something else.

The Underleaf was grown in one long breath. That's what the place looks like, and it's what the walker is: the rest of that breath, held.

Somebody made this garden, or the garden made itself. Whichever it was, at the end of that growing there was a morning, the first one. Every leaf was whole and every stem unscarred. The light soaked through a thousand new layers and came out stronger. Water stood in the cups for the first time, and the first beads of condensation formed on the first leaves. Whatever was there to see it looked at it and could not bear for it to become the second morning.

The walker is that refusal, given limbs.

It isn't cruel. When you watch it long enough, cruelty is the last thing you'd call it. It moves the way someone moves through a house where a person they love is asleep. It checks every room, touches every door, and makes sure nothing has changed. The shrimp's infant stare is the same love: total attention, with no plan beyond this moment, because this moment has to be kept exactly as it is.

It's grief, and the grief is older than any loss. It grieves in advance, for everything that would have happened. The rot would come, and the litter, and the brown shade and the broken branches. There would be fruit, then fallen fruit, then the seedlings that crowd out the old growth. Every beautiful thing here would be replaced by something that was also beautiful but was not this. The walker has decided it won't happen. It walks and walks. It doesn't eat because eating is change. It doesn't rest because resting would let the next second in.

## The Knots

That explains why the knots stay.

A scratch is a wound, a beginning of damage, and the walker takes it back. A knot isn't a wound. When you tie two columns together with braided tendril, nothing gets cut and nothing gets hurt. You've made an arrangement. You've taken what was here and put it together in a new way, and the new way is whole.

The walker doesn't unmake the knots. Travelers who have watched it pass one say it lays a limb along the binding and stays there longer than it stays anywhere else. It moves along the braid as if reading it. Then it goes on and leaves the knot alone.

The people who understood this, and some did, stopped trying to mark the place and started making gifts for it. They built arches and doorways. They made doorways from one part of the gallery into another part exactly like it, which means they made a door from the morning back into the morning. It's a door that doesn't go anywhere, because nowhere is where the walker wants everything to stay. It's the only architecture the walker could love. The knots are messages left by people who learned the language: we won't hurt it, we see what you're doing, we'll keep it too.

## The Ring

The ring of seeds is a different message.

They're huge and dark and stone-hard, and they're black, the only black in the Underleaf apart from the shrimp's eyes. They're this plant's seeds. Somebody found them, probably out on the salt plain, maybe blown or carried out of the hollow long ago, from before the breath was held. Or they came from some other Underleaf that was allowed to finish. They're what this garden would make if it were ever allowed to go on.

Someone carried them down and laid them in a circle nine paces across, and left the center empty.

The walker goes around it. Every traveler who has seen the walker near that gallery says the same thing. It follows the curve of the ring at a distance of a few paces, all the way around, sometimes several times. It never crosses. The root-felt inside the circle is the same as the root-felt outside, but that doesn't matter. What's inside the ring is a question, and the question is: what if you let go?

The seeds haven't sprouted, and the walker hasn't taken them away. The shrimp in the nearest cups watch them the way they watch strangers.

## The Ones Who Stayed

Then there's the hammock near the eastern wall.

It's woven of filament, still perfect, and it hangs between two columns. Beside it, on a cup-plant's rim, sits a bowl folded from a single leaf and pinned with a thorn, with a little water still in it.

Look into the hammock and at first there's nothing there. Look longer, the way you have to look to find the moths, and you may see that the weave sags slightly in the middle, as if something is resting in it. There's a faint shape, no heavier than light, lying on its side with its knees drawn up the way people sleep when they're warm and have nowhere to be.

This is the problem that people who stay a long time talk about.

The walker doesn't take back only the scratches. It takes back every beginning, and a person staying in the Underleaf is made of beginnings. The first grey hair begins, and the first ache in a knee. There's the thought you had yesterday that you meant to finish today, and the small slow changes of a mind turning over the days. The moths find these the way they find a brushed leaf. They settle on your shoulder in your sleep and on the backs of your hands while you drink. Then, very gently, the walker comes and lays a limb along you, and the beginning goes back where it came from.

It doesn't hurt. It's the tenderest thing that will ever happen to you. You won't age or grieve. Nothing will ever go wrong with you again. It's being loved the way the leaves are loved, completely, with no room for tomorrow.

But you are held at the moment just before anything could begin to go wrong. For a person, that's also the moment just before anything could begin at all. Eventually you stop leaving marks. Then you stop being quite noticed, even by yourself. You lie down in the hammock one warm afternoon that's the same as every other warm afternoon, and you sleep. The walker checks on you on its long circuits, and the bowl beside you never runs dry.

The ones who stayed are still there, and they are residents too. They're thin as moth-wings and whole as the first morning, asleep in the hammocks, curled in the galleries, sitting with their backs against the columns and their faces turned outward toward the glow. There are more of them than the travelers know. You walk past a hundred and never see them.

## The Ones Who Left

The ones who left had some of their beginnings taken back while they were there. Most of them don't notice. Some do. When they climb out over the crumbling white lip into the salt and the wind, the first thing they feel is time landing on them all at once, like a weight or a fever. Every second arrives and nothing takes it away.

They say they miss the Underleaf so badly in the dark that they can't breathe. What they're describing, though they don't always know it, is the absence of that attention. Down there, something walked the whole hollow, endlessly, for no reason except to make sure that they, like every leaf, stayed exactly as they were.

## What Lives There

So what lives in the Underleaf?

The moths live there: noticing, in pieces. The shrimp live there: watching, from a thousand wet eyes. The walker lives there, pale and many-limbed, and never rests from holding back the next second. The sleepers live there in their hammocks, weightless, kept. The knots belong to it now, along with the doorways from morning into morning.

Under all of them, and through all of them, lives one thing: a love that could not stand to let the first morning end. It's a breath drawn in and never let out. It walks the galleries touching everything and saying, in the ticking of the stems, *not yet, not yet, not yet*. Across the hollow, very rarely, something tries to fall, and it rings like glass.

The seeds are still in their circle, and the walker hasn't gone inside it. But it keeps coming back to walk around them.
