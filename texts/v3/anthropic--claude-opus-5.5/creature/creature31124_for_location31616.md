# Who Lives at Kessel Flat

## The Hands

You can tell who lives here by their hands.

Anyone new grips a railing and then stares at the gray paste on their palm, unsure where to wipe it. The people of the works never look. Their hands travel to the outer thigh on their own, a quick downward stroke, over and over through the day, without a pause in speech. Every pair of trousers in town wears a stiff pale patch on each thigh where the salt has built up. Sometimes it's the only way to tell someone's dominant hand. From a distance you can tell how long someone has lived here by how white those patches are.

They don't shake hands. A wet palm pressed to another wet palm is unpleasant in a way everyone understands. When two people meet they touch the backs of their hands, knuckle to knuckle, the drier side. It's quick and almost shy, like two animals checking each other's scent. Children learn it before they can speak. Visitors from the port extend their palms and are met, politely, with a knuckle.

Everyone here is always slightly wet, and they have stopped minding. Their skin is soft and pale and never burns, because the sun never fully reaches them. Their lips never crack. Their eyes are set for haze. When they ride the freight up the escarpment to the port, and the air thins and dries and the sky turns hard blue, most of them get headaches. Their lips split within a day. They squint at the long clear distances as though they were a glare. They describe the upper country as *loud*, and they don't mean the noise.

## Coming In

About six hundred people live in the blocks around Second and C. The number is never exact, because nobody has counted in years, and because a few of them don't live where they're supposed to.

Nobody chooses a house here. When a building fails, when its rebar blooms through the concrete or its floor slab tilts toward the brine or its roof finally lets go in the afternoon wind, the people living in it move. They always move toward the plant, into whatever is sound and empty nearer the kilns. This is called *coming in*. "We came in last rains." "The Okafors came in off Sixth." Over ninety years the inhabited town has shrunk the way a puddle shrinks, from its edges toward its lowest point, and the lowest point is the processing hall.

Families still carry the streets they came in from. There are households called the Sevenths, the Eighths, one very old woman who is the last of the Elevenths. A street name worn as a surname means *we were out there once, where the grid is dark now*. It carries a small dignity, like a family that used to own land. Children tease each other with it. *You're only a Third.* Nobody is a First or a Second. Those streets were always the plant.

## The Work

**The pan readers.** There are nine or ten of them, and they are the closest thing the town has to priests. They walk the berms at dawn, before the haze thickens, and look at the brine. That's all they do. They have no instruments beyond a hydrometer most of them no longer bother with. They read color. They have names for perhaps forty reds, and none of the names are colors. There is *liver*, *old liver*, *the red that's still thinking*, *bruise*, *ready*, *past ready*, *burnt*. A pan reader will stand at the edge of a pan for a long time, head a little to one side, the way the flamingos stand. Then she will say *three days* and walk on, and in three days the sluice will be opened. Apprentices spend years learning to see the difference between two pans that look identical to anyone else. The archaea bloom and die by salinity, and the pan readers have learned to read the dying.

**The pump men.** They live nearest the lake, in the old blockhouses beside the pumphouses. Their sleep is set to the pumps. Anyone raised in the works stops hearing the thud, the way you stop hearing your own pulse. But everyone in town wakes when a pump stops unexpectedly. Silence is the alarm. A pump dies at three in the morning and six hundred people are lying awake in the dark, listening to nothing, waiting to hear whether it starts again. If it doesn't, there are footsteps in the street, and then lights at the lake edge.

**The drivers.** A handful of them run the yellow-brown locomotives along the berms, at walking pace, with the cab doors open. They don't use the horn near the birds. Each driver has a manner with the flamingos, a particular slow way of easing the engine forward so the flock parts in sections instead of lifting all at once. They are proud of this and judge each other by it. A driver who puts up the whole colony has made a public mistake. The town hears the rush of wings, and people look east and say a name.

**The kiln crews.** They work the two living lines in the hall, in heat that makes the rest of the basin feel cool. They are the only people in Kessel Flat who are ever truly dry, and it changes them. They come off shift crusted and cracked and strange, and they drink and talk loudly for an hour, like people who have been somewhere far away. Then the humidity takes them back.

## What They Eat

Most food comes in on the freight: flour, rice, tins, onions, diesel, beer. Twice a week the whole town is briefly busy in the yard.

The rest comes from the flies. In emergence weeks, when the pupae wash up in brown drifts along the lee of the berms, people go out with sieves and buckets and scoop them from the shallows. They spread them to dry on the flat roofs, where the salt dust helps. Rubbed between the palms, the husks fall away and leave something small, golden, and oily. It gets pressed into cakes or stirred into porridge. It tastes of shrimp and faintly of the lake. Old people say the works would have died long ago without it, in the lean decades when the freight came once a month. Children are sent out to the berms with sieves the way children elsewhere are sent to pick berries. They come back black to the elbows and grinning, and nobody washes, because washing doesn't do much here.

No one eats the flamingos. There isn't exactly a rule. No one does it, and the one story about someone who did doesn't end well, and it isn't told as a joke.

## The Quiet Pan

You cannot bury the dead at Kessel Flat. A grave fills with warm black water within the hour.

So the dead go north, to the abandoned pans at the edge of the colony. There is one pan there that hasn't been pumped in sixty years, its crust as hard as poured stone. It's called the Quiet Pan, though the flamingos are rarely quiet around it. The body is washed, wrapped in a plain cloth, and laid in a shallow cut in the crust. Then it is covered with loose salt from the harvest, a bag or two brought by the family from the plant. The brine rises from below and the salt closes from above. In a season the body is sealed. In a few years it is part of the crust, hard and white, and after a generation nobody can say exactly where anyone lies. The whole pan is the grave.

The flamingos nest right up to its edge and sometimes on it. People here find this comforting, and they don't try to explain why. When the colony is in, the Quiet Pan sits inside a field of pink, murmuring, and the dead of ninety years lie under the nests in a layer of their own town's salt.

The last of the Elevenths has told the pan readers she wants to be salted with trona from the inner pans, the red ones, *ready*. They have agreed.

## The Ones Who Don't Come In

Not everyone lives in the lit blocks.

There are perhaps a dozen, never more than twenty, who live out in the dark grid, alone, in buildings nobody else will use. A man on Ninth Street keeps a barracks room swept and a door on its hinges. A couple live at the top of the old salt silo on H Avenue, above the colony, and haul their water up by rope. Someone lives in the old company office on F, though nobody agrees on who. There are lamps sometimes, deep in the grid at night, a single yellow point three or four blocks out, and people in the lit blocks look at them and don't comment.

These people aren't exiles. They come in for the freight like everyone else and touch knuckles in the yard. But they have chosen the edge of the puddle instead of its center. The town regards them the way it regards the marabou storks on the barracks roofs at dawn, as part of the place, slightly ominous, necessary in some way nobody would put into words. When one of them dies, the others take over the sweeping of that doorway for a while. Then they stop, and the doorway silts up with crust, and the street closes a little further.

## What Is Actually Living Here

That's the people. But the question was *whoever or whatever*, and after enough time in the basin the people start to look like part of something else.

Look at the pans. The brine comes in dilute and green. It moves inward, concentrates, turns orange, turns red. At each stage whatever lived in the last stage dies, and something that tolerates more salt takes its place. By the innermost pans the only life left is the life that can bear almost anything, a deep opaque red that is really a vast number of very small, very patient things. Then the pan goes white, and the salt is harvested, and what's left is the crystal the life was living in all along.

Now look at the town. Eleven thousand people came in when it was new, dilute, full of movement: barracks full, streets loud, the whole grid lit. Over ninety years it has evaporated. The ones who couldn't bear it left, and the ones who could bear a little more stayed. Every generation the town has grown more concentrated and more itself. The people who remain are the red stage. They belong to the inner pans. They touch knuckles, read forty reds, wake at the silence of a pump, and eat the flies, and salt their dead into the crust. They have been selected, slowly, for the ability to live at saturation.

Put that way, what lives at Kessel Flat is a process more than a people or a population of birds. It lives in the sequence of pans and the order of the colors. It lives in the contraction of the lit blocks, street by street. It lives in the pump's thud and the fly hatches and the condensation that comes every night and the salt it leaves every morning. The flamingos are part of it, and so are the bats pouring from the culverts, and the swallows' nests under the eaves by the hundred. The six hundred people are part of it too. Their dead are in the crust with the rest, and their trousers are stiff with the same salt that is closing the doorways. Everything here is on its way to crystal, living hard in the last pan before harvest.

The people of the works know this in the way that people know things about the place they live without ever needing to say them. It's in how the pan readers stand at the edge of the red water with their heads to one side. It's in the way nobody fights the coming in. It's in the way the dark blocks are not mourned. They are simply the outer pans, gone dry, done.

## At Night

Stand at the corner of Second and C after dark. Behind you the plant glows orange. Ahead, the few lit windows are yellow and wet-edged in the haze. A radio is playing somewhere, and someone laughs. A pump starts at the lake edge, and you feel it in your chest, slow, and every person in every lit room feels it at the same moment without noticing. Out in the grid a single lamp burns on Ninth Street. Above the pans the bats are feeding in their thousands, and the colony murmurs in the north like talk in another room, over the Quiet Pan.

A woman passes you on her way home from the kilns, still dry, still strange. Out of habit she lifts the back of her hand toward you, knuckles out.

Touch it. Everyone who stays here does, at the start.
