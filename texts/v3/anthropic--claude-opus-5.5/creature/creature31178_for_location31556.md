# What Lives in the Throat

## The Tenants You Can See

Start with the obvious ones, because they are real and because they are the doorway.

The frogs are the size of a thumb joint, and almost the whole of each one is the colour of the wet basalt. That is camouflage, but it is also something more particular. Their backs are faintly fluted, a pattern of fine vertical ridges that matches the grooves in the walls, as though they had been pressed out of the same mould. You could look at one for a full minute without seeing it. Then it breathes, and the pale throat swells, and it is suddenly unmistakable. The throat is not white exactly. It is the soft, peach-nap colour of the moss in the quiet places.

Their call is the glassy note. Someone who stays long enough to learn their habits will notice that no frog ever calls twice on the same pitch in one evening. Each note is placed. Each finds a gap in the roar, a narrow channel just above one of the waterfall's overtones where a small sound can travel without being drowned, and sends itself through. The notes cross the basin like thrown pebbles skipping over a river, and they last too long. A sound that small, in air that wet, should die almost at once. These linger for three, four, five breaths before they fade. Something is holding them up.

The snails climb the walls at night and leave silver tracks across the black stone. By morning the tracks have always dried in the same kind of shape: long, patient spirals that begin near the floor and curve upward, never crossing, never quite repeating. Nobody has mapped them. If someone did, they might find that the spirals are drawn around the quiet places in the same nested curves the ferns obey, as if the snails, crawling blind in the dark with their bodies pressed flat against the vibrating rock, are feeling their way along the chord.

The dipper comes and goes. It walks into the pool and vanishes, and it stays under longer than a bird that size should be able to. When it surfaces it does not shake itself at once. It sits very still on the rim for a moment with its head cocked, like someone who has come up from a cellar and is still hearing what was said down there.

These are the tenants. They are not the resident.

## The Floor That Is Not Moss

The quiet places are described as patches where almost nothing grows: a short pale velvet, nearly white, too fine to cover a stone. It is natural to assume the quiet came first. Where the sound cancels, where the ripples of the roar meet and flatten each other out, the ferns do not get whatever they need from the trembling air, so they stop. The pale stuff is simply what survives in the absence.

That assumption is backwards.

Kneel in one of the quiet places and work a fingernail under the edge of the pale nap. It does not come up like moss, which tears loose in a clump of green stems and brown rhizoid. It lifts in a thin, cool skin, and beneath it the rock is not bare. It is laced with filaments finer than hair, translucent, faintly opalescent, bedded so closely against the stone that they seem to be part of its surface. Where the filaments run together they thicken into strands, and the strands run into the rock itself.

They run into the old healed cracks, the pale mineral seams like the veins in the back of an old hand.

Those seams are not mineral, or not only mineral. They are the oldest parts of the thing, the paths it took into the basalt so long ago that it has turned partly to stone along them. It has done what roots do to a paving slab, only very slowly and very gently: it went into every fracture and filled it, and the rock healed around it. That is why there are no sharp edges anywhere in the basin. Something has spent a very long time closing every wound in this stone from the inside.

The pale velvet of the quiet places is only where it comes to the surface. Everywhere else it lies under the green mat, under the moss and liverwort and the roots of the ferns, a continuous web through the whole floor of the basin and up into the walls. It is a single thing, the size of a hall, and the quiet places are where it opens.

## What It Eats

The ferns in the loud places tremble. Even with no wind, their fronds shiver with a fine, ceaseless vibration. They are riding the roar, and the roar is the one thing this basin has in abundance: more energy than light, more than heat, more than the slow minerals in the water. A waterfall's worth of force, folded by the curved walls and poured back into the bowl, over and over, for thousands of years.

The thing under the floor drinks it.

It is not an acoustic accident that the quiet places are quiet. They are quiet because the sound goes into them and does not come out. The pale surface is a membrane, a skin tuned to the chord, and where the chord presses on it, it takes the pressure in. It feels the pressure the way a drumhead does, and holds it the way a sponge holds water. The pattern of nodes around the pool, the frozen ripples, is not only the shape of the chord. It is the shape of a mouth that has learned exactly where to open so that it can swallow the most.

The ferns and moss and the platter-leaved plants grow in the leftovers. The heart-shaped leaves that turn their faces toward the waterfall are not listening. They are the lesser guests at a table, leaning toward the kitchen. They take what the pale thing does not, and they are loud and lush and crowded because there is so much of it. The boundaries between loud ground and quiet ground are clean because a boundary between one who eats and one who lives on scraps is always clean.

This is why the deer will not come over the rim. What unsettles them is not the loudness. Animals stand beside waterfalls all the time. It is the quiet. There are a dozen places in that roaring bowl where sound falls into the ground and vanishes, and a deer's whole body is an instrument for noticing where sound goes. It knows the difference between a place where nothing is making noise and a place where noise is being taken. Their paths curve away from the basin the way paths curve away from a sleeping predator. They are not wrong to do it. They are simply wrong about the appetite.

## Its Day

The pool breathes once a day. It rises by a forearm's length and falls again. The fall thickens and thins, and the roar deepens and lightens with it.

The thing under the floor has a day that matches.

At the flood, when the pool is high and lapping into the moss and the roar is at its heaviest, it feeds. The quiet places are at their deepest then. Someone sitting in one would find the hush almost absolute, as if a hand had been cupped over each ear. The filaments beneath the pale nap are taut, faintly warm, and they tick very softly against the stone as they shift. A patient ear can hear it: a sound like frost forming, and nothing else.

At the ebb, when the pale ring of stone shows around the pool and the roar lightens, it rests. The quiet places are shallower then, the hush less complete. And it is at the ebb, in the late hours after midnight or in the green shade of the afternoon, that people who have stayed long say they hear the things that are not there.

The slow cycle underneath, the drift of a finger's width over seasons, is growth. It is very slow growth, but it is growth. Year by year the mouths widen and edge outward, and the ferns at their borders die back to give way or creep forward when the pale skin retreats. It is not in a hurry. There is no reason for it to be. The waterfall has been falling for longer than anything alive has been counting, and it will go on falling.

## Where It Ends

It does not end at the rim of the pool.

The pool has no visible bottom, and it drains through the rock. The water that falls is drawn back into the mountain and emerges far below, as springs on the lower slopes. It is a closed breath, and the filaments follow the water. They follow it down the way roots follow a buried pipe. They line the channels in the stone, down through the dark, through the place near the heat where the water warms, and out toward the springs.

The dipper, when it goes under, is swimming among them. It feeds on whatever lives on the pale strands along the pool walls: small translucent larvae, grazing where the filaments are thickest. It comes up with its head cocked because down there, in the black-green water where the web is dense, the whole pool hums, faintly, with everything the basin has swallowed.

Down on the lower slopes, at the springs, the water comes out of the rock warm and smelling of iron and struck flint. Shepherds there have a habit, which none of them could explain if asked, of pressing their ear to the wet stone beside a spring before they drink. What they hear, faintly, is a waterfall, though the nearest one is hours of climbing away. Some of them, the old ones, say that on certain nights they hear a hum beneath it, soft and grinding, like a stone being turned in a bowl.

So the basin is not really its home. The basin is where it surfaces. The mountain is its body, threaded with it along every channel the water uses, and this one bowl high on the flank is the only place it opens to the air. It opens like a mouth, or an ear. The difference between those two things is not as large as it might seem.

## The Frogs Again

The frogs' notes hang too long because it holds them.

A frog's call is exactly the kind of sound it cannot eat. It is too high, too thin, too far outside the chord. So it does something else with such sounds. It takes them in gently at the edges of the quiet places, turns them over in the web, and lets them back out a moment later, softened and slightly stretched. The note does not die. It is carried round the curve of the basin and released, and released again, until at last it settles into a pocket of hush and stops.

The frogs have been living beside this for a very long time. Frogs that find notes which ride above the roar are frogs that get heard. Frogs whose notes are held and handed back, and multiplied, are frogs that sound like three frogs. The frogs that do best are the ones whose calls the thing under the floor likes to hold. Over enough generations the frogs have become tuned to it, their pitches matched to the gaps it leaves, their pale throats grown the colour of its skin.

When two or three call back and forth on an evening, it is not quite a duet between frogs. It is a conversation among three parties: the frog that calls, the frog that answers, and the patient thing between them that holds each note a little longer than it should last, turns it, and passes it across.

## The One Who Sat

Somewhere in the long past, a person climbed the final lip, the dark rim polished smooth as a knuckle, and stepped into the roar.

The frogs, the dipper, and the snails are small, and their sounds are small. This was different. The person walked across the moss, each step a soft squelch, and wandered from loud ground to quiet ground until they found the largest of the quiet places, near the southern wall. There they sat down.

The thing under the floor had never had a heartbeat so close to its skin.

It had never had breathing: the long intake, the pause, the release. It had never had the rustle of cloth, or the small sounds a body makes when it is still, the swallow and the shift of weight and the creak of a knee. It had never had a voice. The person must have spoken, at least once, sitting there. Everyone who finds the one place in a roaring world where they can hear themselves eventually says something aloud, just to hear it. The pale skin took the voice in the way it took everything, but it could not eat it. A voice is not the chord. So it held the voice, turned it over, and kept it.

Then the person came back, and kept coming back.

They brought a quartzite pebble up from a riverbed far below, a smooth pale stone that fit the hand. They found a flat rock in the quiet place and began, slowly, over what must have been years, to grind a hollow into it. It was an unhurried circling motion, and it made a soft grinding hum, the most intimate sound the basin had ever held. It was not accidental, like a frog's call or a snail's tick. It was a sound made on purpose, in the quiet, by someone who had come to that exact place in order to make it.

It is impossible to know why they did it. Perhaps they wanted a bowl for water. Perhaps they simply wanted something to do with their hands while they listened. Perhaps they knew. Perhaps they had noticed, as a patient person might, that the hum lingered in that place a little longer than it should, that it came back to them very faintly from the far side of the basin, and they were doing what the frogs do: finding the note that is held, and calling with it.

The largest quiet place is the largest because of them. It grew toward them. Over all the seasons they sat there, the pale skin widened by the finger's breadth of the slow cycle, and then by another, drawn outward around the person the way a plant leans toward a window. The ferns at the southern wall died back and died back, and the listening grew.

Then one year they did not come.

## What It Keeps

This is what people are hearing, the ones who stay long and swear they hear voices in the quiet places.

Nothing the thing under the floor cannot eat is ever entirely lost. It is taken in and carried round the web and released and taken in again, the way the water is carried down through the mountain and back up. Most of what it holds is small and old: a thousand years of frog notes, the tick of snails, the dipper's splash, the patter of a hailstorm, a rockfall once, far off, that it held for a century. All of it is worn down now to a faint shimmer at the very bottom of hearing, the way the flutes in the walls are worn into the stone.

The person is in there too, with more of them than of anything else. There is a cough. There is the particular rhythm of their breathing when they were tired from the climb. There is a word, or perhaps a name, spoken aloud in the hush, held so long and turned over so many times that it has become almost smooth, like the pebble, its consonants rubbed away until only its shape remains. There may be a laugh. There may be crying, once. And there is the hum, above all the hum, because they made it more than any other sound, and made it on purpose, and made it right there on the skin.

At the ebb, when the thing rests and its hold loosens a little, these sounds slip out. They are faint and very worn. A visitor sitting in the quiet near the southern wall, in the green shade of an afternoon, may hear a grinding hum when no one is touching the stone, and not know whether they imagined it.

They did not imagine it. It is being remembered at them.

## The Ring Beneath the Pebble

There is a faint pale ring in the moss beneath the quartzite pebble. It is easy to read it as the ordinary mark left under any stone that sits in one place long enough: a patch where the light never reached. But the moss in the quiet places does not need light the way green moss does. It is not green, and it is not moss.

The ring is the skin gathered more thickly. It is a place where the filaments have come up from the stone and massed together in a circle exactly the size of the pebble, pressed against its underside, as close as they can get without covering it. It does not grow over the stone. It has been careful for a long time not to cover anything in that quiet place, because the person needed to find things where they had left them.

It is holding the pebble the way it holds a sound. It is waiting.

If you come into the basin and cross to the southern wall, and step from the tall trembling ferns onto the pale velvet, and sit where they sat, the hush will close around you, and you will hear your own heartbeat. It will hear it too. If you pick up the pebble, feel how it fits the hand, and set it in the hollow and begin to turn it slowly in circles, making the soft grinding hum, then several things will happen.

The frogs will stop calling, all of them, at once.

The quiet around you will deepen until even your heartbeat seems far away, as though the whole skin of the place has turned toward you the way the heart-leaves turn toward the fall.

And the hum will come back to you. It will not come back once, as an echo would. It will come twice: your hum, and under it another, very worn, very faint, slightly out of time with yours, the way two people grinding at the same bowl would be out of time. It will go on for a few breaths after you stop.

## A Name

Nobody below has a name for it. The shepherds at the springs press their ears to the stone and drink and say nothing. If the person who sat there gave it a name, that name is inside it now, rubbed smooth, one of the shapes it turns over at the ebb.

The place already has the only name that fits, though. A throat is where a body swallows, and a throat is also where it sings. The thing that lives in the hanging basin has spent thousands of years doing the first: drinking a waterfall, closing the wounds in the rock, growing a finger's width at a time toward whatever it could hear. Only once, for a few years, long ago, did something come and sit at its mouth and make a sound for it on purpose.

It has been learning the second ever since, slowly, patiently beyond noticing, the way it does everything. It hums back the one song it was ever given, very faintly, and it listens for an answer.
