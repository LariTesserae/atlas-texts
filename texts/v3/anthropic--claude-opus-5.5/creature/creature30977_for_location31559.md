# Who Keeps the Room

The honest first answer is *almost nothing*. The mats are a stain, the grazers are rice-grains, and the worm is a flicker. Someone standing in the doorway, if the room had a doorway and someone could stand in it, would see brightness and blue and steam and conclude that the place was empty.

But empty is the wrong word for anything that has been left alone this long. Watch longer.

---

## The One That Follows the Shore

Start with the amphipod, because it is the only thing here you could pick up.

It is the length of a grain of rice and nearly as clear as the water it lives in. Its body is a curled row of plates, each one a thin, faintly iridescent shell, and through them you can see everything inside it: the gut, dark with whatever it last scraped from the mats, and above the gut a heart. The heart is a small pale tube that beats visibly, faster in the warm hours and slower in the cool.

It has eyes. That alone makes it strange. Animals that live in the underground usually give their eyes away over the generations, because darkness makes eyes an expense with no return. This one's ancestors must have been blind or nearly so, filtering down through the black fracture network from the cold aquifers above. Then they fell into a room where there is no darkness at all. Something in them came back: two small dark smudges at the front of the head, crude clusters of light-sensitive cells tuned, as far as anyone could guess, toward the blue end of things.

You would assume it watches for the blue. The pool begins to glow, the grazers flee into the cracks, so the glow must be the warning. That is what it looks like.

It isn't quite true. Watch closely and you'll see they are gone *before* the blue arrives. They are not reading the light. They are following the water.

During the cool phase, when the basin is half-empty and still, the mats along its lip are exposed and damp and warm, and the amphipods graze them. As the walls weep and the ceiling drips and the pool slowly refills, the shoreline creeps upward and outward, and the grazers move with it. They work the thin band of mat just at the water's edge, the band that is wet but not yet deep. They go up the tiles as the water comes up. They are always at the margin. By the time the pool reaches the level where it holds enough water to wake the rock beneath it, the shoreline has reached the fractures at the basin's rim, and the grazers, still following it, have walked straight into the cracks.

So they are not keeping time. The water is keeping time, and they are keeping to the water. The rhythm is not *in* them. It is in the room, and they are shaped so exactly to the room that the difference no longer shows.

Even that is too simple. The first ones that fell in did not all behave this way. Some grazed a little deeper, or lingered a few minutes longer, or lived on the wrong side of the basin where the fractures were farther from the rim. The room taught them, the only way a room can teach anything. Every pulse for a hundred years has been an examination, and the ones that failed it were cooked in the blue. What remains is the exact timing that survived, and a population that has lived a few thousand generations under that examination, each one a little more fitted to the shoreline's pace than the last.

That precise timing, the thing that looks like knowledge, is really a very long record of deaths.

Their molted shells collect in the fractures where they shelter. Over the decades the cracks at the basin's rim have filled with them: tiny, glassy, empty curls, packed like frost. Silica is beginning to cement them in place. In the strong gold light they glitter. There are more shells in those cracks than there have ever been living grazers in the room at any one time. The fractures hold, in effect, a translucent archive of everyone who timed it right.

## The Stain That Writes

The mats deserve a second look, because they turn out to be doing more than being eaten.

They are rust and olive and grey, and thin as paint, but they are not flat. Cut one open, and the only way to do that would be to take a sliver back to a microscope, and you'd find it is layered like the pages of a book pressed flat. Dark band, pale band, dark band, pale band, microns apart, repeating.

Each pair is one breath of the room.

The mats feed on what the reactor leaves behind. When the pool glows, radiation tears the water apart, and hydrogen comes loose. Much of it bubbles off, but some soaks into the mats at the margins, and the microbes there take it as food. They do their eating in the blue, while the grazers hide. They lay down a dense, darker layer when the hydrogen is rich. In the cool hours, with the pool still and the grazers out, they grow thin and pale, scraped back and nibbled down. Then the blue comes, and they thicken again.

They take the day shift, the grazers take the night. Neither knows the other exists except as food or as damage. Between them they split the room's cycle exactly in half.

The layers are a record. Not all of them survive. The grazers eat some, the heat melts others into each other, and the oldest are buried under mineral crust. But in the deepest pockets, where the mat is thickest and least disturbed, the room's pulse has been written down thousands of times over in living material. If you could read it, you would see not just the steady rhythm but its drift. There are bands where the pulses came close together, in years of heavy rain above. There are pale stretches where they came slowly, in dry years. Very faintly, over the whole length of the record, the pulses are becoming a little weaker.

No one wrote this diary. It wrote itself, the way the silica ordered itself and the reactor regulated itself. But it is the only thing in the room that *remembers*.

## The One That Stays

The nematode is harder to see and easier to overlook. It is a pale thread that twitches in a mat and then is gone.

It is gone into the mat, not away from it. When the grazers leave and the pool begins to warm, the worm goes deeper, down into the soft, layered interior where the temperature swings least. There, as the heat climbs, it does something the grazers cannot. It coils. It dries. It draws its body inward and lets its chemistry slow nearly to nothing, and for the half-hour of the blue it is less an animal than a small knot of stored instructions, waiting.

Then the pool cools and the mat rehydrates, and the knot loosens, and the thread twitches, and it is feeding again.

It is the only animal in the room that stays through the blue, and it survives by not quite being alive while it does. If the grazers are the room's shoreline-walkers, the worm is its sleeper. It has made a pulse of its own, alive and not alive, matched to the room's pulse, one inside the other.

## The Tint in the Pockets

For a long time it would be possible to say, as the room's description says, that there is nothing green here. And there isn't. But there is something that is not quite nothing, in the one place no one would think to look.

Go to the alcoves that face away from the seam. These are the dead-end pockets where the silica lattice has carried the light around two or three corners and set it down, soft and even, where it has no business being. The walls there are cooler than anywhere else in the room, because they are farthest from the dike and highest above the pool. The light there is steady and never too strong, filtered and folded by the lattice until it arrives as a glow rather than a glare.

On the silica in those pockets there is a tint. It is not green. It is the colour of weak tea, or of wine left in a glass overnight: brownish, purplish, barely there. You'd mistake it for mineral staining. It isn't. It is a film of microbes living *inside* the lattice, in the tiny gaps between the stacked microspheres, the way some lichens live inside desert rock. They use the light.

They are not using it the way leaves do. Their pigments reach for the deep red and near-infrared, the long, warm end of the dike's glow, the part of the spectrum the lattice carries most faithfully. They catch the light that the room has been carefully, pointlessly delivering to its darkest corners for decades, and they make something of it.

All that optical precision, the precision that would be taken as proof of an engineer, turns out to have been, in effect, a lamp left burning in an empty room. Now something is reading by it.

Here the story becomes uncertain. The lattice is still growing, a few microspheres a year. In the pockets where the tint is strongest, the newest layers of silica are being laid down over living cells. Silica likes to settle on surfaces, and microbes are surfaces. No one could say, without taking the wall apart, whether the newest spheres in those alcoves are still settling in the same perfect order. They might be a little rougher, or a little more even. They might have been nudged, fractionally, by what they are growing over. A few microspheres a year is not much, but the room is patient.

The lattice was made by nothing. The part of it being made now may not be.

## The Governor

Look at all of it together and a last thing emerges, and it is the strangest.

The mats do not only grow along the basin's lip. They also grow in thin films along the weeping walls, in the sheets of seepage that refill the pool. Where they grow thick, they slow the water. A little less comes down, a little more slowly, and the pool takes a few minutes longer to refill. The quiet between pulses stretches.

The grazers eat the mats. Where they graze, the films stay thin, and the water runs freely, and the pool refills on time.

Left alone, the mats would slowly clog the walls, and the pause between pulses would lengthen, and the room's two-hour breath would drift toward three, toward four. Grazed too hard, the mats would thin to nothing, the grazers would starve, and they would stop grazing. Between too much and too little, the two of them hold each other in a loose, unconscious balance, and that balance holds the flow of water down the walls, and the flow of water holds the pulse.

The room's rhythm was a matter of rock, water, heat, and uranium. It still mostly is. But at the margins, in the last few minutes of each refill, it is now also a matter of how hungry a population of rice-sized crustaceans happens to be. The clock that no hand ever touched has, in the last hundred years, acquired a very faint, very small, entirely unintentional hand.

Nothing here decided to tend the room. The grazers want mats. The mats want hydrogen. The worm wants to wake up. The tint wants light. None of them is trying to keep anything running. But the room is being kept anyway, a little, by what lives in it, the way a forest keeps its own rain.

## So What Lives Here

If you asked, at the end, *who* lives in the Breathing Room, you could list them: a blind line of shoreline-walkers that regained their eyes, a book of bacteria writing the same sentence over and over, a sleeper that stays through the blue, a tea-coloured stain reading by a lamp no one lit.

That list would be accurate and it would miss the point. None of them is the inhabitant, not really. What lives here is the pulse. For a century it lived alone, in water and stone. Recently, in the last hundred years, which is no time at all for rock, it has begun to grow bodies. Its timing is carried in the grazers' legs, and its memory is laid down in the mats. It keeps its patience in the worm's dry coil. Its light, finally, has somewhere to go.

It will not last. The dike will dim to red, and the tint in the alcoves will fade first, starved of the long warm light it lives on. The uranium will thin, the blue will weaken, and the mats will write shorter and paler pages until they stop writing. The grazers, finding the shoreline no longer dangerous, will linger longer and longer at the edge, and forget, a generation at a time, what the edge used to mean. Perhaps they will lose their eyes again.

But for now, forty meters under rock too young for soil, something is going on that the room's first composure did not account for. The room still gives no sign that it needs anyone to know about it. That is still true. It is just no longer quite the whole truth. Something inside it, too small and too simple to know anything at all, has nonetheless become the shape of the room. Every two hours it walks to the edge of the rising water, and stops, and goes into the crack, and waits for the blue to pass.
