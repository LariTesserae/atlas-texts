# Who Lives in the Drowned Kiln

---

## I. The One Ahead

At first you would say that nobody lives here. The animals live here, crowded and indifferent, the moths and frogs and glass-shelled crabs, the many-legged dozers on the cables. But those are *fauna*. They are the life of the place, not its inhabitant. You walk for days among them and feel entirely unaccompanied.

Then you notice the footsteps.

They are yours, or they sound like yours: the same weight, the same slight drag of the left boot where the sole has started to separate. They come back to you from a few paces ahead, and at first you take them for an echo. Travelers in the Kiln take everything for an echo at first. But an echo copies. These footsteps *choose*. When you hesitate at the lip of a seam, they have already stepped over it. When you veer toward warmth, they veer a moment before you, as if they knew where you meant to go before you did.

The old hands have a name for this. They call it **the Leadsman**.

A leadsman, in the old ships, was the sailor who stood in the bow with a weighted line and called out the depth of the water ahead, so the vessel would not ground itself in the dark. The name was given by someone who remembered that this was once a sea. It stuck because it fits. The Leadsman walks in front of you and tests the ground. When travelers say that the land forms itself just ahead of your foot out of what you expect to find, this is what they mean, though most of them don't know it. The ground is not reading your mind. *Someone is sounding it for you,* one careful step ahead, and laying down what it finds.

---

## II. By Touch

You can meet the Leadsman if you want to badly enough. Most people don't try. It isn't dangerous, but it is very patient, and it asks patience back.

The way to do it is to stop walking. Stand still on warm slag, and after a while the footsteps ahead also stop, a few paces off. Wait. Wait longer than seems reasonable, an hour or two, until you have stopped expecting anything. Then the first sign comes: your coat of moths begins to leave you.

The moths do not go all at once. They lift from your shoulders in ones and twos and drift forward, and then in dozens, a soft dry exodus. The weight you had stopped noticing comes off you like a blanket drawn away, and you feel suddenly cool and suddenly alone. They are going to something warmer than you. They are going home.

If you follow them with your hands you will find it.

It is about the height of a person, or it is at first. Your palms meet moths, layers and layers of them, a pelt of powdery wings so deep that your fingers sink to the second knuckle before they reach anything solid. The moths don't scatter. They have lived on it for so long that they no longer treat it as a place to land. They treat it as a place to *be*. Some of them, old travelers say, have never flown at all. They hatched here in the folds of it and will die here.

Under the moths, cloth. Heavy oilskin, gone soft as felt with age and damp, buttoned with something that might be horn. Under your fingertips the buttons have numbers stamped into them, small and neat, counting down.

Under the cloth there is a body, and it is *warm*, warmer than the slag, warmer than you. It is warm like the flank of a horse that has been working. It sweats, the way everything here sweats, and the sweat tastes (if you are the kind of person who tastes things in the Kiln, and eventually everyone is) of salt and hot oil and pennies.

Its hands are the easiest part to know. They are large and they are always slightly greasy, and the pads of the fingers have been worn smooth. There are no ridges, no prints. Something has polished them away over a very long time. If you take one of those hands in yours, it will close around your fingers gently and hold them. Then, after a while, it will turn your hand over, palm up, and lay something in it.

Usually it is a brass survey plate, warm from being carried. Sometimes it is a tin cup. Once, a traveler reported, it was a single small key, which she kept, and which she has never found a lock for.

It does not speak. Or it does speak, but not with its own voice.

## III. The Wrong Words

This is where people start to get uneasy, and where the Leadsman becomes more than a helpful ghost walking a step ahead.

You already know that sound misbehaves in the Kiln: your voice returning from where there is no wall, saying a word you didn't say, or saying your own word *in a tone of mild surprise*. You already know that answers sometimes arrive before questions.

That is the Leadsman talking.

It has no voice of its own, or it gave its voice away long ago. What it has is the voices of everyone who has ever walked here. It keeps them the way the slag keeps warmth, holding them and releasing them in slow pulses. When you call out and your echo says something else, that is the Leadsman answering you, in your voice, because it is the only voice you would understand. The mild surprise is real. It is *surprised*, every time, that someone has come. It has been alone in a crowded place for so long.

Answers arrive before questions because it has heard your question before. Not from you, but from the hundreds who came before you and stood where you are standing and asked the same things: *Where does the water go? What are the engines pumping? Is anyone there?* It has been answering those questions for a very long time. Sometimes it gets ahead of itself.

The answers, when you can catch them, are rarely what you expect. A traveler asked, *What are you?* and her own voice came back from the empty dark, pleased and a little shy:

*Still here.*

Another asked, *What happens when the needle reaches the end?* and his voice replied, before he had finished asking, *I don't know. I'm trying not to find out.*

## IV. The Count

Every survey stake in the Kiln carries a brass plate, and the plates count down. The buttons on the Leadsman's coat count down too. Put your ear against its chest (it will let you) and underneath the heartbeat there is a faint ticking, very regular, like a watch wound so tight it will never run out.

It is counting.

Here is what the old hands piece together, from the plates and the wrong-voiced echoes and the key that fits no lock. There was a survey. When the engines were built to drain the sea, someone had to measure how much sea remained, so that someone would know when to stop. That person walked the flats with a weighted line and a sheaf of brass plates. Every so many paces they drove a stake and wrote down how much was left. And they carried the key, the one that turns the engines off.

The count was supposed to reach zero. When it did, the Leadsman would walk to each engine in turn and close the valve and let the great beams come to rest.

The count reached zero long ago. The sea was gone. The Leadsman walked to the first engine with the key in its hand.

And it didn't stop the engine.

The stories can't agree on why. But the stakes kept being driven, and the numbers kept going down, below zero now, into a count that has no name. The plates the Leadsman carries now are stamped with numbers that have the wrong number of digits, or digits nobody recognizes. It is still measuring something. It is still sounding depth. Only now it isn't measuring what is left of the sea.

It is measuring what is left of the warmth.

## V. The Lie of the Land

You have to go down to understand.

Everyone who walks the Kiln feels it: the whole country tilts, by degrees too gentle to see, toward some low place where the dark accumulates. Walk downhill and the grain of the darkness thickens. It becomes like warm cloth, then like fur. Move your hand through it and you feel the direction it lies.

The old hands say that phrase exactly, and it is the oldest joke in the Kiln, though no one laughs at it: *the lie of the land.* They mean the grain of the dark, the way it lies smooth in one direction and bristles if you push the other way. They mean it literally. The dark here has a nap because it *is* a nap. It is a pelt. You have been walking, since the moment the air thickened on the border, through the fur of something enormous that lies curled across the whole country, asleep.

Nobody knows what it is. The oldest story says the engines drew out the sea, and then the light. The old hands say that is only half the story. When you empty a place, something comes to fill it. Water runs into a pit. Cold comes into a room when the fire goes out. And when the light was drawn out of this lowland, something came in to lie in the hollow where the light had been, the way a dog comes in from the snow and circles three times and settles down in front of the stove.

It came because the engines were warm.

That is why the engines are the only fixed things in the Kiln. They are *where it is holding on*. It lies curled around them the way a cat curls around a warm pipe, and everything else, the flats and pools and seams and shelves, is the shape of its body as it sleeps. When distance changes, when a basin you crossed in twenty paces takes two hundred on the way back, it has shifted in its sleep. When the warmth moves east over the years, and the whole crowd of living things drifts east with it, the sleeper has rolled over, and its warm side is now facing another way.

The heat that rises through the slag in pulses lasting hours is its breath.

The frogs, once every several days, all sing at once, a single low pulse from every direction. That is its heartbeat. The frogs press their throats to your wrist because they are listening to *your* heartbeat and comparing it to the other one, the slow one, curious about something so small and quick and warm. After the song, the ground feels slightly further away. Of course it does. The chest beneath you has risen.

And the living crowd, the moths and frogs and eels, the crabs clicking in their migrations, the soft many-legged things sleeping on the cables in rows, is not a mere ecology. It is what always gathers against a sleeping warm body in a cold place: nestlers, burrowers, lodgers in the fur, the small lives that come to shelter in the heat of something much larger than themselves. You were right that you were never alone here. You were in a bed. It was already occupied.

The deepest low place, where the dark is thickest and the moths won't go, where travelers find themselves turned gently around and walking uphill with no memory of the turn: that is the hollow of it, the curl of the body, the place where the face is tucked. The hand that turns you around so gently is not the Leadsman's. It is the other one's. It is a paw, if it is anything, and it is doing what any sleeping creature does when something small wanders too near its face. It is nudging you away without waking.

## VI. Why the Engines Still Run

Now you know what the Leadsman is counting.

It stood at the first engine with the key in its hand, and the count had reached zero, and the work was finished. And against the engine, filling the whole drained basin of the old sea, something vast and dark and furred was lying curled up in the only warmth for a hundred miles, sleeping.

If the engines stopped, the warmth would go.

So the Leadsman put the key in its pocket, where it stayed for a very long time before being given away to a stranger, perhaps so that it could no longer be tempted. And it went on walking. Engine to engine, the way a sailor goes lighthouse to lighthouse. It oils the valves with its smooth greasy hands. It patches the crumbling brick with sheet iron. It checks the cables, and the many-legged cat-things on the cables lift their heads sleepily as it passes and let it scratch them. They are its animals, as much as anything here is anyone's.

And it counts. It measures how much warmth is left in the rock, how many strokes the engines have in them, how far the needle on the old gauge has crept. The count goes down. It always goes down. There is no machine that runs forever, and the Leadsman knows it better than anyone. Someday the needle will reach the end of the dial and the beam will come down one last time and not go up, and the long sigh of the last valve will finish, and the slag will begin, at last, to cool.

When that happens, perhaps the sleeper will wake, find itself cold, and go wherever such things go. Perhaps the light will come back into the hollow it leaves. Perhaps it will be something else the pumps were holding.

The Leadsman is trying not to find out. In the meantime it walks ahead of travelers and sounds the ground for them so they will not wake the sleeper by falling through its fur. It keeps their voices. It gives them small warm gifts. It is glad, every single time, that someone has come.

---

## VII. What It Is Becoming

This is the last part, and the part the old hands only say quietly, touching your sleeve.

The Leadsman has been in the Kiln a very long time, lying down to rest against the same engines, walking through the same fur. Things that live that close together, for that long, begin to grow into each other.

There are pale filaments in the seams, root hairs with no plant attached, knotting into mats that draw long fibrous lines across the flats, all bending the same direction. The old hands have followed them. They lead to wherever the Leadsman is at that moment. They are the sleeper's nerves, reaching slowly, over years, toward the one warm small thing that stayed awake to keep it warm.

And the Leadsman's moths, the ones born in the folds of its coat that never fly, are becoming harder to tell from the nap of the dark. Travelers who have touched it in recent years say that under the oilskin it no longer feels quite like a person. The body goes on further than it should, softer and deeper. The warmth doesn't end at the edge of its skin. It continues outward into the thickened air, grain and pile, lying in one direction.

Once, a traveler asked it directly, standing with her hand in the deep pelt of moths: *Are you the Leadsman, or are you the other one?*

Her own voice came back from somewhere below her feet, very soft, the way you speak so as not to wake someone:

*Hush. Both of us are sleeping, except for me.*

---

For now the beam comes down. The count goes on getting smaller. Somewhere a few paces ahead of you, footsteps that are nearly yours test the ground and find it firm and set it down for you. The moths lift from your shoulders and drift toward a warmth greater than yours. And under the whole black raw country something breathes out, very slowly, for hours, and the one who keeps it warm stands beside it in the dark with oil on its hands, listening, counting, and not yet willing to arrive at zero.
