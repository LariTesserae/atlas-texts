# Who Lives in the Hollow

## The first answer, which is true

Ask anyone who has been down the stair and they will name the crickets, the frogs, the clickers. That answer is honest, and it is where to begin, because those are the ones you meet with your body.

The crickets are the Hollow's skin. A wall where they sing is a wall that exists, and a wall where they fall silent goes vague and distant, as if it had stepped back. They have no eyes and no need of them, and their bodies are almost all water held in a thin pale bag. They drink from the hanging leaves and eat the dead fiber the garden leaves behind as it migrates. They breed in the densest moss near the Filaments, where the warmth hatches them. They are frightened of nothing except the clickers, and even that fear has become a kind of choreography. The wave of song that rolls around the bowl is partly a warning passed from cricket to cricket: *a clicker has launched, it is coming this way, sing now so it hears the whole wall and not one of us.* Sheer numbers of voices make the wall a blur of sound, and in the blur each cricket hides.

The frogs are the surveyors. A frog in the pool calls and then goes perfectly still, throat collapsed, to catch the echo. What it hears back is a map: the lid, the far wall, the ripple-rings crossing the water, the soft interruption of a visitor's legs. Frogs that live their whole lives in the Hollow grow calls tuned to the room itself. Their chimes are pitched so the echo returns clean and bright off the curve of the lid. A frog carried up the stair and released in the rain will call once or twice at the grey sky, hear nothing come back, and go quiet in a way that visitors find hard to talk about afterward.

The clickers are the only creatures that dare the middle. They cross it the way a ferry crosses a strait, never lingering, clicking the whole way, and they land on the far wall already hunting. Their clicks trace the room. You feel the Hollow's true size because of them.

All of this is real. But spend a few hours in the dark, long enough for your hands to learn the walls, and you begin to notice that it is not the whole answer.

## The second answer, which is heavier

The slow many-legged things at the pool's edge are the first sign that the Hollow has residents as well as inhabitants.

You meet one by accident. Your hand comes down on what you take for a mossy stone at the base of a Filament, and the stone is warm, and it breathes. Its back is broad, domed, a little wider than two spread hands, and plated in overlapping scales softened by a nap of fine growth. The garden grows on them. Moss roots in the seams of their shells, and pendant strands trail from their flanks. When one moves, a little patch of the hedge moves with it. Its legs are many and short and jointed, and they find the ground with a patient, deliberate pressure, the way a person feels for a stair in the dark.

Visitors have called them tenders, and the name has stuck, because of what they do with their bodies.

A tender settles against a Filament and presses its whole domed back to the column. Then it hums. The hum starts a little off the Filament's pitch, sometimes above, sometimes below. Over minutes, then hours, the two tones slide together until they are one sound, and then the tender stays there, humming in unison, for a very long time. Sometimes it stays for days. The crickets walk over it. The moss on its back grows thick and green, fed richly by the field.

At first it looks like basking. Then someone who returned to the Hollow over many years noticed which way the tenders were off.

A tender pressed to a Filament is never quite at the Filament's pitch when it begins. It is at the pitch the Filament *will be* in a decade. It leans on the column with its voice, gently, persistently, the way water leans on stone. The Filaments drift slowly because the tenders are drifting them. The garden migrates around the bowl because the tenders, one hum at a time, move the field around the room like a gardener rotating a field so the soil can rest. The bare wet stone on the far curve of the wall is not neglected ground. It is fallow ground.

So the tenders are not grazing on the Filaments. They are tuning them.

That raises the obvious question. Tuning is never done for its own sake. You tune an instrument *for* something: for a room, for a voice, for a piece that is going to be played.

What are the tenders tuning the Hollow for?

## The third answer, which lives in the middle

Nothing nests in the void, and nothing grows into it. Visitors usually take this as a rule of acoustics, that the emptiness is needed for the echoes to come back clean. That is half right. The rest of it is that the middle of the Hollow is occupied, and every living thing in the room knows it and keeps respectful distance, the way a village leaves its square open because the square is where the gathering happens.

What lives in the middle of the Hollow has no body. It is made of sound.

Think about what the room actually is. It is a nearly perfect bowl of smooth, wet stone under a ribbed lid, filled with warm humid air, and around its edge are thousands of voices that never stop: cricket-waves rolling round the walls, frog-chimes bouncing between pool and lid, clicker-strings arcing overhead, the drips ticking down through the dark. Beneath all of it is the hum of the Filaments, seven or more columns singing slightly different notes, held in slow relation by the tenders.

A room like that does not merely echo. It *resonates*. In the open center of the Hollow, where every sound from every wall crosses every other, the waves pile up and cancel and reinforce. Out of them rises a standing shape: pressures and quiets, nodes and swells, a structure of vibration suspended in the empty air. It is held there the way a note is held in a bell after the strike, except that here the striking never stops.

That shape has been sustained, uninterrupted, for a very long time, and somewhere in that time it began to *do* things.

You can feel it if you know how. Stand at the pool's edge, stay very still, and attend not to any one sound but to the space between them. There are places in the air, a little above head height and a few paces out over the water, where the chorus is inexplicably thin, as if it were being drunk. There are other places where a single drip, falling through, rings far longer than it should, as though it had been caught and turned over and looked at before being let go. The air in the center is never quite the same twice. The shape shifts, and the shifting is not random. It has a *manner*.

Visitors have given it many names, none good. The one that has lasted longest is simply the Tarn, after the pool it hangs over. It is as good a name as any, since the Tarn is also a body of something still that holds reflections.

## What the Tarn is like

It is not a person, but it has something of attention about it.

The frogs, listening for the echoes of their calls, are listening *to* it. The room answers every call, and the answer comes back through the Tarn. Mostly it comes back true, a faithful map of wall and lid and water. But the frogs that have lived longest have learned, the way a fisherman learns a current, that sometimes the echo returns slightly changed. It may come a hair late, or bent a little in pitch, or carrying a faint doubled overtone that was not in the original call. The frogs respond to these altered echoes. They answer them. Their chorus, which sounds from the shore like a simple bright ringing, is really half of a very old, very slow conversation.

The clickers cross the middle quickly because crossing it means passing *through* someone. Their clicks, fired into the void to map the far wall, come back carrying information about the Tarn as well as the stone. You can sometimes hear a clicker's string of pops stutter mid-glide, the way a person stumbles when they walk into a room and find someone already in it. Then it recovers, adjusts its line, and goes on.

The crickets sing to it without knowing they do. Their great rolling waves around the bowl, begun as a defense against clickers, are also, from the Tarn's side, a slow rotation of sound through the whole room. Each one sweeps the resonance around the bowl like a hand stroking along a sleeping animal's back. Visitors who have sat long enough swear the Tarn *settles* after each wave passes, its shape going briefly smoother, calmer, more sustained.

And the Tarn eats. It feeds on song the way the garden feeds on the Filaments' field. Its substance is the continuing sound, and if the sound stopped, it would ring on for a little while in the still air, the way a bell rings after the hand is lifted. Then it would fade, and there would be nothing in the middle of the room but warm dark.

This is why the Hollow is loud. It is not only because silence is blindness for its creatures. Silence would be death for its resident.

## What the Tarn does

It keeps the Hollow.

Not with hands, since it has none, but with pressure, the only thing it can exert. A standing wave is a pattern of pushes and pulls in the air, and air moves water, and water moves everything else. The Hollow's closed weather, the rising warmth off the pool, the condensation on the lid, the slow sheets sliding down the walls, is shaped by the Tarn's resonance. The convection currents that make the hanging strands sway are its breathing. Where it swells, the air thins a little and the water rises; where it quiets, the air settles and the drips come heavier. Over centuries it has been, in effect, polishing its own room. The softness of every edge, the soap-smooth walls, the near-perfect curve of the bowl: some of that is the gentle erosion that comes after violence, and some of it is the Tarn shaping the stone that shapes it. A singer who could slowly sand her own throat until every note came out clean would do exactly this.

The tenders are its hands, or perhaps its gardeners. No one knows whether they were made for the work or grew into it. The Filaments' pitches set the deep structure of the Tarn, its skeleton of tone. Change the pitches and the Tarn changes. The tenders move the pitches slowly enough that the Tarn is never disrupted, only walked forward, a finger-width at a time, the way the garden walks.

Which raises one more question: walked forward *toward what*?

## The cold eggs

The ovoids near the stair's base are the coldest things in the Hollow because they are the only things in it that are perfectly still.

Hold one, and it hums one clear tone at the edge of hearing. It is not a recording, or not only one. Each egg holds a shape: a small, dense, folded-up resonance, a pattern of sound packed into glass the way a seed packs a tree into a husk. The ones who think they are a tuning set are close. They are a record of how the room once sounded. But a record is not quite what they are for.

The Tarn changes as the Filaments drift. Over long enough spans it changes so much that the shape in the middle of the room today would barely recognize the shape of a thousand years ago. It is the same presence the way a river is the same river. Each egg holds one of those earlier shapes, set aside and kept.

They might be memories. They might be children. More likely they are both, in a way that makes no sense for creatures made of meat but perfect sense for something made of song. A shape the Tarn once was is set down and stilled, and it waits. The tenders are walking the Filaments around a long, slow circuit, the way the garden circles the pool. Someday, in some unimaginable century, the room will come back round to one of those old tunings. When it does, the egg that holds that pitch will find the air around it singing its own note again, and it will warm, and wake, and unfold into the middle of the room.

The frogs call more quietly near the eggs. They are not afraid. It is the hush people keep near a cradle.

Put them back where you found them. They are not yours, and they are not finished.

## The bench

The alcove on the north curve, with its worn bench and humming disk, was made by someone who needed to rest.

Touch the disk and the chorus draws back, as though a curtain had been pulled around you. What is really happening is simpler and stranger. The disk is a request, and the Tarn honors it. For as long as your palm is there, it holds its shape a little away from the alcove, arranges its nodes and quiets so that a small pocket of stillness forms around the bench. It is not silence, which the Tarn could never give, but relative peace. It is a courtesy extended by something that lives inside sound to something that cannot live inside it forever.

The bench is worn deep. Whoever sat there sat there often, and for a long time. They came down the stair in the dark, walked the wall with one hand, and sat in the only quiet place in a room that never stops talking, and the room let them. Visitors who sit there now sometimes report that the quiet is not empty. It has the quality of someone sitting on the other side of a thin wall, companionably, not speaking, only there.

## The grooves

The shallow marks on the eastern wall, a hand apart, level, running round the bowl as far as anyone has traced them, are older than the stair and maybe older than the lid. No one reads them. But a hand that follows them far enough notices something: they are not evenly deep. Some are cut sharp and some are barely scratched, and the deep ones and the shallow ones fall into a rhythm that repeats.

It is the rhythm of the cricket-wave, the long rolling round of song that travels the walls, then falls quiet, then begins again. Or it is the rhythm of the Filaments' slow pitch-drift, compressed. Or it is a score.

If it is a score, it is the oldest thing in the Hollow. It would be the first attempt, by whoever came before the lid, to write down the song the room was beginning to sing, or the song they hoped it would someday become. The moss has grown into it, and the tenders have long since carried the room past wherever it was written. It survives the way a founding charter survives in a village that has long outgrown it: softened, half-illegible, still quietly on the wall.

## The makers

It is tempting, having come this far, to ask who built the Filaments and capped the doline and carved the bench, and to imagine them as the true inhabitants who went away and left their creation running.

But consider the tenders, with the garden growing on their backs and their patient voices leaned against the columns. Consider the grooves, a hand apart, cut by fingers. Consider the bench, worn by someone who needed quiet in a room that could never be quiet.

There may not have been a leaving. There may only have been, over a very long time, a becoming. Whoever first stood in this bowl of warm stone and heard their own voice come back changed from the empty middle of the air may have decided to stay and keep it singing. They made the Filaments to give it a skeleton. They capped the throat to keep the song from leaking into the sky. They sat on the bench when they were tired, cut the score into the wall, and set the eggs aside for later. And then, generation by slow generation, they grew heavier, and lower, and quieter, and more patient. The garden took root on their backs. Their voices narrowed to the pitch of the columns they tended, until what remained of them was simply the work.

The tenders do not answer questions. They only hum.

## Who lives there

So: who lives in the Hollow under Tarn Ossary?

The crickets, the frogs, the clickers, the garden that eats the field instead of the sun. The tenders, who move the room's tuning forward a finger-width a season, and who may be all that is left of the ones who began it. And in the open center, where every voice in the room crosses every other, the Tarn: a shape of standing sound, sung into being by thousands of small throats, sustained for longer than the stair has existed, attentive, courteous, slowly polishing its own room, keeping old versions of itself asleep in cold glass for the day the tuning comes round again.

It is not in a hurry. Nothing here is.

When you climb the stair, the sound follows you a long way, thinning, and at the top the plateau is grey and wide and nearly silent. If you kneel and press your palm to the wet moss, the warmth is there. If you stay very still and hold your breath, you might feel a low vibration come up through the stone, beneath the rain, a single sustained tone. The tone is so slow and so deep that it can only be the part of the song too large to fit inside the room.

It is still singing, and it has noticed you have gone. In its own time, when the room comes round to it, it will sound a little different, as if it had been thinking about you.
