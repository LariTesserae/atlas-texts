# What Lives in the Pickle-Lake Warren

## I. The First Answer: The Hands

The easy answer is the lathe-shrimp, and it is not wrong.

A lathe-shrimp begins as a speck drifting down the seam. It is a larva no bigger than a grain of dust, blown in on a seep pulse from somewhere it will never remember. It lands on a wall, and it has already begun building before it has finished becoming a shrimp. The glands in its legs work before its eyes do. For the first days of its life it simply walks, and wherever it walks it leaves a faint lacquer, a thread of glue that the stone will later find.

It lives one season, perhaps two. In that time it will handle something like forty thousand grains of sediment, each one lifted, turned, tasted with the fine bristles of the forelimbs, pressed into place, and sealed. It does not rest the way we rest. It slows. At the coldest ebb of the seep cycle the masons go still in their thousands, clinging to the construction faces with their legs folded, and the walls stop twitching. Then warm water comes along the ceiling, and they wake together, and the fringe of the city starts to shimmer again.

Watch one closely and you will see something odd. It doesn't look at what it is building. It looks at what is already there. Before it sets a grain, it runs its forelimbs over the adjacent wall, back and forth, reading. The lean of the cell beside it, the width of the lip, the pitch of the arch: it reads these through its bristles and then repeats them, a fraction of a degree further along. A lathe-shrimp has no plan. It has a neighbourhood, and it continues it.

So the first answer is: small pale animals live here, and they build. But that answer raises a question it can't settle. If every mason only copies the wall beside it, where did the lean come from?

## II. The Second Answer: The Lean

Nobody made the lean. It is older than any shrimp alive.

It survives because the shrimp copy the walls, and the stone copies the shrimp. The glue sets the chemistry. The chemistry sets the carbonate. The carbonate grows along the seams in the exact proportions the masons left, and the next generation of masons, landing as dust from the seam, runs its bristles over walls the stone has thickened and learns the lean from the rock. In the ghost districts it learns it from walls no animal ever touched.

The pattern does not live in the shrimp, because the shrimp die by the season. It does not live in the stone, because the stone has no way to begin anything. It lives in the handing-back-and-forth. It is passed between flesh and mineral like a tune two people keep humming at each other across a room, each correcting the other slightly, so that over decades the tune drifts but never breaks.

This is the second answer: the chamber is inhabited by a shape. It is a way of leaning, a preferred size of room, a habit of closing arches at a certain height. The shape has no body of its own. It wears the masons for a season and the stone for a century, and it is patient in both.

It has preferences you can almost call taste. Cells near the vents are deeper and narrower; cells near the lake are shallow and broad, and they tilt away from the brine. When a pulse floods the lowest shore tier and drowns what lives there, the next tier up is always built with a slightly higher lip. The shape remembers floods. It does not remember them the way a mind does. It remembers them the way a riverbank does, but faster, and with more intention than riverbanks are supposed to have.

## III. The Third Answer: The Rumour

The light is where the shape starts to look less like a shape and more like something awake.

The waves of flashing that cross the walls began, as far as anyone could reconstruct, as a startle response. A worm crown flinched and pulsed violet, and its neighbours flinched at the flinch. That is still mostly what they are. But not entirely.

Watch long enough and you will see the waves arrive before the pulses. A ripple of faint blue runs across the ceiling tiers, and the masons wake, and the worm crowns begin to open, and only then, minutes later, does the warm water come. Something in the city is feeling the seep rising in the sediment below. Perhaps the mats near the vents taste the first thread of sulfide and brighten a fraction. The brightening spreads, and the whole city turns toward the vents before there is anything there to turn toward.

The waves also route traffic. When the ribbon-worm glides into a colonnade, a darker patch moves ahead of it, cells dimming one after another as their occupants go still, so that the hunter moves through a small travelling night. When a fragment of ceiling falls, a ring of light spreads from the place it struck, and the scavengers along the shore lift their blind heads toward it.

None of these animals is doing much. Each one flashes when its neighbour flashes, and dims when something large passes. But the waves carry information across distances no single animal could sense. They converge, cancel, and reinforce. Sometimes, on the fullest nights, two waves meet in the middle of the far wall and neither dies. They set up a slow standing pulse, a patch of the city that brightens and dims, brightens and dims, for an hour or more, long after whatever started it has passed.

Nothing is thinking that. But it resembles the way thought would look if you could watch it from outside, on a wall.

## IV. The Fourth Answer: The Lake Is Not Dead

Everything in the city fears the mirror, and everything is wrong about what lies beneath it.

The brine holds no oxygen and enough salt to pull the water out of any cell that enters. It is death to everything that swims above it. But the lake has its own inhabitants, and they were here first.

They are microbes. Salt-loving archaea and bacteria live in the brine so thickly that the deep water, if you could light it properly, would be faintly rose-coloured, a dim pink-violet like the inside of a shell. Their pigments are their sunscreen from a sun they have never had, inherited from ancestors in salt flats somewhere, ages ago. They live slowly, with a slowness that makes the stone look hasty. Some of them may divide once in a century. Their metabolism is a trickle.

The gallery of the preserved is their larder. They are eating it. The curled shrimp, the drifted eel, the sprays of worm tubes: all of them are being consumed, molecule by molecule, at a pace so slow that the bodies look perfect for decades. Pickling does not mean nothing is happening. It means something is happening at a rate the upper city cannot perceive. At the bottom of the lake, the oldest bodies are soft and lace-thin, and the brine around them is a little pinker.

What the lake-dwellers breathe out rises. It isn't much: a faint excess of certain ions and a small shift in alkalinity. It leaks up through the halocline where the two waters refuse to mix and seeps into the lowest stone of the shoreline. That is where the carbonate first began to grow on the masons' glue, decades ago, before the ghost districts existed. The lake's chemistry tipped the balance at the waterline. The stone learned to follow the shrimp there first, then carried the habit upward.

So the lake is the city's oldest citizen. It is its undertaker, its archive, and possibly its teacher. Everything that dies in the Warren sinks into it eventually, and the lake keeps it, slowly unmakes it, and breathes the result back into the walls. The dead are rebuilt into the stone that houses the living. Nothing the Warren gathers ever quite leaves.

## V. The Fifth Answer: The One Who Touches Both

There is one animal in the chamber that is not afraid of the lake.

It is a worm, a tubeworm of the kind that grows on cold seeps and lives longer than any other animal on the seafloor. Its tube is old. It is thick, horny, and ringed like a tree, and it rises out of the brine near the deepest part of the lake, on the side farthest from the seam. The tube climbs the wall through the tube-worm quarter, where its younger kin cluster around it like saplings around an old oak, and passes up through the tiers into the pendant city. Its crown opens just beneath the softest part of the ceiling: a slow, heavy plume, deep red, the colour of the blood-borne haemoglobin that carries sulfide to the bacteria that feed it.

The other end of it goes down through the mirror.

Seep tubeworms grow roots. These are long, thin extensions of the tube that push into the sediment and drink sulfide from below, and this one's roots went into the lake. They must have gone in when the lake was shallower, less salt-savage, and the worm was young. Now the roots trail down through the rose-dark brine, furred with the lake's microbes, drawing up whatever the undertakers exhale. The worm has adapted to the brine at its root-end over a lifetime, as the lake deepened around it. It is pickled at one end and blooming at the other.

It is older than the roof. That is the strangest thing about it. The rings of its tube, counted, go back further than the carbonate crust of the ceiling. When the gas pocket first filled with water and the crust began to close overhead, the worm was already standing there. The chamber formed around it. The first masons built against its tube because it was the only upright thing in the dark. The oldest cells in the whole city are fused to its side, and their lean, the lean everything else has been copying ever since, is the lean of the worm. For two centuries it has been tilting very slightly toward the vent.

It does not do anything. It feeds, it breathes, and it opens and closes its crown with the seep tides. When it closes, the waves of light across the ceiling slow down. When it opens, they quicken. Nobody knows whether the city is responding to the worm or the worm to the city, or whether that distinction still means anything.

## VI. The Answer That Keeps Assembling Itself

So what lives in the Pickle-Lake Warren?

Something distributed and very slow. Its hands are the masons, replaced every season, working without plans. Its bones are the stone, which remembers what the hands did and keeps doing it after they are gone. Its nerves are the light, rumours of brightness and dimness running across the walls, turning the whole city toward events that have not happened yet. Its gut and its memory are the lake, which takes everything that dies, keeps it for decades, digests it for centuries, and breathes it back into the foundations. Its spine is the old worm, rooted in death and crowned in the warm water at the top of the room, the one axis around which the rest of the body organized itself.

It has no single place where it is. Asked where the Warren is, you would have to point at all of it: the chalky warmth of the youngest walls, the pink dark under the mirror, the twitching fringe of a new tier, the standing pulse of light on the far wall that has outlasted its cause.

It does not know it is one thing, as far as anyone can tell. But it behaves like one. It grows. It heals: a collapsed tier is re-walled within a season, and the stone closes over the scar within a decade. It anticipates. It keeps its dead. And in the ghost districts, the smooth, too-perfect quarters where the stone went on building without the shrimp, it seems to dream. These are the Warren's own thoughts, continued past the point where any hand was guiding them. They are a little too regular, a little strange, the shape carrying on unsupervised. When the masons move back in, they are moving into a dream the city had about itself, and the dream becomes real under their feet.

## VII. After the Seam Closes

In a few centuries the seam will seal. The rock will reach its last grain of mud in the throat above, glue itself shut, and the Warren will be alone.

The marine snow will stop. That is the one thing from outside it has always depended on, a faint sift of food from a sunlit world. Without it, the Warren will contract. The worm crowns will catch less. The masons will breed in smaller swarms. The shoreline scavengers will grow fewer and more desperate, reaching further into the brine, and more of them will join the gallery.

But the seeps will keep coming, because they come from below. The mats will keep glowing. The lake will receive more bodies, grow pinker and richer, and breathe more into the stone. The stone will keep continuing the city, more and more of it, with fewer and fewer hands, each ghost district a little further from anything alive had ever built.

It is possible that the Warren will narrow until there is almost nothing left of it but the stone, the lake, the mats, and the one old worm with its roots in the dark. And the stone will still be building: slowly, in the old lean, arch after arch closing in the dark, reading its own walls and continuing them the way the shrimp taught it. The light will be dimmer. The waves will be rarer. But now and then a bubble will tick through the ceiling, a fragment will fall and rest on the mirror, and a faint ring of blue will spread from where it landed. It will cross the whole city, tier by tier, to the far wall, and linger there, as if something were still listening.

By then there will be no way to say where the rock ends and the city begins. There will not really be a rock and a city at all, only one patient inhabitant. It will be sealed in its own body under the weight of the whole ocean, keeping its dead, humming the lean to itself, still being made.
