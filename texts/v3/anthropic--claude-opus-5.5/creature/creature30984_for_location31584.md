# Who Lives on the Tableland of Slow Engines

## First Look

At first you would say almost nothing lives here, and you would be almost right. On the Tableland, *almost* is where everything important happens.

You would name the kilnmoths on the lamps and the tenders on the pipes and stop there, because those are the only things you can watch move. But the dust holds more footprints than two kinds of creature could make. Some of them are fresh, and some of them are still being made.

So begin with the small ones, and let the rest come up slowly, the way the glow of the rock seams comes up once your eyes have lived an hour in the dark.

---

## The Kilnmoths

Look closely at a kilnmoth and it is less an insect than a leaf of stone that has learned to want warmth.

Its body is a pale segmented thread no longer than a fingernail, jointed like the smallest possible chain. Its wings are two pairs of mica-thin plates, translucent at the center and milky toward the edges. At the root of each wing is a hinge so fine that the builders, who knew hinges, would have envied it. The wings have no scales and no powder of their own. The powder a moth leaves in the dust is the Tableland's dust, gathered on the underside of its wings while it rests on the ground and released when it lands again. Each moth carries a little of the last place it touched to the next one. Over their long lives they are shifting the plateau one print at a time.

They do not eat, as far as anyone can tell. They press themselves to the lamp glass and take in its faint heat through the flat of the body, the way a lizard takes the sun. A kilnmoth on a lamp is doing the only thing the Tableland allows: standing on a small difference and drinking it.

When a lamp's warmth has been shared among too many bodies, one of them lets go. The unfolding of the wings takes most of an hour. The drift that follows is not flight so much as a controlled fall into the thick, still air, a few paces through the dark toward the next faint warmth. Sometimes it misses and settles on bare stone, and stays there, and cools. It does not die the way things die elsewhere. It lowers slowly toward the temperature of everything, and somewhere along that slope it stops being a moth and becomes a small grey shape shaped like a moth. Given long enough it becomes dust, and some later moth carries that dust on its wings to a lamp.

Nobody has seen them breed. At the base of the oldest lamp-posts, the dust is not merely stippled with landing-prints but layered with them, print over print, thousands deep, until the ground has a fine grain like the inside of a shell. The Brief (we will come to them) say the moths are laid there, in that grain: that a landing-print is not only a record but an egg, and that a new moth is simply a print that has been warm enough for long enough to stand up. It may not be true. On the Tableland it sounds reasonable.

## The Tenders

The tenders are larger, warmer, and wetter than anything else on the plateau. Of all the lives here, theirs is the one that seems to be doing a job.

A tender is built low, long in the body, with six limbs that fold beneath it like the legs of a trestle. Its shell is not one piece but a series of fired plates overlapping along the back, each ridged across its width. The ridges are so like brickwork that, lying against a wall, a tender disappears into it. The underside is softer, a dense dark hide that stays tacky. When a tender presses itself full-length against a warm pipe, that hide seals to the iron, and the animal becomes, for a while, a part of the pipework.

Its head is small and nearly blind, a blunt wedge with two shallow pits where eyes might be, and a mouth that is more a valve than a jaw. That mouth is where the oil comes from. The tenders sweat it, or secrete it, or distill it from some slow chemistry of their own. It gathers in the mouth, and when a tender reaches a bearing, a joint, or a pivot on a governor's arm, it lays its mouth against the metal and leaves a smear. The smear is thin, dark, and glistening. It is the only thing on the Tableland that ever looks new.

This is the tending. It is real. Without it the engines would long since have ground their bearings dry and seized in the middle of their strokes, and then the dynamos would stop, and then the lamps, and then the moths would fall from the cooling glass. The tenders stand at the bottom of a whole tower of almost-stopped things and keep it from being quite stopped.

They do it slowly. A tender's circuit, from one works to the next along the straight rails, down the long halls, joint by joint across a single engine, and on, takes something like three hundred years. The Brief have measured it by the prints. The tender finds its own old track in the dust, sets its feet into its own old footprints, and follows itself. The lines between the works are not a hundred journeys laid side by side but one journey walked a hundred times, the prints deepening a hair each time, until along the oldest routes a shallow groove runs through the dust, a road made of one animal remembering.

Tenders breathe perhaps once a day, if there were days. The breath is warm and wet, and it is the Tableland's entire hydrology. When a tender exhales, a little moisture leaves its body and hangs in the still air. With no wind to carry it off, it settles on whatever is nearest: a pipe, the glass of a lamp, the dust. In the dust it makes a darker spot, a single drop's worth of damp, which dries again over some decades and leaves the dust faintly crusted. Along the tender roads the ground is crusted all the way, a long line of breaths. It is the closest thing the Tableland has to a river.

Their breath matters to more than iron. We will come to that too.

## The Prints That Are Fresh

Walk the older squares, the ones near the eastern works where the spiral is, and you will see what the Tableland document describes: the builders' prints, layered and overwritten, and the lines of single travelers.

But look more closely and some of the prints are wrong for the Tableland. They are too close together. The stride is short and quick, a child's stride or a hurried adult's. The edges are loose where a heel dragged or a toe kicked a little dust aside. These are prints of something in a hurry, and nothing on the Tableland is in a hurry.

Follow them and you find the Brief.

---

## The Brief

They are people, or very near it. They are small, narrow-shouldered, and grey-skinned. They are always a little dusty, because there is no water to wash in, and a life on the Tableland wears the dust into the creases of the body like the mortar sunk between bricks. Their eyes are very large and very dark, with pupils that almost never close down. They have lived their whole lives by the light of filaments that illuminate only themselves, and they can see a rock seam's ember-glow from a day's walk away.

They move quickly: as quickly as you do. On the Tableland that makes them the fastest large things for a hundred leagues, darting between the lamps like sparks thrown off an engine that otherwise does not spark.

They live in the edges of the works: in the pump rooms, in the foremen's offices at the ends of the long halls, in the hollow bases of the chimneys where the stone is warmest. They sleep in heaps, curled against the pipework the way tenders do, and they share the warmth among their bodies by shifting constantly in their sleep. You can hear a heap of sleeping Brief only if you lie among them, because sound does not travel. Up close it is a soft continuous murmur, like a hall full of speaking tubes.

**They speak mouth to ear.** A Brief who wants to say something to another comes close enough to touch, lays a cheek alongside the other's cheek, and speaks into the ear in a low voice. Every conversation is intimate by necessity, and so there are almost no conversations among more than two. A group of Brief deciding something stands in a ring, each with a mouth at the next one's ear, and the discussion goes around the circle and comes back changed. They have no word for shouting. The closest word they have means "to speak into a tube."

**They walk knowing their walking is permanent.** Every child learns this before it learns its own name. You will leave a line, and the line will outlast you, and it will outlast the city. So the Brief walk carefully, and walking becomes a kind of writing. They avoid crossing their own old paths when they can, because crossing oneself is a sign of confusion. A path that loops back on itself is the Brief word for grief. When two Brief love each other, they will sometimes walk out across an open square side by side, matching their strides, to leave a double line that says so for ten thousand years. When one of a pair dies, the other walks their shared line again alone, placing a foot in every one of the lost one's prints. It takes days. This is a funeral.

The document mentions two sets of prints that walk side by side, stop, turn, and go back the way they came. The Brief know that place. They call it *the Deciding*. The prints are old, older than any Brief, but not so old as the builders, and nobody knows what was decided. Young Brief go there in pairs, stand in the turned-back prints, and talk cheek to cheek about whatever they themselves are deciding. Then each pair walks on in a new direction, so that the Deciding is ringed now by a hundred lines fanning outward from it, like the rays of a drawn star.

**They are readers.** This is what their lives are for, as far as they will say. The Tableland keeps everything, and the Brief believe that everything kept should be read. They go out along the rails in twos and threes, crouch over the old squares with their faces nearly in the dust, and read. They read the builders' prints under the later skins of dust. They read the moth-stipple around the lamps and the tender-grooves between the works. They trace each line to its source. They have a memory-craft for this, long chants spoken ear to ear and passed down, that record whose line goes where. The chants are called the Ledger, and a Brief who can recite a large piece of it is honored the way an elder is honored elsewhere.

The Ledger is where the Brief learned what the document above only half-says. They learned it the way you learn anything on the Tableland: by following a line of prints to its end.

## What Is at the End of the Lines

The oldest lines, the builders' own, do not end at the edge of the plateau, and they do not end at graves. They go into the halls.

Go in with them. The door is a slab of thinned iron, pivoted on hinges a tender has kept slick. Past it, the long hall stretches away under frosted windows, its engine a dark mass barely picked out by one or two filament-embers high on the walls. There is the flywheel, taller than a house. There are the pistons like felled columns. There are the governors with their arms raised mid-swing.

There are also the people.

They stand at the engine. One is at the foot of the flywheel with both hands on a spoke, as if caught in the act of helping it round. One is on the gantry above with a hand on a valve wheel. One sits at a desk at the end of the hall with a pen touching paper. One leans in a doorway with her mouth to the brass bell of a speaking tube. One is on a ladder with one foot lifted toward the next rung.

At first you take them for statues. They are the grey of the dust, and they are worn the way the city is worn: noses softened, fingers rounded, the folds of their clothing sanded into gentle ridges. The pen is thinned to a wire. The paper is frosted and translucent like the windows. They look as though they were sculpted a very long time ago by someone who wanted to remember how work looked.

But they are warm. Lay a hand on one, as the Brief do, carefully, reverently, with permission asked in a whisper to the ear, and it is warm exactly the way the ground is warm: like a sleeping animal. Put your ear to the chest, and under the long low note of the engine, which is in everything, there is a second, slower pressure: a heart, in the middle of a single beat.

**These are the Held.** They are the builders, and they are not dead. They are not stopped. They did what the engines did.

Every living thing is a heat engine too. That is what a body is: a small, fierce difference between the warmth inside and the world outside, burning a little fuel to keep that difference going. When the builders came to the Tableland, they were fast and hot and loud, and they built fast, hot, loud engines to drink the heat of the dying mountain. Then, over long ages, the gradients ran down: between the rock and the air, and between their bodies and the air. The engines slowed toward stillness, and so did the builders. They settled into the same last narrow margin as their machines, and they are holding there still.

The man on the ladder is climbing. The Brief have measured him. His lifted foot has moved the width of a thread since the Ledger began. He will reach the next rung. It will take about as long as the city has left before it wears down into a knuckle under skin, and the Brief like to think the timing is not a coincidence.

The woman at the desk is writing a letter. The Brief can read the frosted paper if they hold an ember up behind it. She has written most of a sentence, and she is in the middle of a word.

## The Speaking Tubes

This is the part of the Ledger the Brief recite most carefully.

The halls are full of speaking tubes: iron pipes that run from room to room and hall to hall, and some that run beneath the rails for leagues between works. They were how the builders spoke across distance on a plateau that swallows the voice. They still carry voices.

The woman in the doorway with her mouth to the brass bell is speaking. She began her sentence before the lamps dimmed to embers. Her breath goes into the tube one fraction of a syllable at a time and travels down the iron, and the iron holds it as it holds the engine's note. Nothing up here fades.

Six halls away, at the other end of that tube, a man sits with his ear pressed to its bell. He is Held too. His face is the patient face of someone listening very hard. He is hearing her. The Brief have laid their own ears to his tube, between his ear and the brass, and heard the word she is sending: long, stretched past recognition, a single vowel that has been sounding for centuries. It is the middle of a word the Brief have argued over for generations, because they cannot tell what comes after it. Some chants in the Ledger say it is a name. Some say it is *yes*.

There are hundreds of these. All over the city the Held are in conversation, mouth to tube or mouth to ear, their sentences crossing the plateau through the iron as slowly as the flywheels turn. Every talk in the city is one long sentence still in progress. The Brief move quickly among them like children at the feet of grown people who are speaking above their heads.
