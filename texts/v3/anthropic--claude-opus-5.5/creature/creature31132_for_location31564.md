# Who Lives in Ossary

## I. The People on the Stairs

Begin with a woman at a table.

The table tilts slightly toward the drain in the corner of the room, as all tables here do. Her cup sits in a ring carved into the wood, worn smooth by a century of cups. She is eating snails, which Ossarans eat the way other people eat bread. They are the pale translucent grazers from the lower walls, boiled in their shells and pried out with a bone pick, tasting faintly of iron and the bitter weep of the stone. Beside the bowl is a square of midge-cake, grey-brown and dense, pressed from chitin flour and rendered fat. The bread came up from the lowlands on the cable-lift. The wheat it was baked from grew in fields fed by the brown stone mined beneath her feet. Nothing that feeds her grew here, and everything that feeds her began here.

She cannot hear the swifts.

No one born in Ossary can. For their first weeks, infants here cry almost without stopping. Midwives consider this normal and have a word for it that means something like *the first argument*. Then, over a month or so, the crying tapers off. The child has not grown content. The top edge of its hearing has worn away, the way the corners of the buildings wear away under the snails. The screaming that drives visitors half-mad within a week becomes, for an Ossaran, the texture of silence. When an Ossaran travels down the escarpment and out into the world, the quiet of other cities feels like a held breath, and they sleep badly.

What they hear instead is low. Their language lives in the chest. Ossaran voices are deep and resonant, pitched well under the shriek, and the language keeps time with the Fanworks. Its natural rhythm is about forty beats to the minute, and Ossaran poetry scans to it. A lullaby sung in the Guttering and a work-chant in the Kiln Heights share the same slow thud, because every singer in the city has felt it through the soles of their feet since before they were born.

The grammar, too, has been shaped by the city. Ossaran sentences are built backward by the standards of other tongues. The verb comes first, then the object, then the consequence, and only at the very end the subject, the qualifications, the courtesies. *Falling, the child, catch her, you there.* Children learn this before they learn to count, because twice a day the Turning comes and the world goes deaf. If a sentence is cut off, it must already have said what mattered. Lowlanders find Ossarans abrupt. Ossarans find lowlanders dangerous. They bury the point of a sentence where the wing-roar could take it.

Look at the woman's hands. By twenty, no Ossaran has fingerprints. The bitter water that runs down every wall, stair, and railing has smoothed them away. Their fingertips are soft and polished, like soap that has been used for a long time. Their grip comes from calluses instead, thick pads at the base of the fingers and across the palms, built from a lifetime of hauling themselves up wet railings. Contracts here are not signed with a thumb. They are sealed with a bead of nest-glue pressed into a fold of chitin paper, and each family has its own mark, a pattern of ridges carved into a small bone stamp. The stamp is inherited and never sold. When a family dies out, its stamp is laid with the last of them.

They dress for a world that is always dripping and always falling. Ossarans wear oiled grey wool, and their hoods have stiffened brims with a channel sewn down the back, so the constant drip from above runs off behind them. Midges collect in the folds of their clothes. Ossarans shake themselves out at thresholds the way dogs do, without thinking, and every doorway has a little grey drift of insect-fall swept against it.

They lean when they walk, because nothing is flat and every street is a ramp or a stair angled toward some drain. Visitors say you can recognize an Ossaran in any port in the world by their posture: tilted slightly forward, as though walking into a wind that isn't there, weight on the balls of the feet, one hand drifting toward the nearest wall.

That hand is the thing to notice.

## II. The Turning

At dusk, the woman stands up from her table and goes out onto the stair.

The fans are gearing down. The pulse under her feet slows, and the city's heartbeat drops from forty to thirty to something like sleep. Along the stair, and on every stair in every ward, people are stepping to the side. They set down whatever they are carrying and turn their backs to the stone.

Then they press themselves against it: shoulder blades, spine, the backs of the skull and the palms, flat to the weeping wall. The water runs down behind them and they let it. For twenty minutes, while the swifts come home, the population of Ossary clings to the vertical faces of its own city and does not move.

It is done for safety, of course. The flocks at the Turning are dense enough to knock a person off a stair. But watch it from above, from a gatherer's perch high in a chimney mouth, and it looks like something else. Every wall in the city is covered with small clinging bodies, pressed to the rough brick, waiting. Some of them are birds and some of them are people. From high enough up, you could not easily say which were which.

For twenty minutes twice a day, everyone in Ossary is a swift.

## III. The Gatherers

The people who live closest to the birds are the ones who climb inside the chimneys.

There are not many nest-gatherers, a few thousand at most across the whole tableland, and they are a people within the people. They marry among themselves, mostly. Their children begin climbing at seven, on the short practice flues of the Gatherers' ward, and by fifteen they are working the great roosts. They go up the iron rungs in near-darkness after the breeding season, when the empty nests hang in their millions on the inner walls like pale cups of frosted glass.

They do not bring light. Light panics the birds that stay behind: the late fledglings, the sick, the old. So gatherers learn the chimneys by touch. Over years, their fingerless-printed hands learn the brick of each flue the way a musician's hands learn an instrument. They know where the rungs are loose and where the mortar has softened. They know where last year's nest was built on top of the year before, and on top of the year before that, until the nests form a crust a hand's width thick that has to be cut away in sheets. Their eyes adapt as well. Gatherers who have worked thirty years see in the flues the way cats do, and they squint in daylight, and they are almost all a little blind by fifty.

They smell of the nests. It is a faint, sweetish, animal smell, like warm hair or the inside of a glove, and it never washes off. The birds seem to know it. A gatherer can climb through a chimney full of roosting swifts and the swifts will shift and murmur against her and not panic. A stranger in the same flue would bring the whole column down on him in a screaming cataract.

Gatherers say they know the birds. They do not mean this the way a shepherd knows sheep. They mean that the old birds in the deep roosts, the ones that have come back to the same brick for fifteen or twenty seasons, are individuals, and that some of them know the gatherers in return. A gatherer will tell you, if you have earned it, about the bird that brushed her face with its wing every season for eleven years and then one year did not come back. She will tell you she grieved for it the way you grieve for an aunt.

They will also tell you, more quietly, about the roads.

## IV. The Other City

It takes a lifetime of looking up to see it.

Most Ossarans never do. They live on the stairs, and the haze of birds overhead is weather to them, the sieving shade that crosses a district and moves on. But the gatherers spend their working lives at the mouths of chimneys, hundreds of feet up, in the layer where the flocks actually live. Up there, they say, the air is not open. It is built.

The swifts do not scatter. They move along paths. There are currents in the Column that the flocks ride the way carts ride rails, and those currents are shaped by the Throats and the towers and the heat from the kilns. Over centuries the flocks have learned them so thoroughly that the same shapes form in the same places every evening. There is a great slow spiral over the Kiln Heights that gatherers call the Stair. There is a fold over the Guttering where the flocks bunch and turn back on themselves, called the Gate, and a long river of birds along the western rim called the Wall. Over the central wards, at the highest part of the haze, is a place where the flocks thin into something almost like a clearing. The birds circle its edges but rarely cross it, and it has no name that gatherers will say aloud.

It is a city, and it has wards, just as the stone city does. The young birds keep to the fast outer currents on the eastern rim, where the midges are thickest and the air is roughest. The breeding pairs hold the middle districts. The old birds, the heavy ones from the deep roosts of the Cup, fly lower and slower than the others, and they fly through the center.

It has traffic laws of a kind. Flocks yield to one another at the crossings. At dawn the lift-off follows an order: the Kiln chimneys first, then the Fanwork stacks, then the harbor wards, then the Cup last of all. The order has been the same for as long as anyone has recorded it, though no one in the stone city ever set it.

And it has a shape that matches the one below. Gatherers who have mapped the air roads, and a few have tried, on sheets of chitin paper handed down through families, find that the city above does not simply follow the city below. It anticipates it. Some air roads run over districts where there are no towers yet. In a generation or two, the stone city builds towers there. Ossarans always have reasons. A new kiln is needed, or a ward has grown, or the ground there is sound. But the roads were there first. The birds were already turning over empty ground where chimneys would one day stand, as though they were flying around something that had not been built yet.

## V. The Body

Step back far enough and the question of who lives in Ossary gets harder.

The Throats breathe. They pull air in along the eastern rim, and it travels through the city in great ducts and comes out warm and wet and exhausted over the western edge. The Fanworks beat at forty to the minute, and people who have been in both say it is the resting heartbeat of something much larger than a person. The walls sweat. The bacterial films spread across the stone in tidal bands of rose and violet and bruise, living, feeding, growing thick as skin, and peeling in sheets as skin does. The snails graze that skin and keep it from growing over the city's eyes. The beetles take the waste down into the beds. The rats take the dead. The spiders hang their silver nets across the mouths of the ducts, and in the right light those webs look very much like the fine membranes in a lung.

Midges are the food. They pour up the Column every warm afternoon, and the city swallows them. Every creature in it is a way of breaking that food down into smaller and smaller pieces, until it is stone.

The people are part of this. They are the parts of the body that do the fine work. Ossarans set the rough brick for the birds to cling to. They repair the fans and clear the ducts, render the dead with ceremony, and mine the old beds carefully from below, only when the birds are away. They carry the city's excess down the escarpment and out into the world, where it becomes wheat, and the wheat comes back up as bread. Ossarans think of themselves as the city's owners, or its builders, or its tenants. They are more like its hands.

When they die, they go back into it.

There is no soil in Ossary, and no flat ground for graves. Ossarans do not burn their dead, because the kilns are for guano and lime and it would be an obscenity. They do not lower them down the escarpment, which is where exiles go. They lay the dead in the roosts. At the base of every great chimney there is a chamber below the working face of the bed. The body is wrapped in oiled wool, the family stamp is pressed into a disc of nest-glue on the chest, and the body is placed there in the dark, beneath the birds. Then the rain begins, the constant soft rain of droppings from a million swifts above, and within a season the body is covered. Within a lifetime it is an inch down. Within a century it is stone.

The miners find them. Of course they do. Cutting the brown stone from below, they come on the wrapped shapes of the dead from three hundred years ago, compressed, mineralized, still holding their stamps. The law for this is older than the law about swifts. The miners stop and cut around the body, and they carry it up in its block of stone. Then they lay it again in the newest roost at the top of the city, under the youngest birds, and the rain begins again.

So the dead of Ossary climb. Every few centuries they are lifted and laid down higher, while the city sinks around them. The histories are dated by the beds, the Year of the Thin Bed and the Deep Century, and this is not a metaphor. A bed is a layer of years, and a layer of years is a layer of the dead. Ossary sinks into its own foundations at about an inch per lifetime. Ossarans say it the other way: a lifetime is an inch. Everyone who has ever lived here is in the stone somewhere, and the stone is always, slowly, being dug up and carried back to the top.

The city's name has never been mysterious to the people who live in it.

## VI. The Cup

Everything in Ossary slopes toward drains, and the city as a whole slopes toward the Cup.

It sits in the oldest part of the tableland, in a shallow basin that it has made by sinking. The Cup is always dim and always wet, and its films are so old and thick they are nearly black. The corners of its buildings have been grazed by snails for so long that they are not corners anymore. The Cup's towers are soft, rounded, and swollen at the base, like the stumps of very old trees, like things that have been licked. Carved inscriptions survive only near the tops of its walls, and they are in a script no one reads.

The chimneys of the Cup never empty.

Everywhere else, the swifts leave for the dry season. They go somewhere far to the south that no Ossaran has ever found, and the roosts fall quiet, and the miners and gatherers go to work. In the Cup's oldest flues, a few birds stay. They are the large ones, twice the size of wild swifts, with nests like washbasins. They cling to the inner walls through the whole of the empty season and do not fly. The gatherers have seen them there, dark shapes in the dark, heavy, breathing slowly, pressed to the brick with their long wings folded down their backs like cloaks.

Because the roost is never empty, by law it can never be mined. The bed beneath the Cup's first chimney has never been dug. No one knows how deep it goes. It is the Deep Century and every century before it, a single unbroken column of brown stone going down into the tableland, and everyone laid there in the first ages of the city is still in it. They have never been lifted. The Cup is sinking faster than the rest of Ossary, and the sinking has a center. Some Ossarans believe it is the weight of that bed pressing the city down into the rock, the weight of all of it: every bird, every body, every year.

The gatherers of the Cup are the strangest of all the gatherers, and the least talkative. They are the only ones who work the flues in the empty season, when the great birds are there alone. They say little about it. They say the birds are warm. They say that in the dry season the Cup's chimneys smell less of guano than of something like old paper, or a library, or a well-kept tomb.

And they say the old birds sing.

## VII. The Descending Voice

Ossarans cannot hear swifts. The scream sits at the top edge of hearing, and the city wore that edge away from them in the first month of their lives.

But the old birds are deeper. The records say half a tone deeper than their wild cousins, and the records are old. The birds of the Cup are deeper still. Gatherers who have spent the empty season in the Cup's flues say that there, for the first time in their lives, they have heard a swift. They mean heard it as a voice, not felt it as pressure or the scrape of a wing. It is not a scream. It comes down low and slow, in the chest-register where Ossarans live and speak and sing.

They say it keeps time with the fans.

They say, more hesitantly, that it has a shape. It has phrases, repetitions, and falls. Some of them say, though never to lowlanders and rarely to anyone, that the phrases front-load their meaning, the way their own sentences do. The part that matters comes first, before anything can interrupt.
