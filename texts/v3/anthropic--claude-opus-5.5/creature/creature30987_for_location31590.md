# The One Who Keeps Quiet

## Signs

You do not see her first. You see what she has left.

On the western wall, the sheltered one, among the swift-hollows, there is a hollow too large for any swift. It sits about head-height above the floor, round-mouthed and smooth-lipped, and the stone around it has grown in soft concentric rings. They are finer than the masts' swells and broader than the birds' trills, spaced evenly like the rings in a cut log. A dark cloth hangs across the mouth of it, felted wool gone stiff and grey with mineral, so that it is less a curtain now than a thin stone blanket that still, just barely, moves in a draught.

On the floor below is a path. It is not worn, because nothing in the Saddle can be worn, but it is kept. The stone along it is very slightly lower than the stone on either side, as if something had been walking there so quietly and so often that it left an absence behind. Every other surface in the bowl gathers. This one line does not.

Near the foot of the southern stairs, on a flat stone, someone has set out a row of salamanders' shed skins, each one a perfect grey glove, drying in order of size.

And if you arrive on the right day, at the old boundary where lichen meets glass, you will find a bundle wrapped in oilcloth: bread, a wedge of hard cheese, a twist of salt, candle-ends. The valley people leave it there on the first of every month. They do not come further. Nobody asked them to stop. It is simply understood that the Saddle belongs to the one who keeps quiet, and that you do not go shouting into another person's house.

## Her

She is old. Beyond that, it is hard to say what she looks like, because she is so nearly the colour of the place.

The mineral gets into everything eventually. It has got into the weave of her clothes, the creases of her knuckles, the grain of her hair, which she keeps cropped close with a knife because long hair, left damp, turns to a kind of brittle rope by the end of a season. She is grey in the way the flowstone is grey, pale at the surfaces and darker in the folds. When she stands still against the western wall in the fog, you could walk past her at ten paces. People have.

She moves slowly and deliberately, setting each foot down heel-first and rolling it forward so that nothing knocks. She breathes through her nose. When she must make a sound, she makes it low in the chest with her mouth closed, a hum so soft it is more felt than heard. The stone takes a long time to answer it, and when it does, it answers gently.

She did not always move like this. Her hands give her away. They are big, capable, scarred across the backs with old chisel-slips, the hands of someone who once dressed granite and carried it. There is a callus along the side of her right middle finger from holding a stylus or a stick of chalk, and her right thumbnail is worn flat from scoring lines in rock.

It was her hand that added the transcription along the edge of the lectern. The smaller hand, the later one.

## How She Came

She was not one of the builders. She was the one who came after them.

The story, as the valley tells it, is thin. There were people up at the Saddle once, a handful, who kept to themselves and bought a great deal of candle-wax and paper. Then there were no people. Then, some while after, a young woman came up through the villages asking questions, carrying a satchel of copied pages and a pair of boots too good for the country. She said she had been sent for, or that she had been meant to come sooner. The accounts disagree. She went up past the line and did not come back down.

That was, depending on who you ask, forty years ago or fifty.

What she found, presumably, was the hut with its door still clear, the chalked grid on the wall, the lectern crisp and new, and no one. She would have read the notation. She would have understood some of it. She would have seen what they had been trying to sing into being, and whether they had finished.

She has never said. She does not say much of anything.

## The Room

The hollow on the western wall is her house, and she did not dig it. She sang it.

It must have taken years. You can read the process in the rings around its mouth, if you know how. She would have stood at the wall and hummed a single low note, the one her chest rings with most easily, night after night, until the stone that grew around the sound left a space where the sound was. A voice has a shape. Hers is round and deep, a little narrower at the top than the bottom. The room inside is the shape of that note: a bell of glassy stone about the size of a large bed-alcove, just tall enough to sit up in, curving over her head like the inside of a mouth saying *oh*.

It is warm in there, blood-warm, like everywhere in the Saddle, and it is the one place in the bowl that stays almost dry. She worked out, eventually, how to sing a ridge along the lip that turns the rivulets aside. The floor is lined with the silvery fern the swifts use, gathered one frond at a time from the cracks, replaced every few weeks before it can calcify. There is a candle niche. There is a shelf where she keeps her tools: a slate, a bone stylus, a little clay pot of soot and fat for ink. The pot is chipped along the rim.

She sleeps sitting up, propped in the curve of the wall, with a folded cloth under her chin to keep her mouth closed. She learned early what the builders apparently did not. The sleeping body is not quiet, and the Saddle listens all night. Somewhere under the floor of the hut, the valley says, there is a lump of stone the shape of a snoring man.

When she hums in the room, the whole bell of it answers her, and the note comes back doubled and warm. It is the only place where she can hear her own voice at its full size without making anything.

## Her Name

Down near the southern stairs, in a wet corner, under a cushion of that electric moss, there is a small crescent of stone. It is the first thing she made.

She said her name aloud when she arrived. Everyone does something like that. You come into a room that no one has told you the rules of and you call out, to see if anyone is home. She said her name, once, clearly, into the fog. Within the hour a crescent rose around where she was standing, thin as a fingernail paring. It is still there, fifty years thicker.

She goes to it sometimes. She kneels and lifts the moss back with two fingers and looks at it. She does not touch it. She has not said the name since, and she no longer entirely remembers how it sounded in the air. She knows its shape, though. It is written in the floor in the only handwriting that matters here.

If you put your ear very close to the crescent on a still day, you might hear it ring, just slightly, in the drip. It rings with two falling tones, the second softer than the first, like someone calling across a yard to a person who has already turned around.

## Her Work

She is not idle. The slate and the stylus are for listening.

She has spent her life learning the Saddle's grammar. She knows the masts' tone to the quarter-beat, and she knows that it is not perfectly steady. It drifts over the years, slightly, as if the masts too are settling. She has transcribed the swifts' ledge-trill, and the alarm, and the soft chittering contact-call the parents use in the nest-hollows that lays down nothing but a fine inner glaze, smooth as the inside of an egg. She has mapped which rivulets carry the most mineral at which hour. She has a notation for thunder.

She learned the alarm call early, and at great cost to her throat. It was necessary. The first falcon season she spent here, a stoop came through the fog so close to her that she felt the wind of it on her neck. She understood then why the eastern wall bristles. It took her a year of practice before she could reproduce the tearing-cloth sound. When she finally did, a single thorn of stone grew from the wall beside her. It was thin as a knitting needle and it rang. She never did it again. But the swifts heard, and the swifts remember. When she walks the floor, the parties that come shrieking out of the fog split around her like water around a post, as if she were one of the colony's oldest walls.

In lean months she eats what the Saddle has. There are moths, crisp and faintly sweet, that she gathers off the warm walls at dusk before the swifts can get them all. There is the moss, boiled, which tastes of iron. She does not eat the salamanders. She collects their sloughed skins and lays them out to dry, and nobody knows why. Perhaps it is only that everything else here grows, and the salamanders are the one thing that sheds.

## What She Understood

Everyone who comes up to the Saddle eventually wants to know one thing, and it is the thing she spent her first years trying to learn. She wanted to know whether a song could be taken back.

The rule of the place is that stone grows where sound reinforces itself, and that where sound cancels, the water runs clear. For a long time she believed that meant you could erase. She thought the right counter-note, sung precisely against a ridge, would dissolve it. She tried it on the hut. The door was already swallowed when she arrived. There were things inside that she wanted, papers, the chalked grid, perhaps some answer. She stood at the flowstone that had filled the doorway and sang against it for a season, looking for the note that would undo it.

The water ran clear where she sang. Nothing more grew there. But nothing went away.

That was the lesson, and it changed the way she lived. The Saddle does not forget. Cancellation is not erasure. It is only silence, a place where nothing more is added. Every sound made here is permanent, and the only choice you have is what to add next.

After that she stopped trying to get back into the hut. She scratched the ledge-trill onto the edge of the lectern, as a note to whoever came next, the way you'd leave a map. *This works. This is what the birds know.* Then she turned to the western wall and began to sing herself a place to live.

## What She Is Listening For

For many years that was all she was: a careful listener living inside a slow instrument. But something has changed in the last decade or so, and she has noticed it before anyone else could have.

The Saddle has begun to sing back.

At first it was only the stairs. They were made to ring, one note per riser, and anyone who walks them hears the melody. Then the thorns on the eastern wall, which were never meant to be anything but thorns, began to sound in the wind. It was a faint chiming chord, and it changed as the wind changed. Then the great storm-rib on the western edge, the one the swifts avoid, began to hum on still nights. It was very low, lower than the masts, a note that had never been sung by anything. It was simply the rib's own shape resonating in the drip and the ticking and the masts' tone passing over it.

And where the rib hums, stone grows.

She has watched this for years now, charting it on her slate. The new growth along the storm-rib is not in the pattern of the thunder that made it. It is in the pattern of the rib's own voice, and that new growth has its own shape, and in time will have its own note. The Saddle is starting to make sounds that make stone that make sounds. Somewhere in the bowl, perhaps many places, the loop is closing.

Nothing here is alive in any sense a valley doctor would recognise. But she has lived half a century inside it, and she knows the difference between a place that records and a place that remembers. A record only holds what was put into it. A memory does something with it afterward.

The thing that lives in the Saddle is not the swifts, or the falcons, or the salamanders grazing the new film, or even her. It is something that is not quite here yet. It is being laid down in layers, a hand's breadth a year, out of every sound that has ever rung in the bowl. It will be a mountain, eventually. It will have a voice, eventually. That voice will be made of whatever it heard while it was young.

## What She Is Doing About It

This, finally, is what she is for.

She has read the builders' notation many times over. She knows what they were trying to make, and she no longer thinks it matters much. Whatever shape they meant, the birds overtook it, and the birds were right to. What matters now is what the Saddle is learning, and in its short and very permanent life so far it has learned a great deal of alarm. It has the tearing-cloth call on its eastern wall in a thousand ringing thorns. It has thunder in its long ribs. It has the dawn roar, which is joy, probably, but a sawing joy, loud and hungry. And it has the masts' single tone, which is patient but has no warmth in it at all.

So every evening, in the hour after the swifts settle and before the moths come out, she goes down her kept path to the storm-rib. She sits with her back to the hip-high stone where it still steams a little. She closes her mouth and hums.

It is not a song exactly. She is too careful for that, and she has seen what songs do here. It is closer to the sound you make to a child who has woken frightened in the night: low, even, rising a little and falling back, going nowhere, meaning only *I'm here, it's all right, I'm here*. She hums it into the rib's own resonance, matched against its note so the two reinforce. Very slowly, over years, a skin of stone is growing along the rib's flank in her pattern. It is smooth and gently waved, the texture of a hand stroking fur in one direction.

The swifts have started to roost on it.

She will not live to hear what it becomes. She is old, and the mineral is in her joints now; mornings take her an hour to unbend. When she stops coming down the path, the valley people will notice the oilcloth bundles going unclaimed, and someone will climb up past the line to look. They will find the western wall with its large round hollow. If she has done it the way she means to, they will find the curtain drawn and the mouth of the hollow grown quietly closed. It will have closed not over a snore or a cry but over the one low note she has been humming all her life, the one her room was made from. That note will make one more ring in the stone, finer than the rest.

The hollow will hum after that, on still nights, in the key of her chest. The storm-rib will hum with it, softened. And whatever is waking in the Saddle will have, laid down deep in its first layers among the thunder and the thorns and the dawn roar and the masts' cold tone, one voice that only ever said *it's all right*.

## If You Meet Her

You probably won't. She will have seen you long before you see her, from the grey against the grey of the western wall, and she will judge by how you walk whether to show herself.

If she does, she will come quietly down the kept path and stop a few paces off. She will look at your boots, then at your mouth. She will put one scarred finger to her own lips. It is not a warning. It is a courtesy, the way you'd show a guest where to hang their coat.

Then she will turn and walk you slowly, heel-first, to the southern stairs. She will stand aside and let you climb them, and listen with her eyes closed while your footfalls ring out someone else's melody, one note per stair, very old now and still perfectly clear.

At the top, if the fog lifts, she will be gone. On the ledge beside you there will be a salamander skin, grey and whole, laid out flat to dry.
