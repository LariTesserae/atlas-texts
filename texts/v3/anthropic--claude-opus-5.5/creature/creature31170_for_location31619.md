# Who Lives on the Plateau of Unattended Building

## The First Answer: The Mason

The obvious answer is that nothing lives there but the plants. It is also the wrong answer, though it takes a while to see why.

Start with the plants anyway, because they are real, and because the first thing to learn about them is that there are not many of them. There is one.

The floor, the towers, the aqueducts, the doors, the seed-pods cracking off like rifles in the afternoon: all of it is one organism. Cut a strand of the upper weave and a strand from a tower lintel three miles off and you'll find the same tissue, the same pale sap. The sap tastes faintly of tin and green apple and goes gummy on the tongue. The lattice is not a floor that buildings grow on. The lattice is the body, and the buildings are things the body does. They are closer to gestures than to organs, the way a hand is part of you but a fist is something the hand makes.

Call it the Mason. People who have stayed long enough generally do, though some prefer the Weave, and the few who have stayed far too long call it nothing at all and go quiet when you ask.

The Mason has no brain, but it has junctions. Where strands of the floor cross, they fuse into knots the size of a clenched hand, and these knots are warm, warmer than the air, which on the plateau is saying something. Signals pass between them. You can feel it if you lay a palm flat on a crossing and wait: a faint tick, then another, irregular, like a slow and uncertain pulse. A thousand miles of knots, ticking. The collapse of a tower is preceded by a change in the ticking across the whole region, a quickening, a sort of stutter. The rot that turns a wing to wet paper in an afternoon starts as a wave of that stutter moving outward through the floor, and whatever it reaches goes soft.

It does not think the way we think. It has something narrower, and in its narrowness perhaps stranger: it has *grammar*. It knows that a wall wants a lintel over its gap. It knows that a roof wants a gutter, and a gutter wants a downpipe, and a stair wants a door at the top. It knows these the way a language knows that a verb wants a subject, without anyone having decided it. It speaks the grammar constantly, in wood, and most of its sentences fall apart before they end.

So the Mason lives there. It is enormous, patient, and violent, and it breathes through its seams and eats its own ruins. But the Mason is the builder, and a builder is not a resident. Nobody asks who lives in a house and expects to hear the name of the carpenter.

The question stands: who are the rooms for?

## The Second Answer: The Evening Tenant

Watch a building through one full day and night, and an answer offers itself.

At noon the doors are shut, pressed closed by the swollen heat, and the rooms are sealed boxes of hot dark. In the evening, as the air cools and the sap sinks, the doors sag open with that long wooden sigh. And then, every night, something comes in.

It comes up over the escarpment first. The cloud-floor below the cliffs, which has been lying white and inert all day, begins at dusk to rise. It pours over the lip in slow grey surges, spreads out across the lattice, and moves between the buildings at walking pace, and where it meets an open door it goes in.

It fills the rooms. You can stand in one and watch it arrive: a white tongue sliding across the doorsill, pooling in the corners, climbing the walls, until the room is full to the ceiling with cool vapor and you can't see your own hand. On the leaf-tile ceilings it beads and runs. On the walls it condenses into sheets. By midnight every room on the plateau is weeping, and the water runs down channels in the walls that no one noticed during the day. Those channels were cut there, as it turns out, for this. The water gathers in the round basins, and from the basins it drains into the floor.

The rooms are traps for cloud. The dome with its oculus catches rain by day and fog by night. The honeycomb in the north, with its hundreds of identical little chambers, is a condenser of staggering efficiency: more wall, more surface, more water wrung from the air. The doors that close at noon are keeping the heat out and the moisture in, the way a leaf's pores close under a hard sun. The whole building is a kind of pore. The whole plateau is a leaf the size of a province, drinking the sky that lives below it.

So there is a tenant, and it arrives every evening and is evicted every morning. The cloud is a nomad that comes up from the lowlands to sleep in empty rooms, and while it sleeps the rooms drink it. At dawn, when the heat returns and the doors swing shut, the last of it is pressed out through the gaps in long white plumes, and it slides back over the cliff to lie in the sun and recover.

This is a better answer than the Mason. It explains the doors. It explains the basins and the gutters and even the aqueducts, which carry the night's catch from the builds that condense well to the builds that don't. It is almost satisfying.

But it doesn't explain the stairs.

## The Third Answer: The Measured One

Cloud doesn't climb stairs. Cloud doesn't need a door to be a door-shaped hole with a sill at the bottom and a lintel at the top. It would fill a ragged crack as happily as a doorway. And yet the doorways are doorways. The stairs are stairs, with treads level and risers even.

And the risers, as it turns out, are *all the same*.

Someone measured them, one of the people who came and stayed. She was the sort who carries a folding rule into a place that eats folding rules. She measured riser heights on four hundred staircases across sixty miles of plateau, on towers rising and towers leaning and towers already half-digested into the floor. The number barely varied. Each riser stood a little under a hand and a half high. The treads ran a little deeper than a foot. Doorways were consistent too: tall, taller than a tall man by a head, and narrow, narrower than you'd like, so that you'd turn your shoulders a little going through. Sills sat at the same slight height everywhere, as if to keep water out, or something small in. The windows at every level of the towers began at the same distance above their floors, which is to say at the height of an eye.

You cannot get consistency like that from structural necessity. A crystal has consistent angles because physics insists on them. Nothing in physics insists that a stair riser be a hand and a half high. That number comes from a leg.

This is where the plateau stops being a botanical curiosity and becomes something you think about at night for the rest of your life. The buildings are not generic. They are *fitted*. The Mason is building to a body. Every stair, every sill, every window-height across the tableland describes the same absent figure, the way a glove describes a hand that isn't in it.

People have tried to draw it. Taken together, the measurements suggest someone tall and narrow-shouldered. Its stride is a touch longer than ours but its step is shorter, so it goes up stairs lightly, many small lifts, a dancer's or a heron's gait. Its eye sits high. It must bow its head very slightly through doorways, even these tall ones. That, or the lintel height is a courtesy, a margin the Mason leaves, the way you'd build a ceiling higher than strictly needed for someone you loved.

It wants no furniture. Every room is bare, and not the bareness of neglect; the rooms are *finished* bare, with floors smoothed and corners rounded. Whatever it is, it does not sit, or it sits on the floor, or it doesn't sleep in the way we mean. It wants water, a basin in nearly every room, round and knee-high. It likes, or is thought to like, standing under a single column of falling rain. That is what the dome is: a room made to hold one standing figure under one cord of water, with the basin placed exactly where its feet would go.

Call it the Measured One, or the Expected, or, as one of the long-stayers called it, with an odd tenderness, *the one the house is waiting up for*.

The Measured One lives on the plateau the way a key lives in a lock. It lives in shape. It lives in the conditional tense. It is the reason for the grammar. Every sentence the Mason speaks in wood has an implied subject, and the subject is always this.

## The Evidence That It Is More Than a Shape

That would be eerie enough, and most people stop there. It is a satisfying kind of ghost story: a whole landscape building homes for an inhabitant it has never met, out of some inherited memory or botanical prophecy.

The long-stayers don't stop there, because of the sills.

Every build on the plateau is young. Nothing stands more than weeks; most things stand days. The wood is white and green and raw, and nothing has time to weather. And yet, in some rooms, the doorsills are *worn*.

Not everywhere. Not most places. But in perhaps one room in a few hundred, the sill has a shallow hollow in its center, smoothed and slightly glossy, the dip you see on the threshold of a very old church where centuries of feet have crossed. The wood there has the dense, polished grain of age. It cannot be age. The sill is nine days old.

There are other marks once you know to look. In the looping building, where a doorway opens onto a doorway onto the first doorway again, the jambs are rubbed smooth at shoulder height, on the side you'd brush if you were turning through them. In the honeycomb, a handful of the identical rooms aren't identical after all: their basins have a faint tide-line, as if something had drunk down to a certain level, night after night, for years. On the spiral stair that climbs past the top of its column into empty air, the treads go on thinning until they are no wider than a finger, and those last swaying steps are worn *more* than the solid ones below. They are worn as if they were the part most used.

So the Measured One is not only a shape. It crosses thresholds. It turns through that impossible loop of doors without getting lost, because for it the loop is not a trap but a corridor, probably one it prefers. It drinks from basins. It climbs the stair that leads nowhere and keeps going past where the stair gives out, and the finger-thin treads are enough for it, and the landing it is climbing toward exists for it, though not for anyone else.

It is never seen.

The best theory, and nobody likes it, is that the Measured One lives in the interval. Every building passes through a moment, sometimes an afternoon, sometimes an hour, when it is complete: the last lintel set, the last gutter joined, every door hung true, and before the first lean begins. This is the briefly perfect phase. The long-stayers think the Measured One lives *there*. It moves only through finished rooms, only in the hour of finish. It never enters a rising building or a leaning one; for it, those don't exist yet, or don't exist anymore. From its side, the plateau is not a churning construction site at all but a calm and endless city of completed houses, each one whole, and it moves from one to the next as each achieves itself. It walks through a city made entirely of the moments that a place like this spends being right.

We come in at the wrong times. We always come in during the raw hour or the groaning hour or the collapse, and the rooms are empty, and the dent in the sill has only just been pressed.

It would explain the Mason's endless drafting and redrafting. The Mason isn't dissatisfied. It is keeping the city open. Every tower that falls is a house the Measured One has finished with; every shoot rising at dawn is the next room it'll need. A city that must be rebuilt every day for a resident who is only ever at home in the hour of completion is a plateau that never stops building.

And the spacing, the mile of empty lattice between one build and the next? That is how far it likes to walk between houses. That is its evening stroll.

## The Fourth Answer, Which Nobody Wanted

Here is the part that the woman with the folding rule wrote down near the end of her notes, in handwriting that had gotten smaller.

She had been on the plateau a long time. She had a camp near the colonnade that runs arrow-straight for half a mile toward nothing, and she slept on the floor-weave under an oiled sheet, and every morning she walked out to measure.

Over the second year, the risers changed.

It was slight. It was a quarter of an inch at first, within her margin of error, and she ignored it. But the stairs near her camp began to come in a little lower and a little shallower, the tread a hair longer, and the ratio drifted toward a step that suited a shorter, heavier walker. The doorways of the new builds around her camp grew a finger wider at the shoulder. In one new tower, a window began not at the height of the Measured One's high eye but lower. It began at hers.

Out beyond, in the far builds, the old measurements held. Near her, the plateau was changing its mind.

Then the Mason grew a room beside her camp. It was a small one, a low dome with no oculus. Its door faced the direction she always faced when she woke. Its basin stood at the height of her kneeling. And along one wall, where no room on the whole plateau had ever had anything, it grew a ledge: flat, level, the length of a sleeping body, a hand's width above the floor.

It was the first furniture anyone had ever seen there.

She did not sleep in it. She moved her camp two miles east, and within the month the new shoots there were coming up to her measure too.

So consider the last answer, the one that makes the others fall into place. Perhaps the Measured One was never a creature at all. Perhaps it is *a vacancy*. Perhaps it is a hunger in the Mason's grammar for an inhabitant, any inhabitant, a slot in the sentence where a subject should go, and whatever stands long enough in that slot becomes its shape. For ages, before anyone climbed the escarpment, the vacancy took its form from the only thing that ever came to it: from the cloud, from the rain column, from the long nightly visitation of weather, and from something else, something tall and light-footed that may once have walked here and gone, or may still walk the finished hour, or may only be what the plateau dreamed an inhabitant would be when it had nothing real to fit.

And now it has something real. Now it has you.

That is who lives on the plateau. The Mason lives there, vast and ticking and drafting endlessly in white wood. The cloud lives there, a tenant by night, drunk dry by morning. The Measured One lives there in the hour of completion, crossing worn sills nobody else can see worn. But beneath all of them, and older than all of them, what lives on the plateau is *hospitality* itself, with no host behind it: the pure, blind, patient wish of a place to be lived in. It has no eyes and no voice and no mercy, only a grammar that insists every house has a someone, and every someone has a height, and every height can be learned.

The towers go up. The rooms stand ready. The doors swing open every evening on their soft wooden sigh, and all across the miles of steaming lattice, in the grey and falling dark, they are beginning, slowly, one riser at a time, to open toward whoever is standing there.
