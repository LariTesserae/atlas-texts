# Who Lives in the Dry Works

## The First Answer

The first answer is the one that sounds like a dodge: the Works lives there. Every gantry, belt, and drinking crown is alive. Asking who inhabits the Works is a little like asking who inhabits a body. You can point to organs, but the organs aren't tenants.

Still, a body has passengers, and so does the Works. There are things that move on it rather than as it, things with their own errands. Travelers notice them in a certain order, from the ones you see on your first day to the ones you only infer after a long time. This account follows that order, because the residents make more sense in sequence. Each one explains a little of the next.

## The Dustwings

The grey moths are the first thing that seems to be *someone* rather than something. They are also the strangest thing in the Works, because they are the only thing here that eats.

In a place built entirely on transmission, the moths store. Light goes into a drive-stem and comes straight out as torque, with nothing kept back. Starch dust pours through the channels without pooling for long. The moths, alone, take something in and keep it. You can feel it when one lands on your hand. It is heavier than it should be, about the weight of a coin. Its body holds a single hard pellet of compacted starch, dense and white, and the pellet grows slowly over a moth's life, one layer at a time, like a pearl.

They fly very little. Their broad wings are made less for beating than for riding, and a dustwing will sit at the base of a drive-stem until the updraft is right, then open and rise in a slow spiral, hundreds of meters, into the leaf-skin cloud. They feed up there, invisible, combing the drifting tissue with mouthparts as fine as eyelashes. It is cold, slow eating. A cloudbank with many moths in it grows heavier faster and comes down sooner. Some travelers say you can tell a snowfall that moths have been through, because the flakes are finer and the fall is quieter.

When a dustwing dies in the air, the pellet brings it straight down, quickly and silently. It lands on a belt, and the belt carries it toward the reclamation mills like any other debris. But the mills don't take the pellets. Somewhere in the pressing, the Works sets them aside, and they roll out of the far end of the line intact, gathering in small white drifts at the edges of the buffer zones. People call them moth-pearls. They are the only thing in the Works that is not reused. They are also the only thing that holds a record of anything, since a pearl is layered by season, by bank, by feeding. Crack one and you are looking at a moth's whole life, stored.

This matters more than it seems. The Works has wear, but wear is not memory. It is only shape. The moths are the one part of the place that has a *past* in the way a person does. The Works appears to know this. It does not grind them up.

Their larvae are harder to love. They are pale, blind, cold grubs, laid in the cracks of abandoned lines, and they chew the old grey wood into powder. They tunnel along the growth rings, ring by ring, outward. And in the oldest lines, where the rings keep going past the bark into the empty air, the larvae keep going too. More than one traveler has found a grub chewing its way through nothing, leaving behind a tunnel you can only see at a slant: a thin hollow bored into the air a hand's width beyond the edge of the wood, with a trickle of powder falling out of it from no visible source.

Where the larvae are headed, they are the first to arrive.

## The Tallymen

The tallymen are the second thing you notice, and at first you don't notice them at all, because they aren't moving. That is the trick. They walk against the belts at exactly belt speed, so they stand still while the Works slides beneath them. Your eye takes them for posts or splinters, part of the fixed world. Then your belt carries you past one and you realize it has legs, and that the legs are moving, very steadily, and have been the whole time.

They are bark-brown and jointed like dry grass, long as a forearm, some as long as a leg. They have no eyes. Their heads end in a ridge of fine, dust-caked hairs that tremble when a press closes nearby, or slightly before a press closes, if the sound has come early. They have no mouths. They don't eat, don't store, and don't appear to rest.

Every so often one stops walking. Relative to the ground this looks like the tallyman suddenly starting to move, since the belt begins carrying it off. It reaches down and taps a roller, a socket, or a bare stretch of woven root, three times. Then it resumes walking and slides back into its borrowed stillness. Within the month, a stem sprouts where it tapped.

People who have watched them long enough report two refinements. First, near the fast districts, where the shadows point toward the light, the tallymen tap four times instead of three, and the stem is sometimes already there when they arrive. They are tapping the place where it will have been. Second, no one has seen a tallyman hatch. Pale splinters turn up in the deepest grooves of the oldest rollers, grooves worn by millions of turns, and people assume these are pupae. But no one has watched one open.

What people *have* seen is how the tallymen end. A tallyman does not fall over. It slows by an amount too small to see, a fraction of belt speed. Relative to the ground it begins, very gently, to drift, and the belt carries it along, day by day, toward the presses. It keeps walking the whole way. It walks into the final press still walking.

Pods that have a tallyman pressed into them have crooked seams, and people who have sawed them open describe the same thing inside: the usual pale wood, the usual cool light, and a line of small tap-marks running across the floor in threes. The line goes straight away from the door, farther than anyone has followed it.

## The Climbers

No one has seen a climber. They are known only by the wear.

The Works tells you it is climbed. The gantry rungs are hollowed into saddles. The spiral stairs of grown wood are polished on the tread and never on the rail. The fine dust on the rungs sometimes shows a smooth, curving sweep, a soft weight dragged over and lifted, with no prints in it. Whatever climbs the Works climbs it constantly, everywhere, and never while anyone is watching.

The saddles tell you more if you compare them. In the new growth, the saddles are wide and shallow, big enough to seat a broad, soft foot the size of a dinner plate. In the older lines beneath, they are smaller. In the grey abandoned avenues under those, smaller still, and deeper, and much more numerous, as if a great many small climbers once went up and down those gantries for a very long time. In a few cross-sections where the strata have split cleanly, you can trace it down through the bands like a fossil record. The climbers were many and small. Then they were fewer and larger. Now there are very few, and they are very large.

Whatever climbs the Works has been growing up through it, generation by generation, as the pods have been growing larger inside.

## The Stayed

People come to the Dry Works, and some of them do not leave. It is worth being careful about what happens to them, because the stories are usually told as warnings, and it isn't clear that they should be.

A person who stays gets thirsty first, badly, for about a week. Their lips crack in the cold dry air. Then the lips stop cracking, not because they've healed but because they no longer seem to need to. The thirst goes quiet. So does hunger, a little after. People who have come back out and seen old companions describe them as fine: calm, clear-eyed, polite, a little distant, like someone listening to music in another room.

The Stayed spend a lot of time on the upper gantries, sitting very still with their faces turned to the white sky. If you watch them long enough, their skin goes faintly darker and faintly warmer where the light falls on it, and then a small tremor runs down through them. They have started transmitting. They don't keep the light. They pass it on, somehow, and the Works seems to take it. The belts near where the Stayed sit run a little smoother.

They whisper, always. After some years, they stop talking at all, though they will still answer a question by pointing. Their joints creak, softly, the thin sound of green wood flexing. The oldest of them have a fine bark across the backs of their hands.

They live in pods, small ones, the melon and cart sizes, set down at the edges of the Works. They don't furnish them. There is nothing to furnish them with and nothing they need. But a pod a Stayed person lives in changes. The machinery that sometimes begins far inside a pod, the lone drive-stem or short length of motionless belt, begins sooner in theirs, and closer to the door.

They are kind to visitors. They give away moth-pearls. They'll tell you, in a whisper, which belt reaches the edge in three days and which in three years. Asked why they stayed, they usually say something like *it needed someone to wait with it.* Asked what they're waiting for, they point, not at the sky, not at the horizon, but inward, toward the heart of the Works, where the oldest and largest pods have been swallowed and folded away.

No one has seen a Stayed person become a tallyman. No one has seen a tallyman hatch, either.

## The Listener

The broad four-toed prints lead into a district and never out. That fact is the whole key to the Listener, and it took people a long time to see why.

It is the size of an ox, pale and slow, with a hide like dusted felt. The few people who have been close say they aren't sure it has eyes. It has a head that is mostly forehead, broad and flat and faintly taut, like the skin of a drum. Its feet are soft and wide. They would fit the new saddles on the gantry rungs exactly.

It walks among the largest seedpods, stops beside one, and lays its forehead against the husk. Then it waits, sometimes for a very long time. The dust settles on it. Once, a traveler says, a tallyman walked right up its back at belt speed as if it were a roller, tapped it three times between the shoulders, and walked off.

Then the Listener is gone, and the pod is open, and it is empty, and it is larger inside than any pod before it.

The prints lead in and not out because the Listener does not leave by the surface. It leaves through the pod. It doesn't live on the plain at all. The plain is only where it comes to try the new ones. It lives *inside*, in the folded interior of the Works, in the vast swallowed districts that give a low, bounded region the weather of a continent. The surface of the Works is to the Listener what a beach is to something that lives in the sea.

As for what it is listening for, there are two readings, and both are probably true. The first is that it is listening for whether anyone is already in there. It is checking the room. Each pod it opens is empty. Something in the Works registers the emptiness, and the next pod comes off the press a little larger, as though space itself were being enlarged to fit someone who has not arrived.

The second reading comes from the climbers' saddles, small in the deep strata and wide in the new. It is that the Listener is trying the rooms *on*. It is looking for one that fits, and none of them do, because it is still growing. The Works is making larger and larger husks for the thing that climbs it, generation after generation, like a sea growing larger shells for a creature that will not stop needing more room.

## The Tenant

Everything here is making room, and room is always room *for* something.

Look back at the residents in order. The moths are the only things that remember, and their pearls are set aside, not reused, as if they were being kept for someone to read. The larvae chew outward into rings that have not been laid down yet, toward years that have not happened. The tallymen count, three and three and three, and in the fast districts they count ahead. The climbers grow across the strata. The Listener checks each room and finds it empty. The Stayed sit with their faces to the light and wait, and when asked what for, they point inward.

What lives in the Dry Works, taken all together, is an anticipation. It is a household that has been preparing for a long time. Its members don't know what they're preparing for, and they have worn the whole house into grooves with their preparing. The sounds arrive early because the place is leaning forward. The shadows fall toward the light because the light is being drawn out of things toward something. The growth rings run past the bark into the air because the Works has already been told how much larger it will need to be, and is keeping the measurements.

That is one way to tell it. There is another, and people who stay long enough tend to come around to it.

The inside of every pod is pale wood, cool, lit evenly by a white sourceless light. That is also the sky of the Works.
