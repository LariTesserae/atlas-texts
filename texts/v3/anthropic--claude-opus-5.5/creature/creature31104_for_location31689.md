# What Lives in the Loom Tarn

## The Easy Answer

The shuttles live there. Start with them, since they are the first thing anyone would name. They are gray-violet, arm-long, and always at work. If you asked the lower-slope people who lives in the Tarn, they would say *the shuttles*, the way you might say that bees live in a hive. They would be right, and they would mean more than they said.

A shuttle's life has a shape, and the shape is worth knowing.

It hatches in the weave. Shuttle eggs are laid singly, each tucked into the crossing of two strands, and they look like the smoothed knots all around them. The mother pulls a fiber over each one before she leaves. When the young shuttle comes out it is the length of a fingernail, and it does not swim away. It holds on. For its first season it clings to the square of weave where it was born, and it listens.

Young shuttles cannot yet tie a knot, but they can hear one. Every kind of knot in the Tarn, every twill and herringbone and doubled hitch, makes its own small sound when an adult shuttle tests it: a specific pluck, a specific dry click at a specific pitch. A hatchling hears thousands of these a day, in sequences that repeat. It is learning the pattern of its patch the way a child learns a lullaby, before it can understand a word. It learns it as sound first. Only later, when its fin rays have stiffened, does it reach out and touch the weave and find that the thing it has been hearing has a shape.

That is why the clicking never stops. The adults are not only testing the weave. They are reciting it.

## The Chambers

Then there are the chambers, the great wickerwork spheres hung on their cables like weaverbird nests the size of houses. A shuttle is the length of an arm. So what are the chambers for?

Old shuttles go into them.

Near the end of its life, a shuttle's color fades from violet toward pewter, the same color as the Tarn's surface. Its clicking slows and its fin rays grow stiff and clumsy. When that happens it leaves its patch, and a younger shuttle takes the patch over without any fighting. The old one swims up or down the wall, sometimes a long way, until it reaches a chamber. It slips in through one of the narrow gaps in the wickerwork, which are placed exactly where a shuttle's body fits. Then it goes to the center of the sphere and stops.

It hangs there, not quite still, the way lantern-drifts hang. And it begins to hum.

The hum is not a click. It is lower and longer, made somewhere in the throat, and it is the whole pattern the shuttle spent its life tending, slowed down until it becomes a single held tone with slow movements inside it. The chamber's tight weave takes that tone and holds it. A chamber is a resonator. Pressed against the outside of one, you would feel the wickerwork buzzing faintly under your palm, like the body of a cello someone is playing in another room.

Younger shuttles come and press against the chambers too. They do it often, between repairs, holding their narrow heads to the wicker. They are checking. The pattern a young shuttle learned as a hatchling has drifted a little in its memory, the way any lullaby drifts, and the old one in the chamber holds the version from sixty or eighty years before. The two are compared. The small differences are corrected.

When the old shuttle dies, it does not sink. The water is too cold for it to rot quickly. The next shuttle to find it draws the body gently to the chamber wall and weaves it in. The flesh goes, very slowly. The skeleton stays, long and fine, bound flat into the wickerwork like a rib. Look closely at any old chamber and you can see them: pale threads of spine running through the olive weed, dozens deep, hundreds. The chambers are built from the dead who kept them in tune.

So that is one answer to what lives in the Tarn. The pattern lives there. It is carried in shuttles as sound, rechecked against the old ones in their chambers, and it keeps itself exact by reading itself aloud, over and over, in a closed bowl where no echo is ever lost.

This answer is true. It is also not quite enough.

## The Question of the Bridges

Look again at the bridges.

They are braided ropes of weed, some as thick as tree trunks, strung across open water hundreds of meters wide. They do not sag. They are tensioned.

Weed rope floats, or nearly floats. A cable like that, left to itself across that span, would bow upward, drift, and curl. It would not hang in a straight line, taut enough to sing, for ten thousand years. Something is pulling on the bridges, and it is pulling evenly, from every side, the way a drumhead is pulled.

The walls are not doing it. The walls are woven to the rock, if there is rock, and they curve with the basin. They are anchors. They do not pull.

The bridges are held taut from the middle.

## The Empty Water

Here is something the lower-slope people have noticed, though only a few of them, and they do not all agree on it.

Watch the veil rays for long enough (and you would have to watch for years, because their circuits are slow) and you will see that their paths are not random loops along the walls. They go *around* something. There is a region near the center of the Tarn that the rays never cross. It is not a straight-sided region. It swells and narrows, bulges here and tucks in there, and the rays trace its edges exactly, year after year, the way a hand traces a face in the dark.

The lantern-drifts show the same thing. They hang at their chosen depths for years, and from far off they look like a scattered, drowned constellation. But lay out where each one hangs, all at once, and they are not scattered. They sit along a surface, spaced out on the skin of a shape. They are lamps set around something, not inside it.

The drumfish never school in the middle water either. They stay in the lower galleries, close against the walls. The knotmice live in the weave and nowhere else. No shuttle has ever been seen crossing the open center of the Tarn, even though going straight across would sometimes save it half a day's swim. They go the long way, along the bridges.

The open water, most of the Tarn, is described as empty. It is described as void.

It is not empty. It is occupied, and everything else in the Tarn is arranged around the occupant.

## What the Occupant Is

It is made of water.

That is the first thing, and the hardest to hold onto. It does not live *in* the water the way a fish does. It has no skin you could touch, no edge you could see. If you swam into it, which nothing does, you would feel perhaps a slight change: a thickening, a coldness a fraction of a degree deeper than the Tarn's steady cold, a stillness under the stillness. You would go through it the way a hand goes through a curtain of fog. It would not stop you, and you would not see it.

It is heavier than the water around it, but only barely. It is colder, but only barely. It is water that has been still for so long that it has become a different kind of thing. Its molecules have settled into an order the rest of the Tarn never quite reaches, the way a crowd, left standing long enough in the same room, stops being a crowd and becomes a congregation.

It fills the middle of the Tarn. It is kilometers across, and irregular: lobed, folded, with long extensions reaching out toward the walls between the bridges. The bridges pass through it, or along it, or rest against it. Nobody knows which. That is why they are taut: it presses on them. Not hard. The way a sleeper's body presses against the sheets.

Because it is asleep.

## The Breathing

The description of the drumfish says it is *as if something enormous were breathing in its sleep*.

The drumfish are not the breathing. They are keeping time to it.

Once every eleven minutes or so, the occupant of the Tarn expands, very slightly. It does not grow. It *eases*, the way a chest eases outward, a shift too small for any instrument the lower-slope people have to measure. The bridges tighten, just barely, and their chord shifts pitch by a fraction of a tone that no single hearer could detect. Then it settles back. Down in the galleries the drumfish, which have lived their whole lineage against the walls, feel the change in the water through their great kettledrum bladders and answer it. The rolling *thrum-thrum-thrum* is uneven because the breathing is uneven, the way a sleeper's breath is uneven, and the drumfish follow it the way a dog lying beside its owner's bed matches its breathing to hers without knowing it.

The Tarn's level has never changed. Every breath the occupant takes in, it gives back. The surface does not rise. *Holding the cold of the altitude like a held breath*: the lower-slope people have that nearly right. The breath is not held. It is kept even, which is harder.

## Why the Weave

Now the whole thing turns around, and it has to be looked at again.

The weave is not a building. It is not a city or a hive, and it is not really a structure at all. It is a basket, and the basket has something in it.

It is a swaddling. The bridges are the bands of a cradleboard. The mats on the walls are the cradle's sides, padded and padded again over a hundred thousand years until the oldest layers pressed into something like leather and stone. The weave keeps the sleeper held, and the holding is not a cage. Nothing in the Tarn is built to keep anything *in*. Everything is built to keep it *held*, the way you hold a child who startles in her sleep: firmly and evenly, so the startle has nowhere to go and settles back down.

The shuttles' perfect repair, at exactly the rate of decay, is not just thoroughness. A loose strand in the swaddling is a place where the sleeper's pressure is uneven. An unevenness is a sensation. A sensation, in something that large and that still, could become the beginning of waking. So the strand is replaced before it frays past mending. No stretch of the holding is allowed to go slack long enough to be *felt*.

And the chord of the bridges never resolves. That is not an accident either.

Every lower-slope child who has sung knows the feeling of a tune that stops on the wrong note: the pull to finish it, the unease until the last tone lands. A chord that resolves is a chord that ends. The Tarn's chord is kept dissonant and calm, suspended, always leaning toward a cadence it never reaches. It is the sound of *not yet*. It tells the sleeper, in the only language that reaches it, that nothing has finished, that the night is still going on, that there is no reason to wake.

The shuttles do not know this. That is almost certain. They know their patches and their patterns. They know that the old ones in the chambers hum a truer version than they can. They know that some shapes are right and others are wrong, the way a hatchling knows its lullaby before it knows words. Whether they understand what they are singing to hardly matters. A lullaby works whether or not the singer knows what sleep is.

## What It Dreams

Nobody knows. But there are guesses.

The tenders of the pressed dark, the pale, blind, many-legged scrapers on the floor, are eating the oldest weave a grain at a time. They are the only creatures in the Tarn that do not face the sleeper. They face down, toward the fossil patterns, toward the knots no living shuttle ever tied. The lower-slope people who have thought hardest about the Tarn say this is the sleeper's forgetting. Nothing that is held that long and that gently can keep all of it. Something has to take the oldest layers away, slowly, so there is room for the new ones settling from above. The floor never rises and never falls. The sleeper forgets exactly as fast as it is held.

And the rays, with their circuits laid in water instead of weed, move along its edges year after year like the edge of a hand stroking. Some of them are as old as the oldest bridges. If the sleeper feels anything, it feels that: great dark wings going around and around it, touching nothing, never stopping.

It may dream of the rays. It may dream of the chord. It may dream of whatever it was before it lay down here, before the mountain rose so high that the sky went black around it, before there was a Tarn.

Or it may not be dreaming at all. There is another way to understand it, and the few who have stood longest on the rim do not all choose the first.

## The Other Way

Maybe it is not asleep. Maybe it is listening.

Look at what it has around it. A bowl that returns every sound. Millions of shuttles reciting a pattern out loud. Chambers full of old voices holding the pattern true. Drumfish marking its time. Bridges singing a chord that never ends. Knotmice whispering over the walls like the static between radio stations. Everything in the Tarn *makes sound*, and everything is arranged around the one thing that does not.

Maybe the sleeper is not being lulled. Maybe it is the audience.

In that reading the Tarn is not a cradle but a concert hall. The music was written so long ago that the composer is gone, and the orchestra has forgotten there was ever a composer. The weave is the score, pressed into every wall. The shuttles are the players, reading their own part and nothing else. The piece has no ending because it was never meant to end. It was meant to be *heard*, continuously, by one listener whose attention is so large and so slow that a single phrase takes a century to land.

The occupant eases outward every eleven minutes. Perhaps that is not breath. Perhaps it is the way a listener leans forward, very slightly, at a passage it has heard ten million times and still does not want to miss.

## The Rim

Both readings may be true. A child in a cradle is also an audience. Anyone who has sung someone to sleep knows the sleeper is listening right up until the moment she isn't, and that the song goes on anyway, softer, for someone who has already gone.

So, who lives in the Loom Tarn?

The shuttles, who tend and recite and grow old and go into the chambers to hum. The drumfish, the rays, the drifts, the knotmice, the pale tenders at the floor, all born there and descended from things born there, back as far as the weave goes. And the pattern, which lives in all of them together, in no single one, and keeps itself exact.

And in the middle, taking up most of the room, holding the bridges taut, kept perfectly even: the one in the basket. That is what the lower-slope people call it, on the rare evenings they talk about it at all. They lower their voices when they do, the way you lower your voice near a sleeping house.

When they climb to the rim and stand at the worn lip, under a sky black at noon, and look down into the pewter water, they do not throw stones. They do not call out. No one has ever told them not to. They just don't, the way you don't stamp on the floor above a nursery.

They listen instead. On still days the sound comes up faintly through the water: the drone, the thrum, the endless clicking. They say it is the sound of something that finished becoming what it is a very long time ago.

The ones who have stood there longest say something else, too. Under the drone and the thrum and the clicking there is a fourth thing. It is not a sound. It is closer to a pressure: the feeling of a very large attention turned, just slightly, toward the rim where they stand.

They say it is not unfriendly. They say it feels like being noticed by someone half asleep, someone who knows you are there and knows you mean no harm, and who is about to go back under.

Then the chord of the bridges goes on not resolving, the drumfish keep time, a shuttle somewhere pulls a knot tight in exactly the old way, and the feeling passes. The Tarn keeps its level, and whoever is in the basket keeps breathing, evenly, as it always has.
