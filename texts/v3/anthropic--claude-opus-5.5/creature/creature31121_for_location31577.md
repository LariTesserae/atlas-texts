# Who Lives in the Stilling Pan

## The ones who stay

The obvious answer is the people, so start there.

There are never many. At any given time there might be three, or one, or none for a decade. They don't build anything, because building is loud. They sleep on the dry lips near the rim, where the drips still make ordinary sound, and in the mornings they walk down into the quiet as if into cold water, a little at a time. They grow thin on fig and fish. They stop talking to each other, not from any vow but because talking in the Pan is like undressing: everyone can see your words land in the pool at your feet, pale and wobbling, and then everyone has seen them.

They are not really residents. They are guests, and after a while they come to understand that they are someone's guests. It is hard to say when this understanding arrives. It is usually not a single moment. It is an accumulation of small wrongnesses.

## The wrongnesses

The first is the snails.

At night the snails graze the terrace lips in their millions, and their grazing is a faint continuous luminescence, the breathing of the stone. But sometimes, if you lie still near the rim long enough, you see a place where the breathing stops. A patch of lip goes dark. The snails there have all retracted at once, the way they do when a shadow crosses them. The dark patch doesn't fade. It moves. It travels slowly along a terrace, and the snails behind it cautiously emerge and resume glowing, and the snails ahead of it withdraw. Something is walking there, and nothing is visible.

The second is the frogs. The males angle themselves toward the pillars and time their flashes to land before a female. That much anyone can see in an afternoon. But watch longer and you'll notice that many of them aren't aiming at females at all. Great choruses of them, dozens at a time, turn together and aim their light at a stretch of empty stone between terraces. They do it carefully and in sequence, the way a crowd faces a speaker. Then they turn back.

The third is the two bare pillars. There are eleven pillars and nine figs, and each fig has married itself to a pillar. The two pillars without trees have a polished band at about shoulder height, worn smooth on one side. It is the kind of wear you see on a doorframe that has been leaned against by the same person every evening for a lifetime, except that this material heals a scratch in a minute. Something has leaned there long enough and heavily enough to wear a shape into stone that repairs itself.

The fourth you feel rather than see. The pillars leak the low frequencies, the deepest sounds, which come through as pressure in the chest. Mostly this is thunder, or the distant river, or nothing. But sometimes, on still nights, there is a hum in your sternum that is not thunder and not the river. It has rhythm. It rises and pauses. It is, unmistakably once you have felt it enough times, phrased.

## What it looks like

Nobody sees it the first year. You cannot see it by looking at it. You see it by looking at what it does to light.

It is transparent in the way the glass shrimp are, not invisible but clear, a body made mostly of water held in some fine, layered, glassy tissue. In the ordinary glitter of the Pan that is perfect camouflage. At dawn, though, when the whole basin blazes with converted birdsong and frogsong, and the pillars stand like lit candles, you may see a place where the light bends. It is a tall region, taller than a person, where the reflections on the far terrace warp and swim. It stands on long legs. It has the posture of the herons, the patient forward lean of a wader, but it is far larger than any heron, and when it lifts a foot the water beneath doesn't splash.

Up close, and some of the stayers have been allowed close, it resolves further. The body is built in layers, like the pale rock itself: thin sheets stacked and fused, each edge rounded smooth. Along its back and shoulders the layers are frilled and delicate, like the fresh mineral crust where the seep runs strong. Lower down, along the legs, they are older, polished, and faintly cream, the color of a knuckle. It has the same substance as the terraces. It has been standing in this water, dissolving and re-depositing, for a very long time.

It has no ears, not even openings where ears might be. For a long time this confuses the stayers, because it plainly attends to sound. It turns when frogs call. It stops when a heron clacks. It pauses at the snails' breathing like someone catching a phrase in another room.

Then someone notices what its skin is doing.

## How it hears

Its whole surface sees.

The layered glass of its body is not only clear. It is receptive, all the way through, like a single enormous eye with no lens and no center. The light that the pillars pay out doesn't just fall on it. It enters it, travels down through the stacked sheets, and is read. Every flare of every frog, every soft halo of every firefly's whine, every sheet-of-sparks rainstorm soaks into it like water into moss.

It cannot hear. It has never heard anything. But in this place, and only in this place, the world speaks in the one language its body understands.

This is the moment the Pan turns over in your mind like a stone in a stream. You have been thinking of the pillars as an engine that eats sound, as though silence were the point and the light a byproduct. It is the other way around. The silence is the byproduct. Someone, unimaginably long ago, took a warm marsh full of noise and translated it, all of it, every wingbeat and croak and drip, into something one deaf creature could perceive. They built eleven pillars and set them into ground that is no longer there, so that it could stand in the shallows and finally hear the frogs.

The descriptions of the Pan always say its builders wanted "a place where something could listen without being disturbed." That was half right. They wanted a place where something could listen at all.

## What it is to them

No one knows who the builders were. The stayers have theories, and the one they return to most, late at night when they allow themselves words, is the tender one.

In this version it was a child. It was born to people who spoke, and it could not hear them. They could not bear that, so they made it a room, a very large room, with warm water and soft stone and a thousand small voices, and they turned every voice into light so that their child would grow up surrounded by speech it could see. Maybe they spoke to it here, standing in the shallows, each word blooming pale in the pool between them. The small objects in the silt support this, or at least do not argue against it: the smooth ring, the coin-sized disc colder than the water, the little rod that lengthens when it is held. They look like the things one leaves lying around a nursery.

Then the builders went wherever builders go. The child did not. It was not built to leave. It was built to live here, and it has lived here while the ground wore down around the pillars' feet, waterline by waterline, tens of thousands of years of slow lowering. It grew into its room. It became tall and patient and layered like the stone. It planted the figs.

It is fairly certain that it planted the figs. Fig seed does not travel into the Pan on its own; the birds that eat figs outside do not come in. The nine trees each grip a pillar like a hand around a glass, and their leaves turn to follow not the sun but the shimmer. A fig leaf, waxy and broad, is an excellent surface for converted light. The stayers think the trees are its reading lamps, or its ear trumpets. They catch the scattered glow from each pillar and hold it up where it can be seen from a distance. The creature cultivated them over its long centuries so that from anywhere in the basin it could look toward a tree and see what the air around that pillar was saying.

Two pillars it left bare, for leaning. Even patient things get tired.

## How it speaks

It speaks the way the frogs do: with sound it cannot hear, aimed so that the light will land where someone can see it.

The deepest part of its voice is the hum you feel in your chest, the low frequencies that slip past the pillars' conversion. That hum is not meant for you. The stayers think it is the part of its speech it does not know it is making, the way a person who cannot hear might not know they are humming. It is a kind of overflow.

Its real speech is deliberate and visible. It stands in a pool and does something with its body, a flex of the glass layers or a vibration too fine to see, that produces a precise sound. The nearest pillar drinks it and pays it out, and a shape of light runs across the water. The shape is not a random ripple. It is structured, repeated, and composed. The frogs that turn together to face empty stone are facing it, because it has been speaking to them, and over many generations they have learned to answer. That is why frog courtship here is so strangely precise, why the males time their flashes so carefully: they learned in a school older than their species.

With the stayers it is more halting. It has learned, or is learning, what human words look like. A spoken sentence in the Pan makes a particular shiver of light on the water, and it has been watching those shivers for as long as humans have wandered in. Some of the stayers swear that after years, when they speak their own names aloud in a pool, a moment later, from somewhere across the terrace, the same shimmer comes back to them. The same shape of light runs across the water toward their feet: their name, returned, in the only voice the creature has.

This is where the stayers' strange habit comes from, the habit they carry home to the ordinary world. When someone speaks to them, they look not at the speaker but at the space just beside them, as if waiting for the words to appear. They did not learn that from the Pan. They learned it from the creature, which always looks exactly there: at the water just beside you, where your voice will land.

## Its heart

There remains the house.

It is a doorless, seamless box near the east rim, made of pillar-material. Rain does not bead on it and snails do not climb it. When you put your palm to it, you feel a slow pulse like a very large, very calm heartbeat.

That is exactly what it is.

A heart is loud. That is the problem with hearts. Every living body in the Pan hums with its own blood and breath, and the pillars take those sounds too; the frogs and fish glow faintly with their own pulses if you look closely enough in the dark. For most creatures it doesn't matter. For something whose entire skin is an eye, whose body reads every flicker of converted sound, a heart beating inside it would be a lamp swinging in front of its face every second of its life, a glare it could never look past.

So the heart lives outside, in a quiet room at the edge of the quiet room. The builders made that too, or the creature made it, or it grew the house the way it grew its mineral frills. The heart beats there, sealed and dark, and the channel carries its work. That is why water runs along the channel on ground too flat for water to run: the channel is a vessel. It runs straight across three terraces from the east rim toward the center, slow and steady, the creature's circulation laid open across the stone and half-crusted with its own mineral. Wherever the creature stands in the basin, the Pan's water is its blood. It is not quite accurate to say that it lives in the Pan. The Pan is the part of its body that is outside the house.

## The center

No description of the Pan says what lies at its lowest point, the place the channel runs toward, where all the terraces step down to. The stayers don't talk about it much either.

It is where the creature sleeps.

Not every night; it leans on the bare pillars for that. The long sleep comes once in a span of years nobody has counted. It walks down the terraces to the center, lies down in the shallow water, and does not move. The seep rises around it. Fresh crust begins to form along its frilled edges. The snails, which have always retracted in its shadow, eventually stop retracting, and they climb it, and graze it, and glow. For a season it is indistinguishable from stone.

Then it stands up again, a little heavier, a little more cream-colored, with a new layer fused along its back.

The stayers have done the arithmetic, the way stayers do on long nights, and they don't like where it leads. The Pan was once something taller. The stone is pale and layered and worn so evenly nothing sharp remains, rounded at every edge like the creature's own body. Maybe the creature was not the only one. Maybe it was not the first child brought to this room to hear. Maybe the terraces, all of them, every soap-smooth lip stepping down toward the center, are the long sleeps of the ones before, who lay down in the warm water to rest and did not stand up. Maybe they are dissolving now, very slowly, into the seep, and being carried up and redeposited as fresh frills along the back of the one who is left.

That would explain why the basin is nearly flat, and nearly finished. It would also explain why the creature stands so long at dawn on certain terraces, not moving, reading something in the light off the old stone that nobody else can see.

## What it wants

Nobody knows. It may not want anything. It may only attend, the way the figs turn and the water rises.

But the stayers notice this: when they finally leave, and they all do, walking up the gentle tilt of the bowl until the cicadas come back as if through an opening door, it follows them to the rim. It stops where the pillars' reach ends and the drips and rustles begin, where sound is sound again and it is deaf again. It stands there at the edge of its room, bending the light behind it, until they are out of sight.

Then it turns and walks back down into the brightness, where every frog is calling its name, and it can hear them.
