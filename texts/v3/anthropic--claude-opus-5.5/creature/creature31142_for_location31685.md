# Those Who Live on the Tableland of Unfallen Walls

## What Does Not Live Here

It is tempting to say that nothing lives here but the plants and the fog, and to leave it at that.

The rosettes stand in their hooded crowds. The cushions add their finger's width per lifetime. The moss creeps across bare stone and pulls back, creeps and pulls back, the whole green skin shrugging in its sleep. The fog is born each dawn and drawn back each noon, and if you wanted to call the tableland itself alive, breathing out and breathing in, you would not be wrong. There are no mice and no moths, no birds, no worms working the peat. The dead do not rot. A traveler with no patience for strangeness could cross the whole table in six weeks, noting fog, moss, stone, old walls, and cold, and go home saying the place was empty.

But the hammerstones are warm.

That fact undoes the emptiness. On a land where the stone draws the heat out of your palm, where frost forms in every hollow and burns off at noon without ever giving any warmth back, there is exactly one class of warm object, and it is the tool that has just been put down. Warmth is the signature of a hand. Something here has hands, and blood or whatever serves for blood, and the heat of effort. It works where you cannot see it, and it sets the stone down a moment before you arrive, every time.

Begin there, with the hands, and follow them.

## The Near

Travelers have many names for them. The oldest and the one that has lasted longest is *the Near*. It is not a name for what they are. It is a name for where they always are, which is almost here.

No one has seen one. Everything known about them has been read from the work, the way you might know a person you had never met from their handwriting, their kitchen, and the wear on the arms of their chair.

**They are not large.** The lintels of the finished houses sit at a height where a tall traveler must stoop a little and a short one need not. The stair treads are slightly shallower than a human stride prefers. People who climb a long flight of Near stairs report a small, insistent sense of hurrying, as though they are taking the steps faster than whoever made them. The sleeping platforms inside the round houses are the length of a person curled, not stretched. The Near, then, are about our size, a little smaller perhaps, or a little more folded. They may sleep curled against the cold, as anything would here.

**They are patient beyond anything we would call patience.** A single block in a Near wall may carry ten thousand peck marks. Each is a small crater a hair's width across, made by bringing a harder stone down on a softer one at exactly the angle that takes a grain away and does not bruise the face. Each is placed beside the last, in overlapping rows so even they read like the weave of cloth. The joints are fitted so closely that the moss, which grows onto everything, cannot find a way into them. A traveler who once spent a whole noon-hour pecking at a loose block with a Near hammerstone, trying to copy the finish, managed a patch the size of a thumbnail. It looked chewed. The Near surface next to it looked poured.

**They are not all alike.** On most faces the pecking runs in slow spirals that turn one way. On a few, perhaps one block in twenty, the spirals turn the other way. Masons who have come to the tableland to study the work argue about this. Some say it shows left-handed and right-handed workers. Others say it shows two kinds of Near. A few say it shows the same worker at two different times of life, the way handwriting changes as a person ages. In the oldest-looking ruins the counter-turning spirals are more common. At the leading edge of building they are rare.

**They leave almost no trace of their bodies.** The moss springs back. The grus gives up nothing. But the stone dust at the foot of a half-built wall is always dry, whatever the fog does, and in that dust there is sometimes a single print. It is bare, broad across the ball, with toes spread wide and long, nearly like fingers. It is the foot of something that grips when it climbs. Only one print is ever found, never a line of them. It is as if the Near stand in one place for a very long time, then are simply somewhere else.

## Their Day Is Our Fog

Put the pieces together and they show a people who keep a different day.

The tapping comes only in the fog. When the fog lifts at midday and the violet sky opens and the distances unroll, the work stops. No stone moves during the clear hours. Travelers used to think the Near hid from the sun, or from being seen. But look into the crown pools of the rosettes, those perfectly still cups of water that do not ripple in the wind. Beneath thick fog, they show clear blue sky. Sometimes they show night.

The pools are not showing another place. They are showing another *hour*. The water at the heart of a rosette is fog-water, combed from the air and gathered, and it seems to keep the sky of the air it came from. That air is not quite ours. When the fog is down and the world is a small white room for us, the Near stand under an open sky, working in clear light. What we call fog, they call morning. When our fog lifts at noon, their sky closes. The pools that show night under our noon are showing the Near asleep.

So the Near are not hiding in the fog. The fog is the place where their clear day overlaps with our blind one. It is the thin seam where two weathers touch. When visibility closes to ten paces, we have come as close to their world as our eyes allow, and the tapping reaches us across the seam. When we walk toward it, we walk along the seam rather than through it. It keeps its distance the way a horizon does.

This explains the dry dust. The dust belongs to their weather, not ours. It lies in their clear, dry morning, and our fog, however thick, cannot wet it. The warmth of the hammerstone works the same way. It is a small piece of their air left behind, as a room keeps someone's warmth after they have gone out.

Once you understand this, the whole tableland seems double. There are two days laid over each other like two sheets of vellum, each showing faintly through the other. We see their walls. They, perhaps, see our fog. Perhaps a Near mason pauses, hammerstone raised, and looks up at a column of white mist drifting across an otherwise clear morning, and thinks nothing of it, or thinks of it as weather, or as a traveler.

## How They Drink, and What They Eat

There is no food on the tableland. Travelers must carry their own, and many turn back for this reason alone. Nothing here can be eaten: not the wool-leaves, not the bitter moss, not the cushion plants packed so tight they will not tear. The tiny white flowers of the cushions taste of nothing and dissolve on the tongue like frost.

The Near seem not to need food, or not in any way we would recognize. They need water, and the rosettes provide it. In the building districts, the rosettes grow in rows in the cistern-gardens, and their crowns are always full. In the ruins, the rosettes are wild and scattered and their pools are low. In the finished districts, where the houses wait, every rosette crown is brimming. Some travelers have tipped a crown to drink and found the water tastes faintly of stone, and warm.

It is possible that the Near live on warmth itself: on the heat of work, the heat of the hammerstone, the heat that comes from striking stone on stone ten thousand times. It is possible that the tapping is not only how they build but how they eat. They would be a people who feed on their own patience. This sounds fanciful until you remember that nothing on the tableland rots. Nothing here takes energy from anything else by breaking it down. Whatever lives here must get what it needs some other way.

## Their Speech

The tapping is not random.

People who have sat in the fog for long hours, listening instead of chasing, report that it has structure. Strokes come in groups. There are pauses of different lengths. Sometimes one stream of tapping stops, and another farther off takes up the same pattern, like an answer or an echo. Sometimes three or four streams tap together in something close to unison, drifting in and out of step, the way people walking side by side drift in and out of stride.

The tapping may be work and speech at once. It may be counting, since some builders count their strokes. It may be a song whose melody is lost on us because our ears were not made for it. A traveler with a musician's ear once claimed that the district tapping he heard over four foggy mornings had a shape. It rose, gathered, and resolved, like a piece that takes days to play. He wrote down what he could. It looked like nothing much on paper. But he said that on the fourth morning, when the pattern resolved, he looked up and the wall in front of him was finished, capstone set, and the fog was pouring slowly *upward* out of the house beside it, the way it does from finished rooms.

If they speak with stone, then a wall is a sentence. The city is something they are saying, a long statement across the whole tableland, one course at a time. The roads may be its grammar. The districts would be its words. We walk through it like illiterates through a library, admiring the bindings.

## Their Old

Nothing on the tableland dies in a way that leaves a body to rot. The dead leaves of the rosettes hang for centuries in their grey skirts. The peat holds a leaf from ten thousand years ago beside one from last spring. So what happens to a Near who has finished working?

No Near body has ever been found, not in the peat, not under the cushions, not in the drifts of red bark beneath the dwarf trees. But the rosettes stand in crowds like hooded figures, and they all face slightly east.

East is the direction the city is travelling. The finished districts lie east of the ruins, and the building districts lie east of the finished ones. The leading edge of rising walls is always toward the morning. A stand of rosettes on the tableland is a crowd of hooded shapes turned to watch the city go.

This is a traveler's story, not a mason's observation, and it should be held loosely. But it is told consistently across many crossings. When a Near has set their last stone, they walk out into the moss and stand still. They let the fog comb itself through them. Their wool grows long and silver, and their old years fold down around them like a cloak. At the heart of them, where a person keeps the warm place behind the breastbone, a small clear pool gathers. It holds the sky of their day for as long as the tableland lasts.

If this is true, then drinking from a rosette crown is a kind of intimacy nobody warned you about. And the rosette gardens in the cisterns, planted in their tidy rows, are something else entirely. The Near would be tending their elders, keeping them watered at the heart of each new district, as the city moves on.

## How One Becomes Near

There is a thing the travelers do not like to talk about.

Shadows lag here. At noon, in the clear hour, a person's shadow sometimes arrives a half-step after they do and settles into place like a dog lying down. This is the first sign. Some part of you has started keeping a different time. It is only a little, a half-step, but the shadow is the part of you that belongs to the light, and the light here comes from two skies.

Then there are the doorways. The same doorway turns up in districts days apart: same cracked lintel, same splash of orange lichen on the left jamb. Sometimes it stands in a finished house. Sometimes it stands alone in the moss, framing nothing. Walking through it changes nothing that you can see. But afterward the tapping is clearer. It is nearer. It no longer retreats quite as fast when you walk toward it.

Travelers who pass through such a doorway more than once start to lose small things. They forget the look of rain. They find their food lasts longer than it should, because they are less hungry. Their hands warm up, which is noticeable in a land where hands never warm. Some report that in the fog they can hear, under the tapping, something like breathing, or like laughter, or like someone saying a word they almost know.

And some do not come back.

The objects in the niches of the finished houses are a copper pin, a bundle of wool, a carved pebble. They are small, personal, and ordinary. They are exactly the sort of thing a traveler carries: a pin for a cloak, a wad of fiber for mending, a stone picked up long ago on some beach and kept for luck.
