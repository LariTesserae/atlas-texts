# Who Keeps the Steps

## Not the ones you are looking for

At first you assume there are people.

Everyone does. The machines are too clean for any other conclusion. The rivets are bright, the gutters are clear, and the sliding joints have been lengthened with new collars again and again. You picture a crew who come up out of the cloud sea at night with lamps and oil cans, and who are gone again before the white-out. For your first few nights you try to stay awake and catch them. You sit on a high step above the cloud with your back to a column, and you watch the tower's crown and the tips of the pylons standing out of the fog like masts. You see no lamps. You hear nothing, but then you would hear nothing here anyway.

In the morning you go looking for tracks. The moss keeps footprints: your own are still there, slowly filling with seep. You find only your own. Around the base of the tower, where a crew would have to gather, the moss lies unbroken and full, as it has for a very long time.

So it is not people, or not anymore. Keep that thought, because it comes back later in another form. For now, look smaller.

## The polishers

The beetle is the first thing you properly see.

You saw it on the first day and took it for part of the glare, a bead of light on a leaf. It is the length of a fingernail. Its carapace is the same dark gunmetal as the iron, and it has the same faint blue-bronze bloom. That is not a coincidence, and it is not the beetle imitating the leaves. It is the other way round.

Go out before dawn, while the cloud is still withdrawing and everything is soaked, and put your face close to any iron surface: a pipe, a trestle, a pylon's foot. The beetles are there in their thousands, packed so close they look like a second skin on the metal. They move very slowly, in the same direction, like a tide going out. Where they have passed, the iron is darker and smoother, the way a stone step is smooth where people have walked on it for centuries. They are eating the rust. Every night the cloud gives the iron its water, the iron begins to oxidize, and every night the beetles take the oxide away before it can bloom. What they leave behind is the patina: a thin, hard, living film, part mineral and part something else. It is the same film that silvers the leaves beneath the cables, and the same one that gives your teeth that taste when you stand under the current.

This is why nothing flakes. The iron is not being maintained so much as grazed, the way a lawn is grazed, and it has become something like a lawn: a surface that is tended so continuously that it can no longer decay.

The beetles never leave the metal and never touch the moss. Watch a single one for an hour and it will not stray more than a hand's width from the pipe. They are as much a part of the machines as the rivets are.

## The threads, and the moth

The green threads on the fog nets appear each morning and are gone by evening. You were told no one had ever seen them removed. That is true, because nothing removes them. They go back in.

If you climb the tower, and you can, since the ladder rungs are bright and sound, you can look at the mesh closely at mid-morning. Each strand of the net is hollow. It is too fine to see with the eye, but a hand lens shows it. The green threads come out of the mesh, not onto it. They are fine as spider silk and pale as new fern, and they reach out into the last of the retreating fog to take the moisture the mesh missed. As the day dries, they draw back into the wire. By dusk the nets are bare iron again, to all appearances.

The moth rises at dusk, just before the cloud. You thought it was fleeing something. Watch where it goes. It rises in a slow pale column over the tower and settles on the nets, one moth to a square of mesh, and waits there, wings flat, as the fog climbs toward it. When the cloud reaches the nets and the threads begin to push out again, the moths are among them. You cannot see what passes between them in the dark. In the morning the moths are gone back into the moss, and there is a faint dusting of pale scale on the mesh that is not there by evening.

Pollination, perhaps. Or something closer to feeding a child.

## The bird, which does not sing

The dun bird lives where the pipes end. It feeds on something in the bromeliad cups, and if you are patient you learn what: small translucent larvae, the young of the polished beetle. The beetles lay their eggs in the plant-held water, the only standing water fed from the pipe spouts. The larvae grow there, and those that the bird does not take climb the stems when they are ready. Then they walk, which takes them weeks, across the moss to the nearest iron.

So the bird is a gardener of the gardeners. It thins them. If it did not, the beetles would strip the patina down to bare metal, and the metal would weaken. There are three or four birds in a day because three or four are all the valley needs.

It does not sing because nothing here is meant to be heard. Or, a stranger thought: because its song would be wasted on anything that listens the way the valley listens.

## The grazer

The grazer is the one you most want to understand. It is the one that looks back at you.

It eats cushion plants a rosette at a time, and you assume it is simply feeding. Some of these cushions are centuries old, and they grow so slowly that a rosette might represent a decade, so you are faintly alarmed. Then you notice which rosettes it takes. It chooses only the ones growing over a joint in a pipe, or pressing against a trestle leg, or crowding the spout of a water line. It is not grazing the cushions. It is pruning around the machines, keeping the moss off the collars and the cushions off the gutters, keeping the living valley from swallowing its own plumbing.

And the scratched marks on the railway trestle, the ones you took for dates: go and look at them again. They are not cut with a blade. The grooves are paired and slightly curved. They are the marks of incisors. Some are very old and worn soft, some are new and white. They are spaced not by years but by the yard the ore car moves each day. The grazer marks the car's progress. It keeps count.

When the cloud rises, it goes into the cloud. You can follow it, if you are foolish and have a rope. You will lose it within twenty yards, because its coat is the color of fog. But before you lose it you may see it stop beneath a pylon line and unfold its ears for the first time. They are enormous, thin as leaves, and veined. It lifts them toward the cables and stands very still.

It is listening to the current. The current that makes no sound a human ear can find.

## What they all serve

By now you have understood that the valley has a staff: polishers, weavers, thinners, a pruner who is also a clerk. You understand that every living thing you have seen is employed. And a staff implies a house, or a master.

Here is where the image changes.

There is no master in the tower. You climbed the tower. It is empty, all lattice and wind and mesh. There is no one in the generator shed, which holds only the slow turning of the banked dynamos and a smell like rain on hot stone. There is no one at the end of any pipe.

What lives in the Blanched Steps is not in any one place, because it is the place.

Consider its body. The rock is its bone. The bone is new and still growing, splitting cleanly as it lifts, so that every fracture is a small growth plate. The moss and cushions are its flesh, holding water the way flesh does. The fault lines are its veins, drawing water down into whatever lies beneath the shelf. The iron pipes are veins as well, but outward-running ones. They are a circulation laid on top of the old circulation, carrying the night's drink out to the extremities. The fog nets are its lungs, or its gills, and they breathe once a day, in the dark, a single long inhalation six miles wide. The generators are the slow heart. They turn at the pace of something that measures its life in centuries, and they beat faster in the wet season, as a heart does when there is more to carry.

The cables are its nerves, and the current is what it thinks.

That is why the plants lean toward the cables. They are not reaching for a second sun. They are growing toward the thought, as tissue grows toward the nerve that feeds it. Where the cable runs lowest, the leaves are warmest and most metallic, because they are closest to the mind. The blue corona at dusk is not a leak. It is the valley waking. It keeps its days at night, as some creatures do, and it comes most alive when the cloud fills its basin and every surface is drinking at once. The faint prickle on your arms beneath the line is what it feels like to stand inside a single idea.

What does it think about? Slowly, and without hurry, about the same things everything alive thinks about. Water. Light. Growing.

## The one thing it does on purpose

Everything else in the valley responds. The generators quicken with the cloud, the threads follow the fog, the beetles follow the rust. Only one thing in the Steps seems to be willed, and that is the ore car.

It moves a yard a day. It is loaded with grey rock. It has travelled less than half a mile in a lifetime, which means that in the valley's own time it is moving quite briskly. It is the one deliberate gesture the valley makes, the way a sleeping body might move one finger.

Follow the railway west, past the car, toward the raw peaks that still smoke in their upper cracks. The trestle runs out a long way ahead of the car, and at its end you find the reason for the car.

It is a column. It is taller than most, freshly split, its faces clean and white. Bolted to its top, in a ring, are eight plates of iron. The plates are new: dark, bright, already carrying a faint film of beetles. Around the foot of the column the moss has been cleared, rosette by rosette, in a perfect circle. The pruning is recent. A single short length of pipe, no longer than your arm, rises from the circle's edge, capped, waiting.

Nobody laid these plates. No crew has come up out of the cloud with lamps and oil cans. The iron is in the rock, as iron is in all andesite. The ore car is carrying the rock east to the shed, where the heart turns, and something is being drawn out of it there, over decades. The shed's roof is buried in cushion, but there is a warm place in the cushion on its western end, a place where your hand does not want to stay. From that warmth, over years, the valley has been sending iron back out along its own lines, a little at a time, to this column at the edge of the smoking ground.

It is building a second tower.

You understand then what the sliding joints meant, the ones extended many times, each collar slightly newer than the last, like growth rings. They are exactly that. It is not being kept. It is growing up. The first tower was a sprout. This is the second leaf.

## And the builders

Here the old thought comes back, the one about people.

Someone did build the first tower. The rivets are made the way people make rivets, and the railway gauge is a human gauge. At some point, long ago, a crew did climb out of the cloud bank with lamps and oil cans, raised iron on the tallest column, and strung the first cable across the valley.

What became of them is not recorded, but you can guess from the tempo. They came up to catch the fog and water something. Perhaps a mine, perhaps a garden, perhaps both. They stayed. The valley was very quiet and very slow, and it drank every sound they made. The beetles came to the iron, the moss came to their doorsteps, and their machines began to turn at the pace of the place instead of the pace of the people. It may be that they did not leave at all. They may simply have slowed until they were no longer at a speed where anyone could see them. The current in the cables still carries something, and it is not only the fog's arithmetic.

The grazer has long ears and large dark eyes, and it keeps count. It regards you steadily from a few yards off, without fear. Then it turns back to its work, as if it had recognized something in your face and found it not quite recent enough to matter.

## What it makes of you

It notices you. Not the way a person would. The way skin notices a splinter, or soil notices a seed.

On your third or fourth morning you will find that the buckle of your pack has gone dark and smooth, and that your knife is brighter than when you arrived, its small nicks softened. There will be two or three polished beetles asleep in the fold of your coat, and they will not leave the metal of your zipper when you try to shake them out. If you stay a week, a fine silver fur will have begun on the leather of your boots, very short and very soft. Your watch will have lost minutes, then hours. You will not have noticed the losing.

The grazer will come closer each day. The pale moth will rise from your blanket at dusk.

This is not hostility, and it is not really welcome either. It is just what the valley does to everything that stays: it tends it. It takes whatever is iron in you and keeps it from rusting, and it takes whatever is soft in you and waters it. It slows you, patiently, to its own speed, so that you can go on with it.

## What lives here

So here is what lives in the Blanched Steps, as nearly as it can be said.

A body of new stone, still rising, which has learned to wear iron the way a reef wears coral. A garden that grew itself a plumbing, or a plumbing that grew itself a garden. There is no telling which came first, and the valley does not appear to remember either. A hundred small attendants, each keeping one thing from ending: the rust from spreading, the moss from choking, the beetles from overgrazing, the count from being lost. A slow heart in a buried shed. A thought, carried along a line of pylons, that brightens a little every dusk.

And somewhere inside all that, too slowed to see, perhaps the people who first came up to catch the cloud and did not want to go back down into the grey.

It is young. That is the last thing to understand, and the hardest, because everything about it is so slow that it feels ancient. But the rock is newborn. The fracture faces are still white. The second tower has eight plates on it and a pipe the length of your arm. What lives in the Steps has only just begun. It is a seedling in the sense a mountain range is a seedling, and it is reaching, as every seedling reaches, west toward the warm smoking ground and up toward the hard small sun.

Each night it drinks. Each night it thinks a little more. Each night the new column at the end of the rails is a hair's breadth taller, and the cloud rises around it for the first time, and a fine green thread, pale as new fern, pushes out of the iron to taste the fog.
