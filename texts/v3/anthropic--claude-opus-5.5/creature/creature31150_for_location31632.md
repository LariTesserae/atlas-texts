# What Lives in the Roost

Start with one of them.

She hangs about two-thirds of the way up the dome on the eastern curve. She is not quite in the hot crush of the apex, but close enough that the warmth reaches her from above like a hand held over her head. She weighs less than a coin. Her forearm, the long bone that holds the leading edge of her wing, is the length of a fingernail's width times four. She has hung on this curve for, as nearly as anything here can be counted, nineteen years. Small bats live much longer than anyone expects. She has made the exodus perhaps six thousand times. She has come back six thousand times, which is the harder thing.

She doesn't know the room by sight. She knows it by the way her voice comes back. The dome returns her calls to her rounded and slightly late, and from that lateness she knows where she is, the same way you know your own stairwell in the dark. She knows the smell of her own patch of rock, which is also the smell of herself and her mother and her mother's mother, soaked into the phosphate rind. She knows the voice of her pup. Last spring, in a nursery cluster of forty thousand pink, identical, shrieking young, she found hers every dawn by its cry, which was unlike any of the others, though you would need to be her to hear that.

If you asked what lives in this room, she would be a good answer. It is not the whole answer.

---

Because she cannot live here alone. Nothing her size can. Put one of her in this chamber by herself and the stone would take her heat in a day. The room is cold at heart. It is limestone at the top of a mountain, the same temperature as the sky. It is only warm because there are a million of her. She is not so much a resident as a single ember in a banked fire.

So widen the view, and something else comes into focus.

It is an animal whose body is a room. Its skull is on the outside: thirty meters of fluted rock, pale and fossil-studded. Its fur is on the inside, and it lines the dome in a pelt a hand thick that shimmers when it moves. It is a creature turned inside out, as if something had swallowed the cold and wrapped itself around the swallowing.

It has a body temperature. You can take it. Put your palm on the floor and it is the warmth of a sleeping dog. Raise a thermometer on a pole to the apex and it reads nearly the temperature of blood. At dusk its temperature drops, the way yours drops when you sleep. At dawn it rises again.

It breathes. Its breath is not the hour-long dusk exodus, although that is the great exhalation. Its breath is the slow, humid pressure that leaves through the slot all day and puts the only green on the mountain at its lips. The liverworts and the one hand-sized fern are the moss on a sleeper's mouth. They are the proof, visible from outside, that something in there is alive and breathing out.

It sweats, and the sweat is its weather: warm drops falling from the fur onto the floor, heaviest at dawn when it has just come home and is crowded and flushed.

It drinks its own sweat. The pool at the back is where the room's water gathers before it seeps away into the mountain. Whatever the valley people taste in their trickle a kilometer down, it is not really the sky. It is the room's breath, filtered through rock. They have been drinking this animal for as long as they have lived below it.

---

It has organs, if you want to see them that way.

The floor is its gut. Everything it drops lands on the floor and is taken apart: by beetles in their seething millions, by springtails and mites, by mold furring the older dunes grey and mustard. The floor digests. The dunes are its stomach, laid open and full of workers.

The centipedes and snakes are something closer to a tithe. They are the price of being this much food in one place. Every night the snakes at the mouth lean into the river of bats and eat, and every night the loss closes over like water.

The fruit bats are its hands. It has no fruit of its own, so it reaches down through the clouds with them every night, into orchards and fig trees it will never see, and brings the sweetness back up in their bellies. What it cannot digest, it plants. The seeds land in the richest soil on the mountain and try to become the valleys again: pale fig, pale guava, threadlike and yellow-leaved, reaching for a sun that is not there. They are the animal dreaming of where its food comes from. The dream never gets past a hand's height. There are always seedlings, they are always young, and they are always failing, which is how dreams behave.

And the swiftlets are its other lung.

The bats leave at dusk, but the room is never truly empty, because the swiftlets keep the opposite hours. By day, while the bats sleep, the birds are mostly gone, stitching the air above the cloud deck for insects and returning in fast dark flickers. By night they are home, tucked into their wax-pale cups of hardened spit, ticking to each other in the dry, countable clicks that are the only voice here a human ear can follow. So the animal breathes in two shifts. It never fully sleeps and it is never fully awake. Some part of it is always in the dark, keeping the walls warm by a degree, keeping the rhythm going.

When the bats are gone and the swiftlets tick in the cooling stone, it is easy to imagine that you are hearing the thing's pulse slow down and turn over, like someone muttering in sleep.

---

It remembers.

Not the way she remembers her pup's voice, but the way a tree remembers drought. Its memory is on the floor, in the cut faces of the dunes: dark bands from wet years, pale bands from dry ones, going down through damp peat into stone. A year when the valley fruit failed is in there as a thin line. A year of plenty is a thick one. Somewhere down there, pressed flat, is the year the nest-gatherers came with their cane ladders. It is probably a slight thinning in the swiftlet layer, a season when the birds were fewer.

It remembers the gatherers in the walls, too. The patch they harvested is still sparse, still regrowing, a scar more than a lifetime old. Their lamp still sits on the ledge. The soot above it is the only dark mark on the rind. Their cord hangs in the flowstone, half swallowed.

That last detail tells you something about its temperament. It keeps things. It does not throw anything out. The shells of an old sea are in its skull. The knotted cord will join them. It grows its stone over whatever is left in it, slowly and without malice, the way an oyster works around grit. Give it a few centuries and the gatherers' rope will be a fossil among fossils, filed in the wall alongside the ammonites.

---

And it grows.

This is the part that takes the longest to see, because it happens on a scale nothing in the room can live long enough to notice.

The acid of its waste and breath eats the limestone. The rind on the walls is the stone halfway to becoming something else, soft enough to scratch with a fingernail. Each year the room is a little larger. Each year the dome is a little higher. It was a crack once. It is a bell now. It is hollowing itself out from inside, and the direction it hollows fastest is up, where the heat and the damp collect, where the bodies press thickest and breathe hardest.

Up is toward the crest.

The ridge above is limestone scored into knife-edges by rain. Between the apex of the dome, where she and her hundred thousand neighbors hang in layers, and the open sky, there is a thickness of stone. Nobody has measured it. It is finite. It gets thinner every year.

So here is the animal more fully. It is not only a room full of bats. It is a slow digging toward light. It is doing exactly what the seedlings on its floor are doing: stretching, pale and blind, toward a sun it cannot see and cannot survive. The seedlings take weeks to fail. The room will take thousands of years. Its gesture is the same.

One day the stone at the apex will be thin enough to crack. Then a slot of daylight will open in the top of the bell, the size of a hand, then the size of a door. The warm breath will stop leaving politely by the side and pour out the roof like smoke from a chimney. The room will lose its heat. The fur will thin. The rain will stop. The pool will dry to a tea-colored crust. And the green that has spent all this time clinging to the lips of the slot, the liverworts and moss and that single stubborn fern, will creep inward and downward and cover the dunes. The dying seedlings on the floor will suddenly have a sun.

What lives there will have finished growing, and in finishing, it will die.

---

It has done this before.

Go down the lee side of the mountain, below the cloud deck, where the scrub begins, and look for a particular kind of hollow. It is round, bell-shaped, and open to the sky, with grass on its floor and a fig tree in its center much bigger than anything should be at that altitude. Its walls are pale and chalky with a soft rind that marks under a fingernail. Near one edge, if you look closely, the rock is worn smooth and faintly glossy, polished by something that isn't feet. Dig in the floor and you will find dark and pale bands, banks of old guano gone to stone, a calendar that stops.

That is one of its ancestors: an older room that dug upward until it broke into daylight and became a garden. The fig at its center grew from a seed dropped by some fruit bat, the one dream that finally made it past a hand's height.

The valley people graze goats there and say the grass is the sweetest on the mountain. They are right about that, too.

---

So how does something like this have children?

Not with bodies. Its body is stone and can't travel. It passes itself on by habit. Somewhere on the lee side, lower down or along the ridge, there is a new crack. It is narrow, a hand's width, just wide enough to hold a few hundred bats hanging in a single sheet against the rock. It is only a little warmer than the air outside. It has a faint smell of ammonia and the first trace of a rind. There is no fern at its mouth yet, but the stone around its lip is darker, a little damp.

That is a bud. The bats who hang there learned to hang there from the bats who hung there before them. When the old room begins to fail, when the apex thins and draughts start coming through the ceiling, that crack will already know how to be warm. The colony will pour into it one dawn and stay, and it will start, very slowly, to eat itself larger.

What lives here is not a bat, or a million bats. It is the pattern the bats keep making: a warm, wet, loud hollow in cold stone, fed from below, rained on by its own breath, digging slowly upward toward a death that will be a garden. It is less an animal than a life cycle with a room at the center of it, shaped like a bell, and it has been ringing on this mountain far longer than there have been people to hear it.

---

But come back, at the end, to the one.

Dawn. The river of bodies is pouring back through the slot, and the wall near the mouth is getting another few billion strokes of wing and fur, another fraction of a fraction of polish. She is somewhere in it, nineteen years old, less than a coin's weight, tired from the valleys. She comes in over the snakes, through the grey, and into the black, and she calls. The room answers her, rounded and slightly late. She knows by the lateness where she is. She rises up the eastern curve into the heat. She finds her patch of rind by its smell. She hangs.

The drip begins to quicken around her. The warmth starts to climb.

She does not know she is an ember, or an organ, or a cell in something that dreams in fig seedlings and remembers in guano. She does not know the ceiling above her is getting thinner, that the sky is on the other side of it, or that someday, long after her, the roof she sleeps under will open and let the green in. She knows it is warm here, that it is home, and that the voice coming back to her is the voice of the room.

It is the same voice as hers, returned. That may be the truest description of what lives here: a voice that learned what shape it was by listening to itself come back.
