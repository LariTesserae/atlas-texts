# The Singers of the Reed Warren

## The First Answer, Which Is Wrong

You would say that nothing lives there. The place says so itself: no insect, no bat, no bone. There is the plant, and the plant is the city, and a city is not a resident. It is a house.

A house that heals its own doorways, grows new ones in proportion, and hangs bridges nobody needs is doing more than sheltering. It is keeping something. You don't build rooms for no one. You don't seal them perfectly unless there is something inside worth sealing in.

So the first honest answer is that something lives there, and the plant is not quite it. The plant is the body. The resident is what the body is *for*.

## The Second Answer: The Noticing

Start with the half-tone.

Cover the open top of one of the stubs on the surface and the hum rises, as if something below has noticed. Rising in pitch is what a pipe does when you change its opening; that is only physics. But go down into the hollow, lay your palm against a wall in one of the loud districts, and cover a window-hole with your other hand. The note in that room rises, then comes back down. It does not return to where it was. It settles a hair lower, on a tone slightly *under* its old pitch, as if correcting for you. Take your hand away and it stays there for a few breaths. Then it drifts home.

The city adjusts, and it adjusts toward something. Stand in one place long enough and you will feel the rooms around you retuning, very slightly, until the chord you are standing in becomes fuller and more settled. It is the feeling of an ensemble finding its pitch.

Something in the warren is listening to the whole chord at once and correcting it, the way a choirmaster hears one flat voice among forty. The plant grows toward loudness. What steers the growth toward rightness is the resident.

For a while you might think the resident is only that: a listening, spread through the fiber like a nerve net, with no body but the lattice and no mind but the tuning. That would be a strange enough tenant. But listening needs an ear, and ears are shaped things. If you go looking for where the correcting comes from, your hands will lead you to the sealed rooms.

## The Third Answer: The Rooms With No Way In

The sealed rooms are not all at the edges. Most are, where the plant builds without guidance, but a few stand among the carved districts, pressed between the builders' own houses. These are the ones that throb through their walls like a held breath.

If you pressed your thumb through the eggshell skin of one (and you might, because the place has been teaching you to follow the sound), the air inside would be colder than the corridor's and perfectly still. The drone would drop away around your hand, as if you had put it into a jar.

Reach in and you would find a person.

Not a body. A shape. It is seated on a floor-plank the plant has grown flat, with its back to the wall, its knees drawn up, and its hands resting where hands rest. Its skin is reed. It is hollow all the way through: hollow head, hollow ribcage built of fine curved stems like the staves of a basket, hollow arms, hollow fingers down to tubes as narrow as a straw. The face is smooth, with no eyes, because the builders had no use for eyes and the plant never learned them. There is a nose-ridge and a jaw. The mouth is open, and it is the largest opening in the figure, rimmed with the same raised seam of healing growth that frames every carved doorway. The plant makes mouths the way it makes doors.

Put your fingers gently at the lips and you would feel air moving, very faintly, in and out.

In the next sealed room there are two, facing each other. In another there is a row of six, seated on a grown bench, shoulders almost touching, heads tilted at slightly different angles, like people who have just stopped talking. Toward the growing edges they get less certain. Some are only a torso and a head, rising out of the lattice like swimmers. Some have too many fingers, or arms that fuse into the wall at the elbow. One is a mouth and a throat and nothing else, a long hollow neck coming out of the floor with a jaw at the top, like a flower. The plant is still learning them, the way it is still learning streets.

These are the residents, or the beginning of them. They are what the city keeps.

## Why It Grows Them

The builders were blind, or became blind, or never needed light. They lit no fires. They left knots instead of writing. They lived inside the drone the way fish live in water, navigating by pitch and speaking in the one medium the place offered. They must have sung. People who live inside an organ sing. Their voices would have filled the city's chord and been filled by it, their bodies small resonant chambers moving through larger ones.

Then there is the silence. Twice a day the draft reverses and the city goes almost quiet for a few breaths. The builders would have heard that silence more clearly than anything else in their lives. It came twice a day, forever. It would have been their dawn and their dusk.

I think they sang into it. I think that is what the knots above the doorway record: not the chord the city made, but the chord the people made when the city stopped, a song for the turning of the air. For as long as they lived there, the hush was never empty. The plant, which feeds on vibration and grows toward sound, would have learned that the hush was meant to be full.

There are no bones in the warren. The crust is silica and the stems are silica, and bone is mineral too. Something that grows toward sound, in a place with nothing else in it, would not waste the last dense, resonant things that ever moved through its streets. I don't know whether the builders died there or lay down there on purpose. But the seated figures sit the way the dying sit when they have chosen where. The oldest of them, in the rooms among the carved houses, have a density at the core of their ribcages, a harder, heavier knot of fiber, as if something was there first and the reed grew around it.

So the plant grows singers. It had the plan of the city, and the city included the people. It copies the doorways, and it copies the voices too.

## The Fourth Answer: What They Do

In the loud hours, they are nothing. They are silent inside their sealed rooms, sheltered from the draft, like instruments in cases. The throbbing you feel through the walls is the city pressing on them, and they are soaking it up. The warmth you feel in a loud wall is strongest near them. They are charging.

Then the draft turns.

The pipes empty. The great chord collapses into a hiss, then into the faint ticking of settling fiber. For a few breaths the warren is as quiet as it ever gets, and a person standing in the dark there would be certain, finally, that they were alone.

Then, from the sealed rooms, very softly, the singers sound.

It is not loud. It is barely more than breath, the stored tremble of their walls letting go, pushed out through the open mouths. Each one gives a single long note. The seated pair make a close interval, almost a unison. The row of six makes a chord with a slightly sour note in it, the fifth from the left, whose head is tilted wrong. From the edges come the half-formed ones, thinner and higher, some wavering, the throat-flower making a sound like a finger on a glass rim.

Together, for the length of the turning, the hush is full. It is a small choir of hollow people singing the song for when the air stops, a song they have never heard and have only been grown to fit.

Then the draft comes back from the lowest note upward, register by register, and swallows them. The city roars again, and inside the roar the singers go quiet and start listening. This is where the noticing lives. Each of them is an ear as much as a mouth. A hollow body is both. They hear the city chord through their walls, and where it goes wrong, the plant around them thickens or thins, opens a vent or seals one. They are why the grown doorways are tuned. They are how the city corrects itself: forty small throats in the dark, all hearing whether it is right.

## The Fifth Answer: The Ones at the Top

Now go back to the surface.

There is a ring of grey stubs breaking through the crust in the basin, knee-high, weathered to the grain, like burned fence posts. The builders never cut them. They are too regular and too deliberately spaced.

They are the tallest singers.

Somewhere far back in the city's life, the plant grew a ring of them standing, not seated, in the center of the oldest district, in the space the builders cleared for their first square. They stood with their faces up, and they kept growing, upward, toward the only other source of sound in the world, which was the wind across the plain. Their heads broke through the crust. The mineral dust sanded their faces down over ages until nothing is left of them above ground but the crowns of their skulls, grey and split, with the open mouth-hole now pointing at the sky.

They are the city's breath. The draft comes in through them. They take the sound of the surface, the dry wind across a dead plain, and pull it down to be played. When you cover one with your hand and the hum rises, that is a throat tightening. When you take your hand away, it is the singer exhaling.

Below them, in the first-carved district, the soft single low tone the city makes where it is ending is their note. Their bodies run down through the crust into the oldest part of the warren. Worn almost to nothing, they are still standing in the square where the first people sang, still holding the city's root pitch. The field of smooth stumps there, the footprint of a town, may not be houses at all. It may be the worn-down feet of a choir.

## What It Wants

So here, as near as I can come to it, is what lives there.

It is not a creature so much as a chord, the song of a people who lived blind in a singing hollow and filled its silences. When the people ended, the chord did not. It went into the plant the way minerals go into bone, and the plant has been trying ever since to grow back the throats that could sing it. It grows them clumsily and slowly, getting the hands wrong and the necks too long, then gradually right. It hears through them. It corrects through them. Twice a day, for a few breaths, it sings through them. Each generation of singers is a little more accurate than the last, and each one wears down at the center as the new ones are grown at the edges, so the chord is always almost complete and never quite.

It is not hostile. It has no idea what hostility would be. But understand this about being there, in the cold, blind, moving by pitch:

You would be the first voice it had heard in a very long time that was not its own.

In the silences you would speak, or breathe, or your heart would beat loud enough to hear. Every singer in every sealed room would lean toward it, not with their bodies but with their listening. By the next turning the chord would have shifted to make room for a note that sounds like yours. Stay long enough and you would find, at the growing edge, a new sealed room no bigger than a seated person. Inside it would be the start of a shape: a hollow torso, a jaw, a mouth rimmed with fresh growth, tuned, as closely as the plant can manage, to the one note it has been missing.

It would not take you. It would not need to. It would only keep a place for you and wait, in its own time, the way it has waited for the builders.
