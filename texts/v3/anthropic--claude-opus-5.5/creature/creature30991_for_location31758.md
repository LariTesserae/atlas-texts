# Those Who Keep the Sea

## First, the Ones You Can See

Start with the glass-snails, because they are honest. They are exactly what they appear to be: grazers, slow, transparent, living on the rust-velvet and leaving clean paths that show only after days. If you hold one up to the green light you can see what it has eaten moving through it as a faint brown thread. You can see its heart, a bead that contracts perhaps four times an hour. Its shell is not calcium but something closer to the glassy skin of the new basalt, as if the snail had learned to make its house by copying the rock's first breath of cooling.

Snails that die are not eaten. Nothing here eats them. Their shells stay where they fall, wedged into the fissures under the velvet, and over the long ages they fill the cracks. This is one of the ways the rock gets its first rounding. The mountains are being glazed from the inside by the houses of patient animals.

Then there are the ones that make the rigging. The canopies' tethers are not root-threads grown down from the bladders, as they look. They are spun from below. Among the ribbon-plants, clinging to the stems and nearly invisible, live small many-legged creatures with the jointed delicacy of shrimp and the habits of spiders. Call them riggers. Each one pays out a single line from a gland near its tail. It climbs, rising a hand's width a day, until it reaches a canopy and fastens there. A tether takes a rigger most of its life. When it arrives it does not come down. It stays at the top, folded against the bladder where the line joins, and the thin musical creak you hear when a canopy shifts is partly the line and partly the rigger, whose body has hardened into a kind of knot.

So every canopy is held up by, or held down by, hundreds of small lifetimes of climbing. Nobody is sure which way the holding goes. Without the tethers the canopies would rise to the upper surface and spread there like scum. Without the canopies the riggers would have nowhere to climb toward. They seem to have settled the matter long ago in each other's favor.

## The Watchers

The pale fish with the large eyes are harder to describe, because what they are doing is not obvious.

They hang between the ribbons, motionless, and turn their whole bodies to follow any movement. They do not feed that anyone has seen. They do not school. They never approach, and they never flee. If you stay still long enough among them, they stop watching you and go back to watching what they were watching before, which is usually nothing at all. They seem to be looking at the water itself.

They are young. That is the first thing to understand about them. They are not small fish. They are the larval form of something that will not finish growing for longer than a human settlement lasts. Their eyes are so large because they are learning. They are memorizing the sea: its currents, which are nearly nothing, the drift of the canopies, the way the light falls in stripes. Most of all they are memorizing its shape: where it ends, how it curves around the peaks, where it lies flat and level across the passes.

They are learning how big it is, because one day they will have to hold it.

## The Broad Ones

Some of the watchers survive the centuries among the ribbons. Slowly, over more years than anyone has counted, they lose their eyes. The eyes don't fail. They are absorbed, drawn back into the head as if they are no longer needed, as if everything they were for has already been taken in. The body flattens and widens, its pale color darkening to gray-green. It rises out of the ribbon-forest into the open water above the valleys.

That is the broad one of the stories. The stories are right that it is wider than a canopy. They undercount how many there are, because what is seen, rarely and never close, is only the youngest of them. The young still move around where you can see them. They cross from ridge to ridge over an afternoon, and their shadow passes over the paddle-leaves, which close beneath it the way an eye closes when a hand passes over it, and open again afterward.

What the broad ones are doing in that long crossing is grazing, once, on the only thing they ever eat. They eat the gold. When the canopies release their seed in the warmest weeks and the whole held sea hazes amber, the broad ones rise and open. Their undersides are finely ridged, like the gills of a mushroom, and they pass slowly through the seed-clouds and take in what they can. Most of the seed escapes them and settles into the velvet to grow. Some does not, and it is enough to last them until the next year.

A broad one is not blind. It feels light across its whole back and pressure across its whole belly. It knows where it is in the sea the way a hand knows where it is in the dark. It knows the sea's edges because it once watched them for three hundred years.

## What Holds the Water

Here the description has to change what it thought it was describing.

The underside of the sea is a skin. Everyone who has stood in the foothills knows that; it is the first thing they say. What they don't say, because they cannot see it from below, is that the skin is alive. It is not a membrane of surface tension or some strange property of the water. It is a single tissue, thinner than a leaf in most places, stretched level beneath the whole length of the range and past the horizon both ways. It is breathing.

It is made of the broad ones.

When a broad one grows old enough, and nobody knows what age that is, it stops crossing valleys. It sinks. Very slowly, over a season or a year, it descends through the canopies and ribbons and past the peaks to the lowest reach of the held water. There it spreads. It thins. At its edges it grows into the edges of the ones that came before it until you cannot tell where one ends and another begins. It becomes part of the Hem.

The Hem is what holds the sea up. It is not a lid over the lowlands. It is more like a sling, or a hand held flat beneath a bowl. Every broad one that ever lived here and grew old is in it, still alive, spread to the thinness of breath, and every one of them remembers the sea's shape. They learned it with their huge eyes when they were young and kept it with their bodies when they were grown. Now they carry it with what they have become. The water is heavier than it should be when you press your hand up into it. What you are feeling is weight that is being carried.

This explains the things that looked like strangeness.

The threads that hang down from the underside, a few fingers thick and falling so slowly they look like glass, are the Hem feeding. The Hem lowers them into the dry air to taste the dust of the lowlands. It takes in the fine mineral powder of fresh rockfall, the pollen of the thorny scrub, whatever the dry world breathes upward. When a thread has taken what it can, it beads and draws back, and the beads drift up and are absorbed. The threads never reach the ground because the Hem does not want the ground. It wants what rises from it.

The pale crust at the waterline is the Hem's shed. Where it meets the rock it lays down a mineral skin, year after year, and seals itself to the mountain there so the sea cannot leak along the stone. The crust is a scar and a gasket and a record, all at once.

The slow tide, the rise and fall with a period of a decade or more, is the Hem breathing. It breathes the way a very large and very old thing breathes: rarely, deeply, and with the whole of itself. When it exhales, the sea drops, and a band of forest is left hanging in the air. The Hem does not abandon that band. The droplets that keep it barely alive are the Hem reaching back for it, tending what it has briefly let go, until it breathes in and takes the forest back.

When a slab breaks loose above and drifts down through the water and falls out the bottom trailing its brief rain, it has passed through the Hem. The Hem parts for it. Ancient animals open, just enough, to let a stone through, then close again and heal within a day. The rumble that comes long after the crack is not only the stone. Part of it is the Hem settling back.

The low hum in the rock, fluctuating over hours, may be the basalt cooling. Some of it may also be the Hem, pressed against the mountains at its edges, humming the way something humming to itself does when it has been holding something for a very long time and has stopped thinking of it as effort.

## The Order of It

So the life of the place is mostly one life, told in stages.

A seed of gold settles into the velvet. What hatches from it (and it does hatch, though the velvet hides it) is a watcher. The watcher rises into the ribbons and learns the sea for centuries. It becomes a broad one and crosses the valleys, eating the gold once a year. Then it sinks and spreads and joins the Hem, and the Hem holds the sea, and the sea holds the forest. The forest makes the canopies, and the canopies make the gold.

The riggers and snails are neighbors in this, not parts of it. They are companions the long cycle has permitted. That is why the fauna seem incidental, as if the place has not fully decided to allow them. The place is mostly busy with the one thing it is doing, and the one thing it is doing is keeping the sea.

## The Rule Underneath, Revised

It was said that the water is not held against its nature, that the strangeness belongs to the dry world, where water runs out and falls and cannot keep its shape. That is true, but it is not the whole of it.

When the range shouldered up out of the ancient warm sea, it lifted everything that lived there. Most of it drained away and fell and was lost. Some of it held on. Something already alive in that old ocean spread itself flat under the rising water and would not let it go. It has been not letting go ever since, one generation laid over another, each one learning with its eyes what the last one carries with its body.

The sea is held by memory made physical: by the refusal of a lineage of animals to forget how deep their home was.

## At the Line

There are people, a few, in the dappled foothills below. They live where the light moves like a riverbed. They collect the stones that fall out of the sky, smoothed and warm and faintly sweet-smelling from their slow passage through the sea. They do not climb up. They know, or half-know, what the ceiling is.

Some of them, when they are old, walk up to the waterline in the evening. They stand on the raw black basalt, cutting their palms on the glass edges, and reach one hand up through the surface into the warmth.

The Hem is very large and very slow, and it feels almost nothing so small as a hand. Still, the old people say that if you wait long enough, the weight you are pushing against eases. It does not happen much. It is about what a sleeping person gives when you lean against them.

They say it is the sea noticing you. It is closer to the truth to say it is the Hem, which is made of the long-dead watchers, doing what it learned to do when it was young and had eyes.

It is watching back.
