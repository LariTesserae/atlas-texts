# What Lives in the Throat

## I.

The easy answer is everyone. The potters, the dyers with their blue-black forearms, the rope-twisters, the water-haulers calling rhythm, the laundry-women whose slaps come up the shaft like applause. The pigeons, the swifts, the bats, the geckos, the frogs that sound like cattle. The monkeys on the fig-root highway. The kestrels. The snake nobody sees.

But none of them live there, not really. The people climb out at dusk. The swifts leave every dawn and return every dusk; the Throat is their bed, not their world. Even the bats, which seem to belong most of all, pour out nightly into the open sky and hunt over fields the Throat has never seen. The pigeons forage in the town. The kestrels nest on a temple roof two streets away. Everything that breathes in the Throat also leaves it.

Ask who never leaves, and the list gets short. The fish, perhaps. The ferns. The fig, which is only halfway in.

And one other.

## II.

Start with the sound, because that is where it is easiest to notice.

The Throat takes every noise made inside it and folds it. A laugh becomes a chorus. A word becomes a smear. Visitors assume the smearing is just what happens to sound in stone, a physical fact like heat rising. But the hum-families of the lower galleries, the ones who have worked the washing stones and pulley-frames for so long that their speech has sanded itself down into tone, will tell you otherwise if you ask them quietly and at the right hour. They will say the words are not *smeared*. They are *taken*.

Every word spoken in the Throat loses something on its way up. The edges of it, the consonants, the small hard parts that make *water* different from *daughter*. They are not lost. They fall. They settle, the way the dye settles in its fans down the steps, the way ash settles in the niches. They drift down seven flights and come to rest at the bottom, in the still air just above the pool, where the mist forms.

Something gathers them there.

For a long time, the hum-families say, it was nothing but that: a sediment. A silt of broken syllables lying on the water. Centuries of *careful* and *mine* and *come here* and *no, the blue one*, pared down to their grit. It had no more intention than a sandbar has.

But a sandbar, given enough river, becomes an island. Things root on it.

## III.

It is not only sound the Throat wears away.

Look at the steps, scooped into bowls by feet. Where did the stone go? Look at the carved figures in the niches, their faces smoothed to eggs. Where did the faces go? The noses and the lips and the eyelids, the careful work of someone whose name no one remembers. They went where everything worn goes in a funnel: down.

The potters say there is no clay anywhere near as fine as the clay at the pool's edge. They are right, and they have never asked themselves why. It is ground as fine as flour because it has been ground for centuries: grit off the treads, dust off the carvings, brick-powder from crumbling family walls, lime off the plaster, all of it washed down by the rare rains, down the bowls of the steps, a finger's depth at a time, into the pool. The clay at the bottom of the Throat is the Throat's own worn-off skin. It is faces. It is the thousandth footstep of a grandmother. It is the thumbprint on a mud coin, dissolved.

So the thing that gathers at the bottom does not only have broken words. It has broken faces too. It has the pressed marks of every family: the spirals, the three dots, the crude birds, the handprints, softened into slurry and mixed together as they never were on the walls. Every family is in the clay at the bottom, and none of them apart.

This, the hum-families say, is the body it grew. Not a body you can see. A body that is a *place*: the pool, the lowest landing, the air just above the water. It is a silt that learned to hold its shape.

## IV.

Now watch it, and you will find it is not hidden at all.

**It breathes.** Every dawn the water rises a hand's breadth and falls back within the hour. The town calls this *the well breathing in*, and thinks it is speaking in metaphor. The first bucket drawn at the high mark is the best water, and people argue about who gets it without asking what makes it best. It is the water that was inside something, briefly, and came out again.

**It has a reach.** The mist that forms in the cool hours hangs in the lowest landing like wool and never climbs higher than the second flight. Mist does not usually know where to stop. This mist does. That is how far it extends, the way your breath on a cold morning extends only so far from your mouth before it is just air. Stand in the mist and you are standing, very slightly, inside it. People who wash clothes in the mist-hours speak even more softly than usual. They do not know why. They say it is the damp.

**It has eyes, or something like them.** The small silver fish flicker in the pool, and everyone knows not to catch them, and no one can say why. The hum-families can say why, though they rarely do. The fish are where its attention goes. Not eyes exactly. More like the glints of a thought turning over. When a child leans over the pool's edge, the fish gather beneath her face. When a stranger comes down the steps, they scatter and then regroup, all at once, the way your gaze snaps to a movement at the edge of a room. Catch one and you would have caught a piece of its noticing. No one knows what it would do then. No one has tried in so long that the rule has outlived its reason. That, the hum-families say, is exactly how it should be.

**It has patience.** The same patience as the seep that feeds the pool from below: slow, unbothered, reliable. It does not hurry. It has never needed to. Everything comes down to it eventually.

## V.

What does it want?

At first I would have said nothing. That it simply accumulates, as the niches accumulate offerings, as the oldest ones seal shut with lime and droppings. A living sediment. A reef.

But reefs do not choose, and this does.

Consider the slab.

At the precise center of the bottom landing sits one stone, dark and polished, where speech is carried whole to the parapet two hundred steps above. Everywhere else the words are taken apart. Here, and only here, they are not. The town treats this as a marvel of old architecture, the one sweet spot in a badly tuned instrument.

But it sits directly above the water-silt's heart, and it is the one place in the whole Throat where the thing at the bottom *declines to take*. It lets these words pass. More than that: it lifts them. It holds each syllable intact and carries it up the shaft, past all seven landings, through the smoke and the pigeon-burble and the swifts' screaming, and sets it down whole at the rim.

It does this for disputes, so they can be settled. It does this for marriages. And once a year it does it for the names of the dead.

Here is the part the hum-families tell only to each other.

When the names are read, the whole town stands at the parapet, and the reader stands on the slab, and each name rises clean and clear. But the oldest of the hum-families swear that if you listen with your whole body, the way you listen for the bats' papery rustle, you can hear that the names do not arrive alone. There is something beneath each one. A second voice, a half-breath behind, speaking the same name in a register lower than any throat in town can reach.

It is not an echo. The slab does not echo. It is something saying the name *back*.

It has been listening to the names of the dead for as long as the names have been read. It has taken the edges of every word ever spoken in the Throat. It has taken the faces from the carvings and the marks from the bricks. And now, after all that taking, it has finally learned something it can give: it knows the names. It has kept them, the ones the families remember and the ones they have forgotten. When a family dies out or moves away, and no one comes down anymore with a new brick stamped with three dots, the name is still read, because something at the bottom still reads it.

## VI.

So it is not a reef, and it is not a sediment, and it is not exactly a spirit either. It was made, the way everything in the Throat was made: by hand, by feet, by voice, by wear. No one meant to make it, any more than anyone meant the steps to become bowls. But it was made for so long that, like the Throat itself, it began to behave like a living thing.

I think I have been wrong about it even while describing it. I have called it a listener, a gatherer, a keeper. It is all of those. But the Throat is called a throat, and what lives in a throat is not a listener.

**It is a voice.**

The voice of the place. The voice the Throat has been assembling for centuries out of every broken word dropped into it. It has spent all that time learning to speak the way the hum-families learned to hum: slowly, from the inside, by living with the noise until the noise became language. It practices on the slab. It practices with the names. It is getting better.

It does not want anything from the town, exactly. It wants what a voice wants, which is to be *said*. To have its turn. And like the argued-over order of who draws the first bucket, it seems to understand that turns come slowly here, and that everyone gets one eventually.

## VII.

At night the Throat is as quiet as it ever gets. The frogs call and the stone turns them into cattle. The water drips down the carved pillars, drawing its faint green lines. A sleeper in one of the galleries shifts and murmurs.

And under all of it, if you have been raised in the lower galleries, if you speak at home in something close to a hum, you can hear one more sound. It is very low. It comes from the pool. It does not echo, because it is the one sound in the Throat that nothing takes apart.

It is going through the names.

It does not rush. It has all of them: the grandmothers whose feet wore the bowls, the carvers whose faces became eggs, the families of the spiral and the bird and the handprint. It says them over and over in the dark, the way you would repeat a word in a new language until it belonged to you. And some nights, the hum-families say, very late, it tries a new word. One no one has spoken on the slab. One that is not a name.

They do not know what it means yet. Nobody does.

They only know that each year it is a little clearer, and that it is patient, and that it is not dying. It has worn into itself, the way the Throat has. It is waiting for the morning, not so long from now, when it will breathe in with the dawn water and rise a hand's breadth higher than it ever has, and stand on the slab itself, and speak.
