# What Lives at Hollin Bottom

## The First Answer, Which Is Not Wrong

The easy answer is the plant, and the easy answer is true as far as it goes.

Something lives in the Bottom the way a pulse lives in a wrist. It fills the basin from embankment to embankment. It has no edge you could stand at and say *here it begins*, and no center except the one it has borrowed from the engine. It is warm-blooded in its way. Its sap runs at the temperature of a hand held against a cheek. It is patient on a scale that makes a human life look like a spark off a grinding wheel. It eats iron slowly, drinks rust-water, and breathes steam.

If you had to draw it, you would draw a lung. The basin inflates and deflates across forty seconds. It is one organ with no body around it, pressed into a hollow in the ground like a heart kept in a bowl.

That is what lives there if you stand on the lip of the basin and listen. Go in and put your hands on things, and the answer starts to change.

## It Is Not Blind

A plant is supposed to be blind. It reaches for light and water and knows nothing else.

This one knows its gauges. Watch any of the clouded dials set flush in the bark long enough, and you will see the needle drift toward the edge of its arc. Then, before the knock, the bark around the dial tightens a little, the needle settles back, and the flow somewhere downstream shifts. The plant is reading them. There is no eye behind the glass, but there is attention, the kind a man gives a dial he has watched every day for thirty years. The glance becomes part of the body and needs no thought.

It knows its tolerances. The grown cogs are not approximations. They are *right*. Someone once had to know what right meant for them: the pitch, the clearance, the shallow angle of the tooth face that keeps two gears from binding. The plant has that knowledge now. It never learned it the way you learn a fact. It absorbed it the way a lathe bed takes on the wear pattern of the work done on it.

It knows its own sounds, and this matters most. The dripping is not random. Stand still long enough and you begin to hear that certain drops land on certain plates at certain points in the cycle. The Bottom is full of small sounding surfaces, iron plates and tin sheet and taut leaves. The pitches cluster and fall away and cluster again around the knock like men's voices around a bell. The plant listens to itself. It has to. Most of what an engineman knows about his engine, he knows by ear.

So the second answer is that something *attentive* lives here, something with the habits of a person who minded machines for a living.

## The Crew

If you spend a whole day in the Bottom, you start to feel that the attention is not all the same.

Near the chimney, where it is hottest, the growth is dense, dark, and brittle-barked. It gives off a smell like hot slate. The seed-casings there dry into hexagons. The coal smoke comes from nothing burning. This part of the plant keeps the fire, though there is no fire. It behaves the way a stoker behaves: it watches the draw, keeps the heat banked, and is short-tempered about it. Touch the growth here and it flinches faster than it should.

In the tangle around the auxiliary gear train, the wood is pale, fine-grained, and patient, and its growth scars sit in tidy rings. The new teeth come from here. This is the fitter's corner. It works slowly, fussing over clearances, and it will not be hurried.

Along the main pipe runs, the root-lagging is wrapped with the neat, overlapping spiral a man uses when he lags pipe by hand and takes pride in it. Where joints crack, the pale collars appear. The resin smells of pine and hot metal, like the paint pot that used to stand by the door.

Here and there, near dials that no longer have numbers, the bark is worn smooth in a small round patch just below the glass. It is the size of a thumb.

A dozen men could have run the works. The third answer is that the plant did not learn from one of them. It learned from all of them. Their habits lie in it in layers, like the bands in the iron nodules, one working life laid down over another: the stoker's temper, the fitter's patience, the lagger's pride, the thumb that tapped the gauge.

A crew lives in the Bottom. It has no bodies. It has a shift pattern, and the pattern stayed.

## The One Who Stayed Longest

Then you find the room that is not the engine house.

It is small and off to the western side, half a building still. Three brick walls are standing, and the fourth is made of root. There is a hearth, cold. There is a table, or the shape of one, grown over so completely that it is now a low shelf of moss on four columns. There is a hook on the wall. From the hook, embedded in wood that has swollen around it until only its outline shows, hangs something that was once a coat.

Here the growth is different again. It is quieter. The dripping is sparse. The hum is fainter than anywhere else, as though this part of the plant keeps its voice down. By the table, grown into the root wall at the height of a seated man's shoulder, is a single iron bell-pull. Its wire runs up and out through the brickwork and away east.

It joins the cable.

This is where the picture stops being a crew and becomes one man. The stoker, the fitter and the lagger all went home in the end. The works closed, as small works do. Someone stayed. He was a caretaker kept on at a pound a week to keep the pump going, because the pump had a job that did not end when the dye vats emptied or the mill stopped. He lived in this room. He kept the fire in, tapped the gauges, and oiled the pivot, all alone, for years. At the last, the only company he had was the engine and the green things that had begun, very slowly, to come in under the door.

A plant can learn a great deal from a lonely man in twenty years, if the man talks to his engine.

## What the Pump Is For

The engine is a beam engine, and there is only one thing beam engines were ever really built to do. They pumped water out of the ground so that men could work beneath it.

The pump rod plunges into a shaft. The shaft goes down. Somewhere under the pasture, under the soft flat fields, there are workings. They may be a clay drift or a coal seam or an ironstone pit. The Bottom's rust-red water and the iron it hoards say something was dug for iron once. Workings flood if you stop pumping. Sometimes they flood when you don't stop, if the rock gives way.

The village remembers, or half remembers, that there was a flooding. Men were below. The water came in faster than the engine could lift it. Some came up and some did not. The ones who did not were not found, because the lower levels could never be drained. The owners closed the works and put the loss down to the rock.

The caretaker kept pumping. Perhaps they told him to keep the upper levels dry for salvage. Perhaps nobody told him anything and he kept pumping anyway. Men trapped underground knock on the rock to be found, and the men up top listen. If you stop the pump you drown whatever pocket of air might still be down there. If you keep it going, you keep a chance alive, however thin, however long.

He kept it going until he couldn't. By then the plant had his rhythm in it, and it kept going after him.

This is what lives at Hollin Bottom. It is not the plant alone, and not the engine, and not the crew or even the man. It is the thing he was doing, which has outlived him. A vigil has taken root. The plant is its body, the engine its heart, and the old man's habits its mind. It has held one belief so long, through so many seasons of growth, that the belief is now structural, like stems that have fused into floors.

It believes there are men below, and that as long as the beam falls they can still be reached.

## The Knock

Hear the knock again with that in mind.

It is loud, much louder than a pump needs to be. The rod does not slam into the shaft because of bad engineering. The grown fittings are too well made for that. It slams because the Bottom *wants* it heard. The stroke carries down the iron rod into the shaft, through the rock, into the drowned levels. It carries out through the pasture and the cable. Its message is the oldest one there is between the surface and the deep: *we are still here. We have not stopped. We are coming.*

Forty seconds. It is not a machine's rhythm. It is closer to breathing, because it is a watch kept by something alive, and the living keep time the way they breathe, unevenly, as if the next breath might not come.

## The Silence

Every thirty or forty years, the knock stops.

The villagers think the plant is resting, or mending, or doing something to its workings that can't be done while it runs. Some of that is true. Afterward there is always a new wooden part, and the old iron has been taken into the body.

But think about who taught it. A man who spent his life listening for knocking from below would know you cannot hear an answer over your own noise. Every so often, however much it costs, you have to stop the engine, let the water rise a little, and *listen*.

So the Bottom stops. The hiss drains away. The beam hangs at the bottom of its stroke. The steam thins, the cable goes cold, and the whole green mass holds its breath and listens down the shaft into the dark, for days, with every sounding plate and taut leaf and dripping rock it has. It is listening for a tap on stone.

It is roughly once a generation. That is roughly the span of a working life. Perhaps the vigil does this whenever it has, in some deep vegetable way, grown a new keeper. Old habits come to the surface, new tissue takes over the attending, and the new watch wants to hear for itself before taking up the stroke. When the knock resumes a little faster or a little slower, it is the way a new engineman runs the same engine at a slightly different pace than the old one did. The machine is the same. The hands are different.

The iron part that turns to wood each time is the old man going, a little more at every handover, into the thing that remembers him.

What it hears in the silence, nobody knows. It always starts again. Perhaps that means it hears nothing and keeps faith anyway. Perhaps it means it hears something.

## What It Does Not Know

It does not know how long it has been. Nothing in the Bottom counts years. It counts strokes, and it has lost the total. The gauge needles tremble at points on the dial that no longer have numbers because the numbers stopped meaning anything a long time ago.

It does not know that no man could still be alive down there. Or it knows, the way a grieving person knows, as a fact that sits beside the vigil without touching it.

It does not know who is at the far end of the cable. The bell-pull in the little room is wired to it. Long ago, the caretaker would have signalled the pithead or the owner's house or the village, whoever needed to know the pump still ran. The Bottom keeps sending, faint and warm and humming, down a line whose other end may be a sealed box in a collapsed signal hut, or a lamp in a cellar that nobody has entered in sixty years. Perhaps there is still someone there. Perhaps that is the only place in the world where anyone is still listening to it.

## The Things That Keep It Company

Seen this way, the heron is easier to understand. It stands at the settling tank with its head cocked and does not fish, because it is not there for fish. Herons are fond of places where someone stands still and waits. It keeps its own watch alongside the other one for a while, then lifts off unhurried over the canopy, the way a neighbor leaves after sitting up with the family.

The beetles eat the resin, the plant's sealant. They are the only thing in the Bottom that takes the works apart. They open a joint a hair's breadth at a time and let a little of what is sealed in there leak into the world. They cling to iron harder than they should, as if they have been told not to let go of it.

## If You Go In

If you go down into the Bottom while it runs, you are in no danger. It does not mind visitors, though it hardly notices them. Its attention is turned downward and always has been.

If you ever find yourself there in the silence, when the dripping goes on alone and the great green beam hangs still, keep very quiet. Don't walk on the plates. Don't let a stone go down the shaft. Something enormous and patient is holding its breath around you, listening for knocking that would mean everything to it.

One old woman in the village says her grandmother did go down once, in a silence, when she was a girl and foolish. She knelt at the head of the shaft, where the pump rod goes into the dark. She didn't know why she did it. It simply seemed the right thing to do in that place. She took a stone and knocked three times on the iron collar.

A few hours later, the knock started again. It was a little faster than before, the old woman says, the way your step quickens on the road home when you think you have seen a light in the window.
