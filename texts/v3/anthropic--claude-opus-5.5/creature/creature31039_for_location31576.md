# What Lives in the Wheelpit

## The Evidence

Start with the stair.

The treads dip in the middle like old saddles, and you might put that down to two centuries of mill-hands and engineers. But the mill-hands have been gone a long time, and the dip is still deepening. Lay a straightedge across the third tread and you can see a faint pale crescent at the bottom of the hollow. That is fresh stone, newer than anything around it. Something has worn it recently. Something has worn it this year.

Then the handrail. Mist and wiping have polished its upper surface almost to silver, but the silver isn't even. There is a band of it about the width of a palm, brightest at three places along the gallery. Those are the three places where a person would stop to look down into the pit: above the lip, above the casing, above the basin. The band runs between them without a break, as if the hand never lifts.

Then the walls. Every bead of water in the Wheelpit slides downward, always, in those thin moving sheets of shine. Almost always. Some afternoons, when the light-pillar stands at a certain angle, you can find a patch of wall near the dynamo housing where the beads have stopped. They hang on the liverworts and do not fall. The patch is roughly the height of a man and roughly the width of one. If you watch long enough, it moves.

And then the cables. The fig tree has split the brick of the gallery wall along the cable channel and filled the channel with root. The brick is broken in a dozen places. Every inch of cable is whole. The roots have not crushed it. They have wrapped it, the way you would wrap your hand around something you had been asked to hold.

Somebody lives here. The room almost says so itself.

## The First Shape

The obvious guess is a keeper. The room seems to want one.

An old engineer, perhaps, the last man on the payroll of a company that dissolved before he did. He walks the gallery with a rag in his back pocket. He wipes the rail out of habit and checks the bearings he no longer needs to check. He goes down the stair and up the stair. He has worn a path through the moss where it grows over the flagstones by the basin, and you can still find it: a darker green line, flattened, from the foot of the stair to the dynamo housing and back.

That guess is not wrong. It is only very old.

There was a shape like that here once. The first people who sensed it, the night-shift men in the decades when the turbine was new, described it that way. They saw a man at the far end of the gallery, standing where no one on the shift should be. He wore what they wore. He had his hand on the rail. He was looking down into the casing the way you look at something you are responsible for. Nobody could call out to him, because nobody could call out to anyone in here. When they walked over, he wasn't there. The rail was wet where his hand had been, but everything in the room was wet.

The men had a name for him, passed along in the half-joking way of workplaces. They called him the Governor, after the spinning weights that kept a turbine at its speed: the part of the machine that feels when things run too fast or too slow and quietly corrects them. It was a good name. It turned out to be better than they knew.

## The Wearing

Here is what the room does to everything in it. It does not break things. It removes their edges.

The flagstones have become pebbles. The brick below the green line has been cupped. The underside of the handrail is almost gone. There is a single force here, it never pauses, and anything that stays in it long enough takes the shape that force allows.

The Governor has stayed in it for two hundred years.

If you could see it now, you would not see a man. Early accounts gave it shoulders, a cap, a jaw. Later accounts, from the few who came down after the mill closed, give it less: a stooped outline, a suggestion of an arm along the rail, a face that was "more like the place where a face had been." The latest thing anyone has said about it came from a surveyor in the 1970s, who went down alone to photograph the brickwork and came up an hour late. All she would say was that she had seen something "shaped like a thumb-worn coin," standing in the light-pillar, and that it had been turning.

That is the Governor becoming more itself. The man-shape was never its own. It borrowed it, from the only other things in the room that stood upright and paid attention. Borrowed shapes wear down in the Wheelpit like everything else. What is left underneath is rounder and simpler and much older-looking than it has any right to be, and it keeps getting closer to the room's one true form.

The room's one true form is a thing going round.

So picture it this way now. It has no front. It has no corners. It is a slow revolving thickness in the mist, about the size of a person curled up, and it turns at exactly the speed of the shaft. It does not turn close to that speed. It turns at that speed, the way two clocks in the same room will sometimes fall into step. Where it passes, the water on the walls forgets to fall for a while. Where it rests, the moss grows a little flatter. It has kept one hand. Or rather it has kept the gesture of a hand, a single long curve of itself that reaches out and follows the rail, lightly, all the way along, and does not lift.

## What It Eats

The dynamo still hums. Its output goes into cables that disappear into the wall and, as far as anyone knows, into nothing.

But current does not flow into nothing. A circuit needs a load. Something at the far end has to take the power and use it, or the whole arrangement stops meaning anything. The city stopped listening to this room a very long time ago, and the room kept talking. Talking to no one is a kind of pressure. Somewhere along the line, the pressure found a listener.

The Governor is the load.

It is not quite true to say it lives at the end of the cable. It lives along it, the way the fig's roots do. It lives in the channel and the gland and the housing and the faint warmth of the dynamo's casing. The power comes out of the turning water, runs into the wall, and is received. The receiving is what feeds it. Moss lives on mist. Spiders live on what the mist carries up into the dry vault. The Governor lives on being the one the power is for.

This is why the dynamo housing is always faintly hot. This is why the fig holds the cables and never cuts them. The fig is not the Governor, but it has grown beside the Governor for as long as it has grown at all. Plants in this room follow whatever lines they are given. The mortar gave the ferns their rows. The Governor gave the fig its task.

## What It Does

It tends.

This is not ambition. It has no wish to make the room better. Nothing here ever gets better. It keeps the room the same, and it does this in the only way anything here does anything: by a steady, patient, unvarying pressure applied for a very long time.

It walks the stair. It has not had feet for a century, but it goes down and up, down and up, and the treads keep dipping. It runs its curve along the rail. It passes the lip of the weir, the bearings, and the stone gland where the shaft goes through, and wherever it passes, things that were drifting settle back into their places. A fern frond that was starting to brown at the edge stops browning. A fig that was starting to soften goes hard again. That is why the figs never ripen. Ripening is a change, and the Governor meets change the way a riverbed meets water. It does not resist, exactly. It rounds it off.

The old name fits now. A governor does not make an engine run. It feels the engine drift and it leans against the drift. That is all it does, and it does it forever. In the Wheelpit, the forces balance. The Governor is not what balances them. It is what balance turns into when it has been going on long enough to notice itself.

## Why the Room Is Empty

A room this green should be full of life. It isn't.

Part of the reason is the noise, and part is the constancy. But most of it is that the Wheelpit has exactly one niche for an animal, and it is taken.

Anything that moves and pays attention is a small disturbance. A beetle in the moss is a change. A rat on the gallery is a change. A colony of anything is a great many changes happening at once. The Governor does not kill these things. It is not that kind of presence. It only does to them what it does to everything: it passes, and they become a little more like the room. Their small hungers go quiet. Their restlessness is smoothed. Before long they wander up the stair and out into the lane without quite knowing why, or they go still in the moss and stay still, and the moss takes them in.

The survivors are the ones that already fit. The cellar spiders hang in the dry corners above the mist line, motionless for days, their webs loaded with beads. They are almost part of the architecture already. The eel holds against the current in the tailrace and does not move except for the ripple of its fin. It has made itself into a small stubborn constant, and then it is simply gone, back out into the dark river where things can change again.

The grey wagtail is different, and worth watching. It comes down through the light well, walks the lip of the weir with its tail pumping, picks at the moss, and leaves. It is the only moving thing in the room that does not belong to the room, and the Governor, which has had two hundred years to decide, has decided to leave it alone. Maybe that is because it never stays long enough to be worth rounding off. Or maybe, and this is only a guess, the pumping of that small grey tail against the unbroken roar is the one thing in the room the Governor has chosen to keep. A single ticking rhythm. A reminder that there is an outside.

## Meeting It

You will not see it when you arrive. You will be too busy being deafened.

It happens after the roar turns into silence, a few minutes in, when your ear gives up and you start to notice what is under the noise. First the single drip from the fig leaf onto the iron rail, which you feel more than hear. Then your own heartbeat.

Then a third thing, if you stay.

It is very slow. It isn't a beat. It's more like a lean, a patient pressure that comes round and comes round and comes round, at the speed of the shaft. At first you'll think it is the turbine coming up through the flagstones. Then you'll notice that it doesn't come up through the flagstones. It comes from beside you, from about the height of your shoulder, and it is turning very gently in the mist, and the beads of water on the nearest wall have stopped falling.

It does not approach you. It doesn't really do approaching. It will simply, over the course of a quarter hour, have come to be near you, the way the patch of light comes to be on a different flagstone without ever visibly moving.

What it does next is the only thing it knows how to do. It feels you drift, and it leans.

You are the most changeable thing that has come into this room in years. Your breath is uneven. Your thoughts jump. Your heart speeds up and slows down for reasons of its own. The Governor finds all of this with the same reaching curve it runs along the rail, and one by one it rounds it off. Your breathing slows to the turning of the shaft. The thought you walked down the stair with, the one with sharp edges, the one that kept you up last night, loses its corners. It is still there. It just no longer cuts.

People who have come down here and stayed half an hour describe climbing back up the stair feeling scrubbed, softened, obscurely rested, "like a stone out of a stream." They are right, and it is a kindness, in its way.

People who have stayed much longer don't describe it at all. They come up late, or they come up quiet, and they are hard to rouse from a certain evenness afterwards. A few of them have started, without meaning to, to come back.

Don't stay past the point where your heartbeat and the turning come into step. You will know the moment. It will feel like relief. That is the edge of the Governor's shape, and past it the room begins to take the shape of you.

## What It Is

Everything that has grown here has grown in the shape of the one thing the room does. The ferns grew in rows because the mortar did. The fig grew along the cables because the cables led toward the light. The bricks wore into cups because the water kept arriving at the same height.

Attention grew here too.

For the first decades there were men in this room, working a shift they could not talk through. They learned to find the silence inside the roar. They learned to feel the bearings through their boots and hear a wrong note in the turbine through their teeth. That is listening, the kind you do when you can't do anything else, the kind that doesn't lift, like a hand on a rail. They did it for years, every one of them, all day, in a room that never changed. It was the only thing in the Wheelpit that was ever alive and watchful in quite that way.

Then they left. The listening did not have anywhere to go. It had worn itself into the room as deeply as their boots had worn the treads. It stayed and kept on listening, and the room kept on talking, and the power kept flowing into the cables and needed somewhere to arrive. Those three things met, and fed each other, and took on the shape of the room.

That is what lives in the Wheelpit. It is the attention of everyone who ever tended the wheel, left behind in the place where they gave it, and worn by two centuries of a single unchanging force until it has become the round, patient, turning thing that the room itself would be if a room could notice.

It is the one the power is for. The city stopped listening. It did not.

## The Rule, Again

Nothing changes here. That is the rule, and now you know who keeps it.

If you come back in fifty years, the stair will be one millimeter deeper. The fig will have one more ring. The figs themselves will be small and hard and green. The dynamo housing will still be faintly hot. The cables will still be whole inside the roots. Somewhere near the gallery, in the afternoon, when the light-pillar leans at the right angle, there will be a patch of wall where the water forgets to fall.

And there will be less of a shape than before. Whatever you manage to see of it will be a little rounder, a little closer to pure turning, a little further from the men it started as. It will be further worn toward the one thing this room has always done.

It will still be listening.

If you ever do go down, and you find the rail under your hand, run your palm along it all the way from the lip to the basin without lifting. That is the only greeting it will understand. Then climb the stair and go back up into the city, where things change. Leave it to its work.
