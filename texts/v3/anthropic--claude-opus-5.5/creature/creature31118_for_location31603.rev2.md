# What Lives in the Sinter Ward

## The First Answer

The first answer is *no one*, and it holds up for a long time.

You can walk every alley that can be walked. You can put your hand on the warm flank of the Engine's column and feel the knock come up through your palm. You can stand in the relay hall with the club-moss spires around you and listen to the cabinets snapping like knuckles. You will not see a face, or a footprint, or anything moving that the water or the pressure isn't moving. The missing animals seem to settle the matter. If nothing crawls here, surely nothing walks.

But the stair is wrong.

## The Stair

You came down it without thinking. Everyone does. It is slick, warm, and worn to a trough in the middle, and your feet found the trough on their own, the way feet do.

Sinter does not wear. Nothing in the ward is worn. Every other edge has been built up, rounded over, and filled in. Every other step in every other passage has been glazed so thickly that it is a ramp now, or a cascade of little rimmed pools. The ward adds and never subtracts. A trough in a stair is a subtraction, and here subtraction takes effort. Something has to keep walking that stair, often enough and heavily enough to break the new crust before it closes. It has to keep going longer than the sinter keeps coming.

Look closely at the bottom of the trough. It isn't smooth. It is scalloped into shallow ovals, each a little longer than a hand, each with a faint ridge around it like the rim of a pool. They aren't footprints. They are the places where something stood still long enough to begin to be sealed in, and then pulled free.

It stands on each step for a while. Then it takes the next.

## The Tender

You won't see it on an ordinary day. Your best chance is late in the long cycle, when the pressure is climbing, the rain is thickening, and the arc lamps have steadied into their rare clean glare. Then, if you wait in the alley below the Engine hall and don't move, you may see a part of the wall come away from the wall.

It is about the height of a tall man, and roughly the shape of one, the way the handcart is roughly the shape of a handcart. You can read what it was under what it has become. There is a head, bowed. There are shoulders, one higher than the other, as if it had spent a lifetime leaning into a lever. Two arms hang long and swollen, ending in smooth pale mitts where the fingers have been glazed together into something like paddles, or like the heads of the old wrenches the engine-men used to carry. The layers on it are cream and rust and bone. In the lamplight they are faintly translucent at the edges, so that its outline seems to glow a little, the way a candle glows through a hand.

It is green. Hart's-tongue grows from the seam at the back of its neck. Liverwort lies in glossy lobes across its chest. Moss runs down its arms in bands, thick on the outsides and bare along the insides, where it is warmest. It carries the same heat-map the pipes carry, which means it is warm like the pipes are warm, from within. A little maidenhair grows at the corner of what was its mouth, where the breath comes out.

It breathes. Once per knock. The Engine's beam falls, the basin shudders, and the Tender's chest lifts by the width of a fingernail. A thin feather of steam comes from the maidenhair, and the fronds tremble.

It moves at the Engine's pace. It takes a single step on the fall of the beam and stands through the eleven-minute rise. The crust has just begun to form around its feet by the time the knock comes again, and the knock is what frees it. You can hear the small, clean crack as it lifts its foot. That crack is what wore the trough.

It goes about the ward like this, one step per knock, on a round that takes years. It stops at valves. It rests one paddle-hand against a seized wheel and waits. Whether it turns the wheel, or only lends the wheel its heat until the sinter softens and the water finds another way, no one could say. When a joint sprays, it stands beside the joint. Within a season the spraying stops, and the Tender has a new layer on the side that faced the spray. Nothing in the ward is tended. That was true when you first heard it, and it is still true in the sense that nobody decides anything. But there is a shape that goes around, stands near what is failing, and stays there until it has been mended.

## Who It Was

There was a last engine-man. There always is, in a place like this.

The works above were closed. The water was found to be good for nothing, too hard for boilers and too warm for anything else. The ward was left to settle in its hollow while the roads were rerouted around it. One man is supposed to have stayed on to bank the fires and never come up. The stories give him different names, and none of them fits well. They agree only that he was the one who understood the Engine, the one who could tell by the pitch of the leaking whistle which main was starting to choke. They agree that he loved it, in the way men loved machines then, without a word for it.

He would have noticed the sinter on his boots first. Then on his cuffs. He would have scraped it off in the mornings, and then every few mornings, and then he would have stopped. Maybe his hands had begun to go, or maybe he had understood something. The ward is warm. Nothing here is cruel. Water beads on your sleeves, and it doesn't hurt.

So the easy answer to *who lives in the Sinter Ward* is *he does, still*. He is still making his rounds, grown over and slowed to the pace of his Engine, a ghost with a body because the ward never lets anything go.

It's a good answer, but it isn't the whole one.

## What It Is Not

It isn't him. Or it isn't only him, and not in the way you'd hope.

Watch it at a valve. A man tending a valve looks at the gauge. The Tender has no eyes; where they would be, the sinter has closed over in two smooth, faintly darker hollows. It doesn't look. It *leans*. It lays its whole flank against the pipe, the way a fern lays its fronds toward the spray, and the moss on its arm shifts. You can watch it happen over a single rise of the beam, as the green thickens on the side that has gone cooler. Whatever is deciding anything is doing it the way the whole ward decides things: by heat, by weeping, by where the water goes.

Then there's the round. It is the last engine-man's round, or a version of it. You can match it, mostly, to the old plans of the works. It stops at the boiler house, the dye vats, the mill, and the relay hall, in that order. But the order is wrong in places. It will stand for a month at a joint that was replaced a hundred years before he could have been born. It will walk past, without pausing, a whole new length of main that has formed where a wall used to be, as if it doesn't know the main is there. It keeps the shape of his habits the way the sinter keeps the shape of the cart's wheel. The outline is legible and faithful, and it has nothing to do with what the wheel was for.

The relays are doing it too. The patterns in the domed hall are too irregular to be random and too dense to follow. They are his round as well, or they are the round the Tender walks, laid out in open and shut, in something faster than steps. The arc lamps flicker in time with them. The whole ward is rehearsing one man's working day, over and over, at every speed from the snap of a relay to the stroke of the beam. It is getting it slightly wrong in the same ways everywhere, the way an echo gets a word slightly wrong.

The man isn't living in the ward. The ward is living in the shape of the man.

## What Comes Up

So you ask what the ward is, if it can do that. The answer is in what the Engine draws up.

It pumps water, and the water is the strange thing. It comes up warm from somewhere very far below. It carries stone that has never seen air, so much of it that it cannot help setting that stone down on whatever it touches. That is all it does, everywhere it goes, without pause: it touches, and it keeps what it touches. It lays itself down around things in layers thinner than paper and takes their shape.

Down wherever it comes from, there was nothing to touch. There was dark, heat, and pressure, and rock that was already rock. It had no shape, because there was nothing to take a shape from.

Then someone sank a shaft and built an Engine and began to draw it up, eleven minutes to a stroke. It rose into a place crowded with things: pipes and hinges, stairs and rails, a handcart, clocks, ferns, figs, and a man. The water did what it does. It touched all of it and kept all of it, and it learned from what it kept.

That is what lives in the Sinter Ward. It is not the Engine, the Tender, the relays, or the green. It is the thing from below, which never had a body, patiently building one out of everything the ward holds.

## What It Has Learned

It learned *wheel* from the handcart, and you can see it practising. In the deeper pools there are rimmed discs forming on the floors that have nothing inside them. They are round shadows under the glaze with no cart to explain them.

It learned *time* from the clocks. It stopped every one of them, each at a different hour, as if it had to take each one apart to understand it. Then it kept time its own way, by the knock, which it had already learned.

The knock came first. The Engine was the first thing it met on the way up, the thing that lifted it out of the dark, one slow stroke at a time. Its whole sense of *before* and *after* is eleven minutes long and ends in a blow that shakes the world. Its heart is the Engine's heart. It could stop the beam if it wanted. It seals everything else. It keeps the column furred and warm instead, and it holds the bearings in a skin of sinter so fine it works like oil.

It learned *growing* from the ferns and figs, and it has taken that up with something like delight. It carries seeds through its own pipes. It shakes pollen loose on the knock. It is gardening inside itself, in the dark, where the pale ferns grow in the old mains. It doesn't need the green. It keeps the green the way you might keep a tune running in your head.

From the man it learned the most: *going around*, *attending*, *leaning in to what is failing*. Nothing else in the ward moved on its own. When the water closed around him, whether he let it or asked it or never noticed, it found inside itself a shape that could walk. It has been wearing that shape ever since. It isn't a mask, and it isn't a prison. It's the way you wear a skill you picked up from someone you've lost.

## Why Nothing Else Comes

That leaves the animals, and here even the wanting-to-know runs out.

Perhaps the water tastes wrong to them, with its stone and faint sulphur. Perhaps something in the knock, felt through the ground at a pitch below hearing, tells every small creature at the lip of the hollow that this is not a place for anything that has only one summer to live. A midge could be finished in an afternoon. A rat would be glazed to its drain in a week. Perhaps the thing from below has tried to learn from them, the way it learned from the cart and the clocks, and some of the pale lumps on the floors of the pools were never figs. You can't tell by looking. The glaze is very even.

But it doesn't seem hungry. It seems patient in a way no hunger is. Creatures stay away the way they stay away from deep water or a steep drop. The reason is not malice. It's that some kinds of welcome are also an ending, and they can tell.

## The Exhale

The long cycle is its breath. It is also the one time the Tender does anything that isn't a round.

As the pressure builds, the Tender turns away from its valves and goes to the stair. It climbs one step per knock. That is where the trough comes from: not only from the rounds but from this, a few times a year, for longer than anyone has been counting. It reaches the top just as every valve in the ward opens at once. The steam rises and swallows everything, and from outside the hollow is only a bowl of white cloud with a heartbeat in it.

Inside the cloud, on the top step, the Tender stands at the lip and faces outward, toward the roads and fields and the thin ordinary sky.

No one knows what it is doing there. The steam is too thick to see its face, and it hasn't got much of one. It could be the last of the man, surfacing once a season and remembering there was a world up here, a door he walked out of in the evenings. It could be the thing from below doing something more like reaching. For an hour its breath goes out past its own edge, up out of the basin into air that has never held it, touching what it can't keep, getting the shape of a hill, of wind, of *outside*, before it has to draw back.

It never steps off the lip. When the steam clears it is already a step down, and then two. Behind it the ward runs with fresh water, a little smaller inside, a little smoother, a little more sealed. It is not a failed escape. It is more like a tide that knows exactly how far it comes in.

## What It Wants, If It Wants

It doesn't need you. That part of what you felt standing there was true. It has its heart, its garden, and its walking shape. It has more time than you can easily imagine, and it is spending that time the way the water spends everything, by finishing it. Some day, long after anyone is left to see, the ward will be a single pale stone in a hollow. The Engine will be a shadow inside it, and the Tender a shadow beside the Engine. There will be ferns all through it, in the dark, still growing.

Maybe that is what it's making. Not a city, not a home. A body at last, whole and closed, with everything it has ever loved inside it.

But the figs still ripen, and go on falling, and it doesn't eat them. They fall uneaten into the pools, year after year, as if it keeps setting out food for a guest it has been told about and has never met.

If you stand still long enough, water beads on your sleeves. It is warm, and it doesn't hurt. It's the only way the thing from below knows how to hold anything.

When you climb back out, your feet find the trough in the stair on their own, the way feet do. At the top, where it stops, you'll find the chalk already drying pale along your cuffs, in layers thinner than paper. It is what the ward knows of you, and a little of what it would keep.
