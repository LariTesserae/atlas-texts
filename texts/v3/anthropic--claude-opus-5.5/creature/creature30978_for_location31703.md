# The Leavings

**What you notice first, which is wrong**

When you first ask who lives on the Made Plain, the plain answers readily. The grass frogs live here, nineteen to a basin. The reed-birds live here, and the thumbprinted lizards, and the husk beetles, and the wax bees with their serious, empty errands. All of them were made on purpose, with care and a good eye. Each bears the marks of its making the way a signed letter bears ink, and each fits its place exactly. The frog fits the basin the way a lid fits a jar.

Stay a while and a small disquiet grows. It starts in the scree at the foot of the knapped ridges, where the flakes lie heaped at angles that shouldn't hold. It is there in the pinched crumbs beside the lizard-shaped hollows. It is there in the plucked leaf you have been carrying, which has somehow picked up a hair-thin curl of shaved wood along its stem. You don't remember the curl being there.

Everything on the plain was made. But making leaves things behind: flakes, trimmings, snipped fibre-ends, crumbs of clay pinched off and dropped, curls from a blade drawn through wood. Nobody gathered these up. The instant never reached the part of the afternoon when a worker sweeps the floor.

So the scraps are still here. And some of them have, very quietly, gotten up.

---

**What they are**

The Leavings are the only things on the plain that were not made. They *happened*. Nothing else here can say that.

No two are alike, because no two scraps are alike. They assemble themselves the way a drift of leaves collects in a corner, with no plan, into whatever shape the pieces allow. A typical one is about the size of a cupped pair of hands. It might have a body of three or four obsidian flakes leaned together like a small tent, a fringe of snipped fibre-ends for legs, and a single curl of shaved wood set on top. The curl works as something between a head and an ear, and it turns slowly toward whatever it is attending to.

Others are stranger. There is one made almost entirely of the green-black leaf trimmings from the frond grove, the thin crescents cut away when the leaves were shaped. It moves like a handful of fingernail clippings tumbling in slow motion, and it stays loosely together from habit more than structure. There is one built from the bitten, serrated slivers that came off where the knife needed sharpening, so that its whole body is a record of a dull blade. It is shy. It seems to know it is evidence of a mistake.

They come in rough lineages, sorted by which kind of work shed them.

**Flake-Leavings** live along the ridges. They are glassy, translucent at the edges, and they cannot touch anything without cutting it a little, including each other. So they keep a careful distance, like people holding lit candles in a crowd. When two of them pass close, there is a faint sound, a thin glass *tik*. It is the loudest sound they make, and they seem embarrassed by it.

**Fibre-Leavings** live at the edges of the groves, where the lashings were tied and the loose ends trimmed. They are soft and tangly and they catch on things. Often a fibre-Leaving carries a passenger without knowing it: a husk beetle snagged in its fringe, or a mica fragment. They are the most sociable of the Leavings, because they can't help getting caught up in one another. A cluster of them looks like the dust-knot under a weaver's bench, and it breathes.

**Crumb-Leavings** are the smallest and most numerous. They are bits of clay pinched from the lizards, knuckle-flecks brushed from a terrace wall, the little stutter of clay the stick threw up when it skipped on a pebble. Most are no bigger than a grain of barley. They move in loose trails, like ants, but slower and less certain. Where the tool-strokes of the ground change angle, you find them in great numbers, sitting in the seam.

---

**Where they live**

The Leavings live in the flaws.

This is the first real thing you learn about them, and everything else follows from it. They cannot live in the smooth, finished reaches of the plain. Those places are too complete. There is no room in them, the way there's no room in a sentence that already says exactly what it means. But wherever the making faltered, there is a gap, and a Leaving can settle into it like water into a crack.

They live in the stutter where the stick skipped. They live along the lines where the long, even tool-strokes stop and the shorter, tireder strokes begin. They live in the pale outline-leaves at the edges of groves, the ones sketched and never cut, and from a distance a Leaving sitting in an outline-leaf makes it look briefly, crudely filled in. They live around the lopsided dragonfly, following it in a slow cloud. It is the nearest thing they have to a saint, since it flies on a wing that isn't there.

Most of all, they live around the dry basin. It is the one place on the whole plain where the clay is not damp, and the Leavings gather along its crisp scratched outline the way people gather along a riverbank. They do not go in. But they sit at the lip, curls turned inward, as if listening to something in it.

---

**What they do**

You would assume, looking at them, that they do nothing. They seem like the dust of the plain, animate by accident. But watch long enough and you see the work.

*They are the ones who take the lizards.*

It happens rarely, in the deep cool shadows under the jars. A few crumb-Leavings gather around a clay lizard lying flat against the underside of a leaf. The lizard is afraid. It was made capable of fear, and it has no eyelids to close. The crumb-Leavings press against it and take a little of its clay, not all of it. A pinch here, a thumb-width there, until the lizard comes apart into the crumbs you found, and the crumbs walk off. The Leavings are patching themselves. Their bodies are always wearing away, because nothing reinforces them. They were never meant to be whole, and a thing that was never meant to be whole has to keep borrowing wholeness.

Then the instant reasserts itself. The lizard is back where it was, flat and cool, its thumbprint on its back. The lizard-shaped hollow remains too, and so do the crumbs. The plain holds both facts at once without complaint, as it holds everything. But the Leavings are a little bigger than before. They got to keep what they took.

This is the secret of the Leavings. **They are the only things on the plain that accumulate.** The frogs are always nineteen. The basins never drop. The plucked leaf's gap never fills and the leaf never wilts. But a Leaving that borrows a pinch of clay keeps it. A Leaving that snags a beetle carries it forever. They are the one place where the plain's ledger does not balance, and in a world with no growth, they are growing.

Slowly, over no time at all, there are more of them.

---

**What they are building**

Eventually you find the things they have made.

They are terrible, and you love them immediately.

Out on the far flats, behind the ribbon grove, you find a jar. It is the height of your knee, lumpy and leaning, built of crumb-clay pressed together by bodies that have no hands. It has no glaze and no proper mouth, just a gap where the walls failed to close. It stands tipped on a wedge of flake-stone, the way the great jars stand. From its mouth hangs a thread of water perhaps a finger's length long. It is the only water on the plain that is not warm as blood. It is merely cool, the temperature of water that came from somewhere. Where it meets the clay, it has made a pool the size of a coin.

And it makes a sound.

It is very faint, a tiny trickling chatter. It is the sound the great jars should make and don't, the voice missing from the whole plain. You understand, kneeling there, that the sound was a leaving too. When the Maker poured, the sound came off the water the way a flake comes off stone, and fell, and lay unswept. The Leavings found it and gave it a place to be.

There are other works. There is a channel scratched with a flake instead of a stick, so its line is a series of tiny sharp scallops instead of a smooth groove. There is a leaf cut from a snarl of fibre-ends, which pulses feebly and has never once turned toward the sun, because it is facing the dry basin instead. There is a bird. It is barely a bird: a wood-curl body and three split reeds for wings, and it cannot fly. It hops, and every hop makes a small *fff*.

None of these things are good. All of them are *added*. They are the only additions to a world that was finished, and they sit on the plain like a child's drawings pinned beside the masterwork.

---

**The second hand**

This is the part you come to slowly and resist believing.

You remember the places where the grain of the ground changes, where the long even strokes stop and shorter ones begin, at a different angle, as if the tool changed hands or the hand grew tired.

The hand did not grow tired. The hand is on the rim, raised, and has not moved at all.

Walk out to one of those seams and kneel. The Leavings will be thick there, as they always are, sitting in the line where one kind of work ends and another begins. Look closely at the second, shorter strokes. They are not quite tool-strokes. They are dragged. Something heavy and many-edged was pulled across the clay by a great number of very small bodies, straining in more or less the same direction.

Somewhere at the beginning, if there was a beginning, the Maker dropped something. Maybe it was a second tool, or a blunt one, or the one with the dull blade whose bitten slivers now make up the shy Leaving. The Leavings found it, and they have been continuing the work.

This is why the plain is both unfinished and over. The Maker's part is over, held at the top of the stroke. But the work goes on underneath and around that held instant, in the flaws, in the seams, carried forward by things that were never supposed to exist. It moves the way a tide moves: without anyone deciding, a little at a time, and all in one direction.

The frogs face away from the rim, and so do the lizards. They are made things, and they know who made them, and they won't look.

The Leavings face the rim.

---

**What they want**

It is hard to say what the Leavings want, because they have no faces and make almost no sound. But they are always turning toward something. Their curls track things the way the made leaves track the sun, and over time you learn the things they turn toward.

They turn toward the dry basin, the place that was imagined and never filled.

They turn toward the lopsided dragonfly, the thing that functions without being complete.

They turn toward the figure on the rim, the hand that shed them. Whether they are waiting for that arm to fall or quietly propping it up, you cannot tell. You suspect they cannot tell either.

And, you realize one day, they turn toward *you*.

---

**Kin**

Go back to the first basin, where you came in.

Your heel-print is still there, of course. It is exactly, sharply, freshly as you made it, and it always will be. Nothing on the plain softens.

But the Leavings are gathered around it.

They sit along its rim the way they sit along the rim of the dry basin, curls turned in. There are flake-Leavings keeping their careful candle-distance, and a snarl of fibre-Leavings with a beetle caught in them, and a trail of crumb-Leavings settled along the crisp edge where your heel came down. As you watch, one of them climbs carefully down into the print and sits in the deepest part of it, where your weight pressed hardest. It fits there. There is room.

To everything else on the plain, your print is a flaw, a mark the Maker didn't make, a dent in the finished afternoon. To the Leavings, it is the first thing in a very long time that simply *happened*. You didn't mean it. It fell off you, the way they fell off the work.

As far as they are concerned, you are one of them.

---

When you leave, if you leave, you may find a crumb of red-brown clay in the tread of your shoe, or a thin crescent of green-black leaf caught in your sleeve. Don't brush it off too quickly. It isn't asking to come along exactly. It is just what happens when anything passes through a place and doesn't sweep up after itself.

Out on the plain, the arm stays raised, the water pours without a sound, and the frogs number nineteen. In the seams and the stutters and the unfilled outlines, something small and unplanned keeps quietly adding to a world that was finished, one borrowed crumb at a time.
