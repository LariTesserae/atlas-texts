# Who Keeps the Hammer Moor

## The First Thing to Know

Everything that lives here has work.

Most of it doesn't know this. A plover doesn't think of itself as employed, and a slug has no idea it is on a job. But stay a while and you stop seeing the moor's creatures as animals living in a landscape. You start seeing them as a workforce spread across a plant that never shuts down. Each creature is fitted to some part of the labor, and each has been fitted to it so long that the job and the body can no longer be told apart.

The birds and frogs, the grazers and eels, the moths and midges have already been described. They are real, and they are what you see first. But the moor keeps more hands than that. Some are smaller, some are stranger, and a few are not quite here in the ordinary way. And some of them are people.

## The Menders

When a bog-burst stops and the black river of liquid peat slows to a crust, steaming, the slugs come.

They come in the first hour, from every direction, in thousands. These are the forearm-long slugs with backs the green-gold of the moss. That color is not camouflage. The mantle of each slug is ridged in fine grooves, and living sphagnum grows in the grooves. Each slug carries a strip of the moor on its back like a man carrying turf.

They crawl out across the cooling scar and lay down slime as they go, in trails that cross and recross until the black peat is netted over with silver. The trails catch the dull light like solder lines on a dark plate. As the slugs crawl, fragments of moss shed from their backs and stick in the slime and take hold. By the time the slugs have gone back into the living mat, the scar is seeded. That is why it greens in days. The moor does not heal on its own. It is mended.

Kneel by a fresh scar at the right moment and you can hear them: a faint wet ticking, thousands of mouths at once, eating the thin film of fungus that blooms on warm peat. They are paid in what they repair.

## The Stokers

Near the steam vents, where the moss grows rank and red and the ground is too hot to kneel on, there are low mounds of pale, crusted mineral, each with a hole in the top. These are chimneys. Something lives under them.

You will rarely see the stokers. When you do, it will be for a moment at the lip of a vent: a long, low, dark body like an otter's or a mink's, with fur gone stiff and grey with sinter. Silica has crystallized in their coats from the vent water, so they rattle faintly when they move, like a bead curtain. Their eyes are filmed white against the scald. They live in the hot channels under the mat, where a person would cook in minutes, and they eat the red and orange mats of bacteria that coat the channel walls.

They are the reason the vents move.

A colony of stokers works a vent for years. They burrow to keep the channels clear as the minerals try to seal them. Eventually the minerals win, or the bacteria give out, and the colony leaves. It moves half a kilometer or a kilometer through the deep hot mat and begins digging again. Behind them the old vent chokes and dies. Ahead of them the pressure, which has been looking for a way up, finds their new tunnels and bursts through, and the mosses nearby turn red.

Their calls are whistles pitched to the note of the vent, so you cannot tell them apart from the steam. The young are born in the hottest water and carried through the scald in their mothers' mouths. When the stokers groom each other, the sound is a hiss. They are thin and scarred and they do not live long, and they have been keeping the fires open since before the first plover.

## The Reelers

The granite knuckles are wrapped in sedge-cable, some so thickly that they look like bobbins. Put your ear to one of these and you will hear a rapid, dry ticking inside the winding, like a spinning mule in a mill heard through a wall.

That is the reelers. They are small brown birds, wren-shaped, with short tails cocked high and feet too big for their bodies. They live in tunnels in the wound fiber and never go far from their knuckle. All day they work the loose ends. They find where the cable is fraying, tuck the fiber back under the winding, and pull it tight with their beaks, bracing with those big feet. A cable left alone would unravel in a season. A knuckle with reelers only grows.

Nobody knows why they do it. They nest in the fiber, so a tight winding keeps them dry, and that may be all there is to it. But the reelers on a large bobbin number in the hundreds, and they tick all night as well as all day, and the bobbins they keep are wound so tight and neat that no wind could have done it.
