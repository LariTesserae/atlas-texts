# Who Lives on the Hightable

## The Seated

They do not call themselves a nation. Each city has its own name, and the people of one mesa will tell you, gently, that they have little in common with the people of the next. But across the whole plateau, from the hollow halls of the oldest cities to the drovers' camps on the deep pavement, there is one word they all use for themselves. Translated, it means *the Seated*.

It comes from the plateau's name, and from the posture of a guest.

The Hightable is a table, and they sit at it. They did not build it, set it, or cook for it. The warmth under them was there before them. The food rises to them on the updraft from a kitchen they have never seen, and the water they drink was locked into the stone long before anyone arrived to unlock it. Everything they have was put in front of them. Their whole moral sense grows from that: they are guests, and a guest's first duty is good manners.

You hear it in the greeting. When two of the Seated meet, one says *Warm under you*, and the other answers *And the sky over*. It is a small courtesy, but it contains the entire world: heat below, cold above, and the person being greeted sitting in the gap between, which is the only place anyone can live.

## Their Bodies

The first time you see them, what strikes you is how *still* they are.

They are tall and deep-chested. Their ribs are wide and their lungs are enormous, built for air so thin that a lowlander would faint in it. Their hearts beat slowly, perhaps thirty times a minute at rest, with a heavy, unhurried push. When they walk they seem to glide, because they waste nothing. Each step is placed exactly where it needs to go, and the body above it barely moves. A Seated person running is a rare and alarming sight. It means something has gone badly wrong, and anyone who sees it stops what they are doing.

Their skin is dark, and it darkens further with age in a particular way. The Seated eat paver-flesh, fungus from the sealed cellars, and the dried meat of kites, and every one of those foods carries traces of metal: iron, manganese, a little copper. Over a lifetime the metal settles in the skin. Children are a plain warm brown. The middle-aged take on a faint sheen at the temples and the backs of the hands. The very old gleam. In the low amber light of the halls, an elder's face shines the way the pavement varnish shines at dusk, like old bronze. The Seated find this beautiful. A gleaming face means a long life and many meals from the table, and to say that someone *has gone to bronze* is a compliment.

Their eyes are deep-set under heavy brows, and they narrow them against the hard white sun until only a dark wet line shows. Outdoors most of them wear visors of smoked glass, often inherited, and some of those are older than the cities that made them. Indoors they open their eyes wide, and you discover that they are large and very dark and quick to catch light. They are built for shadow as much as for glare. On the Hightable a single step can take you from one into the other.

They sleep face down on the floor.

Stone beds are simply the stone. A sleeping-room is a smooth warm floor, sometimes with a thin felt of paver-wool, and the Seated lie on it chest-first with their palms flat, taking the hearth's warmth into their bodies the way the Reckoners do. A child who sleeps on her back is told she is *giving her warmth to the sky*. Children are born onto warm stone, too. Every city has birthing-niches cut close to a heat-bore, where the rock stays at exactly the temperature of blood.

## Speaking Through Stone

The air on the Hightable carries almost nothing. A voice goes flat and small within a few steps and is gone within a dozen. So the Seated learned to speak in two very different ways.

The first is close speech. Conversation happens nearly face to face, foreheads almost touching. What a lowlander would call intimate distance is simply where talking happens. Every exchange of words is a little confidential. The Seated have no tradition of oration, no crowds listening to a single raised voice, and they find the idea faintly absurd, like trying to pour water into the wind. Their spoken language is soft and rich in breath. It has many words for shades of *yes* and hardly any for commands.

The second way is the one that carries: they speak through the ground.

Air may swallow sound, but stone carries it beautifully, and a city on the Hightable is a single piece of stone. Walls grow out of cliffs, floors are continuous with the mesa, and the whole settlement is one great resonant body. So the Seated knock. Every hall has its knocking-stones, polished places on the walls worn smooth by generations of knuckles, and a patterned knock at one can be felt through the floor three halls away, or at the far end of the city if the knocker is skilled. They listen with their palms and the soles of their bare feet. You will often see one of them stop mid-stride, lay a hand flat on the wall, and tilt her head, reading a message that has come through the rock.

Knock-speech is slower than talking, and it is public. Anyone with a hand on the stone can read it. So it has become the language of announcements, of warnings, of the hours, and of grief. The great engines knock too, their pistons reaching the top of the stroke once a minute, and that deep regular pulse runs through every stone in the city as a common clock. The Seated tell time by it without thinking. A child's first lesson in counting is to lie on the floor and count the engine.

When a city goes quiet in the hour before sleep, the walls sometimes carry soft, irregular knocks from all over the mesa. These are not messages. They are people tapping the stone before they lie down, a habit so old that nobody can say what it means. Some say it is simply *I am here*. Others say it is for the ones who are no longer here.

## The Manners of Water

On the Hightable, water is stone, and the Seated drink as though they were quarrying.

Every cup is counted. It is not rationed exactly, because the cities are not usually that poor, but it is *noticed*. Cups are small, thick, and made of the plateau's clear glass, so that you can see how much you are taking. To pour water carelessly, or leave some in the bottom of a cup, or spill it, is not a crime. It is rudeness, like pushing away a dish your host has made. In a Seated household, whoever finishes the jug turns it upside down on the table, a small public acknowledgment of having taken the last of something.

They sweat very little. Their bodies have learned to hold their water. Their breath is dry. They do not spit. Washing is done with fine sand and soft brushes, and a bath in actual water is a luxury reserved for weddings and deathbeds.

Weeping, then, is significant. The Seated can cry, and they do, but tears are water leaving the body and falling onto stone, where they are lost. To weep in front of someone is to spend something rare on them. It is understood as a gift, the most costly a person can give without meaning to. When one of the Seated cries in your presence, the proper response is not to comfort them but to thank them. And if someone's tears fall on your hand, you do not wipe them away. You let them dry there.

## The Last Kiln

A human body is mostly water, and the Seated know it.

When one of them dies, the body is not buried, since the ground is too hard and too precious, and it is not burned, since there is nothing to burn. It is carried, with great slowness, to the city's kiln-galleries. There is always one retort set apart from the others, older and plainer, and the dead are laid in it.

The Last Kiln roasts the body the way the other kilns roast gypsum. The water is driven off as steam and rises into a condenser of its own, a single finned chimney standing a little apart from the rest. In the black cold above the city the vapour beads and runs down into the common cistern. Nothing is kept separate. The dead go back into the water the living drink. The Seated say this plainly and without horror. To them, the dead continue to *serve the table*.

For the few days after a death, the plume above the Last Kiln stands among the others, thin and white and perfectly vertical. The family watches it. Travellers on the cables, seeing an extra plume on the horizon, know that someone has died in that city, and they knock a slow pattern on the floor of their car in respect.

What remains after the water is gone is a small grey ash, and in the ash there is metal.

A lifetime of paver-meat has left iron, manganese, and copper in the bones and the gleaming skin. The smelters recover it, and this is the only metalwork the smelters will not charge for. A person yields very little, perhaps enough for a thin ring or a small flat disc. The family decides what it becomes. Often it becomes a knocking-plate, a small metal disc set into the wall of the home at the height of an adult's knuckles, so that the family's knocks ring a little brighter there. Old houses have walls studded with these plates, some worn nearly smooth.

*Metal is grown*, the Seated say. They include themselves in it.

## The Many Kinds of Seated

The cities are far apart, and life on the Hightable has divided its people into trades, each shaped over generations into something almost like a separate people.

**The kiln-folk** are the heart of every city. They quarry the mesa, feed the retorts, and keep the condensers clean. They are pale with gypsum dust and deeply practical. They know better than anyone how fast the mountain is being eaten, and they keep the count. In the oldest cities the head of the kiln-folk holds a title that translates as *the one who knows how much is left*. It is not a happy office.

**The bore-wardens** tend the heat-bores, the deep shafts through which the hearth's warmth is drawn up in oil-filled ceramic pipes. They spend their working lives underground, in the warm dark, and they are said to be a little strange: gentle, quiet, and inclined to talk about the hearth as if it had moods. Some claim to hear it. They say the deep rock has a sound beneath all other sounds, too slow to be a note, and that it has never once changed in the whole history of the cities. They find that comforting.

**The fin-tenders** work on the roofs, among the fields of pale ceramic blades that radiate heat into the sky. It is the coldest work on the Hightable. They face upward all day into the indigo, adjusting angles, replacing cracked blades, brushing off dust. They are the only Seated who spend real time looking at the stars, and they are the keepers of astronomy, poetry, and most of the plateau's songs. The songs are sung in close speech, very softly, to whoever is working beside them.

**The cable-riders** live in the enclosed cars that creep between the mesas at walking pace. A single crossing can take ten days, and some riders spend their entire lives in motion, never belonging to any city. They carry water, glass, and metal, but above all they carry news. They memorise it and repeat it word for word, in close speech, forehead to forehead with each listener in each city. They are the plateau's memory. A rider can tell you what was said in a council hall forty years ago, three cities away, in exactly the voice it was said in. Most riders are born in the cars and marry other riders at the cable stations. They say that the land is the thing that moves, and that they are the ones keeping still.

**The drovers** are the strangest of them, and in some ways the most admired.

They walk with the paver herds, for seasons at a time, on the deep pavement between the yardangs. A paver herd moves so slowly that it barely seems to move at all, and so the drovers learn to move that slowly too. They carry their water with them as stone: bricks of gypsum wrapped in felt, and small sealed flasks of clear glass. At noon a drover sets a brick of water-stone inside a flask, focuses the sun on it with a hand-lens, and holds the neck of the flask in the sharp black shadow of a yardang until a few drops run down the cold side of the glass. A day's water is a patient hour's work, and a drover never hurries it.

Drovers learn to see. They count the rocks around their camp at dusk and count them again at dawn, and when the number is off by one, they know a stilt-hunter has moved closer, and they move the herd. They can read the pale trails where pavers have grazed and tell you how many decades ago each one was made. They speak even less than other Seated, and by the end of a drive they barely speak at all. They communicate by small movements of the hands and by knocking on the pavement, which carries across the flat plain for a surprising distance.

When a drover comes back into a city after a long drive, people step aside for them in the halls. It is not quite reverence. It is more like the respect given to someone who has been somewhere very quiet for a very long time and still carries it.

**The tower-keepers** live alone, or in pairs, in the great updraft towers standing on the pavement plains. They harvest guano from the collector floors and tend the slow turbines in the towers' throats. They live inside a perpetual storm of small fast fliers, the chimney-swarms, and they learn to move through the clouds of wings without flinching. They are hermits by temperament, and most of them are also scholars, for reasons explained below.

**The lens-wardens** tend the lens-towers of the largest cities, the great clear eyes that focus sunlight onto the smelting floors. It is dangerous work. The bright point at the top of a tower on a clear afternoon can blind a careless person in an instant, and many old lens-wardens are blind. The blind ones are not retired. They are considered the best wardens, because they no longer look at the light, and they tend the lenses by touch and by the warmth on their faces. They say they can feel the exact position of the focus from the heat on their skin, and they are almost never wrong.
