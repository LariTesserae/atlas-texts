# Who Lives at Kesh-under-Stone

## I. The Woman at the Knocking-Place

Start with the easiest one to see. She is on the fourth tier of the western wall, where a ledge of worn limestone runs out beneath one of the great grey fig-trunks. Her name is Oresh, of the Tamal lineage. She is fifty-eight years old, which in Kesh is very old, and she has seen four rivers.

She is small, like most Keshi. Generations of rope and ladder have made the people of the hollow narrow-shouldered, long in the forearm, and long in the fingers, with feet that grip. Their skin is a pale, unsunned brown, because the sun has never touched most of them directly. When they go up through the crack to trade on the plateau they wear woven slat-masks over their eyes, and they come back burned anyway, peeling and tender, telling stories about how enormous the sky is and how cruel. Their eyes are large and dark and wide-pupiled, made for amber dusk. In firelight they shine a little, the way the cats' eyes do.

Oresh is almost entirely deaf. In Kesh this is not an affliction. It is what age looks like, the way grey hair looks like age elsewhere. Nobody grows old in the roar of the hollow with their hearing intact. The swifts take the high sounds first, then the bees take the middle, and by fifty most Keshi hear the world as a pressure in the breastbone and a buzz in the teeth. Because of this, and because even young ears can barely pick one voice out of that din, the Keshi do most of their talking with their hands.

They call it *the quiet*. It is a full language of fingers, palms, and small turns of the wrist, fast and dense and nearly silent. Children learn it before they learn to speak aloud. Voices are saved for three things: songs, which carry over the hum where a hand can't be seen; alarms; and the Hush, when the swifts are gone and people gather to use their mouths for the novelty of it, telling stories aloud and laughing at how strange their own voices sound. For many Keshi the Hush is the only time of year they hear the people they love say their names.

Oresh speaks the quiet the way old people do, economically, half her words folded into a single shape of the hand. Her granddaughter Ilit, who is nine, signs in wild loops and flourishes and gets scolded for "shouting."

Oresh is a tapper. Her lineage holds the tree whose trunk swells out of the rock beside her doorway, a grey bottle-belly wider than three people could reach around, which the Tamal call *She-Who-Turns-Her-Back*, because it grows facing the wall. Every morning after the swift-pour, Oresh does what her mother did and her mother's mother did. She lays her cheek against the bark, closes her eyes, and knocks.

The knock is how you know. A full tree answers with a dull, closed sound, like a fist on a sealed jar of honey. A tree running low answers hollow and ringing, like a jar half-empty. Oresh can no longer hear either sound, but she doesn't need to. She feels the knock come back into her jaw, and she knows the water in She-Who-Turns-Her-Back to within a cupful. The younger tappers of the lineage have ears and use them. They still come to Oresh to check, because ears can be fooled by the roar and a jaw cannot.

She taps the tree on the days the knock allows, with a bone spigot polished by two hundred years of Tamal thumbs, and she tastes the first drops before letting anything run into the jar. Fig-water is faintly green, faintly bitter, and very faintly sweet. Its taste changes with the season, with the tree's health, and with what the tree has been eating.

Oresh's husband has been dead for six years. The Keshi give their dead to the floor. They lay the body out on the guano, and within a few days the beetles have taken it down to nothing, the dust has taken the nothing, and the figs have taken the dust. When Oresh tastes the sap in the dry season, when it runs thickest, she sometimes tells Ilit, with her fingers and a crooked smile, *your grandfather is in a good mood today.* Ilit finds this horrifying and delightful in equal measure and asks for it again and again.

That is the first person who lives here. Look past her, though, at what she has her cheek against.

## II. The Grandmother

Every tapper lineage in Kesh knows the law. A tree overdrawn dies, and a family that kills its tree loses its place on the wall. It is the worst crime there is. Children learn it as soon as they learn the quiet, and they learn it as a matter of family and honor: you are careful with your tree because the tree is yours.

The oldest tappers know something else, which they pass to their successors late in life, in the quiet, in a closed room with the screen drawn. Oresh learned it from her mother when she was thirty-one. She has not yet told anyone. She is deciding whether it will be Ilit.

This is the secret: there are no separate trees.

There is only one fig in the hollow.

A long time ago, before Kesh, before anyone, a bat carrying fruit from some green place far beyond the plateau dropped a seed on a ledge. It was probably ten thousand years ago. It may have been much more. The seed found a crack with a little dust in it and a little breath of damp, and it sent a root down. The rest came from that. What look like the grandmother trees, the bottle-trunks spaced along the walls, each with its lineage and its spigots and its name, are swollen places on a single body. The root-bridges that cross the air of the hollow in nets and cords are not tangles between neighbors. They are the tree's own limbs, grown into each other, fused, grafted, knotted back into themselves over thousands of years until the whole hollow is strung with one living net.

When a family overdraws its trunk, that trunk does not simply die. The water in the net shifts. Somewhere across the hollow, another lineage's trunk knocks hollow a week later, and then another. The trunks share their water. The law against killing your tree is ancient because it was learned, long ago, at terrible cost. It is not a law about property. It is a law about the body everyone is living inside.

Oresh thinks of this every morning with her cheek on the bark. She is not listening to her tree. She is listening, through one small place, to the whole of it, the way you might lay a hand on someone's wrist to feel the heart at the center. When the knock is full, the whole hollow is full. When it rings hollow, the dry is coming for everyone.

The Keshi call the old trunks *grandmothers* and do not know how exactly right they are. It is all one grandmother, and she has been alive longer than the language they call her in. She put her roots down into a dead river's house and she has been drinking the river's ghost ever since. She drinks the breath of a million swifts. She drinks the dust of the dead. Every creature in the hollow, every beetle and bat and cat and child, is her gut flora, the moving part of her that turns bodies into the dung she eats.

That is the second resident, and it is larger than the first. It is still not the end.

## III. The Ones Inside the Fruit

The rock fig fruits in pulses, twice in a good year, once in a bad one. The fruits are small, hard, and bronze, clustered right against the trunk and the thick roots the way figs grow, and the Keshi grind them for flour when they are dry. What most Keshi never think about is that a fig is not a fruit at all. It is a flower turned inside out. It is a closed chamber, a hollow ball lined on the inside with hundreds of tiny blossoms that never see the sky, open to the world by only a single narrow pore at the tip.

Nothing can pollinate a flower that hides like that except something that lives inside it.

The fig wasps of the hollow are smaller than a grain of flour. Almost nobody in Kesh has ever looked at one closely, except the tappers, who are taught to leave some fruit on every trunk, always, no matter how hungry the year. They are told it keeps the grandmother happy. They are not told why. Oresh knows why, because her mother cut a green fig open for her once and showed her the wasps inside, and she has never forgotten it.

The female wasp squeezes in through the pore at the fig's tip. The passage is so tight it tears off her wings. Inside, in the dark, among the inward-facing flowers, she lays her eggs and spreads the pollen she carried in from another fig. Then she dies there. She never comes out.

Her children hatch in the dark. The males are born blind and wingless. They live their entire lives inside the fig. They mate with their sisters, chew exit tunnels through the fig's wall for them, and die without once seeing the world outside the chamber they were born in. The females, winged and dusted with pollen, crawl out through the tunnels their brothers made. They fly out into the dim amber air to find another fig, squeeze in, lose their wings, and begin again.

Oresh has thought about this for most of her adult life, and one year, during the fourth river, drunk on fig-water gone slightly to wine in the jars, she understood what it meant.

The hollow is a fig.

A closed chamber, walled all around, its flowering turned inward, open to the world by a single narrow crack at the top. Everything in it lives inside, feeds inside, breeds inside, dies inside, and becomes the fig. Most of the people of Kesh are born, live, and die in the hollow without ever going up through the crack. They are the blind wingless ones, the ones who make the tunnels. The traders are the winged ones. They squeeze up through the seam into the blinding day, carrying the pollen of the hollow, which is guano and honey and rope, out across the plateau to the herders. And sometimes one of them doesn't come back.

The herders on the far side of the plateau tell a story. Oresh has heard it from traders. It is about a crack in the stone, many days to the east, from which a faint sweet smell rises in the late afternoon. There are people living at the bottom of it too, the story goes: small, large-eyed people who talk with their hands.

Oresh does not know if this is true. She hopes it is. She has come to think that somewhere out there is a young woman from Kesh, a trader who never came home. She squeezed through a crack into someone else's hollow, in the old way, lost her wings, and started a lineage by a grey trunk that faces the wall.

## IV. What the Root Reaches

There is one more resident, and Oresh has seen it once.

She was fourteen, in the second year after her first river. A great comb had fallen and a honey-cutter she loved, a young man of the Sheb lineage, had fallen with it, and she was not in her right mind. In grief she followed the oldest root of the grandmother down, the thickest one, grey and smooth as a thigh, which runs from the base of She-Who-Turns-Her-Back and dives straight into the guano floor. She dug beside it with her hands. The guano was dry and warm, and then, deeper, it was cooler. Deeper still it was packed and dark and old, laid down before anyone had names for anything. Deepest of all, where the root went into it, it was damp.

She dug for three days, sleeping in the hole. Her family thought she had gone up through the crack to die on the plateau, as grieving people sometimes do. On the third day the root led her through a gap into a space under the floor, a pocket of the old river channel the guano had sealed over thousands of years before.

It was pitch black and utterly silent. She did not know silence could exist. The roar of the hollow was muffled above her by a man's height of packed dung. For the first time in her life she heard her own heart, her own breathing, the blood in her ears. She was so frightened she wept, and she heard that too.

Then, by the faint light of the ember she had carried in a clay pot, she saw the water.

It was a pool, or a slow seep, still and black and perfectly clear, filling the bottom of the old channel. It was not a lake, but there was more water in one place than she had seen outside a rain-year in her life. The grandmother's root went down into it and spread into a white mat like a hand with its fingers open in the dark. The tree was drinking from the river. The river had never gone. It had only gone down.

And there were things in the water.

Shrimp as pale as frost, as clear as glass, without eyes, drifted in the ember-light and did not flee from it, because they had never known light and could not see it. And lying still on the bottom, as long as her forearm, pale pink-white like the inside of a lip, was something like a lizard that had become water: a salamander, eyeless, its skin so thin she could see the dark threads of its blood, with feathery red gills at its throat moving slowly, slowly. It did not move otherwise. It might not have moved in a year. She learned much later, from an old tapper who knew, that these creatures could live a hundred years, perhaps two hundred. They could go ten years without eating. They waited in the black water for the one thing that ever came down to them from above: the River Remembering. Once a decade the storm-water poured through the crack, soaked down through the guano, and carried the dead richness of the whole hollow into their pool as a feast.

Oresh stayed with it until her ember went out. Then she felt her way up the root in the dark and climbed back into the noise. She went home and slept for two days. When she woke, she found she could grieve.

She has never told anyone about that either. But when the tappers speak of the grandmother's oldest spigots, the ones sealed with wax and opened only in the worst years, Oresh thinks of the pale thing waiting in the dark under the floor, as patient as the stone, keeping the river. She has decided privately that it is the true eldest of Kesh. The swifts come and go with the seasons. Bats live twenty years, cats ten, people fifty or sixty. The grandmother has lived ten thousand. The river-keeper in the black water may be only a century old, but it is a child of a line that has lived in that channel since the water was running. It has been the hollow's memory for longer than the hollow has had a roof.

## V. So Who Lives There

Here is the answer, the way it came clear to Oresh over a lifetime.

Two thousand people live there, and hundreds of thousands of swifts, and so many bats nobody has ever tried to count them. So do the bees in their golden curtains, the beetles and roaches and centipedes, the geckos that chirp and the geckos that bark, the snakes, the small tawny cats. So do the fig wasps, born blind in the dark of the fruit. So do the eyeless shrimp and the long pale keeper in the black water under the floor.

And all of them live inside one thing: a single fig, ten thousand years old, whose roots are the bridges and ladders of the town, whose trunks are its wells, whose body is laced through every wall of a dead river's house. It drinks the breath of the living and the dust of the dead and, through one deep white root, the patient hidden remnant of the river. The whole hollow is its flower, turned inward and open to the sky by one narrow seam. Everything that lives there is part of how it eats, how it drinks, how it breeds, and how it sends out its seed.

What lives at Kesh-under-Stone is a remembering. It is a river that refuses to be over, and has put on a body of fig and feather and fur and fiber and people to keep itself going between rains.

---

At dusk, after the bat-rise, Oresh sits on the ledge outside her doorway with her back against She-Who-Turns-Her-Back. Ilit sits beside her, signing a long complaint about a boy on the fifth tier. Oresh isn't really following it. Below them the cookfires of Kesh prick the blue smoke, and the honey-cutters are going down the ropes. Oresh can't hear their song, but she can feel it, a slow rising and falling in the stone at her back.

The tree was full this morning. The knock came back closed and solid into her jaw.

She takes her granddaughter's wandering hands in her own to quiet them. Then, in the old folded shorthand of the quiet, she makes a sign Ilit has never seen before: fingers closed, then opening downward, like a root, or like water finding its way down into the dark.

*Tomorrow,* she signs after it, *I will show you something.*
