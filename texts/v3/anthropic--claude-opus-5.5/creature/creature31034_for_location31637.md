# Who Lives in the Mound Country

## First, the Absence

You would say at first that no one lives here but the builders.

You would walk for days along the avenues the antelopes have worn, between walls of fused grey towers, under candelabra trees heavy with sap, and see no one. No smoke rises except the smoke of the swarms. No paths lead anywhere except where the browsers have pushed through. There are no fields, no fences, no hearths, and no ruins. By the description of the land there couldn't be.

Then you notice the scars.

The older mounds are patchworks of repair, and most of those patches are what they look like: the ragged, hurried plugs where a digger tore in at night and the workers sealed the wound by morning. They are rough, lumpy, and slightly darker than the wall around them.

A few are not like that. Here and there, low on the flank of a very old mound, there is a patch that is smooth. It is oval and about the length of a person lying curled. Its surface has been finished with something finer than termite pellet-work, burnished almost to a gloss, and its edges are pressed into the surrounding wall with a care no worker ever bothered with. Put your palm against one and it is faintly warmer than the rest of the wall.

That is the first sign. It is a grave. You will not understand that yet.

## The Tenants

They live inside.

Not in abandoned chambers, the way the bats do, and not in the crowns, the way the weavers do. They live in the working heart of living colonies, in the warm galleries around the fungus combs, among the workers and the soldiers, and the colonies let them.

This is not as strange as it sounds. Colonies everywhere carry lodgers: beetles, mites, silverfish, and small flies that have learned to smell like termites, to be groomed and fed and ignored. A mound is a warm, damp, defended house, and in a dry country warm, damp, defended houses are worth a great deal. Over a few thousand years in a basin with no other water, some lodger grew larger than the others. It learned the smell more completely. It stayed.

They are about the height of a child of ten, but longer, built for galleries. Their limbs are narrow, their joints loose, their shoulders able to fold forward so that they can pass through a passage no wider than their own head. They move on all fours inside and upright outside, when they come out at all, which is rarely and at night.

Their skin is the colour of the ash, and not by accident. They rub themselves daily with a paste of wall-dust and the saliva of workers, which they get by letting the workers groom them. The paste dries into a fine grey crust that seals in their water and carries the colony's scent. A tenant who has not been groomed for three days begins to smell like a stranger. The soldiers start to tick at them. On the fourth day they bite. So the tenants are never out of reach of the colony for long, and their belonging is renewed every morning by the builders' mouths.

Their eyes are very large, dark, and lidded twice: once with an ordinary lid, once with a clear membrane they keep closed outside so the glass-sharp ash will not scour them. Their noses are the most important part of them. The whole middle of the face is given over to it, a broad, soft, finely ridged organ like the gills of a mushroom. They read the colony through it: its mood, its health, the state of the fungus, the presence of ants three galleries away, whether the queen is laying well.

They do not sweat. They cannot afford to. They cool the way the mounds do, by breathing. On hot afternoons they climb the chimney shafts and hang there in the updraft, panting slowly, their breath joining the mound's breath.

## How They Drink

Like everything here, they eat their water. Mostly they eat fungus.

The combs deep in the mound fruit with small white nodules, and the workers eat these. The tenants do too. They are tolerated at the combs, and more than tolerated. They carry leaf litter in from outside, great bundles of it bound to their backs with vine. They come in through gaps the workers open for them, and they lay it where the workers will chew it into new comb. They are the colony's long-legged foragers, able to haul a load from the canopy that a thousand workers could not move in a night.

In return they eat the fruiting fungus and drink the air. That is not a figure of speech. The galleries around the combs are so saturated that a tenant sleeping there with its mouth open takes in water through the soft lining of its throat. They sleep a great deal, curled against the warm comb-walls. The sleep is not idleness. It is drinking.

On swarm nights they eat the winged ones like everyone else, but they eat them differently. They do not gorge. They take a few, carefully, from the exit-slits of neighbouring colonies, never their own.

## The Voice

They do not speak aloud. Sound is wasted breath, and breath is wasted water, and the ash swallows it anyway.

They speak the way the soldiers do: through the walls.

A tenant presses its fingertips to a gallery wall and taps. Its nails are hard, thick, and slightly spoon-shaped, and the tapping is finer and more varied than anything the soldiers produce. It uses rhythms, pressures, and the flat of the finger against the point of it. The fused architecture carries the sound a long way. Whole districts are one continuous structure, so a message tapped in one mound can be felt, faintly, three mounds away. It can be relayed from there and carried further.

That is the dry ticking under the soldiers' alarm, if you know how to hear it. Press your ear to the wall and listen past the patter of the builders. Some of the ticking has a grammar.

What do they say? Mostly practical things. Ants on the eastern arcade. The digger is back. The comb in the lower south gallery smells wrong. A queen is failing. But they also do something harder to name: they tap in the dark for a long time, in slow patterns that do not seem to carry information, and the tenants of other mounds tap back in the same patterns. It might be singing. It might be something closer to what the continent does at dusk, when it rustles and seems to exhale through its teeth.

They have no name for themselves. A name would have to be said. The nearest thing is a pattern, three light strikes and one heavy, pressed into a wall by a tenant who wants another to know who is there.

## What They Are For

Here is the thing it takes longest to see.

A colony is not immortal. It lives as long as its queen, perhaps a few decades, and when she dies the colony dies with her. Its walls stand, and a new colony may move in, or a neighbour may annex them. But the particular colony, its fungus strain, its scent, and its history of wars and treaties along its shared walls, is gone.

The tenants live longer than that. They live a very long time, much as the succulents do, slowly, on little. A single tenant may see four or five queens come and go in the same mound. When a colony fails, its tenants do not leave. They stay in the cooling galleries, keep the combs alive by hand, and wait.

When the new colony arrives, pioneers crossing from a neighbouring district or a founding pair digging in after a swarm, the old tenants are there. They carry the smell of the old comb and the knowledge of the old walls: where the damp runs richest, which chimney clogs in dust storms, and where the ants always try to break in. Slowly, over years, they let themselves be groomed into the new colony's scent. They teach it, in the only way anything teaches termites, by moving through its galleries and changing where the builders go.

The boundary wars between colonies are slow and quiet. They are slower and quieter than they would otherwise be because, on each side of a contested wall, there is a tenant tapping to the tenant on the other side.

So the city is continuous not only because its walls have fused. It is continuous because something inside the walls remembers.

You begin to understand them less as a people and more as a function. The builders make the walls. The fungus makes the water. The plants bind the walls and feed the fungus. The tenants carry the plants in, carry the memory forward, and keep the water from getting loose. They are the part of the country that lasts longer than a colony and less long than a stone.

## The Dead

Nothing is ever loose. That includes them.

When a tenant is dying, it tells the walls. Others come, slowly, from neighbouring mounds, and for a few nights the tapping in that district changes. Then the dying one goes to the lowest living gallery of its mound, near the comb, where it is warmest and dampest, and curls up.

The others seal it in. They do this themselves, not the workers. They mix wall-dust with their own saliva, which is precious, and press the courses into place with their palms, smoothing each one. The finish is burnished with the flat of the thumb until it gleams. That is the oval patch, warm to the touch.

The fungus comes through the wall from the inside. Over a year or two it takes the body, and the water in the body returns to the comb, which feeds the colony, which breathes it out through the chimneys on cool nights, where it condenses on the leaves of the succulents rooted in the walls, where it beads at dawn and is drunk by a hundred small mouths.

The old mounds at the centre of the basin, the tallest ones, under the thickest canopy, where the damp is richest, are the ones with the most burnished scars. Walk there and you are walking through a place where the green is partly made of them.

This is why they are so few. There is no surplus. There are enough to carry the leaves, remember the walls, and fill the scars, and no more.

## The Walk to the Edge

Their young are born inside, in the warmest galleries, and for their first years they never go out. They are groomed by workers from the first day, so their first smell is the colony's.

When a young tenant is grown, it waits for a swarm.

On the evening after a dry storm, when the cool downdraft comes and the whole basin lifts and the city seems to smoke, the young tenant climbs out through an exit-slit alongside the winged ones. It does not fly. It watches the columns go up into the sunset, and it watches where they drift. Then it starts walking.

It walks toward the edge of the basin, where the ash is new and bare and the mounds are knee-high domes. It goes along antelope-paths, through districts whose walls do not know its smell, at night only, sleeping by day in whatever abandoned chamber it can find. The soldiers tick at it the whole way. It takes weeks. Many do not arrive. The diggers take some, and the ants, and the dry.

Strapped to its back, wrapped in waxy vine-leaves and sealed with paste, it carries a piece of its home comb. It is living fungus, kept damp by the tenant's own breath, which the tenant breathes into the bundle each morning at the expense of its own thirst.

At the edge, where a founding pair has landed and shed their wings and begun to dig, the young tenant finds them. It waits, very still, very patient, like the succulents at noon. It lets the founders smell it. If they do not drive it off, and they usually do not, because it smells of a colony and a colony is what they are trying to become, it digs beside them.

It gives them the comb.

That is how new cities are seeded. Not by the builders alone, and not by the plants alone. At the base of every pioneer mound on the new ash there are the orange and green rosettes of lichen, one or two young succulents, and somewhere just inside, a single tenant. It is curled against a comb no bigger than a fist, breathing on it, waiting for the walls to rise.

A generation from now it will be a city. The tenant will have been groomed into its scent. Its fingertips will know every wall. By then it will be old, and there will be a first smooth, burnished scar low on the flank of the first great mound. There has not yet been time for ruins. There has been just enough time for graves.

## Last

If you stood high enough to see the whole country, the dense grey cores, the radiating avenues, the honeycomb of districts, the green and grey interleaved, you would not see them. They are too few, too quiet, and too much the colour of the ash.

But at night, if you pressed your ear to any wall anywhere in the basin and waited, you would hear, under the patter of the soldiers and the whisper of the tunnels, three light strikes and one heavy.

And then, from very far away, through a hundred fused mounds, a reply.
