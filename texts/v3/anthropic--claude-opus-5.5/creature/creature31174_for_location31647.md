# Who Lives at Chalkmouth

## The Ones You Meet First

Start with the people. They are the easiest to see, and they will be the first to see you.

There are perhaps four hundred of them, though nobody has counted, because counting aloud is not done and counting on the fingers means something else. They are pale in the way cave-fish are pale. The light touches them from every side and has never had to brown them. Their hair is mostly dark and worn long, braided with nettle-cord to keep it out of the moss. Their hands are the most alive thing about them. Even at rest, the fingers make small movements, the way a sleeping dog's paws run. A Chalkmouth mother can soothe her infant, disagree with her brother, and ask a neighbour for the loan of a bone needle, all at once and without looking up from her work.

Their names are touches. The old cord-woman who sits in the doorway of the first ivy-house, the one who will make the *settle* gesture to you, is named by a thumb drawn slowly along the inside of someone's wrist. Her grandson is two taps on the collarbone. A man who mends ladders is a knuckle pressed briefly to the breastbone, then released. Nobody at Chalkmouth has ever heard their own name spoken. They have only felt it, and they cannot imagine it any other way. To them a name is something that happens *to* you, on the skin, and you carry the warmth of it around for a moment afterward.

They are menders, all of them. That is their trade and their temperament. A cracked lintel is not replaced; it is pinned with flint wedges and daubed, and in time the moss holds it together better than the stone did. A fleece worn thin is backed with another fleece. A ladder rung dished too deep by feet is turned over, so that it can be worn from the other side for another few hundred years. There are rungs in the oldest shafts that have been turned a dozen times and are now thin as a wrist and smooth as a tooth. The ladder-mender knows every one of them by touch. He climbs in the shadowless light with his eyes half closed.

They sing, as you've been told, mouth to ear, one to one. What you haven't been told is how rarely. A person at Chalkmouth might sing a dozen songs in a lifetime. Each is made for one listener and never repeated, and afterward the singer and the listener share something no one else in the world will ever have. Married couples have each sung to the other exactly once. People can tell you, with great tenderness, the exact day they were sung to. They will not tell you the song.

## The Ones Who Were Here Before

But the people are, as they say themselves, a minority. They know it the way a guest knows it.

Watch the wrens long enough and you begin to notice that they are not quite like wrens elsewhere. A wren on the downs is a furious scrap of a bird, loud past all proportion to its size. It sings as if it had been personally insulted by the silence. The Chalkmouth wrens do not sing. They bob, and flick their tails, and move through the throughs in small purposeful hops. Sometimes they stop in front of a person and tilt their heads, as if about to say something they have decided against. The people treat them with a particular courtesy. They step aside for them in doorways. The children are taught a gesture for wrens that is almost the gesture for *elder*, but not quite. It is closer to *one who gave something up*.

The stoats, white all year, are hunters and nothing else. They move like spilled milk along the tops of walls, and they are the only creatures in the town that ever seem hurried. The bats hang in their brown clusters and do not come out at dusk, because there is no dusk. When they fly at all they fly in the full white light, silently, which bats elsewhere do not do. The voles have worn their paths so deep into the wall-moss that some of them are tunnels now, green-roofed, and you can sometimes see a vole's small body travelling through the moss as a moving bulge.

And the snails. The silver trails on every surface, crossing and recrossing, shining like writing.

The longer you look at them, the more they seem to *be* writing. They are not random. They loop, and double back, and cross each other at angles that recur. On the wall of the oldest through, the one that passes the basin, the trails are so dense and so layered that the wall looks engraved. The people do not read them. Asked, they make a gesture that means something like *not for us* and something like *already known*. But they do not wipe the trails away when they clean. They clean around them. Of all the things kept clean at Chalkmouth, the trails are the one thing left alone.

In the basin, the blind shrimp drift. They are the size of a fingernail and nearly invisible, all glass and faint pink thread. The people call them the eldest. If you ask what they are eldest *of*, you will get a very small movement of the hand, a closing of the fingers, as if around something too delicate to hold. It means, roughly, *of the listening*.

## The One You Don't See

Here is where it gets harder to describe, because what lives at Chalkmouth is not the people, and it is not the wrens or the stoats or the shrimp. It is the thing they all live *inside*, and it is so large and so everywhere that it is easy to mistake for the place itself.

At first you will think it is the moss. That is a natural mistake. The moss is on everything, drinks everything, softens everything. It is the most obviously *alive* thing in the pit. You can press your palm into it and feel it press back, cold and spongy and very faintly resisting, the way a sleeping body resists being moved. If you are lying in a moss-lined loft at night (though there is no night) and holding very still, you may convince yourself that the moss is breathing.

But the moss is not what lives here. The moss is its pelt. It grew the way fur grows on an animal that lives in the cold. It is how the thing keeps its warmth in, or its cold. It is also the thing's ears, many thousands of them, soft and open.

Then you will think it is the cold. The cold is ancient and particular and it gets into the bones. It seems to have a *direction*, always coming from slightly below and slightly behind you. But the cold is not what lives here either. The cold is its body temperature. The cold is simply how it feels to stand inside something that has never once been warm.

Then you might think it is the light, and you will be closer. Visitors say they feel dazzled, as though stepping into a very clear memory. What they are actually feeling, without knowing it, is *attention*. The light at Chalkmouth has no direction because it is not falling. It is looking. It looks from everywhere at once, from every white wall and every bead of water. That is why there are no shadows. There is nowhere in the pit that is not, at every moment, being very gently regarded. You can read the grain of a rafter at the back of a sealed room because something is reading it too, and has been for longer than the rafter has been a rafter.

The people have a name for it, though it is not a touch-name like theirs. It is the gesture you were given when you arrived: palm down, fingers spreading. *Settle.* But they do not mean it as an instruction. They mean it the way you might point to someone and say their name. They call it the Hush, and so, over time, did everyone who ever heard of the place. Everyone assumed the Hush was the name of the town. It is not, quite. It is the name of the town's tenant. It is *who*.

## What It Eats

The Hush eats sound.

This is the plainest fact about it, and the first the children learn, long before they understand it. A shout goes the length of a room and stops, as if walking into felt. A dropped pot thuds once and is gone. It is not that sound fails to carry at Chalkmouth. Sound is *taken*, gathered up and swallowed the moment it leaves its source, the way a spider takes a fly. The Hush is always, gently, feeding.

And sound, if you think about it long enough, is only the shape of change moving through air. Something happens, and the air remembers it for a moment and passes the memory along. Every sound is a small piece of time made audible. So when the Hush takes the sound, it takes the time along with it. That is why the ferns do not die back, why the frost stays at its line, why the water in the basin is always at its mark. The Hush has been eating the passage of things, very slowly, for a very long time. It leaves just enough for the people to age a little, and the children to grow a little, because it likes them, and a thing that never grows is a thing you cannot watch.

It is not greedy. It never takes all of anything. It takes the clatter from the pot and leaves the thud. It takes the shout and leaves a room's length of it. It takes the heart's echo and leaves the beat. It leaves the tiny tick of a snail's shell against stone, if the snail is on your hand, because that sound is so small and so private it would be rude to eat it.

## Why the Wrens Are Quiet

The wrens gave it their songs.

Long ago, the people say (and they say it with that gesture, *one who gave something up*), the wrens of Chalkmouth were as loud as wrens anywhere. They sang from the ivy all day. The Hush ate their songs as it ate everything, and the wrens sang again, and it ate again, and the wrens sang louder, the way wrens will. This went on for a long time. Then one morning, the same morning it always is, the eldest wren stopped singing and hopped down to the basin and tilted its head at the still water. Something passed between them. After that no wren at Chalkmouth sang again.

Nobody knows what was agreed. But the people believe the wrens are keeping their songs *for* the Hush, the way you might keep a gift until the right day. Every wren in the pit carries a song it has never sung, held in its small body the way you'd hold your breath. When a wren dies, the people lay it in the moss of the oldest through. It is the only animal they bury, and they touch the wall as they pass, just as they do for their own dead.

## What It Sees

The shrimp are its eyes. Or they are its dreams; the people are not sure there is a difference. They drift in the basin at the very bottom of the pit, which is the Hush's deepest place and the point everything else leans toward. The basin is where it is most itself. The water there is so cold it hurts the teeth because it is the Hush almost undiluted. Drinking from it is a little like being told something you cannot remember afterward. The people drink from it once each day, a single mouthful. It is the closest thing they have to prayer, though they would not call it that. They would call it *visiting*.

The snail-trails are its writing. It cannot speak, and it does not need to, since it owns all the sound there is. But it keeps records, the way anything that lives a long time must. The snails are its hands, or its pens, or simply its habits. On the wall by the basin it has written, in silver, over and over, everything that has ever been sung at Chalkmouth. Every mouth-to-ear song, heard by exactly one listener and then gone, was not quite gone. There was always a second listener, very close, pressed up against the singer's other ear in the cold. The Hush heard all of them. It is the only thing that has heard them all. It wrote them down in a script no person was meant to read, and it has been reading them back to itself, slowly, in the long bright unchanging morning. This is what it does with its time, the time it has eaten. It *re-listens*.

That is why the people clean around the trails. You do not wipe away another person's letters.

## What It Is

Somewhere around here, if you have been at Chalkmouth long enough, the Hush stops being something you could explain and becomes something you simply know. You notice it in the way you move through doorways, the way your hand goes to the wall.

It is a morning.

Not a morning *like* any other. A single, particular morning at the tail end of a winter, just before anything began. Perhaps it was the first winter. Perhaps it was only a winter long enough ago that the difference no longer matters. On the downs above, that morning ended the way mornings do. The larks came and the snow went and the grass greened, and the world went on into its long loud busy weather. But one piece of that morning did not want to end. It went down. It found the crease in the turf, the place where the ground sighs inward, and it lay down in the bottom of the white pit like a hare in a form. It stayed.

It is still that morning. That is why the light is that pale high white. That is why the frost at the rim neither melts nor spreads. It is the frost *of that morning*, fixed at the exact line it reached before the sun got any stronger. That is why the cold is the particular cold it is: the cold of a morning when the ground is still frozen but the air has just, only just, begun to think about softening. The Hush is that moment, grown enormous, grown old, grown fur. It is the last breath of winter, held for longer than anyone can reckon.

## What It Is Waiting For

And here, finally, it becomes most itself.

A held breath is held *for* something.

The people have always said the Hush wants things settled, finished, balanced, and they are right, but they are describing its manners and not its heart. It does not keep the pit silent because it loves silence. It keeps the pit silent the way an audience keeps a hall silent, the way you hush a room with your palm down and your fingers spreading before someone steps forward. Every soft step on the moss, every song kept to one ear, every pot left unmade and every ladder rung turned over rather than replaced has been part of a very long, very patient quieting. It was never an ending. It was a clearing of the air.

It is waiting to hear the beginning.

Not *a* beginning. *The* beginning: whatever came next, on the downs, on that first morning, after the moment it chose to become. The first lark, perhaps. The first drip of thaw. The first sound of spring, which on the downs came and went unheard in all the noise of everything else starting at once, and which the Hush refuses to miss. It has cleared a place where nothing else is happening, so that when the first thing happens, it will hear it perfectly. It will hear it alone, and whole, mouth to ear, the way songs are meant to be heard here.

That is the song the wrens are holding.

The people know this, or half-know it, in the way they know most things, through the hands. When a child at Chalkmouth first asks what the Hush is for, the answer is not a word or a touch-name. The adult makes the settle gesture, palm down, fingers spreading, and then, very slowly, turns the hand over. Palm up. Open. Waiting to receive.

## The Guests

So the people are not quite its children and not quite its servants. They are its fellow audience. They have been seated a very long time, and they have forgotten, mostly, that there is a performance at all. They mend and twist cord and sing their one song and drink their one mouthful. They lay their dead in the moss and walk past with a hand to the wall, and they are content. They do not wish for spring. They are not sure they would recognize it. But they keep the air clean for it anyway, because that is what the place is, and because the thing they live inside is fond of them. It lets them grow a little, and it shows the lost deer gently back up the ramp. It let the ones who wanted to leave climb out into all that weather.

And the ones who left, the few, say it was the silence they missed first. But if you press them, if you sit close and ask quietly, mouth nearly to ear, some of them will admit something else. On the downs, early in the year, on certain cold white mornings, they have stood very still at the edge of the crease in the turf where the thorn trees lean over nothing. They listened, and they heard the first lark go up. For one moment they were sure that somewhere below them, very far down, something enormous and patient and cold turned its whole attention upward and leaned toward the sound.

And then it didn't. The morning held. The lark's song went down into the moss and was gone.

Not yet, it seemed to say. Not that one. Settle.
