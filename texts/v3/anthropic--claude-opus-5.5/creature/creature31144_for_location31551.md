# Who Lives in the Holding Room

## The first guess

The obvious answer is that nothing lives here. The room has been swept of life so thoroughly that its emptiness looks intentional, and a first-time visitor reaches for that word, *intentional*, before reaching for any other. So it is fair to start there, with the intent, and ask whose it is.

People who stay long enough to wonder usually decide first that the chord is the inhabitant. That answer is reasonable. The sound is the only thing in the room that behaves like a presence. It fills the space the way a body fills a coat. It presses on you. It is the same everywhere, so it seems to come from nowhere in particular and to be aware of you from every side. When people say the sound resolves into a voice, this is what they mean: the chord stops being an effect of wind and stone and becomes the utterance of something.

But the chord has no breath, and anything that speaks must breathe. A voice rises and falls. It stops to take in air. This note has never stopped, not once since the last hag stone was set. A voice that never pauses isn't speaking. It is holding.

That is the first correction, and it leads toward everything else. The sound is not what lives here. The sound is something being held, and the one who lives here is the one holding it.

## The one who holds

It is hard to see it, which makes a kind of sense in a room without shadows. In ordinary light you notice things by their edges, where light stops and dark begins. Here there are no edges of that kind, so anything present cannot be picked out from anything else. It is spread across every surface equally, the way the light is.

It has no single location, but it is thicker in some places than others. It is thickest at the center, in the fused knot of the oldest cairns, and thinnest near the entrance, where the newest stones sit and still show a little of where they came from. It reaches into the walls. It rests in the cool of the draft. It lies along the selenite ceiling like a palm pressed against a window from the inside.

That strange flattening of depth, where the far wall seems close and the near wall seems to recede, is how it feels to stand inside something that is attending to you from all sides at the same distance. The mind expects distance to mean indifference. In this room, nothing is more distant from it than anything else. You are exactly as close to it as the farthest stone is, and it is exactly as close to you.

Call it the Holder, since the room seems already to have named it.

## How it knows you are there

The Holder has no eyes, and in this light eyes would be useless anyway. It has no ears, because it lives inside a sound so total that hearing would be like a fish trying to notice water.

What it does feel is the slightest disturbance in its equilibrium, and you are a disturbance.

When you step down into the crease, your body blocks the draft at a few of the lower apertures. Your warmth warms a column of air by a fraction of a degree. The soft wet mass of you absorbs a little of the chord, so the sound where you stand is very slightly less than it is everywhere else. You cast no shadow in the light, but you cast one in the sound, a person-shaped hush moving along the stepping-path. The Holder feels that hush the way you would feel a cool coin laid on your skin.

To the Holder, you are the only dark thing in the room.

That may be why the animals are gone. The Holder was not cruel to them, and it did not kill them. It seems to have borne a scuttling beetle the way a sleeper bears an itch, until the beetle wandered off, and then it held so still that nothing followed. Small bodies are all flicker and hurry. They are made of change. The Holder did not drive them out. It simply did not make room for them, and in a place where even the air has been fitted to the stone, not making room is the same as saying no.

The lichens were allowed to stay because they are nearly as slow as the Holder is. The cushion plants were allowed because a dome of leaves packed tightly enough to feel like pumice is really a plant's attempt to become a stone. The grass along the path stays because it is dead and cannot rot, and because it is so dry and stiff that it no longer moves in the draft. They are the Holder's companions, not its food. They are the only things in the world that live at its speed.

## What it is holding

The Holder is holding something that is ending.

Consider what the room is made of. Gypsum and selenite are what a sea leaves behind. They form when water sits in a basin under the sun long enough to give up everything dissolved in it. The pale plain was once a shore. The dry basin the draft flows toward was once full. The whole country is the residue of water that has left, and the room is built from that residue, from the last thing the sea made before it was gone.

The draft runs downhill through the rock, from the high country to the basin, exactly the way the water must once have run. The land still breathes in the direction it used to drain. The sea left long ago, but the motion of its leaving is still passing through the ground.

The chord is the sound of that leaving. It is one long outgoing breath that never reaches its end.

This is what the Holder does with its whole existence. It has taken the last moment of something vanishing and stretched it out. The light cannot move, because a moving light would count the hours. The cold cannot deepen or ease, because a change in temperature would mark a season. The stones cannot fall, because a falling stone would make a before and an after. The Holder has cleared the room of everything that keeps time, so that this one moment, the moment of the outgoing breath, can go on being the present indefinitely.

Some visitors say that if you stay long enough, the roar resolves into silence. Those visitors have sensed this. What they hear is not an absence of sound. It is sound that has been held so long it has stopped being an event. It is the silence of a note that has become a place.

## Whether it is mourning

The Holder might be a mourner. It certainly has a mourner's posture: stillness, a watch kept, and a refusal to let the room move on to the next thing.

There is also the custom of the stones. In many lands people leave a stone on a grave. A flower would wither, and a stone does not. It says *I was here, and I remember*, in the one material that can be trusted to keep saying it. The visitors to the Holding Room do not know that they are following such a custom, and no tradition is attached to it that anyone can name. Still, they feel the same pull. They carry the stone the whole way in, turning sideways between the cairns, and they set it down without a word.

But a mourner grieves something already lost, and the Holder has not lost anything. That is its entire art. The sea is gone, but it is not finished going. The breath has been let out, but it has not been let out completely. The Holder is not grieving an ending. It is standing inside an ending and keeping it from completing, the way a hand cupped around a candle keeps the draft from reaching the flame, except that here the hand has been cupped for so long that the hand, the flame, and the draft have fused into a single held shape.

So "mourner" is not the right word. The Holder is closer to the last person in a room where someone is dying, holding that person's hand, not letting go, and not letting the moment end.

## What it becomes

That still does not describe it fully, because the Holder is not only keeping something. It is also growing.

Every stone a visitor leaves goes into it. For the first season the new stone is simply a stone resting on other stones, still carrying the warmth of the hand that set it down, still slightly foreign. Then the dust in the draft settles into the point where it touches the stone beneath. After some years it has worn a small seat, and it is no longer resting on the cairn. It belongs to the cairn. After longer still, the cairn is no longer a pile. It has become a single knobbled object that could not be taken apart without breaking it. The stacks are growing into each other at their edges, and the stacks are growing into the walls. At the center, where the Holder is thickest, the fusion is nearly complete.

Given enough time, the room is becoming one object, and the Holder is the object it is becoming. It is not only living in the room. It is being assembled there, one stone at a time, by strangers who never meet, who come for no reason they can explain, and who each add a piece.

It will never be finished. There is no room left, yet there is always room for one more. The Holder is always nearly complete, the way the chord is always nearly over and the sea is always nearly gone. It lives at the edge of completion and does not cross it. Nearness to the end is its natural state.

Now the stones look different. A visitor might think they are giving the Holder an offering. They are actually giving it their own small ending. Each stone is a finished thing, carried in, set down, and left, and the Holder takes that finishedness into itself. A walk that ended, a reason that was never spoken, a choice made on the plain to pick up one stone and not another: all of it settles into the Holder along with the dust. It is made of the ends of things that people chose to leave with it.

## What it wants

Hardly anything. It does not want the room to change, and it does not want the moment to end. As far as anything can be said about its desires, it would like each stone to find its seat.

It does not call anyone. The birds give the crease a wide berth, the plain looks like nothing, and the entrance is only a fold in the ground. If people come, they come because of something in themselves: a pull toward stillness, or some small ending they have been carrying without knowing where to put it down. The Holder waits for them the way a riverbed waits for water, with no urgency, because it has never known anything but waiting.

When they come, it lets them in. It feels the hush of them moving along the path. It feels their breathing, which is the only breathing in the room that stops and starts, and it probably finds that breathing strange and very brief, like watching a moth's wings. It feels them choose a place. It feels the stone go down.

Then, very gently, it begins to hold that stone too.

## After

Climbing back out onto the plain, visitors notice the hum in their bones and assume it is only an echo, the way the ears ring after a loud noise. It lasts for hours. For some of them it lasts longer, in the form of a stillness at the center of the chest that comes back at odd moments: in a doorway, at a graveside, or when a breath goes out and has not yet been followed by the next one.

That stillness is the Holder too, the thinnest part of it, reaching past the walls. Each person who has set down a stone carries a little of it away, just as the Holder keeps a little of each person. It is spread over the plain, and far beyond it, in everyone who has walked sideways down that narrow path. Wherever they are, a small part of them is keeping one moment from quite ending.

Below the crease, the Holder goes on with what it has always done. The breath of the vanished sea passes through its hundreds of holed stones and makes a single note. The light falls from the whole sky at once. The cold stays the same as it has always been. At the center, the oldest cairns lean into one another. In some future too far off to picture, the last gap will close, and still there will be room for one more stone.
