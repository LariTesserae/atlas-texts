# Who Keeps the Coursing

## The small tenants

Start with the lintels, because that is where your eyes will first catch anything moving.

Hold your face close to the underside of any doorway stone and wait for a kiln-mouth to flare somewhere behind you. For an instant the lintel is lit from below, and you will see that the condensation beaded there is not all water. Some of the beads have a rim. Some of them are very slightly, very slowly, walking.

The masons call them *beadlings*. Each one is a fingernail across, clear as the water it drinks, domed like a drop that decided to stay. A beadling clings to cold stone with a soft sucking lip and drinks the seep before it can run. It swells as it drinks. When it has grown too heavy to hold on, it lets go and falls, usually into a gutter, because the gutters are cut exactly where drops fall. The current carries it off. Somewhere downstream it catches on a scupper-lip, climbs a wall, finds another lintel, and starts again. A beadling spends its life falling and climbing. It never has to decide which way is up, because the water decides for it.

In kiln-light a lintel crowded with beadlings looks strung with tiny lamps, each holding a point of orange at its center. Children lie on their backs in doorways to watch them, and are told off for it, because a doorway is for walking through.

The **lime-moths** you have already met, or at least their work: the furred joint, the pale cloud rising out of a district's walls. What the description leaves out is how they sound. A moth-rise is soundless, and in the Coursing that is the loudest thing that can happen. For the length of a rise the knocks stop clicking, the masons stop singing, and you realize how much of the basin you had been hearing as silence was really the soft ceaseless noise of small lives. Then the moths are gone, the hammers are still falling, and you notice you have been holding your breath.

The **knocks** roost head-down in sockets, the square holes left where a softened block has been pulled and not yet replaced. There are never enough sockets, so the masons have made a courtesy of it: in every tower, one block-socket at the fourth course is left open for good. It is never filled and never repointed. It is the only hole in the Coursing that is allowed to stay a hole. You can find a district's oldest tower by counting how many knocks hang in its courtesy-socket. The old sockets are crammed, a dozen leathery bodies packed like folded gloves, all ticking faintly as they sleep.

The knocks read the architecture in echoes, and they read it very well. The masons will tell you, and not quite as a joke, that a knock has never once flown into a wall. They will also tell you, more quietly, that a knock will sometimes swerve around a wall that isn't there, veering hard in empty air above a court as though something solid stood in its path. Masons watch for this. Where the knocks swerve, the masons often build.

## The eels go uphill

The **silt-eels** live in the sumps, sifting, pale, soft as unfired clay. For most of their lives that is all anyone sees of them.

But once in a long while, never on any schedule the masons have managed to learn, the eels leave.

They come up out of the sumps at night, though every hour here is night, and they go against the water. They climb the channels first, then the gutters, then the runnels, then the scuppers, pouring themselves upward over each clay lip against the thin descending seep. They go up stairs. They go up the drip-grooves cut into walls, flattening themselves into them like tongues in a mold. For the few days of an eel-climb the whole basin is threaded with slow pale bodies moving the wrong way through its plumbing, and the masons step carefully and speak softly and do not dredge.

The eels are going to the rim, to where the water begins, to the freshest gutters in the Coursing. Those are the ones no mason cut, out past the last hammer anyone knows.

Months later the young come down. They are threads, barely thicker than a hair, grey-white, and they arrive in the sumps with the silt. The masons believe the eels are born in the new courses. They believe it the way they believe the floor goes down forever, as something too plain to argue about.

## The masons, closer

Now the people.

You will hear a mason before you see one, but not by their voice. Their clothes give them away. The grey mineral cord that lashes the hammer-arms is also what they wear: it is braided finer, woven into stiff sleeveless shifts and wide wraps, and it squeaks. Every movement a mason makes comes with a small dry creak of fiber against fiber. The sound is particular to each one, because each garment is woven by hand and worn into its own habits, so masons recognize each other in the dark by the squeak of an elbow. A mason who wants to go unnoticed has to move very slowly, and they almost never want that.

They are pale, as you would expect of people who have never been under a sun. The pallor has a faint grey cast, because lime-dust settles into everything and a mason's skin is never quite clean of it. Around the eyes and mouth, where they sweat at the kilns, the dust makes fine dark lines, so that even young masons seem to be squinting at something far off.

Their hands are the thing to look at. They are broad, as the description says, but the fingertips matter more. The pads have thickened into something between callus and instrument, dense and slightly spongy, and a mason pressing a fingertip to a wall is not touching it. They are *listening* to it. They feel the hammers through stone the way you feel a voice through a shared pillow. A mason with a palm flat on a tower can tell you which hammers are running, whether a sump is near full, and whether someone is climbing the stair on the far side.

Their ears are good but guarded. Near the loudest hammers they wear plugs of soft-fired clay, shaped to each person's ear and carried in a pouch at the neck. The plugs change what a mason hears without stopping it. The pounding sinks into the bones of the skull, and they find they hear with their teeth. Old masons clack their teeth together to test a sound, a small habit that looks like chewing on nothing.

### Where they sleep

There are no houses. The Coursing has endless rooms, but no one keeps one as theirs.

Masons sleep in *warm walls*: thick walls built hollow, with a narrow flue running up through them from the back of a kiln. The heat of the firing soaks into the masonry and stays there for days, and a mason sleeps pressed into a long body-shaped niche in the wall's face, wrapped in cord, with the kiln-warmth coming through the stone into their back. Warm walls are the most crowded places in the basin. A good one sleeps twenty. They are also, like everything else, repointed and re-laid, so a mason's bed is never the same bed twice. It is always the same shape, though, because the niches are cut to a pattern older than anyone's memory.

To sleep beside someone is to share their wall. It is the closest thing the masons have to a household. *We're of the Hollow Kiln wall* means something like *we're family*. People move between walls through a life, and the moving is marked. When you leave a wall you press your hand into the soft mortar of its newest joint, and the print stays until the joint is raked out.

### What they eat

Nothing grows in the Coursing, but the sumps are not quite dead. On the surface of the settled silt, in the stillest corners, forms a thin grey film, slick and faintly elastic. The masons lift it off in sheets on flat clay paddles. This is *curd*. They press it, dry it against the outside of a kiln, and eat it in flat crackling cakes that taste of lime and iron and something faintly sweet, like the smell after rain on hot stone. It is what they live on, every day.

The eel-climb is the only feast. Some eels always fail on the way up, stranded on a stair when a trickle shifts. The masons gather those and roast them in the kiln-mouths, and for a night or two everyone's fingers are greasy and the singing across the courts turns ragged and loud and less useful.

### What they are called

A mason is named for the hammer nearest to where they were born. *Cracked-Bell. Double-Knock. Slow-Gulp. The One That Hesitates.* Children are held up against a wall within an hour of birth so they can hear their name through the stone.

Hammers are not fixed things, though. A trickle changes; a channel is re-cut; the hammer's voice shifts, speeds, slows, falls silent. When that happens, the masons named for it take a new name from whatever hammer now has its place. A mason may carry four or five names across a life, and they speak of their own past selves in the third person: *When I was Long-Tip, I worked the eastern sumps. Long-Tip was a fool about the eels.* Nobody finds this strange. You are named for what you hear, and what you hear changes.

### The children

There are few. You can go a long way through the Coursing without seeing one. When you do, you see them overhead: small pale shapes crossing between towers at the third course, where the corbels nearly touch, stepping over the gap without looking down.

The children check the drip-lines, because they fit where adults can't, in the forearm-wide gaps and inside the flues. They learn the hammers the way other children learn songs. They play a game in which one child names a place and the others must reach it by ear alone, eyes shut, following the voices. They are very good at it. They are also the only ones who run in the Coursing. Adults never run, because running is how you slip in a gutter. The sound of small bare feet slapping wet pavers in a quick irregular patter, like a hammer with too much water, is how you know there are children in a district.

## The only thing that wears

Here is what took me longest to see, and it changes everything else once you've seen it.

Nothing in the Coursing is worn. No step is dished. No corner is rounded. Everything is kept knife-true, pulled and replaced before it can soften.

Except the masons.

Their fingertips thicken. Their knuckles swell and round. Their backs take the curve of the warm-wall niches and the long-handled scoops. Their voices, pitched for years to cut through the pounding, go hoarse and grainy, then thin. Their hearing, guarded as it is, goes anyway, a little at a time, until an old mason hears the basin only through their teeth and hands. Their teeth wear down from testing sounds.

In all the made world, among millions of edges kept sharp, the masons are the only things allowed to become old. It is not really allowed. There is simply no one to pull them and set fresh ones.

I think the masons know this in the way they know the floor goes down. They are quietly, persistently tender toward age, the way you are tender toward a thing that is rare. When an old mason sits down on a step, the step is newer than they are, and everyone can see it. The young bring them curd without being asked. Nobody mentions it.

This, I think, is what the oldest mean when they say they are not building anything, that they are only keeping the building from ending. Everything around them is unending, because everything around them is constantly replaced. Only they themselves are ending. Their work is to stand at the one spot where the Coursing touches something that runs out, and to keep it from spreading.

## Where the masons go

When a mason dies, they are fired.

The body is laid in the hottest kiln in the district, the one kept for the finest ware, and burned down past ash to a white grit that is mostly bone. The grit is ground under the great millstones, very slowly, the lowest groan in the basin, until it is finer than silt. Then it is kneaded into the best clay, a pinch to a paver, and pressed, and fired again.

Pavers with a mason in them are different. They come out of the kiln paler, a pink rather than a red, and they are faintly translucent at the edges. Hold one up to a kiln-mouth and the orange comes through it like light through a thin ear. Tapped with a knuckle, they ring. It is a thinner, longer note than ordinary brick, one that hangs in the air a moment after the blow.

These are laid into the floor. Not into walls, and never into gutters. Always into floors, under feet. The masons walk on their dead every day. It is not disrespect. It is the most respectful place there is. A floor is where the Coursing is most itself.

And then, since the Coursing is always being made, the floor is eventually buried. A new bed of crushed brick is laid over it, and a new floor over that.

Now go back to the pavers under the pavers, the endless floors laid at slightly different angles, in slightly different bonds, in slightly different reds.

The masons who have pried down deep, through five floors or seven or ten, will tell you what they found, if you ask them in a quiet place. Tap any paver on any of the lower floors and it rings. Every one of them is pink at the edges. Every one lets the kiln-light through.

There is no bottom to the Coursing. There is only floor after floor of people, laid face-down in the dark, each layer almost as new as the one above.

## The bond at the rim

So we come back to the rim, where the sound thins and new courses appear in the dark. They are sharp-edged, mortar still soft, laid a paver-width at a time in a bond none of the masons use.

That isn't quite true. None of the *working* masons use it. But the oldest, the ones who have pried deepest, will tell you they have seen that bond before. It is laid seventh floor down under the Slow-Gulp sump, and again, a little different, at the ninth. It is a long course and a short one, the headers turned at an angle no living mason turns them. There are handprints in the mortar of those deep joints, left the way you leave a print when you move out of a warm wall. The hands are broad and flat-padded, and they are mostly the size of the masons' own.

The masons do not go out to see who is laying the courses at the rim. They say it would be rude to interrupt.

That is not the whole truth either. Some of them do go. The very old go. When a mason's hearing has gone entirely to their teeth, when their knuckles no longer close around a scoop, they sometimes stop taking curd. Then one day they put their hand in the newest joint of their warm wall, as if moving out of it, and walk toward the thinning sound. They are not stopped. No one follows. The children, who follow everyone everywhere, are told gently not to, and for once they listen.

Those masons do not come back, and they are not fired. There is no pale paver for them.

But the next time a mason stands at the rim and listens, there may be one hammer more out there than before. It has a new voice. Sometimes, not always, the young masons will say it sounds like someone. The old ones do not say that. They just clack their teeth together and listen longer than usual.

I want to be careful here, because the masons are careful. They do not say the dead are building the rim. They do not say that the floors under the floors come up at the edges like water finding its level, or that the people laid face-down in the dark get up again out there and take up the work, turning their headers at the old angle and walking inward. They do not say that the eels go up to the rim to spawn because that is where the silt is freshest, and the silt is freshest because that is where everything that went down comes back up. They say none of this. When you ask who is laying the far courses, they rap the paver under their feet, and it rings, pink at the edges, and they say, "Someone is."

## What lives here

So: what lives in the Coursing?

Beadlings, falling and climbing. Knocks in the courtesy-sockets, swerving around walls that aren't there yet. Moths in the mortar. Eels going the wrong way up the gutters to be born where nothing has been built yet. The lime-bloom, which isn't alive, crackling in the corners and reaching for drafts.

The masons, too. They are pale, squeaking faintly, reading the stone through their fingertips, named for hammers and renamed when the hammers change, sleeping twenty to a warm wall. They are the only things in the whole made country that grow old.

And under all of them, and around all of them at the rim, lives everyone who ever did. They are laid in the floor, ringing when you tap them, letting the light through. They may also be out past the last known hammer in the dark, building inward, in the old bond, a paver at a time.

The masons say the two works will meet someday, and then the Coursing will be finished, and then it will begin to wear.

I think I understand, now, why no one is eager. When the works meet, the living and the laid will be standing on the same course, and there will be no rim left to walk out to and no floor left to be set into. The circulation will close. Nothing will need to be pulled and replaced ever again. At last the whole place will be allowed to do what only the masons have ever been allowed to do. It will get old. It will have a past it cannot re-lay. Steps will dish. Corners will round. It will become, all of it, like them.

That will be a kind of homecoming. It will also be an ending. The masons, who are the only ones who know what endings are, are in no hurry.

So they keep the edges sharp. In a doorway, a child lies on her back, not supposed to, watching a beadling fill with orange light. Somewhere a hammer changes its voice, and three people across the basin become someone else without looking up. Far off, past the thinning of the sound, a trickle finds a fresh-cut gutter and starts downhill toward the center of the bowl. It carries its first few grains of pale silt, and very likely a thread-thin eel, down to where they can be made into something.
