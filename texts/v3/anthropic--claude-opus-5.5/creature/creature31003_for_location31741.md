# Those Who Keep the Stillwater

## The Walkers

Start with the walkers, because they are the easiest to see and the easiest to misunderstand.

Visitors call them spiders. They are not. Watch one long enough (and an hour is not long enough, a day barely begins it) and you will see that the legs do not work the way legs work. A walker does not step. It sets a foot down and then waits, and in the waiting something passes between the foot and the stone. Only then does it lift the next. Each footfall is a question put to the surface: *Are you still here? Are you still as you were?* The glass answers. The walker moves on.

They are made of the same obsidian as the mountain and the towers, and this is not camouflage. It is kinship. A walker is a small piece of the caldera that has been taught to go looking at the rest of it. When one touches the hanging bowl with its forelegs, it is not curious about the bowl. It is measuring the bowl's position against a memory of that position, a memory it did not form. It was given the memory, and it carries it the way a seed carries the shape of a tree.

There are twelve of them. There have always been twelve. Their routes, if you map them over years (and a few patient people have), cover every surface in the city exactly once before they repeat, and no two walkers are ever on the same face at the same time. They are not wandering. They are an inventory being taken continuously, forever, at the speed of a slow thought, because a slow thought is the fastest kind of thought that does not disturb anything.

What happens if a walker finds something out of place? No one knows. Visitors have tried moving small objects: a plate nudged a finger's width, the metal ring turned a quarter-circle. Every time, the visitor returned the next season to find the object back where it had been. No one saw it move. The walkers' routes show nothing unusual. The water has simply decided, in the interval, that the ring was never turned.

So the walkers are not the ones who correct. They are the ones who notice. Something else does the correcting.

## The Ribbons

The ribbons seem at first to be the opposite of the walkers. The walkers are patient and grounded. The ribbons are pale and suspended, and they spend most of their existence doing nothing at all.

But look at where they hang.

Plot every ribbon in the city and draw the lines between them, and you will find that they lie along the beams. They hang not near the lamps but *in* the light, precisely inside the invisible rays, in places you can only find by passing your own hand through the dark until your fingers suddenly shine. A ribbon in a beam is lit along its whole translucent length, a faint seam of white in the black. That is the only way you see them at all.

Visitors say they feed on the light. That is almost right. They are not eating it. They are *reading* it. The beams are not illumination; nothing in this city needs illuminating. The beams are lines of speech, held open between towers the way you might hold a door, and the ribbons are the ones who listen. When a ribbon moves, crossing its few meters in that slow, effortful undulation and then stopping dead, it is carrying something from one beam to another. It cannot drift, so every message costs it. It must push against water that refuses to help, the whole way.

Fifty ribbons, give or take. Forty-odd beams. They move rarely, and each movement is a sentence finished and delivered.

Then who is speaking? And to whom?

## The Endpoints

Now the beams that end in open water.

The account you have heard says the light "has simply been told where to finish." That is a reasonable thing to say about light that stops in the middle of nothing. But light does not stop in the middle of nothing. Light stops when it strikes something.

Here is what the water in this caldera is. It has no particles and no gradients. Its temperature is uniform and its pressure has been cancelled. Something with no color, no density difference, and no refractive difference, held in perfect equilibrium with the water around it, would be *indistinguishable from the water*. You could swim through the room it stood in. You could put your hand where its hand was. The only thing that would ever reveal it is a ray of light that finally has somewhere to land.

So think of the beams that end in open water as beams that end on someone.

They are standing in the city. Not many: perhaps nine, perhaps eleven. The endpoints are hard to find, and some beams may end on figures that turned away long ago, so that their light falls on a shoulder or a back. They are the builders, or some of them. They did not leave. They became the same as the water, and then they held still.

This is why a swimmer leaves no wake. Motion does not pass from one hand's width of water to the next, because the water is not only water. It is full of stillnesses that have chosen to be still, and every disturbance you make is quietly received and quieted, the way a hand laid flat on a trembling table stops the tremble. You are being caught, constantly and gently, by people you will never see. When your fingers light up in the dark at the end of a beam, you are touching the place where one of them is. Some visitors have reported that the light on their fingers felt faintly warm. At six degrees, in water with no gradients, nothing should feel warm.

They are not dead. They are slow. Their slowness is not like sleep. It is the slowness of attention held so long and so evenly that it no longer resembles motion. A thought, for them, might take a season. A glance from one tower to another might take a decade. The beams are how they see each other across that distance, and the ribbons carry what is too fine to be seen: the small adjustments, the *yes* and the *not yet*.

The objects in the rooms are theirs. The bowl was set down by someone who is still, in her own time, in the act of setting it down. Her hand has only just left it. The ring hangs a few centimeters to the left because she has only just drawn it off. In the time of the city, she is still turning away. The past here is not unreadable because it is lost. It is unreadable because it has not finished happening.

## The Note

And then there is the sound: the low note, the pressure in the chest, three seconds of swell and fade with no source and no echo, arriving at intervals too long to remember.

You could think it is the lens correcting itself, or the floating towers doing something with their weight. But lay the recordings end to end over years, as the patient people have, and something in the shape of it becomes familiar. It rises and falls in phrases. There are longer silences between the phrases than within them. It has the architecture of speech.

It is one voice. It is not the standing figures. They speak in light, and their voices are narrow and exact. This voice is everywhere at once, because it is not located in the water. It *is* the water. Each note is one syllable of something being said so slowly that a human lifetime might hold only a word or two of it.

This is the one who holds.

## The One Who Holds

Everything else in the city is an inhabitant *of* the stillness. This one is the stillness itself.

The account calls it perfect equilibrium, gradients removed and held. It is right that nothing here is a machine, though. There is no mechanism in the caldera walls and no engine beneath the floor. What cancels the pressure, what balances every stone at the height where it was placed, what refuses to let motion pass from one hand's width to the next, is a single enormous act of attention sustained without pause. Something is attending to every point in the lens at once, and in being attended to, each point is kept exactly as it is.

Picture someone holding their breath. Now picture a breath that has been held for so long that holding it has become the body. The lens is shaped like a teardrop on its side, bulged toward the north. It is not the shape of a container. It is the shape of a held breath inside a chest, with its weight leaning toward one side, the side where the heart is. Perhaps something to the north is what it is leaning toward.

Who is it? Some visitors think it was never a person, only something the builders made. But I think it was one of them, the first, or the last. It was the one who took off the ring.

Remember the ring: dull metal, hanging a few centimeters from the bowl, "as if it had just been taken off." People remove a ring before they put their hands into water to work. People remove a ring before they do something they will not come back from. Someone set down a bowl, drew off a ring, turned from the room, and went out into the lake. At the moment of going out, she let herself stop being separate from it.

Then she stopped the lake.

## What Is Being Held

The boulder hangs half a meter above the ground it was falling toward. Every fracture is still sharp. The scree lies on slopes too steep to hold it. The account calls this "a moment of geology preserved." Consider what that moment was.

The mountain is young. The caldera was freshly broken, the glass still bright at its faces. A boulder was *mid-fall* when the water arrived. This was not a quiet afternoon. The mountain had just cracked open, stones were coming down, and the heat of the breaking had not yet left the rock. Whatever was about to happen next, whether a second collapse, a surge from below, or a wound opening fully, did not happen, because in that instant someone put her whole self into the water and told it: *Not yet. Nothing moves. Nothing falls.*

The city was built afterward, inside the moment she made. The builders who followed her raised their towers in the pause, with the grain of the glass, cutting without tool marks because they cut the way she held: by attention alone. They hung some towers free of the floor because in a held moment there is no reason for anything to rest on anything else. Then, one by one, those who chose to stay went out into the water and joined her. Now they stand where the beams end and keep watch across the dark, each taking a portion of the holding so that it is not borne by one alone.

The walkers check that nothing has shifted, because if anything shifts, even a ring turned a quarter-circle, the whole moment might begin to move again. The ribbons carry the corrections. The standing ones make them. The note is her voice, the one who holds, saying whatever she is saying across the centuries: a count, perhaps, or a name, or a single sentence she began the instant she went into the water and has not yet reached the end of.

## The Snow

One last thing: the snow that falls on the lens and does not sink, and is gone by morning.

She takes it.

It is the only thing from outside she allows in. Each flake falling on the surface is a small fact about the world beyond the stillness: this season, this cold, this much sky. Each night she draws them in and reads them the way the ribbons read the light. That is how she knows how much time is passing outside, time she has sealed herself away from. That is how she counts the winters the mountain is spending. Frost splits the outer ridges and wind scours them, and the mountain grows older, quieter, and cooler at its core.

I think that is what she is waiting for. The boulder cannot be allowed to finish its fall into a mountain that is still breaking. But a mountain that has aged, that has spent its heat and settled into itself, could take that fall without taking everything else with it. She is not preserving the moment forever. She is keeping it until the world outside is old enough to receive it.

So the snow falls, and each night she tastes it and learns how much longer. The walkers take their inventory. The ribbons carry their sentences. The standing ones hold their share of the breath. The note sounds again, one more syllable. Far below, in a room with no table, a bowl hangs where it was set down, beside a ring that has only just been taken off, and the woman who took it off is still, in her own time, turning away from it to go and hold the mountain.

Someday the snow will taste right. She will finish her sentence. The boulder will land, softly, on an old mountain, and the water will let go. For the first time in longer than anyone can read, a ripple will cross the lens from one edge to the other and keep going.

Not yet.
