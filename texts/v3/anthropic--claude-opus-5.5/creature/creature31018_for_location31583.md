# Who Keeps the Stones

## The First Answer, Which Is Wrong

You would say, at first, that the film lives here. It is the honest answer, and it is where anyone would start.

Crouch by a vent where the mineral dew has feathered the rock white, and you will find it: the dark crust, rust-red at the edges and deepening toward bruise-violet at the center, spread in rings like the growth of a slow tree. It is warm to the touch, warmer than the stone beneath it, which should not be possible for something that only eats. It gives slightly under a fingertip, the way the skin of a sleeping animal gives. In the underglow it shines wetly, and if you hold still long enough you can see the outer ring creep forward across rock that did not exist a week ago.

That is life, of a kind, and you could stop there. Most would. The place is loud with absence, and the film is small enough that it does not disturb the absence. It fits.

But look at the rings.

They are not circles. At first you think the rock is uneven and the crust has simply found the easiest path. Then you walk to another colony, and another, and you see that every one of them has the same flaw. On one side the ring flattens, bends inward, and leaves a bare crescent of stone, as though something had been lying there while the film grew around it and then got up and left.

The crescents are the size of a body curled on its side.

## The Boulder That Breathes

You find one on the fourth or fifth vent, in a hollow where the heat pools so thick that the air above it ripples. You take it for a stone at first. Everything here is a stone at first. It is long and low, dark, mottled with the same red-violet crust as the rock around it, lying in the curve of the hollow where the ground is warmest.

The vent breathes. The orange light brightens over a minute, then fades. When it brightens, the stone rises very slightly. When it dims, the stone settles.

It keeps time with the mountain. It has been keeping that time for so long that its breath and the vent's breath have become one rhythm, and you only notice because, once, the vent stutters and the stone does not. Its side lifts again, a little late, alone.

You do not go closer. You have seen enough to understand that you are not the only warm thing on the plateau that has two lungs.

## What They Were

They were the builders. Or they are what the builders became. Once you know to look, the history lays itself out as plainly as the tilted slabs.

The rising was never fast, not at the scale of a life. A lowland people lived on a valley floor with thick, sweet air, and they set stones in rings and cairns and walls for reasons of their own. The ground under them began to lift. A hand's width in a day is nothing to a grandmother. A grandmother does not notice that the spring is further down the slope than it was when she was a girl. She notices that she tires more easily on the climb, and she blames her age.

Each generation was born a little higher than the one before. Each one breathed a little thinner air and thought it was ordinary, because it was the only air they had. The rivers went downhill and did not come back. The trees thinned, then stopped. The sky darkened, and they had words for that too, and then the words for the bright sky became old words, then songs, then sounds with no meaning at all.

They could have left. At any point in the long ages, some of them must have left: walked down off the edge of the rising country toward air and water, and been forgotten, or become some other people in some other valley. The ones who stayed were the ones who would not leave the stones. And the ground was carrying the stones upward, so they went upward too.

They did not choose the mountain. They chose the shrines, and the mountain came with them.

## What They Are Now

They are not what they were.

They are long-bodied and slow, with chests like bellows and hearts that beat perhaps a dozen times a minute. Their skin is dark and thick as hide, and it holds heat the way the rock does. They cannot make enough warmth on their own anymore; the air is too thin, the food too scarce. So they take it from the ground. They lie in the hollows, over the fissures, pressed flat against stone that is warm as a hand, and they soak up the mountain's wound the way lowland things once soaked up sun.

And the film grows on them.

This is the part that takes longest to understand, because it seems at first like sickness. The crust spreads across their backs and shoulders in the same slow rings it makes on the rock. It lives on the minerals in their sweat and the heat of their bodies. In return, it does something for their breath. You cannot tell exactly what, but the thickest-crusted ones breathe easiest, and the young ones, whose skins are still bare, lie closest to the old ones, as if waiting to be colonized.

When the ground shifts and buries a colony, it waits. When a new fissure opens, it finds it. The description is true, but it leaves out how. The film does not find new fissures. It is carried. When one of them rises from a hollow and goes to lie beside a fresh crack, a little of the crust stays on the new stone, and a new ring begins. The only thing on the plateau that has kept pace with the mountain has kept pace by riding on the backs of the only things that refused to be left behind.

They are not two lives anymore. They are one life in two forms, the crust and the body, each keeping the other just warm enough.

## How They Move

You never see them walk. No one has. There are no tracks, and you think at first the wind must erase them, but the truth is simpler.

They move only in the ash.

When a plume lifts from a vent and the front comes rolling sideways across the plateau, the whole world goes black. There is no underglow, no orange seams, nothing but grit and warmth and the dry whisper of the wind. In that darkness they rise. They cannot be seen and they do not need to see. They know the ground as a pattern, not a map: the east rises, the south falls, the north sheds, the big fissures stay and the small ones wander. They cross the plateau by the ticking of the stones under their palms, by the direction of the heat, by the groan far down that tells them which ridge is being pushed hardest.

When the front passes and the light seeps back, they are lying somewhere else, still as boulders. And there is always a boulder that was upright and is now on its side. Sometimes that is the mountain. Sometimes it is not.

The ash hides them the way deep water once hid fish in a lowland river none of them remembers.

---

## What They Do

They tend.

Not in the way you would expect. They do not rebuild. The wall on the eastern ridge has cracked down its middle and one half stands a hand's width higher than the other. They have not mended it, and they will not. The ring of slabs leans outward like opening petals, and no one pushes the slabs back upright.

What they do is smaller. After a shudder, when a cairn's top stone has slipped, it is back in place by the next ash-front. When grit drifts into the doorway of the corbelled hut, the floor is swept bare again. You found it that way, swept, and thought it meant someone had been there long ago. It means someone had been there days ago. It will be swept again.

They keep the stones exactly as they are. Not as they were, which is gone, and not as they should be, which no one remembers. They keep them as they are now: cracked, tilted, softened. It is less a repair than a witnessing. The mountain takes the shrines apart a fraction at a time, and they do not argue with it. They make sure that the taking is the mountain's alone, and that nothing else, not wind, not grit, not carelessness, adds to it.

The smell of old smoke in the hut is real. Once in a very long while, something burns there. There is no wood on the plateau and never has been. What they burn you can only guess: a scrap of dried crust, perhaps, from one of the old ones who has stopped breathing in time with the vent. Perhaps the old ones crawl through that low doorway at the end, and the smoke is what remains. The floor is swept so that the next one finds it ready.

## How They Speak

The ticking of the stones is the loudest sound on the plateau: a constant, delicate clicking like a thousand small clocks.

Not all of it is the stones.

Lie with your ear against a warm boulder and listen long enough, and you will find patterns in the clicking that heat and cold do not make. A run of taps, a pause, a run of taps. Somewhere across the ridges, answered. They speak with their knuckles against the rock, and the rock carries it, because the thin air will carry nothing. Their voices long ago became too weak to cross a hollow. The mountain is their throat now.

And on the worst nights, when the ash-wind strikes the obsidian curtains and a whole slope sings its pale chord without melody, it is not always the wind. Sometimes they go to the glass and draw their crusted palms across it, all of them together on a single slope, and hold the tone as long as their slow breath allows.

It is the only song they have left. It has no words, because the words were for things they have never seen. Somewhere in the chord, though, there may be the shape of an older sound, worn smooth as the cairn stones: something that once meant *rain*, or *river*, or *morning*, carried up a mile of rising ground by people who kept singing it long after they had forgotten what it was for.

---

## What They Believe

You could ask what the shrines are for, but they no longer know, and they no longer need to.

Whatever the stones meant on the valley floor, a harvest or a death or a god of the spring, that meaning is as gone as the spring. What the stones mean now is simpler. They are the only things on the plateau that remember a different altitude. They are proof that there was once ground that did not move, air that filled the chest, a sky that gave light instead of taking it. The keepers have never seen any of that. But the stones have. The softened edges and the pitted faces are the weather of a world below, carried upward intact.

So the keepers keep them. They keep the shape of a place they have never been, because it is the only shape they belong to. The raw rock under them is days old. The ridges look ancient and are not. The warmth looks like comfort and comes from a wound. Every true thing on the plateau lies, except the stones someone placed. The keepers have organized their whole slow existence around the only things that tell the truth.

---

## The Rule, Revisited

*Everything here is younger than it looks, except what was made by hands.*

The keepers are the one thing the rule forgot.

They look like boulders, and they are alive. They look like newcomers, strange hide-backed things fitted to this fire and this dark, and they are the oldest lineage on the mountain, older than any ridge, older than the plateau itself. They were here before the ground they lie on existed. They were here when it was a valley. They simply did not leave when it stopped being one.

And they, too, were made by hands, their own, generation after generation, reshaping themselves as patiently as anyone ever fitted a stone into a wall: a little more lung, a little thicker skin, a little closer to the warmth, a little more willing to carry the crust. Each one chosen and set so that it leans into the others and holds.

They are few and far apart. Each lies alone in its hollow, breathing with its vent, pressed to the warm stone, listening to the grinding groan far down as the mountain is shouldered upward. One day the rising will stop. Every mountain stops. Then the ash will settle, and the stars will come back hard and scattered across the thin blue-black, and weather will begin, for the first time, to soften the knife-edges of the ridges.

They are waiting for that. Or they are not waiting for anything, and are simply keeping a shape the mountain has not yet decided to break.

It is not always easy to tell those two things apart. Up here, they may be the same.
