# Who Lives in the Held Summit

## Begin With the Bridge

The simplest clue is the rope bridge. Its fibers are fresh, twisted from the long gray beards of hanging moss and something tougher underneath, perhaps split aroid stems soaked and beaten soft. The knots are not sailors' knots or any knot you would learn below. They are wrapped rather than tied, many turns of fiber laid over one another and pressed together, the way a vine wraps a branch. Someone mended this bridge within the last year or two. Someone crosses it.

So the first answer is a person, the mender. Picture them the way the evidence suggests: small, because the huts are small and their doorways are low. Barefoot, because nothing in the Hold is sharp. The travertine is smooth, the basalt is worn soft, and the leaf litter is always damp. Unclothed, or nearly, because in air this hot and wet any cloth would rot on the body within a season. They would move at dawn, when the otter-hunter moves and the chorus is at its lowest ebb, and they would stand still for long periods, because everything in the Hold that survives has learned to stand still.

That picture is not wrong. It is only the first layer, and it gives way when you press on it, the way the Anchors do.

## The Menders

Those few who have glimpsed them call them the Holders, though the name is probably borrowed from the place and not their own. If they have a name for themselves, it is likely a sound and not a word.

They are hard to see. This is partly because they are few, a few hundred at most and perhaps far fewer, and partly because their bodies have taken on the colors of the Hold. Their skin is a deep damp brown that beads with water the way leaves do. Condensation gathers on their shoulders and runs down them in small bright threads, so that a Holder standing still under the canopy looks like one more wet trunk. Their eyes are large and dark and wide-pupiled, suited to the pearl light under the Lid, which never throws a hard shadow. In the flat glare of the outside sun they would be nearly blind.

They are warm to the touch. Every account that mentions touching one says so, always with a kind of startlement: warmer than a fever, like the rock. At the thin places of the body, the rims of the ears, the webs between the fingers, the edges of the lips, the skin is faintly translucent, and a deep violet shows through it.

Keep that detail in mind. It matters later.

They eat what falls. The Hold is generous in the way of places without winter. Fruit drops all year, and the fermenting sweetness in the air is partly their larder. They drink from the bromeliad cups, tipping a leaf-rosette gently to their mouths and setting it back upright with the tadpoles still inside. In the gold season, when the white sedge releases its pollen and the lower basin hazes over, they gather it. They wade into the reeds at dusk with broad leaves held flat and let the pollen settle on them, then scrape it into dense yellow cakes. Those who have tasted the cakes say they are faintly sweet and faintly bitter, and that afterward the frog chorus seems slower.

They sleep in the fig-cathedrals, in the hollow lattices where the flightless ground birds roost. The birds tolerate them, and possibly more than tolerate. The ground birds' harsh, carrying calls sound at intervals through the night, and the rhythm of the calls changes when Holders are moving among the roots. The birds may be their sentries, or their neighbors, or simply their housemates of many centuries.

## How They Speak

At first they seem not to speak at all, and this is the second thing the simple picture gets wrong.

They are among the loudest things in the Hold. They just don't sound like people.

A Holder standing on a terrace at the right hour will lift its chin and make a single note: a pure, low, bell-like tone, indistinguishable from the call of one of the bell-frogs. Nearby frogs answer it. Their neighbors answer them. The surge begins, rolls outward, and moves across the basin over several minutes, the great slow wave of calling that visitors describe as wind through grass. When it reaches the far side, another Holder hears it. The shape of the wave, where it rises, how long it holds, which species join and which fall silent, carries something. It may be information, or presence, or only I am here, I am here, passed across twenty kilometers of wet air on ten thousand small throats.

Not all of the chorus waves are theirs. Most belong to the frogs themselves. But some are, and after long enough in the Hold a listener begins to suspect which ones. They are the waves that start in odd places, stop too cleanly, or pass over a region and leave it quieter than before.

They do use ordinary voices. Close up, in the fig-cathedrals, they hum to one another in a register so low it is felt in the ribs before it is heard. It is the same register as the Anchors. That is the first sign of what they are.

## The Slowing

A Holder does not age the way a person ages. It slows.

The young are quick. Few have seen them, but they move with the flickering speed of the long-limbed canopy primates. They climb the black towers through the moss and orchids, swing across the drip-well gorges before there was any need of a bridge, and chase the dragonflies over the pools. They are noisy, as young things in the Hold are. If the huts on the terraces belong to anyone, they belong to the young. Building is a young thing to do, and the huts have not been rebuilt in a very long time. There may be fewer young now than there were.

Then, over decades, then over a century or more, the pace changes. A mature Holder walks from one side of the basin to the other in a season, stopping to stand beneath a drip-thread for days, letting the warm silver column fall on its head and shoulders. It tends things: it re-wraps the bridge, lifts a fallen bromeliad back into a crotch of branches, clears silt from a terrace rim so the water spills the old way. These are small, patient corrections, the kind that leave a place almost unchanged.

Each mature Holder is accompanied, at a height, by one of the great glide-birds. The pale-winged birds live two centuries, by some accounts, and that is not a coincidence of lifespans. A glide-bird attaches itself to a Holder in the Holder's middle years and rides the thermals above it for the rest of the bird's life. It spirals up nearly to the ceiling and descends in long, lazy loops, always centered roughly over the slow figure below. When you see one of those birds turning above a particular grove for days, a Holder is standing in that grove. From below, the bird vanishes against the ceiling-clouds. The Holder vanishes against the trees. Between them, a column of warm air rises from the rock.

As the Holder slows, the violet spreads.

It starts at the thin places, the ears and fingers and lips, and moves inward. The skin grows smoother and glossier. It stops beading water in drops and begins to carry it in films, the way the black towers do. Pressed, it gives very slightly, like the skin of a fruit, and returns slowly. The Holder's warmth increases. Insects begin to gather on it. Birds come for the insects. The ember-succulent, which maps the heat of the mountain across its tissues, begins to creep toward wherever an old Holder has been standing.

The humming changes too. It becomes steadier and lower, until it no longer stops for breath.

## The Rim

At the end of its walking years, a Holder goes to the edge.

It chooses a place, or is called to one, where the boundary needs holding: where the Lid comes down to meet the mountain and the line between drenched moss and frozen lichen runs its perfect arc across the rock. It stands there, facing inward toward the Hold, with its back to the blue silence and the black sky. The jungle at the rim leans inward, stunted, as if flinching, and it may be leaning toward the Holders who have stood there, not away from the cold.

Then it stops.

It does not stop dying, or stop living. It stops walking. The hum continues. Over the years that follow, the gloss thickens and the violet deepens and the body lengthens, drawn upward very slowly, as if pulled by something in the pearled ceiling above. The glide-bird circles it and eventually lands on it, and eventually dies. A century passes. The figure at the rim is no longer a figure. It is a column, a few meters thick, rising toward the Lid. The water beading on its surface forms standing rings and lattices, the vibration of the hum made visible.

The Anchors have no seams, no joints, no markings. They are not built. They are grown, and they were once walking.

You would know this if you knew where to look. At the base of the tallest Anchors are the broken stumps of older columns in the same material. Anchors do not last forever. They crack, or thin, or fail. When one begins to fail, a new Holder comes to stand at its foot, and the new column rises from the ruin of the old. The stumps are not debris. They are graves, and the columns growing from them are their descendants. Some Anchors stand on three or four such stumps, one generation above another, like layers of travertine laid down over a very long time.

The Anchor that fell, the one in the northwest whose boundary sagged inward for fifty years and left the gray scar of leafless trunks, fell because no one came to stand at its foot. Perhaps there was no one to send. Perhaps the young had become too few. The dead ground is still there. The Holders have not forgotten it. Some of the chorus waves that start in the northwest and stop too cleanly are grief.

## What the Hum Holds

So the Held Summit is held by the Holders, literally. The Lid is kept up the way a note is kept up, by sustained voice. Forty of them, more or less, stand in a ring around the crown of the world, each humming its long unbroken tone. Between them the tones overlap and interfere into a shape, and the shape is a surface, a membrane of kept air a few hundred meters above the peaks. The air stays because it is being sung to stay.

Why the Anchors hum at the edge of hearing, why the Holders hum in the same register, why water patterns itself on the columns' skin: it is all the same work. One is simply younger than the other.

This also explains the Whistles.

The boundary is not perfectly sealed, and at a handful of points warm air streams out through gaps and sings as it goes, pure and sustained, each tone at its own pitch. The simple explanation is that these are leaks, places where the holding has worn thin. That may be true of some of them. But the Holders do not repair the Whistles, and they repair everything else. There are Whistles beside healthy Anchors, in places where the hum is strong. They look deliberate.

A sealed thing can be kept alive, but it cannot be heard. Sound does not cross the boundary. Step outward and the chorus is cut off as if by a knife. The Whistles are the one voice the Hold sends into the silence outside. Each one is a note left open so that the outside can hear the inside, so that anyone standing on the frozen rock can put an ear to that thin, singing plume of vapor and know something is still alive up there, still breathing.

The deepest Whistle, the one felt as pressure in the chest, stands near the oldest Anchor. It may be the oldest note of all. It may be the first Holder's voice, kept open deliberately for as long as the Hold has existed, still speaking outward over the dry brown foothills.

## The Foothills

Now look again at the cairns.

The offerings are all on the inner side of the boundary, beside the Whistles: shells, dried flowers, bits of colored glass, and once a child's shoe. Shells mean the sea, very far away. Glass means kilns and trade, the lowland world of workshops and markets. These are not things the Holders make. Whoever leaves them has climbed through the frost and felt the oven-door warmth and stepped across into the roar, then walked along the rim to where the notes are singing and knelt and stacked the stones.

These are the visitors the traces describe, people who stayed a while, went away, and came back over very long spans of time. They come up from below. Consider the dry brown foothills and what the whole Hold is: a pocket of lowland jungle climate lifted almost to the stratosphere and kept there. Kept from what?

This is not certain, and the Holders cannot say it in words. But the foothills were not always brown. Something happened below, slowly or quickly, that turned wet lowland to dust, and before it finished, some people carried a piece of their world up the mountain and held it. Seeds, soil, frogs in water cupped between leaves, birds, the whole breathing weight of a forest. They held it the way you cup your hands around a flame in wind. The ones who stayed became the Holders. The ones who went back down became the foothill peoples, who have mostly forgotten why they climb, though they still climb.

Once a lifetime, perhaps, or once a generation, someone comes up with the things of the dead and leaves them at the Whistles, where the Hold's breath goes out. That is the only door, and they know it the way people know things they no longer have reasons for.

And the child's shoe: one shoe, not a pair.

The Holders need young. The fallen Anchor proves it. The dwindling of the huts suggests it. Somewhere in the long covenant between the mountain and the plain, there must be children who climb and do not go back down. They take off their shoes at the edge, because nothing in the Hold is sharp. They step across into the warm roar and are met by someone large-eyed and violet-lipped, humming low, who takes their hand. The child's skin, over the years, begins to bead water like a leaf.

The family leaves one shoe at the Whistle so that the child can find the way back if it wants to. The shoe is still there. In the Hold, that is as close to an answer as anything gets.

## The Instrument and the Platform

Two things in the Hold do not belong to the Holders, and the Holders keep them anyway.

The first is the instrument in the fig-cathedral, the size of a head, pale metal and crystal, lifted a meter off the ground by centuries of root, still ticking faintly once every few minutes. The people who carried the forest up the mountain may have had metal and crystal and ways of measuring pressure and heat. This may be the last of their tools, left running. Or it may predate them, and they found it here and built the Hold around it. The Holders did not cut the fig away from it. They let the tree take it, which is how the Hold treats everything precious: it lets growth take it slowly.

Holders visit it. They stand near it in the long pauses between ticks, humming. Some count the ticks, and some who have tried to count say the intervals are not quite even. They may be lengthening, very slowly, on a cycle that would close in about a hundred and nine years. When the ceilingwood flowers and the basin smells of cinnamon and wet iron, the ticking is said to quicken for a single day. It may be a clock. It may be the heartbeat of something the Holders are keeping alive, or keeping asleep.

The second is the platform above the eastern pools: fifteen meters of level black glass in the same material as the Anchors, where nothing grows, where rain spreads thin and runs off without pooling, where birds land and seem uneasy.

It may be a floor, the cut base of a column far wider than any that stands now. It may be where the first Holder stood, before anyone knew what standing would become. Something that size would not have held the rim. It would have held the whole sky above the summit alone, from the center. When it failed, or chose to lie down, the work was divided among forty at the edge. The birds are uneasy there because the hum is gone from it. It is the only piece of that material in the Hold that is silent. Holders do not walk on it. They walk around it, the long way, as you walk around the place where someone you loved used to sit.

## Deeper

There is one more layer, and it can only be guessed at.

The rock is warmer than blood, running a steady, permanent fever. The heat comes up from inside the mountain. The Anchors are warmer than the rock, and the old Holders grow warmer as they slow. The whole system leans toward heat: toward the center, toward the base.

The Anchors reach up to the Lid. Nothing says they do not also reach down.

It is possible that not every Holder goes to the rim at the end. Some may go the other way, into the black honeycombed towers, down through the fractures where the mineral water seeps, into the dense warm dark of the mountain itself. There they would become neither column nor stump, but heat. The fever that warms the rock and keeps the air from freezing would then be the oldest of them, gone so far into slowness that a single breath takes a decade. That is how long the boundary takes to swell and shrink, recording its pulses in bands of different-aged forest along the rim like tree rings.

If so, the whole Hold is one body at every age at once. The young climb and chatter in the canopy. The mature walk and mend and stand beneath the drip-threads. The old stand at the rim, humming the ceiling into being. The oldest lie under everything, keeping it warm, breathing once a generation. The rain is the Lid weeping and the rock sweating, and both are them.

## What Lives There

So who lives in the Held Summit?

The frogs do, in their dozens of kinds, bell-notes and ratchets and ground-deep throbs. So do the glide-birds, the moss-furred browsers, the blind white fish in the drip-wells, and the snails that carry the ember-glow about at night like lanterns.

And the Holders do, in all their ages at once. They are a small, warm, large-eyed people who were once lowlanders, who carried a forest up a mountain when the world below went dry, and who have been holding it there ever since, in the most literal way available: by standing, by singing, by slowing down until they become the thing that holds.

Somewhere on the terraces right now, a young one is re-wrapping the bridge over the drip-well gorge, pressing the fiber down turn over turn the way a vine wraps a branch. Above it, a pale bird turns in slow circles against the pearled ceiling, waiting for it to become old enough to follow. Kilometers away at the rim, a black column hums with water trembling on its skin in rings. They are the same person, separated by three hundred years.

Beside the deepest Whistle, a child's shoe sits in a cairn of careful stones. The plume of breath streams outward over it into the vast blue silence and turns to glitter in the harsh sun. The note it carries has not stopped, and it is meant for whoever is listening below.
