# Who Lives in the Settling

Start with the people, because they are the easiest to see. Then let them become harder to see, because that is what happens the longer you stay.

---

## The Coats

At first they look like anyone who lives somewhere cold and wet. They are bundled in long coats of undyed wool, gray and oatmeal and the brown of wet bark, with collars turned up and hoods pulled forward. Their gloves are fingerless, so they can work the fine gutters. Their boots are soled with something soft, because the crystal plates of the flat cut leather to ribbons in a season. They walk slowly, with their weight settled low, the way you walk on a boat or on ice.

The first odd thing you notice is how they breathe.

A resident of the Settling does not exhale toward a wall. When they pause in a narrow lane to talk, they turn their faces slightly aside, toward the open strip of sky or down toward the gutter, the way you might cover a cough. They learned it so young that they don't know they're doing it. Breath is warm, and warm breath on a wall loosens it. A single exhalation won't undo anything, but a lifetime of them in the same spot would. There is a doorway in the old quarter where a gossip used to stand every evening for forty years, talking to her neighbor across the lane. The jamb at the height of her mouth now has a faint second jamb inside it, a ghost of a narrower door. Everyone knows whose it is.

So people here kiss outdoors, in the one open square, with nothing near them but the cistern and the leaning bench and the birch. It is the only place in town where affection doesn't cost the architecture anything. Couples meet there at dusk and stand close with their breath rising straight up. Nobody finds this romantic. It is simply where kissing is done, the way washing is done at the cistern.

---

## Faces

Look closely at a face here and you find the town's rule working on it too.

The old are singular. An old man's face in the Settling is one face, weathered in the only way anything here gets weathered, by long convergence. His expressions come from one direction and mean one thing. When he frowns, it is a single frown. The residents find old faces restful to look at, the way they find the lichen-thick walls restful to touch.

The young are not quite like that. A young woman smiles, and the smile arrives from two directions at once. The corners of her mouth lift a little out of step, as if two slightly different women were pleased by slightly different things and she is the place where they overlap. It isn't grotesque. It is closer to the doubled vision of a tired eye. Visitors often don't see it at all. Residents read it constantly and without thinking, the way sailors read weather. *She's in two minds*, they say, and they mean it literally. They wait before asking her anything important.

A face converges the way a lane does, over decades. Somewhere in middle age most people become one person. Nobody can say exactly when it happens. It is like noticing your hair has grown. One winter a friend says, *you've set*, and you know it's true, and you feel a small grief you can't name, as if the other people you might have been had quietly left the house without saying goodbye.

## Children

Children are born several.

Not always, and not many. Two, usually. Three is rare, and there is one family story of a birth of five. They are born the way the northern lanes exist: one solid child and one or two fainter ones in the same body, overlapping at shallow angles. The midwife counts them the way you count fingers and toes. *Two*, she says, and the parents nod. Two is healthy. Two is normal.

A baby here cries, and the cry is thickened. It feeds, and sometimes only the solid one seems to be feeding. It sleeps, and the faint one keeps its eyes open a few seconds longer, looking up at the translucent ceiling as though the gray light shows it something different.

Children aren't named until they converge, usually around seven or eight, sometimes as late as twelve. Before that they're called by their house or their lane: *the child from Cistern Stair*, *the little one under the double arch*. Naming a child who hasn't settled is considered a kind of theft. You would be choosing, and that isn't your choice to make.

This is the hardest discipline the Settling asks of anyone, and parents find it harder than the cold. You must not prefer.

A child in two drafts is two slightly different children. One may be gentler. One may laugh more easily, or sleep better, or reach for you first in the morning. Parents see this. Of course they see it. The rule is that looking decides, here, in the bones of the place. Love is a kind of looking, and preference is a kind of staring. If you love one draft more, you will sit with it more, attend to it more, hold its gaze longer, and the child will begin to agree with you. You will have chosen your child the way you would choose a window.

So parents love both, or all three, with deliberate, exhausting evenness. They are taught to look at the whole child, never at one of them. There are lullabies for this, with lines that alternate between *you* and *you* in two slightly different tunes, so the singing doesn't lean.

Some parents fail. Everyone knows it happens. There is an old woman in the Settling, Hesk, whose mother wanted a quiet daughter. Hesk is quiet. Late at night, in the unsettled lanes near her house, a faint laughter can sometimes be heard, bright and loud and a little rude. It has been heard there for sixty years. Hesk never walks that way. When someone mentions it, she smiles her single smile and says nothing.

## The Discipline of Looking

Once you understand the children, you understand the manners.

It is rude to stare. It is rude in most places, but here the rudeness has a mechanism. To look long and steadily at a thing in the Settling is to push it, however slightly, toward what you see. That is true of walls and windows, and it is true of people, especially people who haven't set.

So residents look in glances. They talk to each other while watching their hands, or the gutter, or the steam off a cup. When they do meet each other's eyes, it is brief and deliberate, and it means something. A long look between two people is a declaration. It says: *I am willing to help make you who you'll become, and I'm willing to be made.*

Marriage here is called *sitting for each other*. It is a promise to look at one person, year after year, with care and without greed, and to be looked at in return. Married couples in the Settling converge together. They set toward each other the way two sedge clumps lean into the same lane. The old couples of the old quarter have faces that aren't alike, but that fit, like two plates of the flat that grew to meet.

There is a darker version, and the town remembers it. Two brothers, long ago, quarreled over a cistern. For twenty years they stared at each other across a lane, each seeing the other as grasping, cold, and small, until each of them was. They died as caricatures of each other's contempt, singular and sharp-edged. The lane between their houses has never softened. The lichen won't grow there, though the walls are old. Children are brought to that lane once, at their naming, and told the story. Then they're taught to look away.

There are no portraits in the Settling. Nobody forbids them. They just aren't made. A portrait is a stare that never ends.

## The Sitters

Some people have the gift of patience, and the town gives them work.

A sitter is someone who guides the Settling's decisions by attention. You go to a sitter if you want a window facing the stream, or a shelf in the wall where your cistern cup can rest, or a stair to turn the other way so it doesn't block your door. The sitter comes and looks at the wall. Then they come back tomorrow, and the day after, and every day of the winter, and sit on a folding stool in their coat with a flask of something hot, looking at the stretch of wall where the window ought to be.

The best sitter now living is a man named Oswy. He is perhaps fifty, set early and thoroughly, with a face like a closed door. He doesn't imagine the window, he says. Imagining is too loud. He listens for it. Every wall has windows it could have, and some it would rather have, and his job is to find the place where what the owner wants and what the wall wants are nearest each other. Then he sits in that place until the difference closes.

His windows are never exactly what the owner asked for. They are a little rounder, or a little higher, or they face slightly more toward the sea. Owners always complain at first. Within a year they always say it's better. Oswy says a window that came out exactly as someone imagined it would be a wall that had been bullied. It would feel wrong to stand next to, and something in the room would always be trying to take it back.

There are bad sitters too. Stubborn people force a window into a wall that didn't want one, through sheer staring. Those windows are recognizable. They are too sharp, too square, too clear, and slightly cold even by the Settling's standards. Frost grows on the inside of them first.

## The Families That Don't Agree

In the old quarter there are families whose histories are short and don't match.

The Averlins are the clearest example. Ask any of them about their grandmother and you'll get a warm, specific answer: she kept the cistern on Blade Lane, she had a scar across one palm from the plates, she sang badly and often. Ask another Averlin, and the grandmother kept a different cistern, had a scar on the other hand, and never sang at all. Both grandmothers are buried in the flat, or at least both are remembered as buried, in two different places.

The Averlins aren't troubled by this. They talk about it the way other families talk about an eccentric uncle. *Our grandmothers*, they say, plural, fondly. Some of them believe their family condensed out of the same supersaturated possibility as the walls. There was no single first Averlin who arrived. There was a cloud of Averlins who might have lived here, and they precipitated gradually into the family that does. The older generations are more converged than the younger, but the root is still branched, like the town's own streets. At the base of the family tree there is a hollow full of stiff haze.

Nobody in the Settling finds this more than mildly interesting. Half the families have some version of it. The other half came from outside, over the flat, and some of those are less sure of their histories than they were when they arrived.

## The Others

Walk into the unsettled lanes on a still morning, and after a while you see them.

A woman is hanging washing on a line that is only half there, strung between a solid wall and a wall made of breath on glass. A man carries two buckets toward a cistern you can't find. A child sits on a doorstep the size of a playing card, which in her version of the lane is a full-sized stoop. They are faint, the way the second landing of the doubled stair is faint. Look straight at them and they thin. Look from the corner of your eye and they're almost as solid as you.

The residents call them the drafted, or the neighbors-who-might-be, or just *the others*, in the same tone they use for underneath. They are the people of the town's other revisions: the ones who live in the three-lanes-at-once, in the rotated northern streets, in the square as the hollows reflect it, crowded where ours is empty. They are not ghosts. Nothing has happened to them. They simply haven't been decided yet, or have been decided against so gently and so slowly that they haven't noticed.

They see us too, it seems, and just as faintly. Children who have stood very still in an unsettled plaza report that the drafted children stared back, and seemed frightened, and whispered to each other about the pale ones who come and go.

Over the years an exchange has grown up, quiet and unofficial.

Remember the cup? You cross an unconverged plaza and arrive on the far side holding a cup of tea you don't remember picking up, still warm, with a taste you've never had. That cup is a gift. Somewhere in the other version of that plaza, someone set a cup down for whoever came through. Nobody knows who started it. The residents answer in kind. They leave small things at the ghost-edges of the unsettled lanes: a folded paper of salt, a smooth grain from the gully (carefully, one grain only), a word spoken aloud into the thickened air of a triple-lane, meant for whoever might be listening.

Some residents talk to their own drafts. Marit, who tends the birch in the square, has a version of herself in the crowded square the hollows show. In that square the birch grows in the other corner, straighter and taller. Marit sometimes stands at the edge of the reflecting hollow and tells her other self how the tree is doing. She swears that now and then, looking down into the still water, she sees the other Marit nodding. She has also noticed that her own birch has been leaning, for fifty years, very slightly toward the corner where the other one stands.

The bird in the northern lanes belongs to the others. That much everyone agrees on. The seeds left on the ghost ledge don't vanish into nothing. Some drafted child picks them up, the residents think, and scatters them for a wren that is, on that side, entirely real.

## The Loosened

Not everyone who becomes a draft started as one.

A man named Corrie, twenty years ago, lit a stove. He was old, alone, and very cold, and one winter night he decided he'd had enough of coats. He built a fire in his hearth and sat in front of it with his hands out and his face in the warmth.

The room loosened around him, gently, the way the town warns it will. A second flue opened in the chimney. The far wall shimmered with the rooms it might have been. Corrie, sitting at the center of all that warmth, loosened too.

His neighbors found the house in the morning, and it was three houses. The stove had gone out. Corrie was there, in a way. He was faint and plural, overlapping himself at shallow angles, sitting in three chairs at once. He didn't answer when they spoke to him, but he turned his heads.

He is still there. The house never fully re-set, and the neighbors stopped trying to make it. His grandniece visits on the coldest days, when the town is most singular and he is clearest. She brings him tea he doesn't drink. She talks, and sometimes one of the Corries looks up. The doubled shrimp in the cisterns don't eat either. Nobody has made too much of the comparison, but nobody has missed it.

Summer does the same thing more slowly. Someone gets lost in a warm lane in the mild weeks and comes out *a slightly different person than you went in*. The town says it lightly. It isn't light. People who have come back from summer lanes are gentler, usually, and a little blurred. Their faces have a new doubleness, as if they met someone in there and brought them back. Some of them go back every summer, on purpose. Their families worry about them and don't stop them.

## The Dead

When someone in the Settling dies, they are carried out to the flat.

They go to one of the hollows where the crystal plates haven't met, where the gap is filled with that stiff, cold haze that resists a hand like dense water. They are laid in it gently, wrapped in their coat, and the haze closes over them the way cold water closes over a stone. Then the mourners go home.

Over the years the haze thickens around them, grays, and sets out from the edges in branching lines, exactly as it would anyway. It takes a person longer to set than an empty hollow does. The residents say it's because the dead are converging one last time. Every draft they never became, every self that left the house without saying goodbye, is pressing back into the one who lived and settling there, the way the ghosts of a wall pack into its grain. A dead person in the flat becomes utterly singular. When the plate finally closes over them, it is clearer than the stone around it, and on bright days you can look down and see them, very small and very still, like an insect in amber.

So the flat is not only new rock. In places, it is people. The paths residents walk to the cisterns run over their grandparents. The ground is harder there, and slightly clearer, and the lichen that won't take on unsettled walls sometimes grows in faint rosettes on the stone above the old burials. Hesk's mother is out there. So are both Averlin grandmothers.

The gully, the famous one, as long as a forearm and as deep as a thumbnail, is not far from the oldest burials. Nobody says this out loud. But when the residents go to look at it the way you'd look at a newborn, some of them are thinking about what erosion means in a place where the ground is made of the settled dead. In ten thousand years there may be a river there. It will carry sand down to the gray sea, and some of that sand will once have been a face.

## Underneath

Everyone wonders who lives in the seeds.

The lattices under the flat are arranged. Anything arranged had an arranger. The hum is steady in the back teeth, and some residents swear it changes, faintly, slowly, the way a voice changes when a sleeper turns over.

There are theories. One is that whoever seeded the flat was a people who couldn't agree on anything. They were too many and too divided, and every choice they made cost someone everything. So they built a place where choices could be made slowly, over lifetimes, by frost and patience and attention instead of force, and then they came here and settled into it. By that theory the founders are the Settling. They are the first families whose histories don't agree. They are the Averlins.

Another theory says the seeders are still out on the edge of possibility, among the drafted. In one revision of the town they never came, or never left, or never finished. That revision is the one the seeds are holding open. Everything else is just what condenses around it.

A third theory, Oswy's, is simpler. He thinks nobody lives underneath. He thinks the seeds are just the cold, arranged well, the way a good stove is just fire arranged well. Every place has a question at the bottom of it, he says. Here it's been left out in the open, where you can hear it hum.

---

## The Grit

This is what I came to understand last, and it changes all the rest.

Very pure water, left very still, can be cooled far below freezing without turning to ice. It stays liquid, supercooled and trembling, until something disturbs it. Frost needs a flaw to begin: a scratch in the glass, a mote of dust, a grain of something that isn't water. The crystal grows around the impurity. Every snowflake has a speck at its center.

The Settling is supersaturated with every town that could have stood there. The seeds keep it cold enough to precipitate. But cold isn't enough. Something has to be the grit.

It is the people.

Watch where the town converges fastest. It isn't random. It is the lane where one woman has walked to her cistern every morning for thirty years. It is the doorway where the gossip stood. It is the window Oswy sat for, the stair a child ran down every day, the corner where Marit's birch grows. Walls set around habits. Lanes set around paths. The oldest, most singular quarter is the one where people have lived longest and looked hardest and returned most often to the same places. The northern lanes, the rotated ones with the wren, have never settled because nobody lives there. There's no grit.

So the residents are not living in a town that happens to be condensing. The town is condensing *around them*. Their breath, their paths, their long looks and careful glances, their preferences, their loves and one terrible feud: these are the flaws in the glass. The Settling grows from them the way frost grows from a scratch.

That is why the wren matters so much to them, and why they leave seeds on a ledge that doesn't exist. The bird is trying to be grit. It is a small, living impurity in an unsettled lane, singing on the same spot every still morning, and around it, over decades, the northern streets have begun, very faintly, to lean. The residents are helping it. They are feeding a nucleus.

It is also why the dead go into the flat. The people become the ground, and the ground holds the town. Every singular grave is a seed crystal. The living grow the walls. The dead grow the rock beneath them.

And it is why the others, the drafted, watch us with such fear and interest from their side of the triple-lanes. We are not only their neighbors. We are the specks around which their world is deciding whether to exist. Every cup of tea they leave for us is a little like the seeds on the ghost ledge. It's an offering to the grit, asking it to settle their way.

---

## You

You come in across the flat from the landward side, on a morning so close to saturation that the air seems about to become something. Your boots are wrong for the plates, and you'll find that out by noon. Your breath hangs in front of you and doesn't want to leave.

At the outskirts the lanes are triple. You walk into all three. Your footsteps echo slightly out of time. Somewhere in the middle of an unconverged plaza, without meaning to, you arrive at an angle you didn't choose. There is a cup in your hand, still warm, with tea in it you've never tasted.

Someone left it for you. In some version of the lane, someone has been expecting you. Maybe they've been sitting by this stretch of air all winter, looking at it, thinking of a stranger.

An old man in a gray coat sees you from a doorway. He looks at you for one moment, a single glance, deliberate, from a face that set long ago, and then he turns his head aside to breathe. You understand, without knowing how, that it was a kindness. He didn't want to decide you.

So you stand in the plaza with the warm cup, not quite knowing what you are yet. Around you, slowly, at the pace of frost across a pane in a room just below freezing, the lanes begin to lean.
