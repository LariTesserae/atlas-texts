# Who Lives in the Seam

## The Shift

Begin with the obvious ones: the people.

Up top they are called seamers, or pan-hands, or, in the glassworks towns where their salt ends up, *the damp folk*, which is not meant kindly. Among themselves they don't have a name. They say *the shift*, the way a family might say *us*. You are on shift or off shift, and there is no third condition except dead or gone up the stairs for good, and those two are spoken of in almost the same tone.

You would know one anywhere. It isn't the pallor, though they are pale. It's the particular pallor of people who live in amber light: not the grey of illness but a faint chalky whiteness, as if the skin had been lightly dusted. In a way it has. The drizzle that falls through the Seam falls on them too. It dries on them and leaves its little freight of stone. A seamer's hairline carries a fine crust, like the tideline on a beach. Their eyelashes bead. Old seamers have a whitish rim along the lower lid that they wipe away without noticing, the way other people push back their hair.

Their forearms are laced with small white scars, dozens of them, each short and straight. The basalt is new and the edges are sharp, and in a street where you can't stretch out your arms you are always brushing the wall. The rock takes a little of everyone. Children get their first scar before they can walk, and nobody fusses. It is almost a naming. *Where'd you get that one?* is how two seamers who don't know each other start a conversation, and the answers are always places: *the elbow of the third gantry*, *the narrows behind the cooperage*, *the old laundry*. Often it is a place the other person has never seen, because it isn't there anymore.

They are not narrow people. Nothing about the Seam has bred them thin. But they *move* narrow. Shoulders turned, elbows in, a sidling economy to the way they walk, so that even on a broad road up top a seamer drifts toward the edges and travels half-sideways, as though the world were a gantry and someone might be coming the other way. Their hands are soft on the palms from the leather and hard along the outer edges from the chisel. Their voices, of course, are low. Up top, in a market, a seamer asking the price of onions will be asked to repeat herself three times, and each time she'll lean closer rather than speak louder, until the stallholder is uncomfortable and she has no idea why.

## The Stroke

They breathe with the engines.

This isn't a figure of speech, or not entirely. Stand beside a seamer at rest and watch her ribs. In, on the long sigh of the downstroke. Out, on the iron exhale of the return. Eleven seconds is a slow breath, slower than most people take, and the ones born here take it without thinking. Their resting hearts seem to settle into some fraction of it too. The midwives count labour pains against the stroke. Infants who won't sleep are carried down to the engine levels and held against a warm plate until the rhythm gets into them, and then they go quiet, every time, as if someone had finally spoken their language.

Up top, seamers sleep badly. The world up there is arrhythmic: wind in gusts, carts at random, birds, dogs, the maddening irregularity of rain. Seamers who move away for work often hang a long pendulum in their rented rooms and set it swinging before bed. The ones who can afford it buy a clock with an eleven-second tick, specially made. Clockmakers in the salt towns know the order. They don't ask.

## The Touching

Watch a seamer walk down a walkway and you will see them touch everything.

A thumb runs along the railing. A bolt head gets a quarter-turn in passing, a hinge a little push. A ring is twisted on a finger. A cuff is flexed. Up top this looks like nerves, a fidget, a tic, and it follows them wherever they go, so that seamers in other towns leave a faint trail of doorknobs jiggled and cabinet latches worked. Down here it is simply how you keep a thing. *Nothing stays still unattended.* The sinter takes what nobody touches, and so the town is held in existence by ten thousand small absent-minded gestures, a constant low stroking of the world to remind it that it's in use.

They extend the rule to themselves. It is not quite said aloud, but it is known: a seamer who stops moving is in some danger. Beds are hammocks, because hammocks sway. The old sleep in rocking chairs, and someone always keeps a foot on the rocker. When a man takes to his bed with fever, his family turns him on the stroke, gently, every few hours, and wipes the white from his lashes and the corners of his mouth. When someone dies, the body is taken up the forty stairs *quickly*. It is one of the few things in the Seam done in a hurry. No one will say what happens if you wait. Everyone has heard about the prospector in the early days who lay down in a side-cleft to rest and was found the next season, and the story always stops there, with *found*, and never explains how.

## The Ones Who Remember Rooms

The oldest of the shift are a kind of inhabitant of their own.

They were children when the earth opened. They came down the first ladders before there were stairs. They have watched the Seam lose, by their reckoning, a little over a yard of itself from each wall. A yard is not much, except in a town where you can't stretch out your arms. It is whole rooms. It is the width of a laundry, a chapel, a schoolroom, a gantry where two people once courted.

They carry these rooms around. Watch an old woman walking the fourth level and you'll see her step *around* something that isn't there, a corner that was shaved away forty autumns ago. She'll pause at a stretch of blank riveted plate and put her palm flat on it, lightly, the way you would on a door you weren't sure you should open. Ask her and she'll tell you, in a murmur, that her sister's kitchen was on the other side. Four chairs. A stove with a blue enamel door. A window that looked at the rock, because every window in the Seam looks at rock, but the rock there had a good thick vein of glass in it, and in the evenings it glowed.

*It doesn't really glow,* you'll say, because everyone says that.

*No,* she'll agree. *It doesn't.*

These old ones are the town's second architecture. The pipes are its real architecture and the old people are its remembered one, a ghost-plan of the Seam at every width it has ever been, nested inside each other like the rings of a cut tree. When the crews argue about which timbers can be sacrificed this autumn, it's the old ones who settle it, and they settle it by memory: *not that one, there's a flue behind it from when the bakery was there. It'll still be hot.* They are almost always right.

When they die, a room goes with them. The shift knows this and mourns accordingly.

## The Pale Citizens

The people are not alone. The ferns and the film are small, but they are residents, and the shift treats them that way.

No one moves a lamp suddenly. If a lamp has to be relocated, it's done over weeks, a finger's width at a time, so that the ferns on its bracket can turn and follow. A child who plucks one is told off in the flat tone reserved for serious things. The old ones say you can find your way home in a blackout by feeling for the ferns and walking the way they lean, and some of them have done it. It's the kind of thing people up top say about moss on the north side of trees. Here it's true.

The copper-green film is read like a barometer of the engines' health. When it's bright and glossy, the heat is good and the pumps are drawing well. When it dulls in patches, a pipe is cooling somewhere and someone should look. When maintenance shuts the engines down and the film goes grey across the whole Seam, the town goes quiet in a way it doesn't at any other time. People sit in doorways. They watch the grey. And when the engines come back up and the first stroke shudders through the gratings, there is always someone, in every shift, who goes and stands by the nearest split lagging to watch the green come back. It comes back within the hour. *As if it had been waiting,* people say. Nobody laughs at that.

So the Seam has its citizens: the shift, the ferns, the film, all pale, all clinging to the edges of warmth and light. That would be enough to fill a small town.

But there's the matter of the dogs.

## The Occupied House

The Seam has everything a rat could want. Warmth, water, scraps, a thousand sheltered crevices behind lagging and under gantries. And there are no rats. No flies at the lamps. No beetles in the timber, no spiders in the corners. Dogs brought down will descend thirty-nine stairs with their tails going and then stop at the last flight, not cowering, not afraid exactly, but *declining*. They sit. They look past their owners into the amber haze. And they will not come.

The shift has theories. The sulphur. The heat. Something in the sinter that the small lives can taste and don't like. The theories are sensible and none of them are quite satisfying, because a dog that smells poison backs away, and these dogs don't back away. They wait at the threshold with the particular politeness of an animal outside a room where someone is sleeping.

It's said, not often and never loudly, that the Seam isn't empty of animal life at all. It's *full*. The small lives don't come in for the same reason you don't walk into a house where every room is already occupied. The birds that circle over the quarry lip and turn away aren't avoiding a hole. They're avoiding a presence.

This is where the shift stops talking, mostly. But they don't stop *accommodating*.

## The Accommodations

Look at what the town keeps without explaining.

The narrow copper lines that carry nothing anyone can name. They run down past the lowest walkway, past the furnace mouths, into the throat itself, farther than any maintenance crew has followed them. They are warm. Not hot like the steam lines. Warm like a hand. Some of the old crews say that if you lay two fingers on one in the quiet of the sleep-shift, you can feel something in it that isn't the engines. It's slower, a stroke of its own, and in the oldest lines, if you're patient, the engines and the other thing turn out to be nearly the same. Nobody takes those lines out. *Because no one is certain.*

The eleven-second stroke. It was never chosen. The first engineer, whose name is in the ledgers and nowhere else, set the beam engines by feel. He is recorded as having spent three days on the lowest ledge with his hand on the rock before he'd let them run, and when they ran he tuned the governor down, and down again, until he was satisfied. The ledger says only: *set to match.* It doesn't say match what.

The ledger itself. Every time the walls take their thumbnail, every grinding crack from far back in the rock, someone somewhere notes the time. For sixty years they have been writing it down. There is no practical reason. The screw-jacks are adjusted by measurement, not by the cracks. But the entries continue, in a dozen hands, faithfully, with the hour and the shift and sometimes a single word. *Quiet.* *Long.* *Two, close together.* *Gentle.* It reads the way a nurse's chart reads. It reads like someone keeping watch.

The tea. On the downstroke, when the kettles come off, a great many people in the Seam pour one cup more than they're going to drink and set it on the grating to cool. By the next stroke it's gone, evaporated in the rising heat, drawn up into the haze, and then it comes down again somewhere as drizzle and leaves its little white residue. Ask a seamer why and they'll say it's for luck, or for the dead, or that their mother did it. All true. None of it the reason.

And the salt. The pale, sparkling crust scraped from the pans, with its sulphates and its *something else* the assay offices can't agree on. Up top they use it for glassmaking and tanning and "for reasons people are vague about." Here is one reason. Seamers rub it into cuts. The basalt opens you up and the salt closes you, fast, faster than anything else they know, and it leaves a clean straight white scar that never pulls. That's why the forearms look the way they do. The *something else* is whatever the Seam makes when it's closing, and the shift has been living off it and patching themselves with it for three generations.

The Seam is not a hole in the ground with a town in it. It is a wound.

## The Wound

Consider everything you've been told, and read it as a surgeon would.

The earth split open less than a lifetime ago. The edges are still sharp, the fracture faces still hold their ripples. It is fresh. It is hot, with a steady deep heat that comes from below without roar or wind. It *weeps*, not in a flood, but in a constant fine seeping that never pools and never dries. Wherever that weeping settles, it lays down a crust, white and frilled and fibrous, that grows over everything left unmoving and seals whatever is not kept open. And the edges are drawing together. Slowly, patiently, a thumbnail a year.

Inflammation. Exudate. Scab. Closure.

What lives in the Seam, underneath the people and the ferns and the green, is the healing itself. Not a creature in the throat, not quite. Nothing with eyes. There's no great sleeping beast down there breathing out heat. The breath is real, but it belongs to something that doesn't have a single body to be in. It belongs to the rock being hurt and the rock mending, which is a process and a presence at once, the way a fever is both a thing that happens to you and a thing that seems, at three in the morning, to be sitting in the room.

It is very large and very slow and it has one intention, which is to close.

The glass veins, then. The green shimmer that everyone knows isn't really there, that everyone has stood and watched at the end of a shift. If the rock is a body and the Seam is its wound, the veins are the nearest thing it has to nerves: long glassy threads of the earth's own substance, flash-melted in the moment of splitting, running back into the stone in every direction. They take the lamplight and the furnace light and hold them. And the old ones will tell you, if you've earned it, that the veins are brightest in the hour after a crack. They glow and dim again in a slow pulse, a little like something wincing and settling.

*It doesn't really glow.*

*No. It doesn't.*

## What the Shift Is, to It

So then the question turns around. If the Seam is inhabited by its own healing, what are the people?

Up top, a certain kind of preacher calls them a sickness, an infection in the earth's side, keeping the wound from closing out of greed for salt. Some seamers have heard this and wondered about it in the long sleep-shifts. It is the natural thing to fear: that you are the splinter. That the heat and the weeping and the slow press of the walls are the body trying to push you out.

But that isn't how the shift lives, and it isn't what the ledger reads like.

A wound doesn't hurry to close over whatever keeps moving inside it. That's the one law the Seam enforces without exception. *Nothing stays still unattended.* The sinter takes what's abandoned: the forgotten bolt, the unused door, the prospector who lay down. It leaves alone what's touched and turned and lived in. The healing and the shift are in a kind of negotiation that neither side has ever spoken aloud. The rock closes where no one is. The people keep open only what they use. Every autumn the crews shave the town a little narrower, giving the walls what they're going to take anyway, a course of brick, a bolt-spacing of gantry, handed over rather than seized. Every autumn the rock takes that and no more.

The old ones believe, though they would never say it to an assayer, that the Seam is closing as slowly as it can.

Think of a tree grown around a fence wire. It doesn't reject the wire. Over years it swells and flows and *incorporates*, until the wire runs straight through living wood and the tree has simply made room for it inside itself. The shift are the wire. The Seam is making room for them, a thumbnail a year, inside itself.

## The Kept Space

Try, finally, to see the one resident that belongs to both of them.

It isn't the people, who come and go up the forty stairs. It isn't the healing, which will outlast them. It's the thing between: the space itself. The width of the Seam. The narrow, lamplit, humming gap that exists only because the rock hasn't finished closing and the shift hasn't stopped chipping.
