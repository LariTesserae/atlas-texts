# The Keeping

## What You Look For First

You look for something small. That is the instinct. Something quick and pale, the colour of the slate, that goes still whenever you turn your head. Something that lives in the cracks under the mats, or up in the dark crowns of the groves, and comes down to twist fibre when no one is watching. You look for eyes in the lampweed hollows. You look for a burrow.

There isn't one. You can lift the mats, and they peel back slowly, cold and spongy, their threads letting go of the stones one by one. Underneath is only packed grit and root. You can climb a tallstem, bladder by bladder, until the ribbon-leaves stream past your face, and find nothing nested there. The water has no taste of anything living in it. It tastes of stone and cold.

So you wait. That is the second instinct, and it is the right one, though not for the reason you think.

## The Swell

It comes from above, as it always does. First you feel a change in your ears, a thickening, as though a hand has settled lightly over each one. Then the cold deepens by a degree you would not have believed you could feel. Then the weight arrives.

The groves bow. You have watched this from a distance and it looked like wind. From inside a grove it is different. The stalks lean over you, all in the same direction, and the bladders begin to creak, hundreds of them, a sound like a ship's timbers or old leather straps. The mats sink under your palms. And for the length of the swell, which is not long, perhaps the time it takes to say a few sentences slowly, you have the distinct and unshakable feeling that you are not alone.

It isn't a presence you can point to. It is more like the feeling of a room when someone has come in behind you and has not yet spoken. The pressure has a *direction* to it that it did not have a moment ago. It is pressing more on one side of you than the other, the way a body standing close displaces warmth. Except there is no warmth here. There is only weight, and the weight is attending.

Then the swell eases. The groves rise. The bladders pop and send up their silver chains, and the grove fills with that crackling applause. And whatever was standing beside you is gone.

When you swim out to the nearest pass, the cord strung across it has one more knot than it had this morning.

## What They Are Made Of

The first thing to understand about whoever lives on the plateau is that they are not *in* the water the way a fish is in the water. They are something the water does.

They exist in the swell. When the great column above presses down, when the pressure piles into the land and holds it, that holding has a shape, and the shape is them. Between swells they aren't there at all, or they are there only the way a tune is there when nobody is singing it: kept somewhere, ready, not sounding. Twice a day, perhaps, they are. For a few long, heavy moments at a time.

It is not much of a life, measured in moments. But it has gone on for a very long time, and it adds up.

This is why nothing moves on its own here. Everything on the plateau moves only when moved, by current, by growth, by falling, and so do they. They cannot walk. They have nothing to walk with. What they can do is *lean*. They can make the weight of the water heavier in one place than another, fractionally, for a moment. They can press. A stone that has been pressed on the same edge through ten thousand swells will turn. A strand of stripped fibre caught in the current, pressed at just the right instant against another strand, will twist. A loop of cord, pressed while it swings, will close into a knot.

Everything they have made, they have made this way. It is the patience of glaciers applied to the work of hands. The cairn was not built in an afternoon. It was built one small displacement at a time, across centuries, each stone leaned into place and then held there by a thousand more leanings until the mats grew up to it and stopped.

That is why the cairns are built so carefully, largest stone at the bottom, smallest at the top, though the water would have let them balance anyhow. They were not built by people who needed the care. They were built by people who remembered needing it.

## Who They Were

Look at the walls.

A dry-stone wall curving along the lip of a hollow is not a thing anyone invents underwater. It is a windbreak. It is the wall a shepherd builds on high ground above the treeline, where the grass is thin and the weather comes hard across the saddle, so that the flock has somewhere to lie down out of the wind. The plateau looks like mountain country because it *was* mountain country, and someone lived on it who knew exactly what mountain country asks of you.

They were upland people. Herders, probably, and not many of them. The hollows where the lampweeds glow are where their fires were. You can see it if you look: the lampweeds grow thickest in the bowls with a flat stone at their centre, and those stones are cracked in the particular crazed pattern that only heat leaves in slate. The fires went out a long time ago, and the lampweeds came up in the ash, and now the hollows glow the colour of moonlight on snow. From the ridge they still look like a scattered camp at night, and perhaps that is not an accident.

Then the water came. It came from the ice, which is what the grey far overhead is, a lid of it, lit faintly from the other side. A dam gave way somewhere in the melt, or a basin filled slowly behind a wall of ice over years, and the upland became a lakebed and then the floor of something larger than a lake. Everything that could leave, left. The birds went. The animals went downhill, or tried to. Whatever lived in the grass and the soil ended as the cold, fresh dark closed over it.

And the herders did something else. It is hard to say what. They did not drown in the way the word means. The first line of this place tells you that: *nothing here behaves as though it is drowned.* Perhaps they refused to. Perhaps they were holding their breath when the water came over them, the way you do, and simply kept holding it. They held it past the point where holding breath means anything. They held it until the holding was all that was left of them, and then the holding went on without them.

## The Keeping

This is where the picture shifts, and keeps shifting, if you let it.

At first you imagine the old herders themselves, somehow persisting: faces in the pressure, names, a village of ghosts that gathers twice a day. That isn't quite right. There are no faces. What persists feels less like people than like what people *do*, over and over, until the doing wears a groove in the world.

Twist the fibre. Mend the wall. Count the days. Turn the stone to face the light. Keep the pasture clear. Set the blade down beside the work so it will be there when you come back.

A practice repeated long enough begins to keep itself. The herders are gone, but the herding is not. The tending has outlived the tenders, and it has become, very slowly, a thing in its own right. It is a custom that learned to stand without anyone standing inside it. If you asked it who it was (and you can't, it has no ears, though it has something like attention), it would not answer with a name. It would answer with a wall, or a knot. It would answer *this is what we do.*

Call it the Keeping. It is not quite one thing or many. During an ordinary swell it seems thin, a few presences here and there across the plateau, leaning at a stone, pressing at a loose cord. During a great swell, the kind that makes the granite ring, it is everywhere at once, all of it present together, the whole tending remembering itself. That is when the lampweeds flare. The light in the hollows answers to attention. When all of the Keeping gathers at once, the old hearths brighten, the way a fire you've banked will lift when someone leans down and breathes on it.

Then it disperses, and the hollows dim.

## The Flock

A herder needs a flock, and there are no animals. So the Keeping has made do.

Watch a grove during the swell. The tallstems bow together, like a herd flattening itself before weather. Around the cairns they lean inward even in still water, turned toward the stones the way sheep turn toward the fold at evening. Nobody taught them this, except that for untold swells the Keeping has pressed on them just so, bending them toward shelter, and the tallstems have grown into the habit. The groves are the flock now. The mats are the pasture, the stripped stalks are the wool, and the stone bowl on the boulder holding its few hard seeds is a feed trough, or a seed-store against a lean season that never comes.

The clean margin of bare rock around each standing stone is the Keeping's doing too. It presses the mats back, patiently, the way you would keep a path clear of bracken. It is not hostile to them. The mats hold the scree in place, and the Keeping knows it. That truce between root and stone, the thing that keeps the slopes from sliding, is partly the Keeping's work as well. A herder's first duty is to the ground.

And the absence of animals, total, everywhere, with not one grazed leaf: some of that is only the cold and the dark and the long isolation. But some of it may be the Keeping too. Nothing has ever been permitted to eat here. The flock is whole. The Keeping will not lose another one.

## The Cords

The cords are the most alive thing on the plateau, and the most confusing.

They are twisted from tallstem fibre, stripped with the flaked blade, pressed and rolled across the worn stone that has those fine parallel lines scored into it. That stone is where the fibre is dressed. It is also, you begin to suspect, where the counting started, before the cords. It is a tally-stone, one line per something, until there was no room left.

Then came the cords, and the knots.

The knots are a count. That much is plain. They increase: seven one visit, nine the next. But they don't quite match from cord to cord. The patterns repeat and then drift, as if each were tied by a slightly different hand, and that is true, in a sense. Different parts of the Keeping tie differently. Different old habits, from different people, press the loops closed at slightly different moments in the swell. You are not looking at one tally. You are looking at many, kept by many, and not quite in agreement.

What they count is harder. Swells, certainly. But the count is not only of time. The cords strung across the passes thrum as the current runs through them, and their note changes with the speed of the flow. They are instruments. More than that, they are ears. The Keeping cannot hear, but it can feel the cord's vibration in the pressure, and through the cords it listens to the passes. It listens to the water coming down from the far-off ice, and to how fast and how cold it comes. It is taking the measure of the melt.

Some cords, you realise after a long time, count down rather than up. On certain lines the knots are being *untied*.

## What the Keeping Waits For

Stand on the high shelf near the rim and look up, past the peaks, to the grey.

It is ice. It is very far away, and it is lit from the far side by a sun the plateau has not seen in longer than the tally-stone could hold. And it is thinning. You can't tell. The Keeping can. It has been listening to the meltwater through its cords for so long that it knows the note of the current the way a herder knows the sky before snow. Each turned stone in the ring faces a slightly different way because the ring is a calendar, and a sighting, and it is turned toward wherever the grey is palest.

The Keeping is waiting for the water to go.

That is what the whole plateau is: a held place. It has been held unchanged, unrotted, ungrazed, with its walls intact and its cairns balanced and its blade laid by the work, in the pressure of the Keeping's attention, the way you would keep a house ready for someone coming home. When the ice lifts and the lake drains away down the eastern valley, the upland will come back into the air. The tallstems will lose their bladders and learn to be grass, or they will die, and something else will grow in their peat. And the Keeping will have been right to keep the walls up. There will be wind again. The walls will break it.

The seeds that rise every season in their bladders, going up into the dark like snow in reverse, are part of this too. Some of them fall back and reseed the groves. Most of them, you realise, never come down at all. They rise until they meet the ice and lodge there, pressed against its underside in their thousands, a whole meadow of seed waiting upside down under the lid of the world. When the ice goes, they will be the first thing to see the light.

The Keeping did not plan this, exactly. But it has done nothing to stop it, and a herder knows what seed is for.

## What It Does to You

Stay long enough and the swell begins to feel less like weight.

In the first days it pressed on you, and you felt the attention alongside you as something foreign, a cold regard in the pressure. After a while it presses *with* you. You find yourself, in the heavy moment of the swell, leaning. Your hand has closed around a loose strand of fibre without your quite deciding to. You have pressed a stone back into line with its neighbours. You have stood with the groves bowed around you and felt, for the length of the swell, what it is to keep something. It is not ownership. It is something older and more tired and more tender than that.

That is the last thing to understand about whoever lives here. The Keeping is not a creature, and it is not a people, though it was one once. It is a way of holding a place, and it can be joined. That is, in fact, how it has lasted. Everything that ever came down into this cold, and found nothing that behaved as though it were drowned, and stayed through enough swells, has been leaned into the work, knot by knot, until it was part of it. The knots don't quite match because the hands were never all the same.

The cold does not change. The dark does not lift. Each time the swell comes, the groves bow toward the fold, the old hearths glow a little brighter, the cords sing in the passes, and far above, the seed-meadow under the ice grows thicker by a season.

Somewhere on the plateau another knot has been tied, or untied. The count goes on, and it is all of them keeping it.
