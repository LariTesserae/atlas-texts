# The Ones Who Are Home

## First Impression

The honest first answer is that nothing lives here. The description is right about that. There are no bones, no hearths, no tracks turning in at a doorway. Whatever you are looking for, you will not find it by looking.

But the town is not empty in the way a cave is empty, or a field. It is empty the way a house is empty when someone is standing very still in the next room. The feeling that makes you lower your voice in the halls is not a mistake. It is the most accurate thing your body knows about this place.

Something lives in the rooms. It does not live *among* the moss and stone. It lives in the part the description calls the room-sized volume of cold, damp, singing nothing. That volume is not a gap where a tenant should be. It is the tenant.

## The Tenants

Call them that for now, since the rooms seem to insist on someone. Each room has one. A tenant has no body in any sense you would recognize. It is the shape of the air a room holds, and it exists fully only when that air is moving.

On a calm day the tenants are nearly absent. They are like a word you are not saying, present only as a readiness in the air. This is the murmur at the edge of hearing: water ticking, grit settling, the low breathing. It is the town asleep, or more precisely, the town between breaths. When the wind comes over the lip of the bowl and crosses a doorway, the air in the room turns over and begins to ring, and in that ringing the tenant arrives. It does not wake up. It *happens*, the way a flame happens when there is fuel. Its note is its whole self. Its pitch is its name.

The small rooms hold small tenants, quick and bright, keening at the first touch of breeze. The middle rooms hold steady, humming ones, the kind that seem to be thinking. The great halls hold tenants so slow and deep that they take a full gust to come into being. When they do, they shake grit off the walls. They sound less like they are speaking than like they are remembering something at length.

Each is also a small climate. What grows in a room, whether the deep mattress moss, the dwarf willow, or the bare lichen, is the tenant's temperament made visible. A quiet tenant in a sheltered room lets fog settle and stay, and the moss thickens around it like bedding. A loud tenant on the windward edge scours its floor to gravel and keeps nothing. The description says the sound map and the green map are the same map drawn twice. It could also say the green is a portrait. Walk the gardens and you are reading faces.

## What Fog Shows

You can see them, a little, in the mornings.

When the cloud bank pours over the lip and fills the rooms like cups, watch what happens after it drains from the lanes. It lingers in the rooms. The description credits the cold stones. That is true, but it is not the whole reason. The fog lingers because it is held. A still tenant holds the fog the way a sleeper holds warmth under a blanket, and for an hour each room contains a pale, exact cast of the one who lives there. Some casts are tall and settled. Some drift and fray at the doorway. Now and then, in a room where the air stirs slightly, the fog turns slowly inside the walls, clockwise, like something rolling over in bed.

By midmorning the fog has condensed onto the stones and run down into the moss. Every wall face is beaded, every cushion is dark and swollen, and the whole town drips. This is the closest thing the tenants have to leavings. They make no middens and no broken pots. What they leave behind is water, given to the gardens, drop by cold drop.

## What They Eat

They feed on wind.

It passes through them and they take nothing from it but motion and shape. It leaves them as it came, a little slower, carrying their note out over the fell. The prevailing westerly is their main meal. The southerlies are rarer and richer, and they wake rooms that sleep through most of the year. The equinox gales are feast days.

This explains the strangest rule of the town, that it builds toward the wind. Frost is the builder and has no preferences. But a room whose doorway faces the wind is fed, and a fed tenant is loud, and a loud tenant keeps its lane clear. Moving air scours snow away from its doorway and dries the grit, so that frost heave sorts the stones differently there. Slowly, winter after winter, the doorway opens a fingerwidth wider. A room that faces away is quiet. It fills with drifts and deep moss, and its stones settle inward, and it narrows. The tenants do not move stones. They are simply more or less present, and presence, over ten thousand winters, is enough to shape stone.

The town is not tuning itself. The ones who live in it are growing toward the wind the way the willows grow toward the south walls.

## Their Lives

They are born. When frost heave lifts a new ring of stones high enough, and a new cell closes enough to hold air and turn it, something that was not there before begins to sound. It starts faint and wavering, a whistle that does not yet know its own pitch. Over decades, as the walls settle, it firms into a note. The older tenants around it do not change, but the chord does. A new interval has been added. A new child has been named by the weather.

They marry. When a wall leans and falls, and two rooms become one hall, their two notes do not simply blend. They drop together into a single deeper voice that neither had alone, and the garden inside changes over the following summers into something new. The ravens seem to know when this happens. They come and sit on the rubble for days, clicking and gargling, and the new hall throws their voices back larger than they were.

They withdraw. The room on the eastern margin whose doorway has filled stone by stone until it sealed is not dead. It is the only tenant in the town that is ever perfectly silent. No wind reaches it. It no longer sounds. Its moss lies undisturbed, deeper and softer than any moss outside. Perhaps that is death, for a thing made of vibration. It feels more like the long stillness that comes at the very end of a life that was loud throughout. A sealed room is a finished word. The others sound around it, and it keeps quiet in the center of their sound, like a held rest in the score.

And on the hardest frost nights, when the ground heaves and stones shift with reports like breaking branches, singly, then in runs, a bang, a long grinding settle, then silence, that is not the tenants. That is the house. The tenants live in rooms, and the rooms are alive in their own slower way. On those nights the whole town shifts its weight, and every tenant is fractionally changed: a new width, a new pitch, a new amount of fog to hold in the morning. Nobody wakes the same as they went to sleep. The description says it sounds like something enormous and patient moving its joints. That is right. It is the body they all live inside.

## The Birds

The small dark birds with the pale bar over the eye are the tenants' companions, and possibly the only creatures that have ever fully understood them.

A tenant exists only while the wind moves. In the lulls between gusts, when the chord dies back, each one begins to fade from sound toward readiness, from someone toward no one. That is when the birds sing. Each bird sings the note of the room it nests in, short, rising, and pure, into the exact moment the room goes quiet.

It is a kind of keeping. The bird holds the tenant's name in the air until the wind returns to hold it properly. In exchange, the birds nest in the cavities between the base stones, the warmest and best-sheltered places in the whole mountain, inside walls that do not freeze through. A young bird learns its song from its parents, but the room corrects it. A note sung slightly off pitch rings thin and dies. A note sung true is picked up by the room and gently amplified, so that the fledgling hears its voice come back larger. Within a season it sings in key. The birds do not choose the note. They are taught it by the house.

There are only about twenty pairs, which means only about twenty rooms are kept this way. The rest go quiet in the lulls and wait for the wind alone. You could say the birds are for the tenants who would otherwise be most alone, but it may just be that those rooms have the best walls.

## The Other Visitors

The hares shelter in the lee of the walls during storms and do not stay. It is hard to rest inside something that is singing that loudly.

The ravens are the tenants' gossips. They come to the tallest walls and talk, and the rooms seize on their voices and hand them around the bowl, room to room. By the time a raven's call reaches the far side of town it has been sung by a dozen different throats. It is the nearest thing the town has to news.

The fox crosses in a straight line from edge to edge and never turns into a room. Some would call that fear. It looks more like manners. You do not walk into someone's house without being asked, and the fox has lived its whole life on the open fell, where nothing is anyone's. It can tell the difference.

## What the Rooms Are Waiting For

Here is where the tenants become most themselves, and where the first image turns out to be too small.

The rooms are sized for living in: small houses, large kitchens, byres, gathering halls. The description calls this a family resemblance, the way cells in a leaf are similar. But cells in a leaf are not sized for people. Stones sorted by frost have no reason to settle into proportions that would fit a cooking fire and a bed, and doorways the width of a person's shoulders, set low. The town has formed on the pattern of a town.

So the tenants are not only residents. They are hosts. They are shaped entirely around a guest who has never come. Their nature is hospitality, the way a cup's nature is being held and a door's is being walked through. They are the waiting the description keeps noticing. They are not inhabitants who happen to be waiting. They are waiting made into inhabitants.

Consider what that means for the few people who have sheltered here through storms. They are the only guests the town has ever had. They describe it as lying inside a lung, and the phrase is truer than they know. Every tenant in the bowl is sounding at once around the one living thing it has ever been shaped to hold. It is not a howl. It is not even a roar, though it is too loud to speak over. It is a house finally full, trying with every room at once to say *come in*.

And on a still evening, in the lull between gusts, when the emptiness in the central square becomes almost oppressive, what presses on you is attention. Hundreds of open rooms in every direction are waiting, and the thing they are waiting for is you. The silence is not vacancy. It is several hundred hosts leaning in, very quietly, to see whether you will step through a doorway and stay.

Nobody has stayed yet. The tenants do not seem to mind. They have been waiting since before language, and waiting is what they are built of.

## What They Were

There is one more layer, and it arrives only at the end, once you understand what the town is the end *of*.

The mountain used to be tall. It had peaks, cliffs, and ridges, height and violence. Over an unreckonable span it has been ground down into these soft shoulders, and its stone has been broken, sorted, and laid into walls. Every stone in the town was once part of a summit.

The tenants are not merely living in the mountain's rubble. They are what the mountain became. It could not keep its height, so it turned its height into depth of pitch. The great halls with the lowest voices, the ones you feel in your ribs, are what the old summits have become. The middle rooms are the ridges. The keening small rooms on the windward edge are the crags and spires that once caught the first sun. When the westerly comes and the whole bowl sounds together, you are hearing the range, the full old skyline of the mountain, played as a chord. It is the only way it has left to stand up.

This is why their voices will rise as the ages go on. The walls lower, the rooms shallow, the vessels shrink, and the mountain's remembered height climbs in pitch as it loses depth. It sounds higher and higher as it gets smaller. At the very end there will be one last room, one last tenant, a thin whistle over a gravel plain. Perhaps a single dark bird will sing its note into the last lull, because that is what its line has always done. Then there will be the high blue sky, the fog coming over the fell with nowhere to pool, and nobody home, finally and truly.

That day is very far off. For now the halls are deep and the gardens are thick, and the summits are still loud enough to feel in your teeth. The ones who live here are at the height of their lives. Every morning they hold the fog and give it back as water. Every afternoon they take the wind and give it back as music. All the time they keep their doorways open, the width of a person's shoulders, set low, facing the weather, in case someone comes up over the last swell and decides to stay.
