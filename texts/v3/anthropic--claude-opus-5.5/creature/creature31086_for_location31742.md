# What Lives in the Terraces

## First, the Obvious Answer

Nothing lives here. The description says so plainly: no birds, no insects, no worms in the calcite sand, nothing in the moss but moss. You could stand at the lip with the cloud at your ankles, look across the gold-lit staircase, and say with confidence that this is an empty place.

That answer is wrong, but it is worth holding for a moment. It is the answer the animals give.

## The Tenants

The plants live here, of course. They are the easiest residents to see.

The copper tree is the eldest of the ones still moving. It leans out from the wall's foot as if listening to something below it. It does not drop its leaves so much as set them down, one at a time, each onto a pool it seems to have chosen. The leaves land flat and whole, and the water begins at once to close over them. The tree has never had its bark scored by a claw or its fruit split by a beak. It has never been visited. It has had to become its own bee, its own bird, its own scattering wind, so it drops its children at its own feet and lets the stone decide which of them may root. Its seedlings stand downhill in their separate pools like guests at a gathering where no one speaks, each far enough from the others that none will ever shade another.

The great pillar at the back of the basin, the tree fern that is almost entirely stone, is the grove's patriarch, or the closest thing it has to a memory. Its living crown still unrolls a new frond now and then, slowly, like a hand opening in sleep. Beneath the crown, the long green thread runs down through the travertine to roots no one has seen for a very long time. It is not dying. It has simply moved most of its life inward, into the part the stone has sealed.

The herbs with their closed cup-flowers, the moss holding its breath of warm water, the fern with its lowest fronds preserved a hand's depth down in amber: these are tenants. They live here the way people live in a house. They grow, they flower in private, they put out their slow children.

But tenants are not the householder.

## The Householder

What actually lives in the hollow is the thing the plants live inside.

It has no single body you could point to. Its body is the terraces: every scalloped rim, every pool floor of pale sand, every sleeve of travertine around every stem. Its blood is the spring, warm and heavy with dissolved stone, rising from the finger-wide crack at the back. Its breath is literal. The water gives up what it carries into the air as it spreads and cools, and wherever it breathes out, it lays down a skin of itself. Every exhalation leaves a thin layer of body behind. It has been breathing like this for longer than most mountains have had their current shapes, and it has grown the way coral grows or a pearl grows: by patience applied evenly to everything.

Call it the Keeping, because that is the only thing it does.

It has one sense, which is something like touch. It knows the weight of whatever rests in its water. It knows a leaf landing, the soft tock of a fruit striking a rim, the minute pressure of a root pushing down through its own clear flesh. It knows warmth. It knows its own edges, where the moss begins and the basalt closes it in, and where the open lip lets it spill in a pale tongue down the slope. That tongue is the only part of it that leaves, and it leaves so slowly that it has barely gone.

It has one motion, the thickness of a fingernail in a human lifetime. It experiences this, as far as it experiences anything, as a single long gesture of closing its hands around what it holds, a gesture it has not finished and will not finish, because finishing is not the point.

And it has one appetite, which is the strangest thing about it. It is the opposite of hunger.

## Why the Animals Stay Away

Every animal that has ever lived knows what a mouth is. An open mouth is the first thing a creature learns to fear and the last thing it forgets. The hollow is, in a sense the animals understand without thought, a mouth: an opening in the mountain, lined with dark basalt, full of warm wet stillness, into which things fall and are taken.

But it is a mouth that never chews. What it swallows it does not break down, spend, or turn into anything else. It only holds.

To an animal, a mouth that takes and does not consume is more unsettling than one that devours. A creature is made of eating and being eaten. It is a small fire passing matter through itself, and it depends on everything around it doing the same: rotting, feeding, crumbling, flowing onward. A place where nothing is spent is a place where the animal's whole way of being has no purchase. A bird crossing the ridge would feel it as a sudden heaviness in the wings, a longing to land on one of those golden rims and simply stop. Something older than the bird's thought would recognize that longing and wheel it away down the mountain.

The Keeping does not drive the animals off. It has no malice and no teeth. It simply does not include them, and so they are not included. Even the microscopic ones, the rot-workers and the moss-dwellers, are absent. This is why the leaves are perfect, why the fruit lies whole under glass, and why nothing here decays. Decay is a kind of eating, and eating was left out.

It was left out on purpose. Here the portrait has to go deeper, down to the highest pool.

## The Circle at the Source

The Keeping is not as old as the spring.

The spring is ancient. It was rising from its crack, warm and mineral, laying down ordinary travertine, long before anything else happened here. In those days, one supposes, the hollow was merely a warm place high on a cold mountain. Beetles overwintered in the moss. Birds came to drink. Leaves fell and were eaten and rotted and fed the moss, as leaves do.

Then someone came up through the cloud carrying two things: a bundle of black glass from very far away, and a fruit.

The fruit was red, dull, the size of a plum, from a tree that did not grow on this mountain. The copper tree now leaning over the terraces descends from something that came up the same path. It may have come from a seed of that very fruit, or from another the traveler carried and planted, or from one that fell from their hand. That part is not kept, and so it is lost.

What is kept is this. At the source, in the warmest water, the traveler knapped or unwrapped eleven long blades and laid them in a ring beneath the surface, points inward, evenly spaced. In the center they laid the fruit. Then they did whatever they did next, and the water began to close.

When I first pictured the ring, I read it as a jaw: eleven black teeth around a morsel, a mouth set into the mouth of the mountain. That reading is not quite right. The blades point inward, but they do not bite. They hold the fruit at their center the way a cage holds a bird, or the way ribs hold a heart.

That is what they are: ribs.

The traveler built the Keeping a chest and put a heart inside it. What they made was not a shrine or an offering. It was a vow cut into the living stone with eleven edges sharp enough to sever something, and what they severed was this hollow from the long chain of eating that runs through the rest of the world. Whatever the traveler had lost below the cloud, whatever had been consumed, spent, rotted, or taken from them, they came here to make one small place where that would never happen again. Nothing would be eaten. Nothing would be spent. Whatever entered the water would be held, unspoiled, still living, forever.

The spring took the vow the way it takes everything, into itself, slowly, and kept it. The vow did not end when it was spoken, because nothing here ends. It is still being made, one thin skin at a time, and that is what the Keeping is. It is not quite a spirit of the spring or a god of the mountain. It is a wish, one person's single wish, that has been held so long in warm stone that it has become a living thing with a body of honey-colored rims and a breath of mineral air.

## The Heart

The fruit at the center is almost black now, far darker than any of the others and older than any of them by a great depth of stone. It has not split. The younger fruits below the copper tree sometimes crack and send pale roots down through the travertine, but this one has never tried. It is not a seed anymore, or not only a seed.

The spring rises directly beneath it. The warm water comes up through the finger-wide crack and passes, before it goes anywhere else, through the stone around the dark fruit, through the ring of ribs, through the heart. Only then does it spread out across the terraces to do its work. Every pool below is fed by water that has touched the center first.

The warmth is part of it. The description calls the spring warm as a held hand, and I have come to think this is exact rather than approximate. The only animal warmth the Keeping has ever known is the palm that set the fruit down among the blades. The traveler knelt at the source with their hand in the water for as long as it took to place eleven stones and one fruit, and perhaps longer. The Keeping, new and forming, took the temperature of that hand as the first thing it held. It has never let it cool.

So the spring rises exactly as warm as a hand, and the whole hollow is kept at that warmth. It is the temperature of someone who carried something precious up a mountain and set it down with great care.

## What It Is Like, Being It

It does not wait. The description is right about this, and it matters. The Keeping is not a guardian expecting its maker's return, nor a grief sitting in suspense. It has already become what the wish asked it to be, so there is nothing left for it to anticipate. It is complete in the way a held breath is complete, or a word that has been spoken but has not yet stopped sounding.

Its days, if it has days, go like this. In the early warmth the cloud below the lip rises and pours into the basin, and the Keeping feels itself surrounded, filled, softened. It receives the mist onto every surface at once, a vast gentle touch that beads on the moss and runs down the fronds and gathers on the rims. This may be the closest it comes to pleasure. Then the sun burns the cloud back down the mountain and the hollow empties, and the Keeping lies open to the light. The light enters its thin rims and travels through them and glows back out amber, green where it passes through the sealed stems, gold everywhere else. At midday the whole staircase shines softly from within. In a place with no animals to see it, the Keeping is lit from inside like a lantern.

A leaf lets go of the copper tree. It lands. The Keeping begins to close its hands.

A fruit falls. Tock. The Keeping begins to close its hands.

A fern unrolls one more frond at the top of a pillar that is almost entirely the Keeping's own body. The green thread inside the pillar lengthens by a fraction. The Keeping feels it the way you would feel a vein under your own skin, faintly and constantly, and it holds that too.

## If You Came

It would not harm you. It has no means of harm and no reason for it.

But it would notice you. You would be the first warm body it had felt since the hand, and it would notice you as the moss notices the mist, all at once, everywhere you touched. Your footsteps across the rims would be the loudest thing to happen in a very long time. When you knelt at the highest pool and looked down through the forearm's depth of clear stone at the ring of black ribs and the dark heart, you might feel the water rising warm around your hand, exactly warm enough, and understand that it recognized the gesture.

You would also feel the thing the birds feel: the heaviness, the invitation, the enormous gentle wish to stop. It would not be a trap, only an offer. The Keeping would take you in the way it takes everything, slowly and without spoiling, and keep you, green inside the gold, not dead, only held. What would be kept is the thing you had carried up the mountain, and the warmth of your hand around it.

Most would go back down through the cloud. Perhaps the traveler did. Perhaps they walked down the pale tongue and into the white and lived the rest of their life among things that were eaten and spent and lost, knowing that one thing, at least, was not.

Or perhaps they lay down in one of the upper pools, beside the source, and are there still, too deep in the stone and too long in it to see, a dim green column somewhere under the terraces like the one inside the pillar fern. The description says no bone was found. It does not say none was kept.

The Keeping knows, since it holds everything that entered it, but it has no way to say and no wish to. It goes on breathing out stone. It goes on being the wish. It lies warm and gold above the cloud, a living vow with a black-glass chest and a heart that will never be eaten, adding one thin skin at a time over everything it was asked to keep.
