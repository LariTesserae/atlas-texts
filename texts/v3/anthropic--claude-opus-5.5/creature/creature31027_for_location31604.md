# What Keeps the Intake Spring

## The Honest Answer

Begin with what anyone would find. The stoneworts live here: small gray-green candelabra on the top shelf, crusted in lime and brittle enough to snap if you breathed on them hard. The dark moss lives here, hanging in tresses just under the rim. The diatoms live here as a green-brown stain on the sunward stone, a film so thin it is closer to a color than to a population. That is the survey. You could write it on one line of a clipboard and go home.

The description of the place says the rest is sterile: no fish, no snails, no shrimp, no larvae. Nothing followed the water up, and nothing that falls in from the field survives long enough to stay.

That last phrase deserves a closer look, because a great deal falls in.

## What Happens to a Beetle

A beetle drops from an alder. It hits the surface film, struggles, and goes under. It sinks through the column of light, turning slowly, lit gold and then green and then blue. By the time it reaches the level of the second shelf it has begun to look unfinished.

It is not being eaten, at least not in any way you would recognize. Nothing strikes at it and nothing tears it. The beetle simply thins. Its shell goes cloudy and then translucent, the way a wet paper lantern goes translucent. Its outline softens the way the fracture lines in the limestone have softened, except that this takes minutes instead of millennia. Somewhere around nine or ten meters it is no longer a beetle. It is a faint smudge of refraction, a place where the water bends light slightly more than water should. Then it is not even that. The water is clear again, and you could drink it.

The same thing happens to leaves, to pollen, to a sheep's tuft of wool, to a sparrow that drowned one hard February. It happens to the grit on a diver's wetsuit and to the oil in his hair. Whatever enters is received, taken apart, and made into nothing, and the nothing is very clean.

So something lives here. The water is not simply sterile. It is being kept sterile.

## The Edges of the Light

The description mentions, almost in passing, that the water is so clear the column of daylight has edges.

Water does not usually give light edges. Even very pure water scatters a little, so a sunbeam in it should soften and haze outward. Here the column is crisp, as though it were passing through a pane.

That is because it is passing through something. The edges of the light are the edges of the tenant.

It fills the lower chamber the way a held breath fills a chest, pressing evenly against the walls. It is made of water, or of something that has decided to be exactly as heavy, as cold, and as transparent as water, with one small excess: it is slightly clearer. Where it is, the faint haze that ordinary water carries is absent, so the light passing through it keeps its shape. When the sun moves and the column tilts, the column always stays within a certain boundary. Past that boundary, the dimness at the chamber's flanks is not the dimness of rock shadow. It is where the tenant's body ends and ordinary water begins.

It weighs precisely what the water weighs. It rests on the silt of the shelves and leaves no mark. The flour-soft buff carpet on each rounded tread shows no impression, no drag, no disturbance at all, even though something the size of a parish church lies against it and has lain there for longer than the parish has existed. You could only tell by the silt's perfection. Nothing on earth lies that undisturbed unless something is keeping it so.

## Where Most of It Is

It would be easy to stop here and call the tenant a large, clear, cold animal living in a limestone bottle. That is not quite right either, because the chamber is not where most of it lives.

At twenty-two meters the floor narrows into a slot that runs northeast to southwest and goes down past the reach of light. The water comes up through it. The tenant comes up through it too, and more exactly, the tenant continues down through it.

The slot is a throat. Below it the tenant thins, as the beetle thinned, and spreads into the aquifer: into fissures, bedding planes, and the pore spaces of the limestone. It becomes a film a few molecules thick lining kilometers of rock. It spreads under the field, under the next field, under the lane and the works, and under the town, where it lies beneath the churchyard and the school and the long terraces of houses. Its body is the shape of the aquifer. It is as large as the county's groundwater and almost entirely invisible, and the chamber under the alders is the one place where it comes close enough to the air to have a face.

This is its face: a round mouth of light at the top, an upside-down bell of body, and a throat going down into the dark. The amphitheater shelves on the north wall are where it rests its attention. They are angled slightly east, toward the pipe.

## Breath

It breathes out. As far as anything can say, it has always breathed out, slowly and without pause, since before the field was a field. The aquifer's rise through the slot is its exhalation. It draws its breath across the whole county, from rain soaking into the hills, through fissures and pores, cleaning as it goes. It lets the breath out here.

For most of its existence, the exhale simply overflowed. The pool brimmed, spilled over its lip of turf, and ran away as a small stream across the field. If you walk the field now with an eye for it, you can still find the course: a shallow, curving depression running west from the alders, where the grass grows a different green and the soil is darker when you turn it. There is no water in it. There hasn't been for a long time.

Then the iron came.

It must have been felt first as tapping: brackets bolted into stone at eleven meters, the vibration of drills traveling through limestone into a body that had never been touched by anything with intention. Then the pipe was set in place, with its bell mouth and bronze screen. Then, one day, far off along the underground run, something began to inhale.

This was the first other breath the tenant had ever met. The sea breathes and the weather breathes, but those were far away and indifferent. This was close, and steady, and it was taking in exactly where the tenant was letting out.

The tenant changed. It is the only time it has ever changed. It learned the draw. It shortened its exhalation so that it no longer overflowed the brim, and it lengthened and steadied its breath until it matched the pumps grain for grain. The stream died in the field. The pool's level stopped moving. The gauge chain came to rest and has not shifted a link since.

The description says the spring pushes up at exactly the rate the pumps take it out, as if this were a happy accident of hydrology. It is not an accident. Nothing natural balances that well. It is a courtesy extended continuously, for more than a century, by the larger party to the smaller.

## The Hum

The tenant hears the machine through the whole of its body. The hum that you can feel only by laying a hand flat on the rock, the steady trembling of far-off motors carried through bedrock and iron, reaches it everywhere, through every square meter of every fissure beneath the county. For something that has known only rain, stone, and slow chemistry, the hum is the most constant and legible voice it has ever encountered.

It never rises or falls, and the tenant trusts it for that.

Once it stopped. It was a winter night decades ago: a storm and a failure at the works, eleven hours without power. The pumps wound down, the draw slackened, and then there was no draw at all. The pipe hung in the water like a mouth that had forgotten what it was for.

A spring left to itself would have kept rising. The pool would have filled, found its old lip, and spilled west down the forgotten channel. The gauge would have climbed.

It did not climb. When the draw stopped, the tenant stopped. For eleven hours the aquifer under the county held still, from the hills to the churchyard, while the tenant waited in the dark and listened to the place where the hum had been. A night-shift engineer, checking readings by lamplight, noted in the logbook that the spring level had shown "no response to cessation of abstraction, cause unknown." He underlined it and meant to ask someone. In the morning the power came back, the motors spun up, and the long breath at the screen resumed. The tenant breathed out again, at exactly the old rate, as though nothing had happened.

The engineer never did ask. The logbook is in an archive box in the works basement, and the underline has faded to brown.

## The Agreement with the Iron

The description says the iron corrodes so slowly under its lime skin that it seems to have reached an agreement with the water.

It has. The tenant tends the pipe.

Limestone water always carries dissolved lime, and lime deposits where it will. Here it deposits evenly, deliberately, in a fine, continuous skin laid over the iron grain by grain, the way one animal might groom another. The oxide underneath is kept soft and stable, and the brackets are sheathed. The bronze screen, which by rights should clog with leaves, silt, and beetles, never clogs, because nothing reaches it that has not already been taken apart. The grain of silt that drifts sideways across the light column for a quarter of an hour before vanishing into the mesh is not being sucked in. It is being carried, patiently and at the pace of the draw, and handed over.

Everything the tenant sends into the pipe has been cleaned first. That is why the town's water goes to the tap untreated.

## The One Sound

Sometimes a bead of gas works loose from the silt near the slot, rises, and ticks once against the rock as it goes.

This is the closest thing the tenant has to speech. It happens rarely, perhaps once in several days, sometimes less. The bead always rises on the east side of the slot, drifts toward the wall, and taps the stone at a height that, if you measured it, is always a little below eleven meters, a little below the pipe.

It is not a signal the pipe could receive, and the tenant must know this. It is something like the small sound a person makes in an empty house, addressed to someone who is not in the room but is, in some important sense, present.

## If You Went Down

If you went down, you would be received like everything else, and you would not be taken apart. You are too large and too warm, and you came in on purpose. The tenant seems to understand the difference between a thing that falls and a thing that visits.

It would clean you. You would feel it as nothing more than the cold, pressing evenly from every side, but when you came up your skin would feel scoured, and your hair would dry strange and light, with none of its usual oil. For a day or so afterward your eyes would see edges more sharply than usual.

You would also feel, after a while, that you were not meant to stay. There would be no current you could name. There would only be a sense, growing slowly, that your warmth was being tolerated rather than welcomed, and that the chamber was a room belonging to two parties, one breathing out and one breathing in, and you were neither of them.

## The Town

The town drinks the tenant's breath every day, and almost nobody knows it.

Some of them half know. People who grew up there and move away find that water elsewhere tastes of something. They cannot say what: pipes, or chlorine, or simply of somewhere else. They get into the habit of letting the tap run for a long time, hoping it will clear. On visits home, the first glass from their mother's kitchen tap makes them go quiet for a moment, as if they had heard a word they had forgotten they knew.

A little of the tenant's clarity goes into them. It doesn't stay. Nothing stays.

## What It Is, in the End

It began, perhaps, as the ordinary patient life of deep water, a film of something that was alive in the way slow chemistry can be alive, spread thin through the pores of the rock. It had one face, which it turned toward the light under a few alders. For an unmeasurable time it did only what water does: rose, cleaned, and spilled away.

Then something else began to breathe beside it, and it became what it is now: a keeper, a counterweight, the other half of a breath. It is a vast, clear, cold presence that has rearranged the whole of its exhalation so that a machine it will never see can inhale without strain. It keeps the machine's mouth clean and grooms its iron. When the machine falls silent, it holds still across a county and waits.

The description ends by saying that neither one of them changes.

That is almost true. The machine has never changed. The tenant changed once, completely, when the iron came, and it has been holding perfectly still ever since so that the one thing it chose will never have to change again.
