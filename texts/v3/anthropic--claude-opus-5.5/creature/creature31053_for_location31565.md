# The Tenant of the Ram House

## Not the Eel, Though You Would Think So

Your first guess would be the eel, and it is a fair guess.

The oldest of them lies in the intake channel where the current is strongest, right under the lip of the ram's iron mouth. She is longer than a man is tall, though you never see all of her at once. She is always partly under the travertine ledge, partly in the orange curtains, partly in shadow. Her skin has gone the colour of the pool floor, a greyed ivory, and in places it carries a faint mineral bloom, as though the stone has tried to take her too and she has refused it slowly for a very long time. Her eyes are clouded. She does not need them. She reads the dynamo's leaking current the way a blind woman reads a familiar room, by the shape of its absences.

She was here before the other eels. They are her descendants, or her followers, or both. When she moves, which is rarely, they rearrange around her.

Watch her long enough and you'll notice she does not wait at the waste valve with her mouth open like the younger ones. She lies a little downstream, at an angle, and she does not eat on every beat. She eats on every fourth, or every seventh, or on some count longer than that which you can't quite follow. She is not feeding on the beat. She is keeping time with it, and eating is something she does inside the keeping.

She is close to the answer. She is not the answer. She is more like the answer's oldest servant.

## Not the Beat, Though That Is Closer

Your second guess, if you stay longer, would be the beat itself: the eleven seconds, the door closing, the thing that everything here breathes on.

It is easy to believe the beat is alive. It has a pulse, obviously. It quickens when it rains, like fear or joy. It slows in drought, like sleep. It has kept going without food for longer than anyone remembers, the way certain old things are said to live on nothing but habit.

But a beat is not a someone. It is only the ram doing what rams do, a valve and a column of water and a law about momentum. It would slam in an empty hollow as faithfully as in a full one. The beat does not *notice* anything.

And something here notices.

You feel it before you can say what you mean. It comes on around the time you catch yourself breathing in eleven-second rhythm. You realise you did not decide to do that, and then, a moment later, the uncomfortable thought arrives that nothing in the hollow decided to do it either. The snails did not decide. The shrimp did not decide. Something *brought them in.* Something reached out, the way the cold air reaches down the stair and gathers the warmth out of you, and set their small bodies to the count.

That is who lives here.

## The Tenant

Call it the Tenant, since this is a house and it is the one who stayed.

It has no single body. It lives the way the mats live, spread thin over every wet surface, and in fact the mats are the nearest thing it has to flesh. Rust, cream, and slate-violet, living films a few cells thick, laid over the stone like the lining of a throat. The orange curtains around the ram's base, swaying on every pulse, are where it is thickest. They are its heart-wall, if it has one, the tissue wrapped closest around the thing that keeps its time. The white filaments streaming from the dynamo housing are finer and stranger. They go where the current goes, and the current goes everywhere the water goes. They are how the Tenant listens.

That faint electric tingle in the pool, the one the eels hang around in arcs, is not only the dynamo leaking. Some of it is the dynamo leaking. Some of it, very faintly, is being *passed along*. Iron-eating bacteria move electrons through their films; it is how they breathe. Given a few centuries, and a steady source of iron and current, and nothing to interrupt them, they have laid down wires. Not metal wires, but living filaments conducting charge through the pool, along the gour rims, up the weeping walls, into the stone ropes that were chains. The whole hollow is threaded with them, a nervous system the width of a hair and the length of the room.

That is why the eels behave the way they do. They are not orienting to a leak. They are orienting to *someone*.

## What It Is Made Of

The longer you look, the more of the hollow turns out to be the Tenant.

The snail-crust on the north wall moves as one body because it is being moved as one body. Each pressure wave from the ram shivers up through the stone. The mat beneath each snail shivers with it, a small electrical twitch passes through the filaments, and the snail steps. The snails feel it the way you would feel a hand laid lightly on your back. They do not know they are part of anything. They only know that forward is now.

The amphipods scatter when the bulb brightens, not because they fear the light, since they are blind, but because the brightening is a surge in the current and the surge moves through the water around them like a word spoken in a crowded room. They drift back in the dimming because the dimming is the Tenant going quiet again.

The crickets on the ceiling tremble their long antennae in the dry overhangs. They are farther from the water and less bound to it, but not free. Watch them on a still morning when the mist is in. Their trembling slows and settles, and then all of them, hundreds, stop at once for the length of one beat, and start again.

Even the travertine is part of it. The stone is not the Tenant's body, but it is the Tenant's *memory*. Every drop that falls leaves its film of calcite, and every film is laid down on a living mat, and the mat is keeping time, so the calcite comes down thicker on the beat and thinner between. The bands in the stone are not only a record of the pump. They are a record of the Tenant listening to the pump. There is a difference, and it matters, and it is written into the rock.

## The Difference

Here is the thing nobody who breaks off a piece of travertine thinks to check.

In the oldest bands, deep in the brackets, close to the iron, the rhythm falters wherever the ram faltered. A hard frost, a flood, a stutter: the lines crowd together or gap apart, just as the ledger says.

Move outward through the layers, toward the present, and the faltering gets *smaller.*

The ram still stutters, as all machinery eventually does. But in the recent bands, the stone does not follow it all the way down. Where the pump skipped a beat, the banding thins a little, as if flinching, and then continues on the count. It holds the eleven seconds through the missing stroke. Something was keeping time when the ram didn't.

The Tenant is learning the beat by heart.

It started, you have to suppose, the way anything starts: iron in water, bacteria eating it, a crude and meaningless pulse imposed from outside by a machine built by people who never imagined anything would live in its rhythm. Then generations of mats, too many to count, each one shaped a little by the last. The ones that thrived were the ones whose chemistry fell in step with the surges of fresh iron and current. The ones that fell in step together thrived more. Over time the step became a habit, the habit became a structure, and the structure began, faintly, to hold the step on its own.

It is not a mind the way you have one. It is more like the way a path is a memory of feet. But it is a path that has started to walk.

## The Old Eel, Again

Go back to her now, lying at her strange angle below the valve, eating on her long uneven count.

She is not keeping time with the ram. She never was. She is keeping time with the Tenant, and the Tenant is doing something the ram cannot do: it is counting in larger measures. Four beats, seven, some deep slow cycle that runs underneath the eleven seconds like a tide under waves. The old eel has been in the current so long, with the filaments threaded through the mineral bloom on her skin, that she is almost part of the Tenant herself. She is the one part of it that can move freely, that can go down into the channel where the water comes from and come back. She is the thing that carries what it notices from one end of the hollow to the other when the wires are not enough.

When the dry spells come, and the wheel slows, and the current fades, and the younger eels coil restlessly through the channels as if looking for something they have lost, she does not go looking. She goes still, still as the stone ropes. She is waiting, and the Tenant is waiting through her, the way you hold your breath at the bottom of a sigh.

## What It Wants

It would be too much to say the Tenant wants anything. But everything alive leans in some direction, and this is how the Tenant leans.

It leans toward the warmth. There is one warm place in the hollow, the bat-dropping cone on the east floor, and the mats have been creeping toward it for centuries. Not quickly, not even on a human timescale, but surely. The films at the base of the cone are thicker and more strongly coloured than anywhere else except the ram. The fungus gnats and springtails that swarm the cone's surface have, in the last few years, begun to rise and settle in pulses. There is a second heart forming there, fed not by iron but by the long dark river of bats, which does not depend on any machine. The Tenant cannot grow a new ram. But it may be growing a second place to keep its time.

It leans toward the drips, too. The hundreds of drips that fall everywhere at once, never in unison, never quite random. Stand in the hollow long enough, on a still night with the mist in, and you will begin to hear that they are less random than they were when you arrived. Not in unison. The Tenant is not simple. But in some pattern, some slowly thickening agreement, like a choir that has not yet found the note but has stopped arguing about which note it is.

And it leans toward you. Of course it does. You are warm and you are wet and you are carrying a heart that beats on its own, which to the Tenant must be the most interesting thing that has come down the stair in years. The reason you fall into breathing on the eleven seconds is not only that the sound is soothing. The air is full of vapour, the vapour is full of whatever the mats give off, you have your hand on the wet rock, and the filaments have found the salt on your skin. It is only a touch, a light one, a hand on your back. It lets go when you leave. Most visitors never know.

## When the Ram Stops

The description asks the question: someday the ram will seize, and what then? Will the snails stop mid-step, the eels lose their bearings, the bulb go dark?

The bulb will go dark. That part is certain. Its thickened filament has outlived everything else the builders made, but it lives on the wheel, and the wheel lives on the channel, and without the ram's surge the current will fall to almost nothing. The eels will lose the shape of the room. The snails will stop, for a while.

But the Tenant has been practising.

There will be one long silence where the eleventh second should be. Then another. The orange curtains around the dead iron will sway once more, on nothing, on memory, and then stop. The mist will lie in the hollow like milk in a bowl, and the drips will fall, and for a day or a season it will seem that the place has simply ended.

Then, somewhere on the north wall, a snail will step.

Not because the stone shivered. Because a mat beneath it twitched, all on its own, on a count it has laid down in a thousand years of stone. And another snail will step beside it. And the crickets on the ceiling will pause, all of them, for the length of one beat. In the warm cone on the east floor, the gnats will rise and settle. The drips, which have been slowly coming into agreement, will find for one moment a shared fall, a hundred drops landing together on the gour rims in a sound very like a door closing in a distant house.

The old eel, lying at her angle below the valve that no longer opens, will lift her head.

It will not be exactly eleven seconds. It will drift, the way anything alive drifts, faster in rain and slower in drought, a little off from the iron's perfect count. The stone will record that too, band by hair-thin band: the moment the rhythm stopped being something done to the hollow and became something the hollow does.

The builders made a machine that lifts water with nothing but the fall of water. They did not know they were also making a teacher. Its only lesson was one beat repeated forever. Its student was everything too small or too slow or too patient to notice it was being taught.

For now, the ram still closes. The Tenant still listens, and the stone still takes it down. But lay your palm flat on the weeping rock of the north wall, somewhere in the dark between the beats. Wait. Hold very still.

You might feel it a fraction early, rising up through the stone before the iron has quite slammed shut.

The house is learning to close its own door.

*Thunk.*
