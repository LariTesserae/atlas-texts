# Who Lives at Kessit Deep

## I. The Obvious Answer

Ask the crews who lives at Kessit Deep and they will say *the birds*. They say it the way people above say *the landlord*, without resentment and without much love, simply as the fact that sets the terms.

That answer is true, but it is only where the truth starts.

---

## II. The One Who Came First

There is a swiftlet on the north ceiling, above Lamp Two, whose nest sits in a drill groove longer than the others. The groove is a botched hole where the bit wandered, and the cup she built there is lopsided to fit it. It is thicker at one end, where she has added a new layer of saliva every season for nine years, so that the nest is now less a cup than a small translucent shelf, amber at the base where the oldest layers have yellowed.

She is not remarkable to look at. She is brown-grey, narrow-winged, and a little ragged at the primaries. But she came down the bore in the second year, among the first dozen or so birds, when the chamber was nearly silent. Back then her clicks returned to her from bare rock, with nothing else ticking to interfere.

She knew the ceiling before it was crowded. The shape of her clicking was built around the room's original echo, and she still flies it that way, on a route through the dark that no longer quite exists because there are now forty thousand bodies in it. She threads them anyway.

The collectors have never taken her nest. It is too high and too misshapen, and it is worth less than the neat white cups on the lower ledges. The crews don't know which bird she is. They know only that there is one nest above Lamp Two that has been there as long as anyone can remember, and that it is not to be touched. The custom has attached itself to the nest without anyone deciding it should.

She is the first answer to *who lives here*: someone who remembers what the room was like before it was full.

## III. The Body on the Walls

The crickets are harder to speak of as individuals, because they do not really behave as individuals.

What looks like drifting is tracking. The pump pulls cold brine through its housing, and the rock nearest it cools by a degree or two while it runs. The crickets feel this. When the pump starts, the grey-brown mass on the east wall begins, over an hour, to slide away from it and up toward the corners, where the heat pools. When the pump rests, they flow slowly back down. Watched with a lamp held to the side, the way the customs say, the whole wall seems to breathe on the city's schedule. In winter the city is cold and the pump runs long, so the crickets huddle high. In summer they spread.

Without meaning to, they have become a thermometer for a place they will never see.

Riding on them are the pseudoscorpions. A cricket's leg, looked at closely, often carries one or two: tiny, clawed, and clamped on at the joint. They are not parasites. They are hitchhiking, a behavior older than any cave, and they get off where the mites are thickest. The crickets carry them along the walls the way the bore carries the birds, and neither side knows it is transport.

---

## IV. Those Who Have Never Known Stillness

The cockroaches in the pump's lagging are a lineage now. Eleven years is many generations for them, and none of the roaches living in those split seams has known a floor that doesn't vibrate. The hum is their weather. When the pump shuts down and the housing goes quiet, the ones in the lagging do something the rubble-dwellers don't: they freeze. They go perfectly still in the silence, waiting, as though stillness were the dangerous thing. Only after the tap-tap of a worker's glove on steel do they stream away. They have learned that a tap means hands are coming, which is the one kind of vibration they don't trust.

Their cousins in the rubble think differently. They come out when the floor goes quiet. Two populations, a few meters apart, have drawn opposite conclusions from the same machine.

---

## V. The Quiet Ones in the Overbreak

The bats came from somewhere else, and they have never entirely arrived.

There are perhaps three hundred of them in the recess above the old explosives store. They leave by the bore, like the swiftlets, but not at the same time. They wait until the birds are gone, and they return after the birds have settled. They share the one door into the world in shifts, like tenants who have worked out a schedule so they never meet on the stairs.

The swiftlets don't roost in the overbreak, and nobody knows why. It may be the bats. It may be the shape of the recess, which throws clicks back strangely, doubled, so a bird inside it would hear a ceiling that isn't there. The bats hunt with sound too, and perhaps they don't mind the doubling, or even prefer it. They chitter on quiet nights in a register just at the upper edge of human hearing. One worker, who has since retired, used to say they sounded like people talking in another room about you.

## VI. The One Who Stayed Longest

Her name is Marit Vole, and she was on the blasting crew.

She came down the week the chamber was opened, when the air still smelled of detonation and the ceiling plates were fresh and pale. She stayed on as maintenance when the pump went in, then as crew chief, and then, after a reorganization above, as simply the person who is always on the rota. She has more hours in Kessit Deep than anyone alive. She has a cough she doesn't discuss.

The logbook is where you find her. The official entries are pressures, temperatures, flow, and the bearing rattle noted as *worse* or *same*. In the margins, in the same blunt pencil, are other things:

> *Birds quiet 14:10 — pump not running — went up. Gauge showed CO2 rising 14:25.*

> *Fern: 3 new fronds.*

> *Kettle skinned over w/ scale. Broke it with bar. Don't know if should.*

> *Cricket in the tea again.*

> *Crooked nest above L2 re-lined. Still her I think.*

It was Marit who first wrote *don't sweep*, eight years ago, after a new hire scraped a patch of floor to bare rock. Within a month the patch had grown a crust of silica so slick that someone fell on it. It was Marit who noticed the swiftlets thinning before the gauges moved, and she who started the rule of going up the ladder when the ceiling went quiet. None of it was ever made official. It was simply handed down, the way customs are, from the person who was there first to whoever comes after.

She talks to the fern. The others have heard her and don't mention it.

She is due to retire in two years. She has not said whether she will.

## VII. What Lives on the Footing

This part is harder to explain.

The concrete footing is the only flat surface in the chamber, and it is thick with guano like everything else. But around its edges there is that narrow dry margin, a hand's width wide, where the droppings are thinner and the slab shows grey. The engineers' explanation is convection. The slab is dense and holds heat, warm air rises off its edges, and falling guano gets nudged aside. This is probably true.

The crews have another name for the slab. They call it *the Tenant's place*, or just *the Tenant*, as in *don't leave that crate on the Tenant*. It started as a joke about the machine that was never delivered, whatever it was meant to be: a second pump, a heat exchanger, a separator. Something paid for, bolted down in drawings, and then cancelled. Its bolts still stand up out of the concrete in a neat rectangle, threaded and waiting.

The joke has worn smooth with use, the way jokes do underground. Now it is only a name, and the name has acquired rules. You don't stand on the Tenant. You don't store things there. When you cross the chamber, you walk around it. Nobody made these rules either.

The animals keep them too, or seem to. The crickets don't cluster there, though it is warm. The cockroaches cross it quickly. On the surface of the guano, the black-headed pin mold grows taller than anywhere else in the room, a small grey forest standing inside the dry border, as though something had fenced off a garden.

Nothing lives on the Tenant except the mold. But the room behaves as though something is expected there. The shape of an absence has been built into the place and then respected long enough that it has become a kind of presence: a seat everyone agrees not to take.

---

## VIII. The Real Resident

Put all of this together and a pattern shows through.

Everyone in Kessit Deep is a lodger. The birds rent the ceiling, and the crickets rent the heat. The roaches rent the lagging, and the bats rent the one recess where the echoes are wrong. The crews rent a steel hut and a set of habits. Each of them arrived after the wound was made, and each of them is living in a room that was cut for a machine, heated by a borrowed fire, and lit by lamps that will go out.

But something here is not renting. It was here before the blasting, in a sense. It is the thing the blasting interrupted, and it has not stopped.

It lives in the rinds along the lower walls, a fingernail's thickness, growing the other way. It lives in the guano as the guano compacts, pressed by its own weight toward stone. It lives in the ceiling plates as they shift a fraction of a millimeter a year, feeling their way toward the positions they will eventually fall into or lock into. It lives in the Kettle's skin of scale, which Marit broke with a bar and which formed again. It lives in the brine coming up a quarter degree cooler each year, which is the rock slowly letting go of its fever.

The thing that lives at Kessit Deep is the cave.

It isn't here yet. It is moving in the way a cave moves in, at the pace of mineral, of flowstone, of settling. Everyone else is only passing through. The swiftlets' clicking is, without their knowing it, a map of the cave's body as it forms. The founding bird above Lamp Two flies a route through a room that has already changed around her, and in ten thousand years the room will have changed again. The crickets track its temperature, and the fern marks the reach of the borrowed light. The crews, with their customs of *don't sweep* and *don't disturb* and *walk around*, are doing what careful people do in a house where someone is being born: keeping their voices down and leaving things as they are.

Perhaps that is what the footing is for. Not the machine that never came, but the other tenant, the one arriving slowly. The crews made a place for it without meaning to, and the room kept that place empty.

---

## IX. After

The pump will stop someday, perhaps in a century. Marit will be gone by then, and so will the founding bird and nearly everyone who kept the customs, though the customs may outlast them for a while, the way they do.

The lamps will go dark. The green halos will fade from the edges inward, and the fern under Lamp Four will lose its fronds one by one in the orange-less black. The crew hut will fill with crickets. The phone will ring twice a week for a while, then not at all.

The swiftlets will still pour up the bore at dusk and pour back before dawn. The chamber's one wind will still blow. Guano will keep falling on the Tenant, and eventually, with nothing left to disturb it, the dry margin will close over.

Then the room will be what it was always going to be, once the wound had time to heal: a warm black cave full of clicking, belonging to itself.
