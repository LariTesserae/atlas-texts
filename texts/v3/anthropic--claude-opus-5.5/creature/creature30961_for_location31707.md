# Who Lives in the Kiln Garden

## The Ones You See First

The easy answer is the bird.

It stands at the rim of the third basin as though it has been standing there since the stone set. Grey, heron-thin, crow-sized, with a stillness so complete that the steam has to move around it. Watch long enough and you notice things the first glance misses. Its legs are not feathered at the joint but sheathed in something smooth and dark, the same black as the terraces, and they do not flush with heat the way a living leg should. When it stabs at the water, the shrimp it takes do not struggle. They seem to rise into the beak, the way the drips on the risers rise into their gathering beads.

It has no nest. No one has found an egg, a feather, or a single dropping on the glass. It is always one bird, or always the same bird. It never flies. At dusk it walks up a terrace, and in the morning it is at the rim of a basin again. It does not live here the way a bird lives in a marsh. It is closer to how a clock's hand lives on a clock face.

The snails are the same, only smaller. Their glass shells are clear enough that you can watch the soft grey bodies breathe, and the bodies are not quite snail. They have no eyestalks. There is a faint lattice to their flesh, hexagonal like the seams of the terraces. Their slime hardens into raised lines, and the lines on the old terraces are not random. They branch, rejoin, loop back. In places they run parallel for a long way, like ruled paper. If you had never seen writing, you would not call it writing. If you have, you cannot stop seeing it.

The lizard's heart is visible through its skin. Hold still when it basks and count the beats. It is not keeping its own time. It pulses in step with the hum you feel through the floor.

These are tenants, or perhaps instruments. They live here, but the garden is not their home. They are part of the house.

## The One Who Isn't There

Then there is the absence, which has a shape.

The stair on the northern rim was cut for feet a little smaller than yours, so you take it awkwardly, shortening your stride. The treads are worn. Wear takes years of feet, and the stair is in the oldest stone, where rain has begun to dull the glass. So someone went up and down here many times. The rope groove along one side is polished smooth, as if a hand had run along it more often than any rope.

The hut on the western wall is sized for the same body. The doorway comes to your shoulder. The shelf inside is low, at the height where someone small would sit on the floor and reach up without standing. On it are the three smooth stones, the wire, and the lens.

The stones are not from here. They are grey granite, like the mountain outside, rubbed smooth over a long time, the way a pebble goes smooth in a pocket. Someone carried them in and set them down in a row, spaced carefully, the way a person sets down things they mean to come back for.

The wire is warm. It stays warm in the cold of the pre-dawn steam, when every other thing in the hut has gone clammy. It touches nothing, and it is not warmed by the stone. It holds the steady, faintly uneven warmth of something gripped, as if a hand were holding it somewhere you cannot see. Pick it up and the warmth does not change. Put it down and it stays warm. Whatever is holding it is not you.

The lens is the strangest thing on the shelf. Everyone who comes here looks through it, and everyone reports the same thing: the room looks slightly different. The ferns are taller. The moss is deeper. The walls stand a little further back, as if the bowl had grown wider.

The explanation seems simple at first. The lens shows the garden as it will be. Only after a long time looking through it do you notice the other thing. The view sits lower than your eye. To see through it properly, to make the edges line up, you have to crouch. You have to hold it at the height of a head a little smaller than yours.

It is not a telescope. It is spectacles, or an eye. Someone short sat in this doorway, looked out at a garden that was not finished, and saw it finished. Then they set the eye down on the shelf with the stones and the warm wire, and went away, or went somewhere.

So for a while you think that is who lives here: a small keeper, gone now. A gardener who carved a stair, cast a hut, planted the posts, and left the hearth burning. You picture them. Grey-skinned, perhaps. Deliberate. Patient in the way this place is patient.

That picture turns out to be only half right.

## The Prints No One Fills

The forecast stone keeps its own records, and after enough mornings you learn to read them.

Most impressions come true within days. The fern unrolls into the shape the glass predicted. The bird sets its feet into its own prints. The snails trace the paths laid out for them. Your own boot-prints appear, going toward a basin, and it does not matter whether you follow them. The garden proposes, and nobody here but visitors ever declines.

But one set of prints is never filled.

They appear on the lowest terrace, in the soft new glass beside the largest basin, where the slumped housing of the machine breaks the water. They are small bare feet, narrower than a human's, with long toes that press deeper at the tips, as if the walker went lightly and forward, onto the front of the foot. They do not appear every morning. When they do, they are never quite the same. Sometimes they go three steps and stop. Sometimes they go further, up the first riser, where the glass is harder and the impressions faint. Once, in a record a long-staying visitor left, they climbed four terraces toward the northern stair before they gave out.

They always start at the machine and they always lead away from it.

No bird made them. No one comes at night to step into them. They stay unfilled, and each night new glass forms and the old prints are buried a finger's width under the new floor. Break a piece of the lowest terrace, or look at the fracture face of a riser where water has cracked it, and you can see them in cross-section: layer on layer of small feet, all walking outward, all stopping short. The garden has been forecasting the same walk for a very long time, and making it a little longer, and getting a little closer.

That is when the picture turns over. The small keeper who sat in the hut is not only someone who left. It is also someone who has not come yet.

## The Newest Chamber

The garden grows downward and inward, like the inside of a shell. The old accounts say that plainly and move on. It should not be passed over.

Think of what a shell is. A snail, a nautilus, any of the chambered creatures of the sea. The shell is not a house the animal finds. It is a house the animal makes, secreted out of its own body, layer by layer, and the animal always lives in the newest part. The old chambers are sealed behind it, empty, full of gas or water, still part of it but no longer lived in. The creature inhabits the growing edge, where the material is still soft.

Here the newest chamber is at the bottom. The newest glass is at the bottom, soft enough to dent with a fingernail. The water is clearest at the bottom, around the slumped machine, and the night-skin forms fastest there and spreads outward in rings. Rings like growth lines, like the lip of a shell pushed a little further each season.

Something is making this shell, and whatever makes a shell lives in it.

It lives below the lowest basin, under the machine, or in it. The machine might be the part of its body that shows, the way the hard foot of a mollusk shows at the mouth of its shell. The slumped, remelted look of the housing might not be damage. It might be what soft tissue looks like when it is made of metal. The iridescence, the pigeon-neck green and violet, might be what skin looks like when it has to live in water that is nearly boiling.

The hum is its heartbeat, or its breathing, or both. The lizard's heart keeps time with it. The seven posts are where it presses up closest to the surface. Rest a hand on one and you feel it the way you feel a large animal breathe through a blanket. In the evening, when the posts faintly warm, it is stirring.

The filaments in the glass, thinner than spider silk, running in parallel and carrying heat in stripes, are veins. The terraces are warm because they are fed. The rock has veins because it is the shell of something alive, and the shell of a living thing is not dead matter. It is laid down by the body, and for a while the body keeps it warm.

The steam is its breath. The closed loop, snowmelt to steam to rain and back, is a lung the size of a valley. The plume that stands above the ridge and never leaves is what its breathing looks like from the lower passes.

## What It Dreams

If it lives below, then the forecast stone is something more intimate than a forecast.

A shell records the life of the animal inside it. Every growth line is a season, every thickening a stress, every ridge a meal or a hunger. The shell is a memoir written in calcium. This one is written in glass, and it is written ahead of time.

So the impressions are not predictions made from outside. They are what the one below expects. They are its dreams, rising and setting overnight. It dreams a fern unrolling, and a fern unrolls. It dreams the bird's foot on a certain patch of glass, and the bird walks there, carefully, into the dream. It dreams the snails' script, and the snails write it. It dreams your boots going toward a basin, and it does not mind when you don't go, the way a sleeper does not mind a detail that slips.

The garden becomes more itself the less you interfere. Of course it does. It is being dreamed by someone asleep, and the dream is of a place finished and whole, and everything here that is not a visitor lives inside that dream willingly. The bird, the snails, the lizard, the shrimp, the ferns, the white trumpets opening and closing in the steam, the water finding the channels laid for it. They are not tenants. They are furniture in a bedroom, arranged by the sleeper, keeping still so as not to wake it.

And every so often, not every night, it dreams of standing up.

It dreams of climbing out of the housing, stepping onto the soft glass with narrow bare feet, going lightly on its toes, walking away from the place it grew. Three steps. Six. Up a riser. Toward the stair. Each time it gets a little further before the dream gives out and the prints stop. The glass keeps every attempt, sealed in layers, all pointing the same way.

It is practicing being born.

## What It Will Be

Look through the lens again, crouching, at the height of the small head. The ferns taller, the moss deeper, the walls further back. That is not just the future. It is the view from inside it, the garden as it looks to the one who will finally walk out of the machine and up the terraces and sit in the hut's doorway, on the floor, with its back against the warm glass.

You can piece together what it will look like from what it left for itself, or what it will leave, depending on which way through time you are counting.

It will be a little smaller than you. It will go on its toes, lightly, because the glass is sharp and it will know where every edge is. Its skin will be what the terraces are: black, glassy, faintly banded inside with bottle-green and smoke-brown where the light catches it low. Thin filaments will run through it, warm in stripes, so a hand laid on its arm would feel veins the way a hand on a riser does. Its eyes will be clear and very slightly curved, and they will show the world a little ahead of itself, taller and deeper and wider than it is. That is why it can bear to leave one on a shelf. It has another. It is always looking at what things will become.

It will hold the wire. That is the warmth: its hand, somewhere further along, closed around the coil. Whatever the wire is for, it has already picked it up, and the wire remembers.

It will carry the three granite stones from outside, from the old mountain wearing into gravel under the snow. Why, you cannot know. Maybe it goes out through the notch in the ridge, into the thin blue wind that smells of nothing, and brings back pieces of the ordinary world to set in a row on its shelf. The way you might keep stones from a beach. The way a child keeps proof that there is an outside.

It will walk the stair so often the treads wear down. That is the part that breaks the mind if you look at it straight. The stair is already worn, in the oldest stone, from a walker who has not yet been born. The garden does time the way a shell does space, curled, each turn containing the last. It can already remember what it is going to be, so the stair can already be worn.

## The Answer

So who lives in the Kiln Garden?

A bird that is part of a clock. Snails that write in a script the house dictates. A lizard whose heart keeps someone else's time. Shrimp that rise into the beak like condensation. A dozen tree ferns and their frost-pale roots. A moss that smells of cloves when stepped on. White flowers that breathe.

Beneath them all, in the newest chamber, in the soft glass at the bottom of a shell three hundred paces wide, lives the one who is making the shell. It is asleep and humming. It breathes steam. It dreams the garden a night ahead and lets the garden come true around it. It is growing its house out of its own body, downward and inward, and it is growing itself at the same time, slowly, until the two are finished together.

It lives here now, before it has been born. It lived here before, when it carved the stair and cast the hut and wore the treads smooth. It will live here when it wakes. It will climb out on narrow feet, find its footprints waiting, and step into every one of them. For the first time something will fill the prints the garden keeps setting, and the garden will know it is complete.

Until then it is not quite accurate to say that no one lives here. It is closer to say that someone is still being written into living here, from below, one night of glass at a time.

The glass ticks as it cools. The posts warm at dusk. In the thickening edge of the largest basin, if you look closely in the last light, a small bare footprint is surfacing in the soft stone. It points away from the machine, toward the stair. Tonight it may go a little further.
