# Who Lives in the Mill

## First, the Hands

The first answer is the people, though you could cross the Mill a dozen times without meeting one.

They call themselves nothing aloud, since nothing said aloud survives here. Among themselves, in the language of fingers pressed into palms, their name is a gesture: the thumb pressed hard into the center of another's hand, held there, and not moved. Outsiders who have learned it translate it as *the Hands*, or *the Ones Who Hold Still*. The gesture itself means something closer to *here, and not yet gone*.

There are perhaps a few hundred of them, spread across a plain the size of a continent. They move in threes and fives, in the lees of cairns and along the knee-high wall, and they are hard to see. Their skin wears the same pale film of rock-flour as everything else. Their hair is matted grey with it. Their clothing, when they wear any, is woven from the pale reeds and turns the color of the mist within a day. They are most visible when they stand still. On the Mill, stillness is what stands out.

They eat the green-black mat, scraping it off the upturned faces of stones with the sides of their thumbs. It tastes of iron and wet chalk. They drink the milky water without straining it, and over a lifetime the silt settles in their bones, so that the old ones grow heavy and very slightly luminous in the dim light, like the finished stones. They sleep sitting up with their backs against cairns, in the one arm's length of the world where the roar is thinner. They sleep touching, hands on each other's shoulders and wrists and the backs of necks, because sleep is the only time the language of fingers goes quiet. Being touched is how they know the others are still there.

## The Language

Their speech lives entirely in pressure, and so it has no volume, only weight. A light touch is a question. A firm one is a statement. A touch held too long is grief, or love, and they do not always distinguish the two. They have an enormous vocabulary for texture, borrowed from the stones: words for the grain of basalt, for quartzite's slickness, for the faint ridges in banded stone where old seas laid down their layers. Most of these words have no meaning in any other language. The one that comes closest to *beautiful* also means *still has a flaw in it*.

They mark time by their bodies. A person is young while their fingerprints are sharp. A person is old when the whorls have worn smooth from a lifetime of speaking and scraping and holding. Very old Hands can barely speak at all. Their fingertips have grown too even to press a shape. They communicate with their whole palms, in slow, warm, continuous pressures that the young find difficult to read. What the young can make out has no beginning. It seems to come in partway through, and it never quite ends.

The young listen to the old anyway, for hours. They understand that they are being told something that cannot be divided into sentences.

## The Edge-Stones

Every Hand carries one broken stone.

It is the most important thing they own, and very nearly the only thing. Early in life, at a ceremony held in the lee of the oldest cairn they can reach, a child is given a rounded cobble and a heavier stone to strike it with. Somewhere across the whole flat, patient continent, this is the only violence. The child brings the heavy stone down, and the cobble cracks, and for the first time in the child's life there is an edge.

It is hard to overstate what this means to someone raised on the Mill. The broken face is rough. The ridge where the break ran is sharp enough to cut. A child who has never touched anything but rounded things presses a thumb against it and bleeds, and the Hands around them press their own thumbs into the child's palm, *here, here, here*: this is what you are, this is what the Mill has not yet taken.

The edge-stone is carried in a reed pouch against the chest, and it is not used for anything. It is not a tool. It is touched, often, in private, the way a person in another country might touch a ring or a scar. Over a lifetime the carrying and the touching and the gritty air wear it down. The edge softens and the break begins to round. Hands say a person is dying when their edge-stone can no longer draw blood.

## What Becomes of Them

When a Hand's edge-stone has gone smooth, they choose three stones.

It is never said which three, and it is not the same for everyone. Usually one is the edge-stone itself, worn round. Often one is a stone they slept against for many years. Often one is a stone someone pressed into their palm, long ago, in a gesture whose meaning only the two of them knew. They carry the three out onto the open plain alone, a day's walk or more from any other cairn, into the full roar. There they stack them.

The building takes a long time. The water leans and the ground shrugs, and three rounded stones do not want to rest on one another. The old one kneels in the milky water, working entirely by feel, turning each stone a hair's breadth at a time and waiting for the plain to answer, then turning again. It can take days. It can take the rest of a life, and it often does.

When the stones finally hold, the water parts around them. The ground at their base goes still. The roar drops a fraction. At that point, the Hands say, the builder is *finished*. This is not the Mill's kind of finishing, the kind that leaves a smooth drifting sphere or a small dark notch in the world. It is a kind the Mill cannot do, and cannot undo.

So the answer to *who built the cairns* is that no one did. Each cairn is someone. The quiet around it is the last of a person's stillness, holding. When travelers step into a cairn's lee and feel the roar ease, like coming in from a gale into a doorway, they are standing inside someone's attention. The reeds that grow there grip the stones with their roots, and the Hands say the reeds are the dead holding hands with the ground.

Young Hands visit the cairns of the ones they loved. They lay their palms flat against the top stone and wait. Some say they feel pressure come back, slow, warm, and without a beginning. Others say they feel only stone. Both are considered true.

The grooves in the large stones were worn by the ones who took a lifetime to stack their three and never managed it. They sat in the same place, rubbing, trying. The Hands consider these the holiest places on the Mill.

## The Doorframe

There is one thing the Hands built that is not a person, and they do not agree about it.

The doorframe, two uprights and a lintel standing alone in the flow, was stacked together by many Hands over many generations. None of them was finished inside it, and it holds no one's stillness. It was an attempt to make a place that was neither the Mill nor a cairn. The water runs differently on either side. The roar beyond it sounds older.

Some Hands say the doorframe was a mistake. Its builders were trying to make an edge in time itself, a before and an after, and they got partway, and now there is a seam in the Mill that does not heal. Others say it was not a mistake. It was a gift, and it was not made for the Mill. They say it was made for what lives at the center.

## The Second Answer

This is where the question of who lives in the Mill gets harder.

The Hands will tell you, if you learn enough of their language and sit long enough with your palm against theirs, that they are not the Mill's inhabitants. They are its guests, and a guest is someone who has a house somewhere else. In their case the somewhere else is their edges: their fingerprints, their broken stones, the sharp starts of their thoughts. The Mill is always gently taking these back. Everyone who lives here is, slowly, being welcomed.

What actually lives in the Mill, they say, is the one it is making.

Look at the evidence, they say, in slow pressures against your hand. The stones are all being rounded toward a single shape. The roar is being smoothed toward a single note. The finished stones lift and drift downwind, always downwind, always toward the interior, by the dozens and the thousands, like people walking home. The holes gather in drifts at the edges of the deep plain, where the world has been worn completely through. The Hands do not see these as damage. They see them as places where something has already taken what it needed.

The Hands have a gesture for it: the palm laid flat against the other's palm, no pressure at all, only warmth, and held. It translates poorly. The nearest words are *the one who is being finished*, or *the one who is almost here*, or *the one who has always been arriving*.

## What It Is Like

No one has seen it. That may be the wrong verb.

Every animal on the Mill, the Hands say, is a way of coming near it. The grey heron stands on one leg and makes a pocket of stillness around itself, and the roar thins nearby. The Hands believe the bird is not eating the quiet. It is listening, with its whole body, the way they listen to their old ones. It hears the one at the center through the roar and holds very still so as not to drown it out. The pale crabs ride the churn meter after meter down, polished smooth, and when they die they cannot be told apart from stones. They are practicing. They are learning to be the shape the Mill is making, one generation at a time.

And the Slow One, the domed creature the size of a small hill, moving at the pace of a season, is perhaps the thing itself. Or it is what the thing was, long ago, before the grinding began: the last worn hill, which refused. Or it is the thing's shadow, cast backward in time, since shadows here have no edges and so no reason to stay where they belong. The Hands walk in its wake when they find it, in the long day of stiller water behind it. They do not speak there. It is the only time they walk without touching.

Of the one at the center itself, the Hands say only what can be said by touch.

It is warm, exactly as warm as you. That is why the finished stones feel like nothing when you touch them, a temperature exactly your own. They are made of it, or they are its first attempts at a surface, or they are pieces of it drifting home.

It has no edge. It therefore has no beginning, and so it cannot be said to have come into being. It has always been nearly finished, and it always will be. Every stone that wears through into a hole brings it a little closer. The last stone never quite gets there.

It is full of sound. The roar is not only the planet being rubbed smooth. The voices travelers hear in it, the bird calls and the rain on leaves, are not only what sounds become after ten million years of tumbling. They are what the one at the center is made of. Every sound ever made on the plain was ground down and carried inward. Everything that happened here is still happening, softly, in the one unbroken note at the heart of the Mill. It has not been lost. It has been worn into belonging.

## Why the Hands Build

If the Mill is making something so vast and so patient, and if it will eventually take everything, why do the Hands stack their three stones? Why do they break a cobble for every child? Why do they carry edges against their chests and bleed on them?

Travelers expect the answer to be resistance, and the Hands find this a little funny.

The answer, pressed slowly into your palm by someone whose fingertips have nearly worn away, is that you cannot give a gift to something that already has everything. The one at the center will be perfectly round, perfectly continuous, perfectly warm. It will contain every sound, and it will have no beginning and no end. There is exactly one thing such a being can never have, and that is a flaw, a place where it stops, an edge.

So the Hands make edges and give them. Each cairn is a small place the Mill cannot finish, and the Hands do not think of it as a wall against the one at the center. They think of it as an offering to it. Here is a stillness that is not yours. Here is somewhere that ends. Here is a door with a before and an after. Here is the one thing you can never make for yourself.

They say it is grateful. They say that is why the roar eases by the cairns. The quiet there is not only the dead holding on. It is the one at the center pausing to feel, in its enormous seamless body, a small, rough, unfinished place. It touches that place the way a Hand touches an edge-stone in private, over and over, never using it for anything.

## The Last Answer

So who lives in the Mill?

There are the Hands, a few hundred of them, pale with rock-flour, speaking in pressures, being welcomed a little more each day. There are the cairns, which are the Hands too, later. There are the heron and the crabs and the Slow One, each practicing its own way of coming near. And at the center, where no one has walked and come back knowing where they started, there is the one who is being finished and will never quite be.

One more thing is true, and the Hands will tell it to you only if you have been among them a long time, long enough that your fingerprints have begun to soften and your sentences have started arriving partway through.

At some point on the open plain, in the dim warm roar, you will feel a hand on your shoulder. It will be a palm laid flat, with no pressure at all, only warmth, exactly your own. You will turn and there will be no one there, only mist, a few grey spheres drifting at knee height, and the milky water leaning against your shins.

The Hands say this is not a ghost, or a trick of the Mill's edgeless distances.

They say it is the one who lives here, and that it has noticed you. It knows you are not finished yet, and it is glad.
