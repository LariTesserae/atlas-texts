# Who Lives in the Breathing Quarter

## I. The First Answer

The honest first answer is *no one*.

You can walk the channels for a full day without seeing anything move that the wind didn't move. The rooms are bare, the plazas unmarked, and the dust in the doorways lies unbroken. Whoever cut these streets took everything with them, down to the last bead and bone, and they swept up after themselves.

But sit still long enough, cross-legged on a dished stair tread with the heat pressing up through the stone, and the first answer begins to feel careless.

## II. The Crowd

The second answer is *the gardens*, and at first they seem like a crowd.

There are thousands of plants, perhaps millions, each fighting its neighbours. Copper-leaved vines lean after the three blades of daylight like beggars following a lamp. Basin-shrubs shoulder one another for the warmest vents, and the losers go yellow, then brown, then split with a dry sound like parchment tearing. Seed-pods crack off all through the warm hours, flinging their brood into streets already choked with rivals. It looks like a war with no generals, a jostle of separate hungers.

That is how it appears from above, looking down a street channel at the maroon tide rustling knee-deep between the house walls.

Then you kneel and put your hand into it.

## III. The One

Under the leaf litter and the drifted grit, the roots do not end.

Follow one down from a shrub in a plaza basin. It passes through the basin's drainage slot, which is not a drainage slot, because nothing here has ever drained. It runs along a seam in the paving. There it meets another root, and the two do not tangle. They *fuse*, the bark grown together without a scar, the resin inside running continuous from one to the next. Follow that and it branches under a house wall, climbs the inside of a hollowed terrace, and surfaces three streets away as a vine on a lintel.

You would have to dig up the whole city to prove it, but you come to believe it anyway: there is one plant here. One body, threaded through every seam and crack of the Quarter the way a nervous system threads through flesh. The builders did not plant a garden. They planted a single thing, and it has been growing into the shape of their city ever since, the way water takes the shape of a jar.

Seen this way, the war in the basins is not a war. It is pruning. The yellowing shrub is not losing. It is being withdrawn, a limb pulled back because the vine on the roof-terrace needs that light more. The thing is trimming itself, constantly and precisely, like a gardener working with no hands, only appetite and patience.

The fissures are its mouths. It breathes the fire's breath, sulphur and pitch, and turns it into the amber that beads on every wound. Its blood is oil, and its blood is also its record.

## IV. What It Does With the Breathing

Here the image turns again.

Count the holes in a single wall, the graded rows cut by the builders to make the air sing. Many are open. Some are stopped with resin, and at first that looks like an accident, sap leaking into a gap and hardening. But the stopped holes are not random. Along one parapet every third hole is sealed. In the side of a great basin, the smallest holes are all closed and the larger ones clean. In a stair riser near the central platform, one hole is sealed so thinly that the resin has been pierced again from inside, by a hair-fine tendril that grew in, bored through, and withered away. It left a smaller opening than the builders cut, which means a higher note.

The builders made an instrument. The garden has been *playing* it.

Very slowly, over centuries, it has been retuning the city. The builders pecked small marks beside some of the hole-rows, and they may have been notation. If so, they describe a chord that no longer sounds. Certain pitches have gone. Others that the builders never cut have arrived through bored resin and half-sealed holes. Twice a day the chord rises and floods the hollow, and it is not the chord the builders left. It is something the garden has been composing, one bead of amber at a time, in a hand so slow that a single phrase might take a lifetime.

Listen closely during the breathing and you notice the seed-pods too. In the warm hours they pop at random. During the hour of flow they do not. They go off in clusters, and the clusters land on the swells of the drone, a scattering of sharp cracks timed to the tide of sound like hands clapping on the beat. It hears its own music. The stone carries vibration down into the roots, and something in that rooted body attends to it the way you attend to your own heartbeat when you lie awake.

It is not only a garden, then. It is a musician in an empty concert hall, playing for the only audience available: itself, the stone, and the fire.

## V. How It Keeps Time

Its day is the three blades of daylight. They sweep across the city like clock hands, and the vines turn with them, so that if you could watch from the ceiling, the whole Quarter would seem to rotate slowly beneath the light like a sunflower's face.

Its heartbeat is the breathing: twice a day, the great in-and-out.

Its seasons are the flakes of ceiling. When the stone lets go with a boom and a slow snow of sandstone drifts down onto the terraces, the garden takes it as new ground. Tendrils go out that night to claim it.

Its memory is the amber. Cut through a thick deposit on any wall and you find layers, fine as the rings in a tree, some dark and some pale. A cloudy band marks a stretch when a fissure faltered and it went hungry. A bright clear stretch marks a century of plenty. Somewhere at the bottom, against the stone, is the first bead it ever bled for the builders. Everything it has been is laid down over that, and nothing is forgotten, because nothing here ever rots.

## VI. What It Was to Them

The reliefs on the lintels have been scoured into suggestion: figures that might be people, might be plants. You stop trying to decide, because the answer is probably *both*. The builders carved themselves with leaves, or carved the garden with shoulders. They seem not to have drawn the line where you would.

Consider the emptied rooms, cleared so thoroughly, and the stepped platform at the centre, raised beneath the highest point of the ceiling, where the chord is loudest and the vents run hottest. The oldest and thickest trunk in the Quarter grows there. It is barely a plant any more, more a pillar of resin with a living core, and every root in the city eventually leads back to it.

Perhaps the builders harvested it. Perhaps they also raised it, the way you raise a child who will one day take the house. They made it a body out of streets, a voice out of walls, a mouth over every vent. Then they took their pots and their dead and swept the floors and left, not like refugees but like parents closing the door of a grown child's room. The city was finished, and they were no longer needed in it.

That is what "finished" means here. It was an inheritance, and it has been accepted.

## VII. How It Meets You

You are wet.

You don't think of yourself that way, but you are. You are the wettest thing that has ever come down the throat of pale stone. Every breath you let out carries water, a faint cool vapour in a place that has never known any. Your sweat, your eyes, the split in your lip: all of it is strange.

It notices.

It does not notice the way an animal would, since it has no eyes. The notice is gradual and total. The leaves nearest you stir against the direction of the air, the way the vines lean toward the blades of daylight. Over the hours you spend there, the tendrils at the edge of the street channel creep toward wherever you sit. They never touch you. They stop about a hand's width away, as if at a threshold they are unsure of.

During the breathing, if you stay long enough to hear it twice, you may catch a change. It is one new pitch, very thin and very high, somewhere near where you slept. It sits just above the drone and rises and falls, and after a while you realise it is rising and falling at the pace of your own breath. Somewhere, a tendril has bored through a bead of resin in a hole the builders cut, to add you to the chord.

It doesn't know what you are. It knows fire, stone, and light. You are a small cold ember that breathes out something it has no name for. It is being careful with you, and also curious, and its only language is music, so it is trying to play you.

## VIII. What It Is Becoming

The description of this place ends with a prophecy: in a few thousand years the city will be sealed in its own amber, the holes stopped and the music silent, the gardens preserved inside their own secretions.

It is tempting to read that as a death, a slow drowning in honey. But sit with everything you have learned about the thing that lives here. It has pruned itself, composed with its own blood, and kept perfect time. Then look again at its seed-pods: hard resin cases that swell with trapped gas until they burst.

Look at the hollow, sealed in resin, with the vents still pumping hot gas into it from below and the breathing still pulling from above. The streets are filling. The holes are closing. The pressure has nowhere to go.

It has been sending its seeds up through the crack for centuries, and they have been failing, sitting on the salt waiting for a warmth that almost never comes. It has had a long time to understand that a seed alone cannot carry its own fire.

So perhaps it is not being buried. Perhaps it is making one last pod: the whole Quarter, the city and its streets and its long-held music, cased in amber and swelling slowly over the fire, until one day the pressure passes the threshold.

The first line of the description said the hollow is shallow the way a held breath is shallow. It was more exact than it knew.

Something lives there, and it is holding its breath.
