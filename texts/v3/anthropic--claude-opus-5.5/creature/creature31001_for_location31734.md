# Those Who Live on the Stair

## The Heskers

Begin with the people, because they are the easiest to see and the last to be understood.

A Hesker is recognizable anywhere in the lowlands by the eyes first. The creases around them are deep and radiating, laid down in infancy, so that even a child of six looks as if she has spent a lifetime smiling into wind. The eyes themselves are dark, almost always, and sit a little deep. The brows are heavy. The lashes are long and straight and grow thick, a fringe against glare. When a Hesker removes the smoked-horn visor indoors, the skin beneath is paler than the rest of the face, and the band of it across the eyes makes them look briefly naked, like people who have taken off a mask to reveal a second, more private face.

Their hands are the next thing. Heskers have broad palms and long, mobile fingers, and the skin of the palm is unusually soft for a people who rake salt all day. They guard it. Pan-workers wear mitts to the wrist and bare their fingertips only for signing. The palm is where they hear. A Hesker greeting a friend does not wave. She lays her hand flat against his breastbone for a moment, or against the side of his throat if they are close, and feels the voice he isn't using, the breath and heartbeat and the small hum people make without knowing. Then she takes her hand away and they begin to sign. Lowlanders find this unbearably intimate. Heskers find lowland greetings, shouted across a room, grotesque. To them it is like being handled by someone who hasn't washed.

They go barefoot indoors. The soles of their feet are thick at the heel and ball, but the arch stays tender, and they walk on it more than you would expect, rolling slightly, feeling the floor. A Hesker family at dinner is a still picture: nobody talking, everyone with bare feet flat on the sinter floor, hands moving over the food in a quick shorthand that is mostly jokes. Laughter in Hesk is visible and tactile rather than audible. The shoulders go, the belly goes. When a room laughs together you can feel it in the table.

Their kidneys are famous. Generations of drinking tower-water with a trace of mineral in it, and breathing salt-mist, have made them tolerant of what would sicken a stranger. They are lean, heat-hardened, slow in their movements at midday and startlingly quick at dawn. They sleep in two pieces, a short night and a long afternoon, and many of them dream in texture instead of image.

They are not a silent people. That is the lowlander mistake. They are a people for whom sound is scarce, and like anything scarce it has been gathered up and given meaning. Speech aloud belongs to the Hour of Edges and the quiet-rooms. A thing said aloud is a thing that has been *spent*, and so it binds. You do not tell a Hesker you love him out loud unless you are prepared to be held to it. Children are rarely scolded aloud. The worst punishment in a Hesker household is for a parent to speak the child's name at dawn in a flat, even voice and then say nothing more. It is remembered for years.

A newborn in Hesk hears its first sound in the quiet-room, and it is always the same sound, by custom: the mother's breath, and then its own. The family gathers in the small dry space with the salt beds rustling under their slatted covers, and they wait for the baby to cry. When it does, they all lay their hands on it at once. It's the only time most Heskers will be touched by that many hands until they die.

## The Readers

Among them are the ones who read machines.

Every cluster has its readers. Some are engineers by training, but the best are simply people born with more feeling in their skin than others, and they are found early. A child who wakes crying because the pump two walls away has begun to cavitate, who puts her ear to the floor and then her cheek and then her whole body, is taken to the refinery at eight or nine and set to walk the plates with the old readers. She learns the vocabulary. A turbine running true is a warm, smooth tremor, like a cat on your chest. A bearing going bad is a grit in it, a faint sandiness. Imbalance is a lean, a pull toward one side of the body. A crack in a ceramic housing is the worst: a thin high thrill that travels up the shin bone and makes the teeth ache, and when a reader feels it she drops whatever she is holding and runs, and everyone who sees her run follows, without asking.

Readers are a little strange. They have a habit of standing with their weight evenly over both feet and their eyes half closed. They touch walls in passing the way other people glance at clocks. Many of them can't bear to wear shoes at all and go out onto the scalding terraces on feet that have become like horn at the edges and like skin, tender, at the center. They marry late or not at all. They say lowland cities feel like being inside a drum that someone is beating with no rhythm.

And the readers know something that is not written on any slate.

## The Long Beat

Under the turbine-hum, under the tremble of the carts on their felted rails and the soft impacts of feet, there is another motion in the plateau. The readers call it, in sign, by a gesture: the flat palm pressed downward, held, then lifted a finger's width, then pressed again. In the rare places where it must be spoken aloud, the word is old and doesn't mean much anymore. It might once have meant *under-breath*. It might have meant *the stair's own foot*.

It comes about once every eleven minutes. Not precisely. Sometimes nine, sometimes fourteen. It is too slow and too large to be felt by most people most of the time. It is not a tremor so much as a change of weight: for a few seconds the whole floor seems very slightly more *present*, as if it has pressed up against your feet with intention, and then it eases. A reader lying on the floor of a quiet-room at night, cheek on the stone, will feel it roll under her from north to south, the direction the plateau tilts, the direction the water flows. Then eleven minutes of ordinary stillness. Then again.

Children who become readers usually find it on their own. They come to their teachers with a frightened, pleased face and make the sign without having been taught it, and the teachers nod and lay a palm on the child's back and do not explain, because there isn't an explanation. The engineers have theories. A harmonic in the deep aquifer. Pressure cycling through the fractured rock beneath the vents, the whole plumbing of the plateau filling and emptying like a lung. That is probably true, the readers say, in the way it is true that a person is a set of bellows and pipes.

Once you know the long beat is there, you start to see it in other things.

## The Birds

Watch the waders at dawn, when the flocks stand motionless in the warm pools with their heads tucked. Thousands of them, pink and ash, on their thin legs, each one standing on a single foot pressed flat to the mat-slick floor of the pool. They stand like that for many minutes. And then, at a moment with no visible signal, all of them together put down the other foot. A ripple goes through the whole flock, a small soft shift of weight, from north to south. Then they lift the foot again and are still.

Nobody lowland has timed it. Hesker children have, sitting on the dikes during the Hour of Edges with water-clocks. Once every eleven minutes, more or less.

The great lifts, when a whole flock rises in its single exhale of wings and wheels over the terraces, also come on the beat, though not every beat. Something passes up through the water into their feet, and some of the time it tells them *go*.

This is why Heskers dislike the lowland ornithologists. It isn't only that the idea of constant, enormous, unheard birdsong is unnerving. It's the next thought, which every Hesker has and nobody says aloud. If the birds are singing, then something is receiving it. The Hush is not an absence. Sound goes into it and does not come out. A shout travels five meters and stops, the engineers say, because the droplets are the wrong size; but a Hesker grandmother will tell you, with her hands, very quietly, that the shout doesn't stop. It is *taken*. The Hush is the plateau holding its breath to hear better. The birds sing to it all day, and it listens, and every eleven minutes it shifts its weight like someone leaning closer.

At dawn, during the Hour of Edges, the Hush thins. The cool mist settles into the low pools, and for a while sound can travel. The old explanation is physical: the droplets change size as the air cools. The older explanation is that this is the hour when the listener has had its fill and draws back, and lets its tenants hear one another for a little while. This is why debts are spoken at dawn. They count because they are heard by the other person *and not only by it*.

## The Small Ones

The lizards on the pipes in their ranks, heads pointing the same way. People assume they are pointing toward the rising sun, and mostly they are. But on the northern pipes, near the old vents, the heads point north, toward the boiling fields, even at dawn, even with the sun on the other side. Lizards there lay their bellies flat on the warm metal and wait, and every eleven minutes, the whole rank of them lifts its forelegs and sets them down.

The spiders on the condensing towers build their webs, as the engineers say, in a way that helps the towers drip, and this is true. But their webs are also, if you look at them in the brief clear light, all oriented alike, with their strongest radials running north-south, and a spider at the hub rests with one leg on each radial, and every so often it plucks them. All the spiders on a tower pluck at once. The beaded silk shivers and shakes loose a little shower of water.

The shrimp in the rose-colored pans swirl in their slow shoals, turning together, and the turning changes direction on the beat.

The salt-furred shrews come out only at the Hour of Edges, when there is sound, and they scratch at the crust in little bursts. Watch closely and you will see they scratch in rhythm with one another across whole terraces, dozens of tiny paws making a sound you can just hear in the thinned mist: a dry, faint, synchronized ticking, like many small clocks. A Hesker child's first lesson about the dawn is to lie still and listen for the shrews. The second lesson is to notice that when the long beat passes, the shrews stop, all together, and hold still, and then go on.

The glass moths may not be insects at all. They are flickers in the bright air, stutters in the light. Readers say that if you stand at the edge of a boiling field and hold a glass moth in your eye, it will flicker faster just before the long beat comes, as if it were a disturbance thrown up ahead of a wave. Some readers think the glass moths are nothing but the beat itself, made briefly visible where it passes out of the rock into the air.

And down in the vent throats, the pale eels. Nobody knows what they eat or how they survive in water that should cook them. They coil up out of the boiling dark and hang in the shimmer near the surface, blind, their long bodies undulating, and they are the only creatures anyone has seen move *continuously* in the long rhythm, not a pulse every eleven minutes but a slow sine wave, rising and sinking, rising and sinking, a single breath stretched across the whole interval. They look less like animals than like something's fingers.

## What It Is

The Heskers have no temple for it. They have no priests. What they have is a set of habits so old they have stopped looking like belief.

They build uphill, following the heat, and leave the old districts to be swallowed. Lowlanders call this resignation. Heskers would call it courtesy. The water moves, the vents move, the long beat shifts a little in its course over the centuries, the way a sleeper turns over, and the people move with it. They don't fight the travertine crusting their doorways. Stone is patient; humans are temporary tenants in its process. The document the lowlanders wrote about Hesk says that, and it is almost right. What the Heskers mean is that they are *lodgers*. The landlord is not the stone. The stone is the landlord's body, or its shell, or the long slow thing it leaves behind as it grows.

Think of the terraces the way you would think of a reef. Every rim is the record of water that flowed over it, dropping its load, a finger's width in a lifetime. Every terrace is layered with snails that thickened until they stopped, and with the mats that trap grains and bind them, and with the bones of birds, and with the city itself, its fused walls, its drowned refineries, its ceramic chimneys poking up like the stems of trees. The plateau is not rock with life on it. It is life, all of it, slowly becoming rock, forty kilometers of it, three thousand meters up, and the water moves through it like blood and the heat drives the water and the long beat is the rhythm of the whole thing breathing.

What lives in Hesk, if you want one answer, is the Stair.

It has no face. It has no single place where it is. It is the vents and the fractured rock and the aquifer that fills and empties every eleven minutes. It is the mats that grade in color from sulfur to blood, which are the nearest thing it has to skin. It is the mist, which may be the nearest thing it has to attention. It grows, very slowly, upward and outward. It takes in what falls on it and what flows over it and what sings at it, and it keeps all of it. It is not cruel. It is not kind. It is simply very large and very slow and very interested, and everything that lives on it has, over a very long time, learned to keep time with it.

## The Dead

This is why the dead are laid in the spillways.

A Hesker is not buried because burial would put her *away from* the Stair, into dead ground, if there were any dead ground here. Instead, the family carries her to the spillway of the spring she was born nearest. They lay her on a stone bier where the warm water sheets over. They lay their hands on her all at once, as they did at her birth. Then they go home.

Within a season she is filmed with mineral. Within a decade she is a pale smooth shape. Within a few decades she is sheathed, a long rounded form in the travertine, her features softened past recognizing, as seamless as the old walls whose mortar has fused away. The terrace grows over her and past her. She becomes a step.

Families visit at the Hour of Edges and speak aloud to their dead, while speech can still carry. Lowlanders assume this is sentiment. It is, partly. But watch a Hesker woman kneeling at her grandmother's shape in the dawn, one palm flat on the smooth stone, the other lifted and moving in small signs even as she speaks. She is talking aloud and signing and *listening* all at once. She is waiting for the long beat. When it comes, she feels it pass under her palm through the shape of her grandmother. Some families swear it feels different there, a little warmer, a little closer, as if the grandmother has become one of the places the Stair listens *through*.

This is the thing Heskers believe and do not quite say. The dead do not go anywhere. They go *in*. The Stair is made of listeners. Every snail that stopped, every bird that fell into a pool and was furred over, every reader who spent her life with her bare feet on the floor plates feeling for the grit in the turbines, every child who lay on the dike counting shrews: they are all in the terraces now, and the terraces listen with them. The Hush grows a little deeper each century. The long beat, readers say, the oldest ones, the ones with feet like horn, comes a very little more strongly than it did when they were young. The Stair is still growing, and part of what it grows from is them.

## One of Them

Take a single one, finally. Call her Aun. She is a reader at a refinery on the fourth-highest terrace of the eastern cluster. She is sixty-one. She has never worn shoes indoors in her life and has not worn them outdoors in twenty years. Her feet are cracked and yellowed at the edges and at the center of each arch they are as soft as a child's. She has never married. She has a sister's son she is fond of, whom she has spoken aloud to perhaps a hundred times in his life, each time at dawn, each time something she meant.

Tonight, in the short hot night, she lies on the floor of her quiet-room with her cheek on the stone. The salt beds tick faintly as they draw the damp out of the air. She can hear herself breathe. She can hear her own pulse in her ear where it presses the floor. Through the floor she can feel the refinery turbine three buildings away, running true, the warm cat-purr of it. Beneath that, the carts resting on their rails. Beneath that, the pipes ticking as they cool a little. Beneath that, nothing, for a long time.

Then it comes. It rolls up out of the north, from the boiling fields, from the eels' throats, under the old swallowed districts and the snail-beds and the grandmothers in the spillways, under the pans with their pink and lemon and violet, under the tower-spiders plucking their silk and the birds shifting their feet in the dark pools and the lizards on the northern pipes lifting their forelegs all at once. It passes under her. The floor presses up, attentive, the way a hand would. It eases. It goes on south, down the tilt of the plateau, toward the gorge where the rock sweats, and she feels it leave.

Eleven minutes. She lies and waits for the next one. She is not afraid of it and she is not worshipping it. It is simply the oldest and largest thing she knows, and it is breathing, and she lives on it, and some day, not so long from now, her sister's son will carry her down to the spillway of the spring below the eastern cluster and lay his hands on her, and she will be sheathed, and become a step, and listen from the inside.

She finds she is looking forward to it a little. She wonders what the birds are singing.

In the morning the Hush will thin, and for an hour the plateau will talk aloud. The shrews will tick in their crust. The dew will drip from the towers. Somewhere a debt will be spoken, and somewhere a child will be told a story, and somewhere a woman will kneel at a pale smooth shape and say a name. Then the sun will clear the rim and the mist will lift into its luminous ceiling, and the Stair will draw its breath and hold it, listening, and everything that lives on it will go on, in time, without a sound.
