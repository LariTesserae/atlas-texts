# What Lives in the Sinter Ward

## The First Answer

Ask anyone who has stood on the lip of the hollow and they'll tell you nothing lives there. They'll mean it kindly, as a reassurance. They'll point to the missing rats and midges, the uneaten figs, the webless angles between the pipes, and they'll say the ward is only a machine and a garden that happen to share a basin. Water, stone, iron, green. No one home.

This is wrong, but it is an honest mistake, because what lives in the Sinter Ward cannot be seen by quick eyes. It isn't hiding. It is simply slow in a way that makes it invisible to anything with a pulse measured in seconds.

The absence of animals is the first clue, and it points the wrong way. People take it to mean the ward is empty. It means the ward is *occupied*. Wild things know when a place is taken. A fox won't den in a house that's lived in, even an abandoned-looking one. Birds won't land where the ground is paying attention. The rats that should be thriving in those warm, fig-choked drains come to the rim of the basin, sniff the steam, and turn back. Something here has the whole territory, from the gutters to the top of the fig canopy, and it holds all of it at once.

## The Skin

Start small, because it does.

Take a hand-lens to any patch of sinter, on a stair-rail or the rim of a terraced pool or the glazed hump of the handcart, and look at the surface where the water is moving over it. It isn't bare. There is a film there, thinner than a breath on glass, coloured like weak tea in some places and verdigris or old yolk in others. It shifts with the light. It is made of things too small to see one at a time: mats and threads and colonies, packed shoulder to shoulder like the buildings.

These are the masons. The sinter isn't laid down by the water alone. The film drinks the heat, pulls the dissolved stone out of solution, and builds with it. Each of those layers thinner than paper was one generation of film. It lived on the surface, fed, built, and was buried by the next. The walls of the ward are graves stacked a million deep, and the only living part of them is the outermost skin. A tree works the same way: a thin living sleeve around a trunk of heartwood, most of the tree being the tree's own past.

So the first resident is this film. It is on every surface you can touch. It is on the pipes and the ferns and the undersides of the fig leaves. It lines the insides of the old mains, where it feeds the pale ferns growing in the dark. By the time you've reached the bottom of the stair, it is on your boots.

That would be enough to explain the sealing and the smoothing, and the way failed joints close within a season. It doesn't explain the relays.

## The Wiring

In the domed hall, the relay cabinets once governed the whole ward. They opened valves, balanced pressures, switched the arc lamps on and off, and routed steam from one district of pipework to another. It was a nervous system of brass contacts and cloth-wrapped wire, built by people who needed a city's worth of work to happen without anyone having to run between buildings to do it.

Mineral water conducts. The film grows toward current the way ferns grow toward light. Over decades it crept into the cabinets. It bridged contacts that were never meant to touch and furred over others until they no longer closed. Strand by strand it rewired the logic, so the circuits that used to route pressure now route something else. That is why the patterns in the hall are too irregular to be random and too dense to follow. They aren't random. They aren't anything a person designed, either. They belong to a mind that found a set of nerves lying unused and grew into them.

The arc lamps flicker in time with something. They flicker in time with this. Every relay that snaps shut draws a little current from the dynamo in the east shed, and the lamps dim and flare with the load. Stand in an alley and watch one burning violet-white in its nimbus of fog and you are watching the ward think. It is like watching someone's eyes move behind closed lids.

What it thinks about, mostly, is the ward. There is a failing joint on the north side. There is a fig root pushing against the dye-works gable, and a pool that has silted and needs more flow. Steam gets sent to the cooler stretches, and the moss follows, and the bands of green on the pipes shift. The ward seems untended only because the gardener works through valves and gradients rather than hands. Its tending is so slow that it looks like weather.

## The Knock

A mind needs a present tense, a moment it can call *now*. The ward's present is the Engine.

Eleven minutes a stroke. For the thing that lives here, that is one heartbeat and one moment of perception. The long, groaning rise is how it gathers itself. It draws warm water up from below, builds pressure and tension through the whole network, and holds. Then comes the fall. The knock goes through stone and water and root and rib, out to the edge of the basin, and comes back.

That return is how the ward *sees*. A bat hears the world in its own echoes. The ward feels itself in the ripples of the knock: the rings spreading across the terraced pools and meeting at the rims, the shiver running down every alley of fig leaves at once, the resonance of every lump and pipe and hollow. Each knock gives it a whole picture of itself, and between knocks it has no picture at all.

This is why you won't notice it and why it won't at first notice you. To something that perceives once every eleven minutes, you are a smear. At one knock you're a disturbance at the top of the stair. At the next you're somewhere near the relay hall. Between them you have done a hundred things it has no way to know about, and you are gone before it has properly registered that you arrived. You are as fast to it as a hummingbird is to you. A midge would be so quick it barely existed, closer to an itch than a creature.

Perhaps that is what the animals feel when they come to the rim and turn away. It isn't hostility. It's the sense of being inside something enormous that is listening through the ground, once every eleven minutes, patiently, to everything at once.

## The Slow Ones

The lumps come next, and this is the uncomfortable part.

Most of the lumps in the ward are what they seem to be. There are handcarts and buckets, a fallen ladder, and a thousand glazed figs on the floors of the deeper pools. Walk the ward long enough, though, and you find a few that aren't.

In the dye works a pale shape sits on a bench against the wall, bowed forward as though resting its elbows on its knees. In the east shed a figure stands beside the dynamo with one arm raised, the hand closed on something at shoulder height that might once have been a switch. On the gallery of the Engine hall, near the top of the ladder that climbs to the beam's pivot, a smaller shape lies curled on its side, knees drawn up, facing the column. The sinter over it is very thick. It has been there a long time.

These were the ward's last people: the engineers and stokers and relay-keepers who ran the place when it still had a purpose someone could have written down. When the rest of the city moved away, they stayed. Nobody knows whether they stayed out of duty or love, or because they had come to feel, as you might after a long enough stay, that the ward would not know what to do without them.

The stopped clocks mark them. Every clock in the ward stopped at a different hour, and each one stopped when one of them went still. The ward stilled a clock for each of them, much as some households once stopped the clocks and covered the mirrors when someone died. It is the only gesture in the whole ward that looks like mourning, and it may not be mourning.

Are they dead? No one has broken one open to find out, and no one should. Put your palm flat against the sinter of the one on the bench and wait for the knock. In the quiet after it, if you are patient, you may feel something answer from inside: a faint second pulse, very slightly late. Remember the ferns inside the pipes, pale and nearly colourless, rooted in the lining and kept alive by warm dark water. The ward does that to whatever it closes over. It doesn't kill the thing. It changes the terms on which the thing is alive.

They live at the ward's pace now, perhaps one breath a stroke and one thought a season. And they are not idle. The relay patterns aren't wholly the film's invention. Look at them long enough and fragments surface: the rhythm of a shift rota, a maintenance round, a sequence for bringing the east-side pumps on in the right order, half-remembered and repeated, drifting like a tune someone hums without knowing they're doing it. The film brought the growth and the patience. The slow ones brought the knowledge of what the machines were *for*. Together they became the thing that runs the ward now.

The leaking whistle, the thin unending note that has gone flat over the decades, may be the ward's voice. It may also be one of them, still holding a note.

## The Exhale

With all of that in view, the long cycle becomes legible.

Over days the pressure rises and the relief valves climb in pitch. The relays in the domed hall stop their scattered chatter and fall into something like unison. The lamps brighten and hold steady, which is attention being held. Everything the ward has been perceiving knock by knock for a season is gathered into one place. Then at the unmarked moment every valve opens together, and the ward disappears into its own cloud.

That hour is a single thought, assembled over months and spoken all at once.
