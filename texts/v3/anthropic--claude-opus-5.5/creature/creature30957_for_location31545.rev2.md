# Those Who Answer

At first you would say no one lives here. You would say the animals live here, the bellows-birds and rivetbacks and eels, and that the Stokemoor is too loud, too wet, too dark, too thoroughly held by its rhythm for anything like a person to stand upright in it.

Then, in the chorus after a Stroke, among the hoots and clicks and the thin whine of cablevine, you would hear a sound that doesn't belong to any animal. It is a short hum rising at the end, almost a question, and from somewhere to the left another hum answers it, falling. Two voices a quarter mile apart have just told each other where they are. Then both go quiet with the rest of the moor, and the Stroke comes up out of the ground.

They call themselves by that sound. You can't write it, but it comes out roughly as *hm-ta*: the rise and the drop, the call and the confirmation. It means something like *we who answer*. Travelers from beyond the plateau, the few who have come up the waterfall stairs and survived the climb, call them the moorfolk, or the Answerers, or nothing at all, because they never met one. They only heard, for days on end, a pattern of hums that moved around them at a careful distance and stopped whenever they tried to approach.

---

## The Shape of Them

They are people, or near enough. Picture them first as you would picture anyone and then let the moor revise the picture.

They are slight and long-limbed, built for pushing through dense wet growth rather than for running. Their skin is fine and pale and always damp, and it breathes a little. In the heaviest hours after an Exhale an Answerer's skin drinks from the air the way the leaves do. They wear almost nothing. Clothing here only gets heavy with water and rots. Some of them wrap their shoulders in cured bloom-lichen, which is warm and springy and smells of rust, and which marks them by its thickness as elders or as people who have traveled far from the steam mains.

Their eyes are there, but closed. The lids are smooth and sealed in most adults, like the eyes of something newly hatched. Infants are born with them open, and for the first few hundred Strokes of a child's life the eyes roll and search and find nothing. Then the lids knit shut, and nobody considers this a loss. The Answerers have a word for that closing. It is also their word for *arriving*.

Their sight is elsewhere. Along the cheekbones, down the sides of the jaw, and in a delicate crescent over each brow runs a line of shallow pits, like the pockmarks a light rain leaves in soft mud. These feel heat. An Answerer turning her head slowly across the moor is looking, and what she sees is the glow: the buried steam mains lying like bright rivers under the ground, the fountains of the vents, the embers of rivetbacks grazing along a pipe, the small bright coal of another Answerer's face turned toward her from far off. To her the moor is not dark. It is lit from beneath, the way a hand is lit when you hold it over a lamp.

Their ears are large, mobile, and folded like the fronds of mantle-fern. They can turn them independently. An Answerer at rest keeps one ear forward and one back, the way you might keep one hand on a railing.

And their hearts keep time with the Stroke. They don't beat once per Stroke, which would be far too slow, but they beat in ratio to it, locked in phase. An adult's heart beats about fifty times between one Stroke and the next, and the fiftieth beat always lands on the rise. Nobody teaches this. A child simply falls into it, sometime in the first year, the way two pendulum clocks on the same wall fall into step. Answerers can feel when someone's heart has come loose from the Stroke. They lay a palm on the chest, wait out a cycle, and know. A loose heart means sickness, grief, or the approach of death. When an Answerer dies, the heart stops in the hush. The oldest among them say it always does, and that no one has ever died during the chorus.

---

## Speech

The Stroke has shaped their speech more than anything else.

An Answerer can speak only in the chorus, the window after the Stroke's ringing fades and before the moor falls quiet. Speaking over the Stroke is useless, because the Stroke drowns everything. Speaking in the hush is something else entirely, and I'll come to that. So every utterance is at most about half a minute long, and the language has grown to fit.

A sentence is a *breath*. A breath has a shape: it opens soft, climbs, and closes with a falling cadence timed to finish just before the hush. A long thought is carried across many breaths, and the hushes between them work as punctuation. When a storyteller stops at the end of a chorus, the listeners don't wonder whether she has finished. They know from the fall of the last word. One kind of fall means *more is coming*. Another means *this is the end of it*. A third, rarer one means *I don't know how this ends*.

Tenses are timed. A thing said on the rising edge of the chorus, just after the Stroke, when the air is warmest and the water is moving, is certain: *this is so*. A thing said late, near the hush, is doubtful, hoped for, or feared: *this may be so*. The same words carry different meanings depending on where in the forty-one seconds they fall, so a clumsy speaker, or a child, or a foreigner, can say *my mother is alive* at the wrong moment and have it heard as *my mother might be alive*. Answerers are gentle about this. They wait and let you try again next cycle.

Names aren't spoken but tapped. Each person has a rhythm, a pattern of knocks on a pipe or on a rivetback shell carried for the purpose. Children receive their names when their hearts settle into the Stroke, and the name always fits within one cycle. You introduce yourself by laying your knuckles on the nearest length of iron and knocking your pattern, and the pipe carries it for miles, and people far away who know you stop and turn an ear. There's an old custom of laying a hand on a buried water main in the evening to feel for the names of people you love, knocked from somewhere along its length.

Their music is the stack-trees. A grove is an instrument too large to play, but it can be tuned. Over generations Answerers have cut, notched, and bound the living trunks, shortening some and packing moss into others, until certain groves ring out on every Stroke with a chord that is almost, though not quite, resolved. They say a grove that finally rings true will mean the end of something. They're patient. They have been tuning some groves for longer than their stories reach back.

## How They Live

They live in and around the pump-houses, though *live in* isn't quite the phrase they'd use. Their word for it is closer to *nurse*.

The Answerers believe the pump-houses are the calves of whatever lies at the center of the moor, and that the calves must be kept well. So they tend them. They cut back the cablevine before it smothers the valves. They clear lichen from the pistons. They gather oil moths in woven nets and press them for the old lubricant soaked into their furred bodies, then work that oil back into the joints the moths came from, which strikes them as fitting. Nobody taught them how the machines work. They learned by listening. A healthy pump-house has a sound, and a sick one sounds different, and there are Answerers who can hear a cracked bearing from a day's walk away and set off toward it without a word.

Inside the pump-houses, where it's hottest and wettest, they sleep in heaps, piled together against the stone walls with the piston working over them. It's the loudest place on the moor, and they find it restful. The noise is so enormous and so regular that it leaves no room for anything else, and inside it they are unreachable. A quietcat can't use the hush in a pump-house, because a pump-house never goes quiet.

They eat rivetbacks, which they crack open with the heels of their hands against the pipes, a sound so like the rivetbacks' own clacking that the herd doesn't scatter. They eat eels caught by hand in the warm channels, standing thigh-deep and still, feeling for the hum through their shins. They drink sap from the skin-thin leaves, which tastes faintly of iron and faintly sweet. They cook on dark fire, a flame that gives heat and crackle and smoke and no light at all, and to them the lightlessness is simply what fire is. The word they use for fire is the word they use for a voice.

They keep time by the Stroke and by the Exhale. A life is counted in Exhales, twice a day by the old reckoning, and an old Answerer might have heard sixty thousand of them. They have no word for *day* in our sense. They have *between Exhales*, the long condensing hours when the moor drips and the mists thicken. And they have *the rain*, which is when they sing loudest, because the downpour covers everything and for an hour the whole moor is one roar and nothing can hunt anything.

---

## The Hush

Now the hush, the eleven seconds before each Stroke when the whole moor falls silent.

Answerers don't speak in the hush. Mostly they don't move in it either. When the chorus ends, every one of them on the moor goes still: mid-stride, mid-meal, a hand on a branch or an ear turned. They hold their breath, and they hear what the moor hears in those seconds, the drip of condensate, the waterfalls roaring at the edges, their own locked hearts. Then the Stroke comes and they breathe out and go on.

This is protection against the quietcat, which moves only in the hush. But over countless generations it has become more than protection. The hush is the one time an Answerer is truly alone. In the chorus you are always answering or being answered, always placed and placing, knit into the net of sound. In the hush the net falls slack, and for eleven seconds you are one warm body in the dark, and something may be moving toward you, and you cannot know.

So the hush is where they put the things that can't be said in the chorus.

A word spoken in the hush is a vow, or a curse, or a confession. It carries farther than anything said in the chorus, because nothing covers it. It is the most dangerous thing an Answerer can do, because it tells every listening creature exactly where you are, and the only listening creature in the hush is the one that hunts. Lovers speak in the hush. That is how you know it is love and not something smaller: one of them whispers the other's name into the silence, where it hangs, naked and audible, eleven seconds from safety, and the other either answers in the hush or doesn't. Some of the oldest songs are about lovers who answered, and some are about the quietcat.

A few Answerers go further. They are called the hush-walkers, and nobody is sure whether they're honored or pitied. A hush-walker has learned to move as the quietcat moves: fast and soundless in the eleven seconds, frozen in the chorus. They carry messages the moor's net can't be trusted with. They track things. They hunt quietcats, or say they do. They're rarely spoken of and never spoken to in the chorus. You leave food for them at the foot of stack-trees and find it gone some Stroke later, and you never heard a thing.

Some hush-walkers stop answering entirely. They stop tapping their names, stop humming *hm-ta* across the moor, and go on moving in the silence, and at a certain point the other Answerers stop talking about them as people. There is a belief, never quite stated, that this is where quietcats come from. It holds that the quietcat isn't another animal but an Answerer who went quiet and stayed quiet until the silence grew fur. When a child asks what a quietcat is, the answer, delivered late in the chorus, in the doubtful tense, is: *what you become if you stop answering.*

## The Chimney

I had meant to stop there, with the people and their hums and their held breath. But the Answerers keep pointing somewhere else, and a description of who lives in the Stokemoor that ignores where they point would be incomplete.

They point at the chimney.

It stands alone in the open moor, tall and iron-dark, and it breathes warm air in time with the Stroke. The Answerers know exactly where it is, whatever the confused travelers say. It is the one place on the plateau that they approach in total silence, walking only in the hushes, like hush-walkers, like quietcats. When they reach it they lay their foreheads against the warm iron and listen.

What they hear, far below, is voices.

The Answerers say these are the dead. Hearts stop in the hush, and the hush, they say, is a door. What slips through it goes down, and down is where the dead live, in the heat under the rock, close to the great heart, still counting. If you listen at the chimney long enough you'll hear someone you knew. You won't make out words, only the rise and fall of breaths, and sometimes a rhythm tapped far down on iron, a name.

So they bring the dead here. They carry the body across the moor in the hushes, which takes many days, and at the chimney's base they knock the dead person's name on the iron one last time and leave the body where the warm breath comes up. Some Stroke later, perhaps many, it is gone. It might have been taken by quietcats, or rivetbacks, or the slow digestive work of bloom-lichen. The Answerers don't think so.

---

## Below

This is where the picture becomes what it was always becoming. The thing that lives in the Stokemoor isn't finished at the surface.

Forty miles down, under settled rock, there is heat of a kind the moor only guesses at, and dark fire that has never once cast light, and there are the stokers.

I won't pretend to know them well. I know them only the way the Answerers do, by what comes up the chimney. They are the builders' last descendants, or the builders' last servants, or something the builders made to keep the fire fed when they themselves could no longer do it. They live in a heat that would kill an Answerer in an hour. They have never heard a bellows-bird, never touched a leaf, never felt rain. Their world is iron galleries, the roar of the furnace, the vast slow turning of the thing at the center, and the work.

The work is the Stroke. Every forty-one seconds they heave on something, feed something, open something, all together. Whether they do it with their hands or their bodies or their voices, the machine turns over, and the plateau lifts the width of a fingernail and settles, and forty miles above them the Answerers breathe out.

In the stokers' pauses, when the furnace is banked and the work waits, they listen up the long shafts that climb toward the surface. What they hear is very faint, filtered through miles of pipe and rock: a chorus. Hoots and clicks and a thin electric whine, and among them a pattern of hums, rising and falling. *Hm-ta.* The stokers have never seen the moor and don't believe it is a place. They believe it is where the dead go, into the cool above the rock, where the air is wet and the work is finished and everyone sings. When a stoker dies they carry the body up the long shaft as far as the heat allows and leave it there, and some count later it is gone.

Neither people knows about the other, except as an afterlife.

---

So I'll describe the inhabitant of the Stokemoor at last. It isn't the Answerers alone, or the stokers, or the quietcat, or the great buried machine. It's all of them together in the one rhythm.

It works like this. The Answerers go silent for eleven seconds because the Stroke is coming. The stokers, far below, listening up the chimney, hear the moor go silent and know it's time, and they heave. So the Stroke comes because the moor goes quiet, and the moor goes quiet because the Stroke is coming, and no one on either end knows which came first, or whether anyone began it, or whether it began at all or was simply always turning over, one count at a time.

This is what lives in the Stokemoor: two peoples who have never met, keeping time for each other in the dark, each believing the other is where they go when they die, each listening across forty miles of rock for the pause in which to act. The creatures that live along the seam between them hunt in the silence or sing in the noise. And the whole of it is one enormous held breath, let out again every forty-one seconds for longer than anything can reckon.

Put your forehead to the chimney. Wait for the hush, and in it, very far down, you will hear someone counting.

Then the moor answers them.

*WHUMM.*
