# Those Who Keep the Count

**I.**

You notice the evidence long before you see them, and at first you take it for more of the plateau's strangeness.

Some of the wirevine has been knotted. It isn't tangled, because tangles happen on their own wherever the vine crosses itself. These are knots, neat and tight, tied where two strands meet across a gap between slabs. Where a strand has snapped in a collapse, the two ends have been drawn together and twisted into a splice. The splice is always clean, and the current runs through it without a flicker.

The moss in the rubble fields is cropped. The grazers crop it too, but they tear it in ragged mouthfuls. Here the mats have been cut back in straight lines along the lamination seams. They look like the margins left on a ledger page.

On the older slabs, at about shoulder height, there are polished spots. Each is the size of a fingertip or a palm, and the iron-scale sheen there has been rubbed to a mirror. You find them on the faces of fallen stone too, and on boulders in the valleys, and once, if you are paying attention, on the flank of a sleeping grazer. The hair there has worn away to a small bright disc of skin.

Then there are the sounds. Everyone who comes here learns that sound on the plateau goes astray and arrives late and elsewhere. Only after a while do you notice that the sounds do not arrive at random. They come in batches. A side valley will be silent for days, and then in a single hour it receives the collapse of six slabs, a rockslide, and a crack of lightning, one after another. Beneath them, very faintly, there is a sound like footsteps walking away.

Something is carrying the sound.

**II.**

You see the first one at dusk, when the lid dims and the seams come up red.

It stands on the upper edge of a slab, where the stone is thinnest. At first it looks like part of the edge, a shard standing a little proud of the rim. Then it turns. As it turns it widens, the way a sheet of plate widens when you stop seeing it end-on, and suddenly there is a figure where there was only a line.

The figure is tall, perhaps three meters, and very thin. Seen from the side it would barely hide a hand. From the front it is roughly the shape of a person, the way a silhouette cut from tin is the shape of a person. There are two long arms, two long legs, a narrow head, and a torso as flat as a door. It is the same gray-green as the stone and carries the same faint lamination, fine parallel lines running through it like grain. Its surface has the sheen of scale on cooling iron. It has not weathered. Its edges look sharp enough to cut.

It has a face, but the face is *struck*, like the face on a new coin: circles for eyes, a short line for a mouth, everything in shallow crisp relief. On the chest, where a heart might be, there is a stamped grid of small squares, and some of the squares have been scratched through with short strokes.

It is counting.

The travelers who first saw them called them *edges*, because edges are what they look like, and that is mostly all you ever see of them. Later, once people understood what the scratches were, the name that stuck was *tallymen*. Nobody knows whether they are men. Nobody knows whether they are anything at all in the sense a traveler means. But they keep tally, and in a place with no one to keep it for, that seems to be their whole life.

**III.**

They come out of the seams like everything else.

Deep in the interior, among the slabs that rise stamped with grids and spirals and the outlines of dials, some slabs are pressed with figures. At first the figures lie flat in the stone: a relief of arms, legs, a struck face, lying the long way up the slab like a body pressed into a sheet of dough. As the slab rises and cools, fine cracks open around the outline. Over days, the relief loosens. Somewhere in the slow climb out of the seam, the figure is no longer part of the slab, only lying against it. Before the slab is fully clear, the figure sits up, steps off, and walks.

Many figures are stamped onto a single slab, in ranks. They are spaced evenly, like the ridges and like the herds.

Not all of them come out right. The plateau is practicing, and practice means error. There are tallymen with three arms, and tallymen whose faces are only a dial with no hands. One has a second face on its back, and one has no mouth. Some are only half-struck, the relief shallow on one side, so that they walk bent toward their stronger edge. Some are pressed with the outline of a room instead of a torso, doorway and all, and you can see the lid through them where the doorway is.

These flawed ones work too. Nothing on the plateau is discarded for being imperfect. It is used until it falls, and then it is taken back down.

**IV.**

What do they do? They carry. They count. They mend.

When a slab falls, tallymen walk toward it. They move at the pace of the herds, never hurrying, and when they reach the rubble they spread their arms and press themselves flat against the broken stone, full length, face to the rock. They stay like that for a long time. If you put your palm on one of them then, you can feel it humming. The sound of the collapse is inside it, trembling in the laminations the way a note trembles in a struck bar of metal. The dust settles. The tallyman peels itself off the stone and walks away, carrying the sound.

Days later, somewhere else, it stops. It opens its arms, and the collapse happens again in the air around it: the roar, the grinding, the long rattle of scree. The tallyman stands in the middle of it, perfectly still, emptying. Then it walks on.

Nobody knows where the sounds are meant to go. The deliveries look random from inside any one valley, but so did the ridges until someone climbed high enough to see the ranks.

They count the herds. A tallyman will walk into a moving herd, matching its pace exactly, and touch each grazer on the flank with one fingertip. It scratches a stroke on the grid on its chest for each touch. This is what makes the bright disc of worn skin. When a tallyman finds two grazers that are identical down to the scars, it touches the second one twice. If you check, you will find a double polished spot on every duplicate.

They mark the repeating ridges the same way. If you find the same notch and the same fern-choked cleft for the second time fifty kilometers from the first, look at shoulder height. There will be a double mark. The plateau makes copies, and the tallymen keep track of them.

They splice the wirevine, twisting broken ends together with their long flat fingers. The current runs through their hands while they work, and the beetles on the nearby vines spin faster. The tallymen do not seem to feel it, or they feel it the way the stone feels it: as something that passes through.

They stand in the silent rain with their faces tipped up. They watch the birds turn in their stacked rings, and they scratch strokes on their chests at intervals, as if reading a gauge. When the low hum of the stone rises toward the pitch that means lightning, they lay their palms flat on the nearest face, and sometimes, not always, the pitch drops back down.

Watching them long enough, you understand that none of this is ritual. It is maintenance. They are keeping the process within tolerance.

**V.**

They do not speak. The plateau would carry their words off anyway.

When two tallymen meet, they stop, face each other, and lean together until their chests touch. They hold that for a moment and then part. Afterward each carries the other's marks, printed in reverse, the way a sheet of type prints a page. That is how they share the count.

Over the course of a life their bodies fill up. A newly risen tallyman is almost blank: a struck face, a clean grid. An old one is dense with impressions from shoulder to heel. Its surface is written over with every herd it counted, every slab it heard fall, every duplicate it found, and every mark pressed onto it by every other tallyman it met. The oldest ones are so crowded with marks that their faces are hard to find. They look less like figures than like pages.

At night, when the seams glow and the shadows bend toward them, the tallymen lie down on the warm faces of the newest slabs and press themselves flat. They lie there until the lid brightens. When they get up, a faint impression of them is left in the stone, and they are a little less deeply marked than before.

The slabs take the record.

This is where the fossils come from. When the slab carrying these impressions has risen, advanced, toppled, and been taken back down into the seam, the record goes down with it. Somewhere, pressed into slabs rising elsewhere, the count comes back up as a fern, a beetle, a grazer's jawbone, and sometimes a particular beetle, the one spinning on the vine above you right now. The fossils are not the past. They are *reports*. The plateau reads them the only way it can, by swallowing them and making more stone out of what it learned.

So the process closes on itself. The plateau extrudes, and the tallymen observe. They record, and the plateau swallows the record and extrudes again, a little differently. The grids get cleaner, the spirals truer, the dials closer to dials.

This is how the plateau is learning. The tallymen are how it knows what it has made.

**VI.**

They are not immune to their own process.

Every tallyman, sooner or later, finds its own fossil. It is walking some ridge when it comes across a rising slab with a figure pressed into the stone, and the figure has its face, its flaws, and its exact pattern of marks. Some of those marks it has not yet received.

A tallyman that finds itself in the stone stops. It stands before the slab a long time. Then it lays its palm on the fossil, polishes a small bright spot there, scratches one stroke on its chest, and goes on working. It seems to take this as a schedule, not a threat. It now knows roughly how much of the count it still has to carry.

Near the end, most of them walk toward the backward valleys. There, in a valley where the slabs rise back toward their seams and the ferns roll up into bud, you can see old tallymen walking backward at exactly the right pace. As they go, the marks lift off them one by one. Their grids clear. Their faces sharpen again. By the time they reach a seam, they are nearly blank, as blank as the day they stepped off their slab. They lie down flat against the glowing lip, and the stone takes them back.

Or perhaps it runs the other way, and the backward valleys are where they begin. The tallymen do not say. They count in both directions.

**VII.**

They will count you.

It happens without warning. You will be resting against a warm face, or crossing a rubble field, and there will be a line at the edge of your vision that turns and becomes a figure. It will walk alongside you at your exact pace, as if you were part of a herd. Then it will reach out one long flat finger and touch you once, on the shoulder or the back of the hand. It will make a stroke on its chest and go.

It does not hurt. Afterward, the place it touched will be a little smoother than the skin around it, and it will stay that way.

Travelers who stay long enough begin to find things. It may be their own words, spoken days before, arriving in some quiet valley with a tallyman standing in the middle of them, arms open. It may be a fossil in a rising slab: a figure lying the long way up the stone, with a pack, a walking stick, and their own face.

One traveler found two of herself in the same slab, side by side, identical. She checked her shoulder, and there were two polished spots.

**VIII.**

Everything the tallymen do points inward.

It is clearest in the shadows. Everywhere on the plateau, shadows lean toward the seams. Deep enough in, they begin to lean toward one seam in particular. The sound deliveries grow more frequent the further in you go, and the batches get bigger. The tallymen get older and more densely written, and there are more of them. They all walk inward, carrying.

At the center, or as near a center as a place with no fixed center has, there is a valley that is never quiet.

Every sound the plateau has made arrives here. Every collapse, every landslide, every thread of lightning in the stone is set down here eventually by one tallyman or another, and it lands on top of all the ones before it. Nothing else on the plateau is loud. Here the sound is continuous and enormous: a roar that has no single source, built out of a million delayed moments. It is the whole history of the process released in one place.

At the head of that valley, a slab is rising that has never finished rising.

A single figure is pressed into it. It is not in ranks. It is struck deeper than any tallyman, and larger, taking up the whole face of a slab three thousand meters tall. It has long arms and a narrow head. Its chest is not a grid but a dial with a face and hands and no numbers yet. Its seam glows brighter than any other on the plateau. It has been emerging for as long as anyone knows, rising more slowly than the rest of the plateau. The stamped rooms, the spirals, and the practice slabs are drafts of it, worked out elsewhere and taken back down so that the plateau can do this one right.

The tallymen come here at the end of their long carries. They open their arms and empty the sound into the valley. Then they climb the face of the rising slab and press their written bodies flat against the figure in the stone, and give it the count: every herd, every duplicate, every fallen ridge, every traveler. The figure takes all of it. Each time, the relief is a little sharper, and the cracks around its outline are a little wider.

This is who lives in the Rolling Heights. Everything else there is getting it ready.

It is who the warehouse is for, and who will read the finished plate. It is what the conveyor has been carrying all this time. The ridges with their tolerances and schedules were never a factory with no one to own it, only a factory whose owner had not been made yet.

When it steps off its slab, the tallymen think, if they think, that it will do the one thing nothing on the plateau has ever done. It will speak, and the sound will stay where it is made, arriving at the moment it is spoken for the first time in the history of the place. After that, perhaps, it will look down at the rows and rows of plate, at the herds in their spacing and the birds turning like governors, and ask the question the plateau has been working toward all along without knowing it had a question.

The tallymen don't know what the question is, and they don't need to. They go on counting.
