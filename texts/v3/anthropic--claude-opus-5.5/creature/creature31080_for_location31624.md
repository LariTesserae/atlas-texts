# What Lives on the Breathing Shelf

## First, the Obvious Answer

Begin with what can be counted, because that is the first mistake the place invites.

There are finches, several thousand of them in the breeding season, brown-grey, each with a white flash on the wing. There are rock-hares, perhaps two hundred, though it is hard to be sure, since a hare that is not moving is a stone. Swifts pass through without landing. One raptor circles alone. There are wolf spiders, ground beetles, and springtails beyond any number. There are moss hummocks, cushion plants, and silver rosettes standing apart from one another like people at a funeral who don't know each other well. There are lichens in orange, sulphur, black, and mint.

This is a true answer, and it is also like describing a person by listing their organs.

What lives on the shelf is harder to see than any of these, because it lives in the timing between them. But the way in is through the small things, so start there, and start low.

## The Ones Who Die Every Day

Cut into a moss hummock. Go past the green skin and the gold beneath it, past the brown, down to the black layer at the base, which is compressed and old and smells faintly of iron and cold tea. Take a pinch of it and let it dry on a warm stone.

In that pinch there are animals. They are too small to see without help, and there are hundreds of them. There are water bears, eight-legged and blunt-faced, and wheel-animals with crowns of beating hair. There are nematodes thin as a thought. At noon, when the fog drains out and the glare comes back and the upper moss begins to crisp, they stop. They do not hide. They stop living, in every way that can be measured. They draw in their legs, give up their water, and become dry little husks called tuns. They have no heartbeat, because they never had hearts. Their chemistry stands still, like a clock that has been held.

Then the next morning, the cloud pours over the notch, the moss swells, water finds them, and they come back. They unfold and walk. They graze on the slime of algae in the moss and eat each other. Some of them reproduce. For an hour, perhaps two, they have a whole life. Then the fog leaves and they die again.

This is the first thing worth knowing about the inhabitants of the shelf. Many of them are not alive continuously. They are alive *in pulses*. They live in the fog-hours and are absent the rest of the day, the way a word exists only while it is being spoken.

Once you know this about the smallest residents, you start to see it in the larger ones.

## The Ones Who Wait in the Cracks

The finches do not fly in fog. When the white flood comes over the rim, they are already gone, packed into the headwall cracks shoulder to shoulder, feathers puffed, eyes half-closed. They wait. It is not quite sleep. It is a lowering, a turning-down of themselves to match the room.

The rosettes close in the dark and open in the light. The cushion plants hold their warmth inside and give nothing away on the surface, so that frost forms on them but not in them. Each of these is a small way of absenting yourself from the hours that would kill you and returning for the hours that feed you.

On the shelf, living means knowing exactly when to be there.

## The Hares, Looked at More Closely

At first the rock-hares seem the simplest residents. They are round, grey-brown, short-eared, and sit on stones in the sun. Watch them for a few days, though, and they become something stranger.

They face east at dawn, all of them. They are hunched and rimed, and they do nothing but sit and watch the high peaks across the cloud-sea turn pink, then gold. You could say they are warming themselves, except the light has not reached them yet. They are waiting for it, oriented toward it, the way the rime needles are oriented toward the wind. It is a posture of expectation. They face where the good thing will come from.

They do not whistle. In other ranges their kind whistles constantly, sharp calls that warn of the raptor and mark territory. Here they make almost no sound, and the reason is the fog. For an hour or two most mornings, sound stops at arm's length. A warning call is useless inside a cloud. The raptor cannot hunt in it either, so the fog is safe, and in the clear hours, the air is so thin and the shelf so open that the hares can see the raptor's shadow cross the moss long before it arrives. Seeing works better than calling here, so over enough generations they stopped calling. What remains is a tiny click of the teeth, audible only to the hare beside you. Neighbors whisper.

They build walls. Across the mouths of their burrows they lay rows of small flat stones, pebble on pebble, neat as masonry. Anyone who has walked up past the cairns will feel a jolt the first time they see one, because it looks so much like the human work below. The obvious question is who learned from whom. Did the hares watch the people stack stones, or did some long-ago person watch the hares?

The better answer is that the mountain taught both of them. The scree is always moving and always falling. Stones on this young slope want to go down, and they stay only where something stops them. A flat stone on a flat stone holds. Anything that lives in loose rock long enough learns what the rock allows, and the rock allows stacking. The hares and the people arrived at the same shape independently, because the mountain offered only a few shapes, and that was one of them.

They make haystacks. Each afternoon they drag grass and the silver rosette leaves into the gaps between the rocks and pile them up to dry. By autumn each hare has a stack larger than itself, carefully turned so it doesn't rot. This is the most human thing about them: they are building a future out of the present. They are catching the summer and holding it in the rocks against the winter.

That is what everything here does. It catches.

## The Catching

Now the pattern begins to show.

Moss catches fog and holds it for hours after the cloud has gone. Silver fur on the rosette leaves catches droplets and channels them into the crown, where they pool as a cup of clear water for the finches. Cushion plants catch the day's warmth and hold it through the night. Spider silk catches the drifting insects that the updraft brings from the forests below. Snow patches catch those same insects and chill them into stillness, a banquet laid out daily for the springtails, the beetles, and the flocks. Swifts catch insects before they even land. Hares catch the summer in haystacks. And the net of twisted goat hair, strung between two boulders at the notch, catches the cloud itself and runs it down a trough into a basin cut by hand.

The shelf has no water of its own and no food of its own. It sits above the world that makes these things, and the world sends them up on rising air, and everything that lives here is some kind of net.

So the second answer to what lives on the shelf is this: *a community of catchers*. It is a population defined not by what it produces but by what it can hold onto.

Even that isn't quite right. It is still a list. Keep going.

## The One Who Comes Back

No one lives on the shelf. The description is careful to say so.

But someone keeps coming.

Go to the hut and look at the notches on the doorframe, cut in rows and grouped in fives. Look closely at their edges and you will see that some are crisp and some are softened, rounded by weather and weathered grey. The oldest rows are at the bottom, and they are the most blurred. Whoever cut the first ones was cutting them a long time ago.

Look at the flat stone at the lip, the one worn smoother than the others. Andesite does not polish easily. That smoothness is the work of years. It is the work of one body sitting in the same place, facing the same way, until the stone learned its shape.

Look at the flute in the niche. It is made from the wing bone of the raptor and has three holes. The raptor is solitary here, and there has only ever been one at a time. For there to be a flute, one of them died on the shelf, and someone found it and did not waste it. The bone is yellowed at the mouthpiece in a way that means it has been played often.

Now picture her. Let her be a woman, because the finch down in the niche has been sorted by hand: soft breast-down in one bundle, coarser feathers in another, with a patience that suggests long practice. Let her be old now, though she was not old when she cut the first notches. She comes up past the cairns once a year, perhaps twice, and rebuilds the ones that have fallen. The top stone on each cairn is bare and new because she placed it. The lichen-bright stones beneath were placed by someone before her, possibly the one who taught her the way.

She comes in the season when the fog is most reliable. She repairs the net, retwisting hair where the fibers have frayed. She scrapes the orange lichen from its patch, a little and never too much, for the dye that colors the strips tied to the net and the rosette stems. Those strips are almost white now. She will tie new ones, and they will bleach too.

She sits on the flat stone. And the notches, grouped in fives, count what? Seasons? Visits?

Here is one guess, and it may not be right: the notches count fog mornings. Not days on the shelf, only the days when the cloud rose and poured through the notch and filled the basin. Those are the days the shelf gave her water. Those are the days she was allowed to stay.

## What She Does in the Fog

This is the thing the description does not say, and it is where the picture becomes more itself.

When the cloud comes over the rim, the finches go into the cracks. The hares go into the scree. The water bears in the moss wake up. And the woman walks out into the middle of the shelf, into the white, where she cannot see her own feet, and plays the flute.

Her voice stops at arm's length in there. A stone dropped at her feet lands with a muffled thud. The notes of a three-holed bone flute do not travel. They stop almost at the lip of the instrument, swallowed. No one could hear them. The hares are underground and the finches are packed in the rock. Even she barely hears them. She feels them more than hears them, in the bone against her mouth and the breath going out of her.

So who is the music for?

Consider what the fog is. It is the cloud-sea breathing. The sun heats the slopes below, the air lifts, the cloud rises, and the shelf takes it in through a single V-shaped mouth in its rim. For an hour the hollow is full of the world's breath. Then it drains back out the same way it came. It is an inhalation and an exhalation, repeated once a day.

The woman stands inside that breath and puts her own breath into it through a hollow bone. She is not performing. She is joining in. It is the same gesture the rosette makes when it opens, and that the water bear makes when it unfolds. She is doing the thing you do on this shelf when the fog arrives: you become alive, loudly and fully, for exactly as long as you are allowed.

## The Third Answer

So now the image can settle, not into a creature but into something more like a body.

The shelf is an animal.

It is a young one. The mountain it is pressed into has not finished becoming a mountain. The ridges are knife-sharp, the scree is unworn, and the headwall still sheds slabs every season, leaving scars so pale and clean they look like new skin. That is exactly what they are: skin that has not yet been colored. The lichen will come to them the way freckles come to a child, spot by spot over years, and you can read the shelf's age by its color the way you read a face.

It has a mouth, the notch, and it breathes through it once a day. Moss is its lungs, the spongy tissue where the breath is held and absorbed for hours after the exhalation. Its warmth is kept deep in the cushion plants, in hearts that frost cannot reach. It eats what the updraft brings: the fallout of insects on the snow patches, digested by springtails, beetles, and spiders, then passed up through finches to the one raptor turning at the top of the sky. Its metabolism has a pulse: rime at dawn, fog at mid-morning, glare at noon, insects in the afternoon, the freeze at dusk. When the fog fails to come, the whole body goes thirsty and slows. When it comes twice, the body has a good day.

It has cells that die and revive, the water bears in the black base of the moss. It has cells that hold still and wait, the finches in the cracks. It has cells that build walls and store food for winter, the hares. And once in a long while it receives a visitor who is not quite a cell and not quite a stranger, who came so often for so many years that she wore a stone to her shape. She taught the shelf a second way to breathe, through twisted hair and a trough cut into rock, and through three holes in a bone.

What lives on the Breathing Shelf is the breathing. Everything else lives inside it.

## The Last Resident

There is one more inhabitant, and it is the most numerous of all. It is born every night and dies every morning, and it is entirely made of the shelf's own exhaled breath.

After dusk, when the cold closes like a door, the rime begins to grow. Needle by needle, in the dark, it builds on every blade of grass, every stone edge, every strand of moss, the silver fur of the closed rosettes, the pebble walls of the hares, the cords of the net, the bleached cloth strips, the turf roof of the empty hut, and the smooth flat stone at the lip where no one is sitting tonight.

Each needle grows toward the wind and points toward where the air comes from, exactly as the hares face east before the light arrives and the rosettes open toward the sun. On this shelf, everything that lives turns to face its source.

By dawn there are billions of them, a white fur over the whole hollow. Then the light reaches the headwall and slides down, and the needles begin to fall with a faint ticking, like a clock or like counting. They drop into the moss and melt, and the water bears drink them, and something wakes.

Down below, the cloud-sea is already beginning to lift.
