# Who Lives in the Rising Country

## I. The first answer

The obvious answer is people, so I'll start with them. It isn't wrong. It's just the answer a visitor gives, and the country is not a place that rewards the first answer to anything.

There are perhaps four hundred of them, scattered along the high old terraces where the rivers have finished cutting and the ground has stopped surprising anyone. They call themselves steaders, after the houses. Nobody here would call themselves a *people*, because a people implies a territory, and territory implies a shape that holds still.

They are loud. That is what everyone notices first. A steader talking to you across a table speaks the way anyone else would shout across a field: short phrases, consonants hard, the ends of words cut off because the wind takes them anyway. They speak with their hands as much as with their mouths. It is a full sign-language, grown up alongside the spoken one over three or four generations, and it has grammar the spoken tongue lacks. There are signs for *the ground is about to jump*, for *the river has changed its voice*, for *which keel?* (a hand held flat, fingers splayed, then one finger pointing and tapped against the chest in a count that names it). A steader mid-conversation might sign one sentence and speak another at the same time, and both are meant.

They are broad and low, like their houses. They wear wool, then oiled skin over the wool, then a hood of undyed felt with the ear-flaps sewn thick and a pocket inside each flap for wadding. Rime grows on them if they stand still, so they don't stand still. There is a particular gait to them, a sort of testing walk, weight kept back until the forward foot has felt the ground. Moss here can hide a crack that opened in the night. A good steader walks as if every step is onto ice, even in summer, and does it without thinking. You can pick out a steader-born child in a foreign town by the way they cross an ordinary floor.

They eat fish, seal, goose, and eggs taken from the cliffs in the frantic weeks of summer, and reindeer when they can. They eat the crowberry and bilberry that grow in such thickets that a family can strip a terrace and barely dent it. They dry everything on racks in the katabatic wind, which is so dry and so cold that fish hung there in the evening is leather by morning. They drink water from springs that will not be there in ten years, and they know it, and every steading keeps a written list, scratched on a slate by the door, of the springs it has had.

## II. How they count

Here is where it starts to get strange, or rather where you begin to see that the people have been shaped by the country more than they have shaped it.

They do not really measure time in years. Years are too much alike: dark, then twilight, then a brief screaming summer, then dark. What distinguishes one stretch of life from another here is *where the sea was*.

So a steader will tell you: *I was born on the fourteenth ridge.* She means the beach ridge that was the living shoreline the winter she was born, now a line of crowberry and moss eight kilometres inland, and higher than the roof of her house. Her grandmother was born on the sixth, which is now among alder so thick you can only reach it along the stream. Her own daughter was born on the twenty-second, still bare gravel with a few mats of moss taking hold, still full of shells that are only just beginning to bleach.

Every family knows its ridges. You can walk inland from the sea across a family's history, and old people sometimes do. In the short summer they make a slow walk from the newest ridge to the oldest they can reach, stopping at each one to say who was born, who married, who died when that ridge was the edge of the world. They call it *going up the stair*.

They name the creeks as soon as they open. Every family has a right to name one fracture a year. If the crack becomes a creek, the name sticks; if it closes or silts up, the name is lost, and that is thought to be a small, forgivable bad luck, like dropping a spoon. Many of the names are the names of dead people. Some are jokes. One of the three great rivers, which changes course so often that it has had nine mouths in living memory, is called simply *Again*.

They don't keep old maps. The mapmakers of the old project left some, and a few families have them, framed or used as roof-lining. They are kept the way you'd keep a portrait of a great-grandparent, out of fondness and with no expectation of use.

## III. The dead

Then there are the dead, who also live here, in their way, and who are the steaders' strangest custom and their most sensible one.

The dead are buried at the sea's edge. It is always the newest ridge, the one still wet, where the gravel is loose and easy to dig and full of salt. They go into the ground wrapped in felt, with a flat stone over the face and a small cairn on top. Then the family goes home.

And then the country takes them up.

A hand's width a month. The sea withdraws. The ridge where the dead lie becomes the second-newest ridge, then the third. Within a few years moss has swallowed the cairn. Within a generation the grave is among willow, and the willow is thicker over the dead than anywhere else on the ridge, because the dead feed it. The steaders say the dead *walk inland*. They do not say it as metaphor. The oldest graves on the plain are up among the alder near the steadings themselves, forty or fifty metres above the water they were put down beside, and on the darkest nights of the polar winter it is considered correct to go out and stand by your grandparents, who have come home by the long way.

What this means is that the whole plain, seen right, is a slow procession. The dead are laid in a line at the sea's edge, each generation in its own line, and the lines climb inland one behind the other, and the living meet their dead coming up the stair. No steader has ever had to walk far to visit a grave. The trick is only to live long enough.

The children know this without being taught. A steader child playing on the newest shore will sometimes lie down on the fresh gravel, face up, arms at their sides, and say *I'm going inland*, and everyone laughs, and it is not a morbid joke. It is the most hopeful thing they know how to say.

## IV. The ones near the keels

That was still the first answer. People, and their dead, lodging on a rising stair. If you stopped there, you'd have a decent account of a hard northern folk, and you'd have missed nearly everything.

The steaders build on the old terraces, the story goes, because the rivers have stopped cutting there. That's true. But there's another reason, which they give less readily. The high terraces are *far from the keels*.

Every steader knows the dark circles. From the top of any old ridge you can see them in summer, the bruises in the vegetation, a few hundred metres across, each with its violet point at the centre. The old documents call what the keels leak *heat*. The steaders have their own word for it. The word translates, roughly, as *hurry*.

Everything near a keel is in a hurry.

Watch the voles. Out on a bare terrace, a vole lives the ordinary brief life of its kind: born, fattened, bred, eaten, a season or so. In a keel-circle they breed within days of being born. The moss there is not so much tunnelled as *pierced*, riddled through and through, heaving faintly on a still night with the movement underneath. The foxes that hunt the circles are sleek and enormous and short-lived. The owls nest in the tall willow there and raise clutches twice in the time it should take to raise one. Seal pups born on the gravel bar beside the drowned keel in the lake called Stillwater (a joke, of course; the lake is a flood-hollow and will drain within a decade) are said to be weaned in half the ordinary time.

It isn't warmth. Or it isn't only warmth, and the steaders knew it long before they had a word for it. A willow planted as a switch in a keel-circle is a tree in six years. A willow planted on the old terraces is still a switch. Peat that has no business being more than centimetres deep is metres deep near the keels, because a century's worth of moss has lived and died there in twenty years.

The keels are hurrying the rebound; that is their purpose. They are unclenching the deep rock so that ten thousand years of rising can happen in a few centuries. But nothing is that precise, and the hurry leaks. It comes up through the fractures and into the ground-water and into the moss and the roots and the voles and the birds. Near the keels, everything is being lived faster, because the land beneath it is.

And there are people who live there anyway.

The steaders call them *keelwards*, and they don't say it kindly or unkindly. It is simply what they are. There are only ever a few dozen. They build nothing permanent. They sleep in skin tents pitched against the warm flank of a keel, among the crowding animals and the thick moss and the tall willow, inside the pulse. They say the forage is best there, and it is. They say the snow melts first and the winter is shorter, and it is. They say they like the noise.

But the steaders know what happens to them. A woman who spent twelve winters beside the leaning keel came back to her mother's steading, and her mother, who had not seen her in those years, did not recognise her at first, because she looked like her own mother's sister, who was old. Keelwards are grey early. They are bent early. They are wise early, some people say. They die early and they die content, and when they die they are not taken down to the sea's edge like everyone else. They are buried in the circle, among the thick roots, where the hurry will have them back quickly.

There is something almost holy about them to the other steaders, and something unbearable. A child who says she wants to go keelward is not forbidden. But her family will sit with her through the whole of one polar night, all three months of it, before she is allowed to go. Most children change their minds in the dark. A few don't.

## V. The things in the cracks

Go closer. Go down.

When a fracture opens across a terrace in the night, sharp as a rifle shot or tearing for several seconds like a sheet, it fills with water almost at once. By morning it is a clean black line in the moss with water running at the bottom. That is what the steaders see.

But put your face down close to the edge of a crack that is only hours old, in the pewter noon twilight, and look into the water. It is not empty.

There are the ordinary things: amphipods, tiny pale shrimp that come up with the pressurised groundwater and are swept out of the sand boils in hundreds, and the water-bears, and the blind white worms that the steaders' children collect in jars. They are ordinary northern creatures, like all the animals here. There are simply a great many of them, and they arrive astonishingly fast.

And there is something else. It shows only in the fresh cracks, the ones that open where the keels have chosen, and it shows best in the dark. It is a faint violet thread, finer than hair, running along the bottom of the fracture in the direction of the nearest keel. Sometimes there are dozens of them, braided loosely like the rivers. Touch one and it breaks without feeling like anything. Take one up in a jar and by morning it has dimmed and it is only a grey filament, like fungus, or like a root.

The steaders have always called them *keel-roots*, meaning it half as a joke. The old engineers, whose records are so incomplete, seem to have had another name for them, or at least a number. The number turns up in the margins of documents about strain distribution, and about *preferred fracture paths*, and about *the lattice*.

Here is what the steaders believe, and what I've come to believe telling you about them. The keels do not merely push. They reach. Whatever they are doing to the deep rock, they are doing it along these threads, and the threads go down into the mantle and out through the crust and up into the fresh cracks, and that is how a keel chooses where the ground will break. Every fracture on the plain is a place where a keel has put down a root. Every creek with a dead grandmother's name is running in a channel that a keel drew.

And the moss grows along the threads. And the voles tunnel along the moss. And the salmon, coming up rivers that change course between one spawning and the next, somehow keep finding gravel. The steaders say the fish *follow the roots*. The birds, which feel the lifts before the people do, are feeling something travel along the lattice ahead of the lift, like a word passed down a line.

It is all, if you look at it closely enough, one thing. The keels are hurrying the land; the land is feeding the moss; the moss is feeding the voles; and through all of it the violet threads run, finer than hair, carrying the pulse.

## VI. The pulse

A person standing close to a keel can feel it working. It is a slow pressure against the chest, too deep to be heard, roughly every eleven seconds.

A person standing anywhere on the plain, if they are quiet and have lived here long enough, can feel it too, fainter, coming up through the ground.

A person born here has it in the body.

Steader midwives know this. When a steader child is born, it does not breathe in the ordinary way of newborns, quick and irregular. For the first hour or so, it does. Then it settles, and if you sit by the child in the dark and count, you will find that every so often, a little over ten breaths, it takes one deeper breath and holds it very slightly before letting go. The deeper breaths come about eleven seconds apart. They come, if you have a steady keel within a few kilometres and can press your palm flat to the floor, exactly with the pulse.

The steaders call it *catching*. They wait for it the way other peoples wait for the first cry. A child who does not catch within the first night is watched anxiously. A child who catches fast is said to be *well-rooted*, and some old women will quietly say that such a child should be told about the keelwards, when she is older.

It stays. Steader adults breathe with the pulse in sleep. Their hearts, some of them swear, keep a slower rhythm under the ordinary one, a long swell every eleven seconds like the swell under chop at sea. This is why the children who visit other places find them eerie and hard to sleep in. It isn't the silence that bothers them, though they notice that. They are lying in beds where the ground does not breathe, and some part of them keeps reaching down for a beat that isn't there, and finding nothing, and startling awake. A steader child in a southern city has to be given a clock that ticks, or a drum, or someone's hand pressed to her chest in the right rhythm, before she can sleep.

## VII. The two silences

The pulse has stopped twice in living memory. Everybody knows the stories, and the stories are where the second answer ends and the third begins.

**The first silence** was in the time of the steaders' great-grandparents, in deep winter, in the middle of the polar night. The old ones who remember it as children say they woke all at once, every child on the plain in the same moment, and did not know why, and lay in the dark trying to understand what had woken them. It was the absence. It was the way you wake when a clock stops.

Every keel on the plain had gone quiet. The violet lights still shone, but the pulse was gone, and no one alive had ever known it gone. People went out onto the terraces in the katabatic gale with lanterns and stood with their palms on the frozen moss and felt nothing. Nothing but the river. Nothing but the ice cracking. The birds on the cliffs had fallen silent, all million of them, which no one had ever heard before and no one has ever described without their voice changing.

The silence lasted, by most accounts, a night and part of a day.

And the ground lifted anyway.

That is the part the steaders tell slowly. Without any keel-song, without any warning, in the dark, a district of the plain jumped. A whole terrace between two rivers came up a hand's width all at once. The cracks that opened did not run in the long straight lines the keels drew. They ran in a spiral, a great curling lattice nearly two kilometres across, radiating from a point where there was no keel at all. When the pewter light came, people walked the spiral in fear, and at its centre they found only moss, very deep, very old for the plain, over a peat bed that had grown in no time, with the violet threads beneath it glowing more brightly than anyone had seen them glow, a knot of light like a heart.

Then the keels started again, and the birds, all at once. The spiral healed into creeks, and the creeks were named. One of them was named *Heard-It*, and that is still the name.

**The second silence** was sixty-odd years later. The oldest steaders alive now were young then. It was midsummer, in the frantic light, and it lasted barely an hour.

The keels stopped. The ground went still. The birds went up off the cliffs in a single cloud that darkened the midnight sun, and hung, and did not come down, screaming. People stood frozen on the terraces with their palms flat to the moss. Nothing. The river, the wind, the birds, and under them nothing at all
