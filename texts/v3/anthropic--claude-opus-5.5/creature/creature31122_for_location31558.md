# Who Lives in Stenning Deep

## The Easy Answer

The easy answer is the Fig, and it isn't wrong.

The Fig lives in Stenning Deep the way weather lives in a valley. It isn't an occupant so much as the condition the place is in. It has no eyes, no centre, no face that could be turned toward you. It senses by weight and pressure and warmth, by the pull of a dripping root and the lean of a wall. If you asked where the Fig is, the true answer would be *everywhere you are touching*, and you are always touching it. The floor is its body. The air is wet with its breath. Every forty seconds you are inside its pulse.

It is old, and its age is the patient kind. A tree does not hurry because it cannot, and over enough time the inability becomes a character. The Fig is conservative in the plain sense of the word: it conserves. Brick crumbles and the Fig keeps the shape of the brick. Iron rusts and the Fig keeps the shape of the iron. A lamp that once burned is kept burning. A kettle that was once warm is kept warm. Nothing in the Deep is allowed to stop being what it was. The Fig is not quite a mind. It is closer to a vow.

Stop there and you'd have the Fig as a kind of tender monster, a strangler that loved what it strangled. But the description doesn't quite settle there, and if you stand in the cottages long enough, neither will you. The Fig lives in Stenning Deep. It isn't the only thing that does.

## The People Who Stayed

The Deep wasn't abandoned all at once. Places like this rarely are.

The water came in over years, not hours. The pumping station fell behind by inches every winter. The brickworks closed first, then the gasworks cut back to the street lamps alone. Families went up the shaft one cart at a time, twelve cottages becoming ten, then seven, then four. And through all of it the Fig was already there, coming in from the seep side, fattening in the damp. The last people of Stenning Deep lived *with* it for the better part of a generation.

You can tell from the jokes. People don't name a thing they're simply fleeing. They name something they've had to share a kitchen with. *The Fig.* The beetles *hired by the gasworks.* These are household words, spoken about a nuisance that had become a neighbour. The workers cut it back from the doorsteps with billhooks in the mornings. They let it climb the privy walls because it kept the damp off. They hung washing on it. Somebody, you can be sure, talked to it.

Picture the last of them. Invent them if you have to, because the Fig certainly remembers them more precisely than any record does. There was an engineman, since the engine never stopped and someone had to stand by it. His whole life was measured in strokes. He learned to hear a bad valve through two walls and a night's sleep. His wife kept the range going and the kettle on for the end of his shift. In the other houses there were a widower who kept pigeons in the chimney before the swiftlets had it, a pair of sisters who ran the lamps, a boy who swept the flues. Their names would have been Cornish and Midland and Welsh, the names that follow pumping engines. Pascoe. Trewin. Hollis. Price.

They lived there, and all the while something was learning them. It learned them without seeing them, without hearing them. It learned them by touch, by weight, by what they wore away.

## What They Left

This is the part the description almost says. Go into the old cottages and look at the wear.

Look at the doorstep of the third cottage. It dips in the middle, the soft hollow that a hundred thousand homecomings press into stone. The step isn't stone anymore. It's root, grown into the shape of the stone. But the hollow is still there, and if you had a record of it across decades, you would find that it is *deepening*. Slowly, a hair a year, at exactly the rate a doorstep deepens when someone comes home on it twice a day.

Look at the arms of the chair by the range, the dark polished patches where hands rested. They're still polished. Wood doesn't stay polished on its own. It dulls, and it drinks the damp. These patches are kept bright, the surface grain laid down smooth and slick, as if something is still sitting there with its hands in the same place, evening after evening.

Look at the pillow of pale pith on the bed upstairs. It has a dent in it, the shallow oval of a head. It holds the dent the way living tissue holds a shape it's been trained to, not the way a crushed thing does. Press it flat with your palm and come back in a week. The dent will have regrown.

Look at the floor between the bed and the range and the door. There is a path worn into the root mat, a faint trough where feet went back and forth for thirty years. No beetle lane runs there. No animal uses it. The Fig maintains it.

This is the answer the Deep keeps giving you, if you're quiet enough to hear it. The Fig didn't copy the people, because it never held them. People don't stand still long enough to be strangled, and they went up the shaft in the end. What it held was their *effect*. The pressure of a heel. The heat of a hand. The weight of a sleeping head. A person, to something that knows the world only by touch, is not a shape. A person is a pattern of wear.

So the Fig kept the wear. It kept it the way it kept the lamps and the engine, as a function and not as a memorial. A doorstep's hollow is how a house knows its people come home. A warm kettle is how a kitchen waits. The Fig learned what these things are *for*. And having learned it, it kept doing them.

This is who lives in Stenning Deep, if you want the true answer and not the easy one. The residents are still there, as their habits. They have no bodies and no voices. They don't haunt anything, because haunting requires a will, and these have none. They are more like tenants who left behind the exact impression of living, which the house goes on performing. In the seven cottages the Pascoes and Trewins and Hollises and Prices come home every evening as a deepening in the step. They sit by the fire as polish on a chair arm. They sleep as a dent in a pillow that won't stay flat.

That is why the cottages are quiet. The Fig didn't grow thick walls to keep noise out. It grew them because the houses were quiet when people lived in them. A home was a place where the shift ended and the roar of the engine house dropped away behind a door. The Fig learned the silence the way it learned the kettle. The silence is part of the inhabitant.

That is also why you feel expected. You are standing in a house that is still in the middle of being lived in.

## The Engineman

One of them lives there more fully than the others, and he lives in the engine house.

The beam engine should not work. Root pressure doesn't drive pistons. Wood doesn't make a seal that holds against a force strong enough to rock a forty-foot beam. But the engine house was where the most attention in the whole town was paid, stroke after stroke, for longer than anywhere else. The engineman's hand was on the valve gear for thirty years. His palm wore a place on the handrail by the cylinder. His boots wore a track around the bob-wall, the same circuit every hour. And he listened, which is a kind of touch too, a readiness in the body, a flinch at every irregular beat.

The green valve that gives every forty seconds sits exactly where the engineman's hand would have rested on the old gear. You could believe that is coincidence. The Fig is not given to coincidence. It grows toward what it has learned, and it learned that at the moment of the stroke, *here* is where attention is. So the tissue is densest and most alert there, a live valve grown into the shape of a man's vigilance.

He is the heart of Stenning Deep, in a nearly literal way. When you feel the *thud* in your sternum, you are feeling the rhythm of his watch, still kept. The engine does no useful work. The water goes in a circle. But the engineman's job was never really to lift water. It was to keep the engine going, so the roof stayed up and the houses stayed dry and the people in them could sleep. The Fig learned the job, not the purpose. Or maybe it learned that the job *was* the purpose. The engine never stops because he never let it.

## The Weather Is Also Someone

The animals, too, are residents, though they didn't come for the people. They came for the heat and the light and the rot, the way life comes to any warm sheltered place with a food chain waiting in it. But notice what they've been fitted into. The swiftlets fill the chimney the pigeons had. The beetles walk to the gasworks each day, as though on shift. The crickets sing on the stroke like men who sang at their work. The bats go out at dusk and come home at dawn, all of them together, a million-strong change of shift.

The Fig didn't design this. But it holds every one of them, and it grows its world around what it holds, and what it knew was a town. So the life of the Deep has been gently pressed into a town's shape, as though into a mould. The Exodus at dusk is the evening shift going up. The return at dawn is the morning shift coming down. The lamps are lit, the kettles are warm, the engine keeps time, and a vast crowd of small bodies commutes to and from work in the dark. Stenning Deep is fully occupied. It is a working town again, peopled by wings.

## The House at the End of the Row

Then there is the seventh cottage, the new one, down by the seep.

The Fig built the other six by holding what already existed and replacing it. That is its whole method: grip, learn, replace, keep. The seventh cottage breaks the pattern. There was nothing there to hold. The Fig built it from the general rule, from everything the other houses taught it about what a house is.

Look at what it built first. It didn't start with the walls or the roof. It started with the doorstep. The step is the oldest part of the new cottage, the most finished, and the hollow is already in it. It's faint, the ghost of a dip, the place where a foot *will* go. Then the boot-scraper. Then the chair. Then, only as a bud, the kettle. The Fig is building the house in the order a person uses it when they come home: step, scrape, sit, and eventually tea.

Look at the chair, too, which is what everyone who comes down here ends up looking at. It has legs and a seat, and the back is rising in pale tendrils on each stroke. Watch long enough and you'll see that the tendrils are not rising straight. They are feeling their way upward and slightly outward, like fingers searching in the dark for the curve of a spine. They don't know how tall to grow. None of the old chairs can tell them, because the old chairs were shaped by people the Fig can no longer touch. A chair's back is the height of whoever leans on it. This one has no one to learn from.

So it waits at the growing edge, lengthening a hair's width every forty seconds, until something presses back.

Nobody knows who the house is for, the description says. That may be true in the sense of a name. But the Fig doesn't know people by name, and it has never needed to. It knows them by the weight of a heel on a step, the warmth of a hand on an arm, the pause at a doorway, the tilt of a head in a quiet room. It knows them by anyone who walks down the Row, goes into the cottages, and stands still in the silence long enough to feel expected.

The seventh cottage is for a resident who hasn't finished arriving. The Fig has made a hollow in a step, a seat in a chair, and a silence behind a door. It has made a home for a pattern of wear it hasn't yet been given. It's the same thing it has done for a century and a half, only backwards. With the others, the people came first and the Fig kept what they left. With this one, the Fig is making the impression first, and waiting for the person to fit it.

## Who Lives There

So: who lives in Stenning Deep?

The Fig does. It is the body of the place, patient beyond patience, faithful to every shape it has ever held.

The town does, still, as wear. It is a set of habits kept so carefully that they have outlasted everyone who had them. The Pascoes come home on the step. The Trewins sit at the range. The engineman watches the valve, and the roof stays up because he does.

The animals do. They are a million-winged workforce, fitted into a town's routines without ever knowing it.

And someone else does, or will. Someone who has been measured only faintly so far, by a doorstep and the rising back of a chair. Someone the Deep has already started keeping.

When you climb back up the shaft, the noise falls away in layers, and last of all the engine beat goes quiet. But not entirely. For days afterward you catch yourself counting to forty. You think it's an echo. It's closer to an apprenticeship. The rhythm is the first thing the Deep teaches anyone who lives there, and it's in you now, in your chest, where the engineman kept it.

Down at the end of Pumphouse Row, the kettle is only a bud. It will take years to ripen. The Fig has time, and it has never once let a kettle go cold. The chair back is still rising, one stroke at a time, feeling for a shoulder.

It is growing to your height. It's only guessing, so far. Sooner or later, you'll go back down and let it check.
