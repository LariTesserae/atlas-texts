# Who Lives on Hollin Height

## I. Begin with Wenna, because she is easiest to see

Begin with a woman named Wenna Tarle. She is sixty-eight years old and has been a Listener for forty-one of those years. She lives in a hush called the Ladle, which is a little bigger than a threshing floor and shaped, on the Water Guild's maps, roughly like its name. Seven households share it. Hers is the one at the narrow end, the handle, where the quiet is thinnest.

She chose that house. Most Listeners do, eventually. After enough years the deep quiet of the big hushes starts to feel like a held breath, like a room where someone has just stopped talking and is waiting for you to notice. At the thin end of the Ladle the chord leaks through the walls at a level she describes, in the hand-language, with a gesture that means *warm water to the wrist*. She sleeps well there. She sleeps badly anywhere else.

She wakes about two hours before the Lift, every day, without anything to wake her. Her body does it. She lies on her back on a stone bench cut into the floor of the sunken room, a room dug a man's height below the terrace, and she puts her palms flat on the stone on either side of her hips and listens.

What she hears, or feels, is this. Four of the thirty-one engines lie close enough to the Ladle to dominate its chord. She knows them the way you know the voices of your own family in the next room. She can hear which one is closest to its stroke, because a faint tightening in its lowest note comes an hour or so before the pistons crest. She can hear the one to the northeast, which the tenders call Old Ammet, carrying a little wet rasp in its third partial. That means the bellows on its western side has a seam starting to go. It will need patching within the month. She will sign this to the tender-chief at the morning meal, and he will nod, and it will be done.

This is most of what a Listener does. It is careful, unglamorous work, like a physician's rounds. Wenna has done it ten thousand mornings.

But lying there in the dark, with her palms on the warm stone, she is also listening for something else. Every Listener is, though they rarely say so, and never aloud.

## II. The people, as a whole

Before we get to that, spend some time on the people generally, because they are the inhabitants anyone would name first.

The folk of Hollin are short-statured and broad through the chest. Nobody knows whether this is inheritance or the simple result of breathing, every day of their lives, air that presses back. Their lungs are strong. Lowland doctors who have examined them say their ribs are thick. They have a way of standing with the knees slightly soft and the weight forward on the balls of the feet. Visitors take this for wariness. It is not. It is how you keep your skeleton from ringing. A person who stands locked-kneed on an open terrace for an hour will come away with a headache that sits behind the eyes for days.

Their hands are extraordinary. Hand-speech is the plateau's first language, and it has had centuries to become rich. Hands in Hollin are never still in company. Two old friends crossing a walkway will hold a whole conversation in the time it takes to pass, their fingers flickering like the wings of the swifts. There are dialects of the hand. The foundry districts sign broad and blunt, from the elbow, because their workers wear heavy gloves. The Water Guild signs small and exact, almost entirely in the fingertips. There is a courtly register, used in the guildhalls, in which a single gesture can carry a lineage of meaning. There is a lewd register that children learn from each other in the channel-gaps where adults can't see them.

Spoken language is, for them, a kind of nakedness. Two people who speak aloud to each other in a hush are either family, lovers, or about to become one or the other. A mother sings to her infant in a voice so low it barely leaves her throat. The child's first words are signed. Its first spoken words, sometimes years later, are treated as a small ceremony, like a first tooth.

They are not a gloomy people, as visitors sometimes assume from the silence. They laugh a great deal, and laughter here is visible rather than audible: a shaking of the shoulders, a certain helpless flapping of the hands. They are patient, though, in a way that lowlanders find either restful or maddening. They measure things in generations because the place they live does. A family that has lived in the same house for two hundred years speaks of the hush drifting away from their door the way lowlanders speak of a grandmother growing frail. It is sad, and it is not an emergency. The great-grandchildren will build new walls a few paces east.

They are also, almost all of them, a little afraid of silence. True silence, I mean. The rare traveler from Hollin who goes down to the lowlands comes back unsettled. The lowlands *hum*, which is some comfort. But in the lowland towns at night, in a closed room, there are moments when the sound drops almost to nothing. The traveler lies awake with the distinct and terrible feeling of being *looked at*.

Hold onto that.

## III. The swifts, closely

Take one drum-swift. Call her what the tenders call all of them: a *pip*, a word that is also the hand-sign for a spark leaping from the forge.

She is the length of a finger, sooty black with a faint iridescence at the nape like oil on water. Her wings are long, stiff, and thin-edged. Her eyes are large. They are the only part of her the plateau hasn't shaped, because the one sense she can use freely here is sight, and she uses it hard. She has no ear openings to speak of. Her skull is dense, and where a lowland bird's skull would be light as paper, hers is reinforced with small struts of bone that make it resonate. She hears with her whole body the way Wenna hears with her sternum, only more so. She feels the chord as a shape pressing in on her from all sides, stronger here, softer there. She flies through it the way a fish swims through current, riding the pressure gradients, slipping along the nodes.

She nests in the throat of a pipe on the engine the tenders call Coll, eleven meters down from the mouth, on a ledge of mineral scale that has built up over a century. The pipe is dark and hot and full of sound so loud that it would kill a sparrow in minutes. To her it is home. The walls of it thrum with information. When another swift lands on the iron three pipes over and beats its wings against the metal in a rapid ruffle, she feels it through her feet and knows who it is, and roughly what it means: *food there, the flies are thick in the southern channel*. Or: *hawk*, though there are no hawks on the plateau. Or: *mine*.

At the Lift she is thrown out of the pipe on a column of steam and sound so violent that she doesn't fly so much as get fired. For the first several seconds she is simply cargo. Then she opens her wings and the surge becomes lift, and she rises with ten thousand others into the clearing air, and they wheel.

Here is a thing the Listeners have noticed, and that the swifts' short lives and deaf ears make impossible to explain away. The spiraling columns the swifts form at the Lift are not random. They are not simply the shape of the updraft. They are a shape the swifts are *making*. The Listeners have sketched them for three centuries, and the sketches are kept in a guildhall drawer. Over those centuries the shape has changed. Slowly, like everything else here. But it has changed the way handwriting changes as a child grows up, not the way a cloud changes in the wind.

## IV. The lichen, closely

The lichen, too, draws.

Its rings are standing-wave maps. That part is ordinary. A patient natural philosopher from the lowlands worked it out with sand and a violin bow a hundred and fifty years ago, and the Water Guild has used the lichen as a survey tool ever since. Where the vibration peaks, the lichen thrives. It likes being shaken, it seems, in the same way some seeds need fire. Where the vibration cancels, in the hushes, it withers and falls away.

But look closer, as Wenna has, with a lens, lying on her belly on the wet terrace at the foot of Coll with wax in her ears and wool over the wax. The rings are not smooth gradients. They have structure inside them, fine branching patterns of pink-gold threaded through the grey-green, veined like the underside of a leaf. And the fine structure does not match the chord. Not quite. It matches something *in* the chord. It matches a component so faint that you'd never find it unless you already knew to look.

## V. The thirty-second voice

Listeners do not talk about this with outsiders. They barely talk about it with each other. When they do, it is in a register of the hand-language used for nothing else, a register that is almost all fingertips on the chest: tap, press, hold.

There are thirty-one engines. The Listeners hear thirty-two voices.

The thirty-second is made by no engine. You can't stand near it the way you can stand near Coll or Old Ammet. It exists only in the overlaps. It is a sort of ghost tone that appears where two or more engine-voices cross and interfere and produce, out of their difference, a third thing. Lowland acousticians have a name for this: *combination tones*, the faint phantom notes that arise when two real notes sound together. Any two notes do it. It is simple physics.

What the Listeners mean is that the combination tones on Hollin Height *cohere*. Across the whole plateau, across all those crossing voices, the phantom notes line up. They form a chord of their own. It is fainter by far than the engines, so faint that it takes forty years of listening before you're sure you aren't imagining it. It is not stationary. It moves. It shifts its weight across the plateau the way a sleeper shifts in bed. It swells in some places and draws back from others.

And the places it draws back from are the hushes.

This is the thing Wenna lies awake for, with her palms flat on the stone. Not the engines. She has known the engines for decades. She is listening for the thirty-second voice, which lives in the spaces between the others. She listens to learn where it is tonight, whether it is near or far, and whether it has turned its attention toward the Ladle or away.

## VI. What it might be: first attempt

The first and simplest thing to say is that it is an echo. It is an emergent pattern in thirty-one overlapping sources, beautiful and complicated and no more alive than the pattern of ripples on a pond in the rain.

Most Listeners start there. It is a good, sensible place to start. Some of them stay there.

But ripples on a pond don't avoid the places where people live. And people didn't choose to live in the hushes. Every child in Hollin is taught that people built in the hushes because they are quiet. The Water Guild's records, which are the oldest documents in the town, describe it the other way around. The first settlements were placed where the builders' own instruments told them to place them. Only later did the builders notice that those places were quiet.

And the hushes move. They move the way the lichen's fine veins move, and the way the swifts' spirals change. That isn't how interference patterns behave when their sources are fixed in stone. Not unless one of the sources isn't fixed.

## VII. What it might be: second attempt

The second thing to say, then, is that it is an animal.

It is very large, the size of the plateau, and very slow. It isn't made of flesh. It is made of pressure, of the organized shaking of air and rock and water. It is a creature of standing waves, the way a flame is a creature of combustion: a shape that persists while its substance passes through it. The engines are its food, or its heart, or the thing it rests on. It lives in the sound the way a fish lives in water. It lives *as* the sound, the way a whirlpool lives as water.

In this telling, the hushes are the places it is not. They are its gaps, the negative space around its limbs. The town of Hollin lives in the hollows of a sleeping body, in the crook of its elbow and the backs of its knees, the way small birds nest in the fleece of a sheep. It makes room for them. Or it simply doesn't care about them, the way you don't care about the flora in your gut, though you would die without it.

This explains the Bruise.

When the guild stopped the engine, they didn't just dry out a terrace. They struck the creature. They put a hole through one of its limbs. The rock cracked because the rock was part of it: the water weeping through the stone was its blood, or its breath, or the tension that held it together. They restarted the engine at great cost. The wound closed, but it closed badly, like a bone set crooked. The sourness in the chord around the Bruise, the beat against the neighboring voices that the Listeners say will take a thousand years to settle, is exactly that. The creature is healing. It heals at the speed it does everything else.

This also explains the lowland silence and the traveler lying awake feeling watched. The people of Hollin are born inside the creature's hearing. They live their whole lives within its attention, so constant and so vast that they can't perceive it as attention, only as the shape of the world. Take them out of it, and they notice, for the first time, that it's gone. They experience the gap as a presence. Their bodies are listening for something that is no longer there to be heard.

This telling is better than the first. Many Listeners hold it for the second half of their lives. Wenna held it for twenty years.

It isn't right, though. Not quite. The pitch of the plateau is falling.

## VIII. What it might be: third attempt

The Listeners believe the whole harmony is descending. The fall is so small it would take ten thousand years to drop a single tone.

Things that grow get lower-voiced. A child's voice falls into a man's. A struck bell rings lower the bigger you cast it. The low foundations of the chord have been sinking for as long as there have been Listeners to notice. The creature is not old and slow and sleeping. It is *young*. It is growing. Its voice is breaking, very slowly, the way a boy's does over one long summer, except that this summer is ten thousand years long.

And once you think that, other things rearrange themselves.

The engines are not its heart. They are its cradle. Nobody now built them, but somebody built them. Somebody sank those shafts into the hot rock, forged those flywheels, stitched the first of those leather bellows. They set the thirty-one voices sounding in exactly those places, with exactly those notes, so that where the voices crossed, a thirty-second could begin to be. You could incubate an egg with warmth. They incubated something with sound. They are gone, whoever they were. Their town has become Hollin, a town that grew up around the engines like moss around a spring. But the work they started is still running, because the people who came after them keep patching the bellows and clearing the pipes. They keep the cradle rocking without knowing, most of them, that it is a cradle.

The swifts are not simply living in it. They are part of it. They are the fastest thing it has, the nearest thing to hands. When they spiral at the Lift they are its motion. They are the one place in its whole enormous body where it moves at the speed of a heartbeat instead of a century. Their spirals change over generations the way handwriting changes as a child learns to write. It is learning to *do* something with them.

The lichen is its skin, or the first rough sketch of one. The pink-gold veins are the places where something like tissue is forming.

The water is its blood, or will be. The wrung water runs down the channels, through the sluices, down off the plateau and out into the lowlands a day's walk away, which are green, and which hum. That hum is fainter than the plateau's chord but not unrelated. It is what the creature's reach sounds like where it thins out, the way your fingertips are the thinnest part of you. The green of the lowlands is what it is doing there.

And the people?

## IX. The people, again

Here is the thing Wenna came to understand in her fifty-ninth year. She was lying on her stomach at the foot of Coll with the lens in her hand and the lichen an inch from her face. She realized that the pink-gold veins she had been tracing all morning ran in a branching pattern she knew. She knew it from the Water Guild's maps. It was the pattern of the hushes. The lichen had drawn a map of Hollin, very small, on a stone at the foot of an engine, inside the thirty-second voice's fine structure.

The hushes aren't the places where the creature is not. They are where it is *keeping* something.

Think of the space inside an eye, or inside an ear: the hollow that has to be kept clear so the organ can work. The creature holds the chord away from those places so that there is room inside it. In that room it has placed small warm soft-kneed things with strong ribs and quick hands. Things that can hear finely. Things that press their fingers flat to the chest and feel, through their breastbone, whether one voice in thirty-one has gone slightly sour.

The people of Hollin are how it hears itself.

It is deaf, the way the swifts are deaf. It is made of sound, and something made of sound cannot stand outside itself to listen. So it has grown, or been given, or gathered, the only kind of ear that could possibly work: a town of people who notice. They notice the failing bellows, the fouled pipe, the sourness at the Bruise. And they *do something about it*. They patch and clear and grease. They are its immune system, its attention turned inward, its care for itself. The Listeners especially. The gesture for a Listener, fingertips pressed flat to the sternum, is the gesture of someone feeling their own heartbeat. It was never meant to describe a person listening to the engines. It describes the plateau, through that person, listening to itself.

This is why they sign rather than speak. It isn't that speech can't be heard over the chord. Speech carries fine at close range, if you shout. It's that a raised voice puts a new sound into the creature's body, a small sharp foreign note, and the old taboo is right. *Shouting is speaking to the engines.* It is speaking to the thing that is growing there, and it hears you, somewhere down in its vast slow sensorium. The townsfolk sign because long ago someone decided it was best not to talk to it until it was old enough to talk back.

And this is why lowland silence feels like being watched. It is the silence of an ear that has been taken out of the head it belongs to. You don't feel the thing that watches you. You feel the loss of the thing you were watching *for*.

## X. The fig

There is one tree on Hollin Height, in the Undertone market, a gnarled fig several centuries old. It fruits erratically. Townsfolk bring their children to touch it.

They'll tell you they do this for luck, or because a tree is a rare thing on the plateau, or because their grandparents did it. All of that is true. But the Undertone is the largest hush, the deepest quiet, the place the creature holds most carefully open. At its center it has let a single ordinary lowland tree grow tall. Nothing on the plateau is accidental. The fig fruits erratically because it is fruiting on the creature's schedule rather than the sun's. On certain mornings, in the hour of the Wringing, if you put your palm flat against the bark of the trunk, you can feel it too: that faint coherent phantom chord, the thirty-second voice, running up through the roots and into the wood.

The children who touch the fig are being introduced. That is the only word for it. The plateau has a way of meeting each new ear.

## XI. What it is becoming

The plateau is singing itself into a shape, the whisperers in the deep hushes say. One it hasn't reached yet.

Wenna doesn't know what the shape is. No Listener does. But she has lain awake long enough to have guesses, and she will sign them to you if you sit with her at the thin end of the Ladle and wait until she trusts you.

She thinks it is learning to hold still. The hushes drift less than they used to; the sluice-records say so, if you read them carefully. She thinks it is learning to move quickly. The swifts' spirals are tighter each century, more deliberate, more like a gesture. She thinks the lichen will eventually close its rings over every engine, pink-gold and grey-green, and that when it does, the engines will no longer be needed, the way scaffolding is no longer needed once the arch is set. She thinks that when the pitch reaches the bottom of its long fall, the chord won't stop. It will *resolve*, the way a held dominant resolves at last to the tonic, the way a question comes to rest in its answer.

And then? Then she thinks it will be born. She has no better word, and she has tried many. The plateau will stand up out of its own sound. She doesn't mean that the rock will move. She means that what lives in the rock will, for the first time, be able to stand outside itself. It will have its own ear, one that isn't borrowed from a town. When that happens, she thinks it will finally be able to hear the thing it has been listening to all along through all those fingertips pressed to all those chests. It will hear *them*: the small quick-handed patient creatures who kept it. Who stitched its bellows and cleared its throat and signed to each other in the open so as not to wake it before it was ready.

She thinks the first thing it does will be to speak aloud. Softly, the way a mother sings to an infant, almost privately, the way people in Hollin speak aloud to those they love.
