# Who Lives in the Rootward Reach

## The First Answer: *We Do*

Ask anyone at the lip of the shaft who lives down there, and they'll say *we do*. They'll say it quietly, because they are already practising.

About nine thousand people live in Stave, depending on the season and on how you count the frontier crews who sleep in the rail cars. They are easy to recognise on the surface. They stand still when others fidget. They touch doorhandles with the back of a knuckle first, to let the static go. They carry small flat tins of middle-fraction balm and work it into their lips without thinking, the way surface people push their hair back. Their clothes are pressed felt and oiled canvas, soft grey and dun, and they smell faintly of pine resin and struck flint whether they've been down a week or a decade. Their voices sit low in the chest. When a lorry backfires in the surface town, every one of them stops talking, and their eyes go distant. They are counting.

Down, they are a people of schedules. Stave runs on the Lid's fourteen hours and the rail bells, and on the chalkboards at every platform where the day's crack-count is posted. Each crack is logged with its echo number and a guessed direction, in a column that goes back eleven years. Nobody is required to keep it. It is kept anyway.

Take Hesper Valk, who has been a tap-warden since the first year. She walks the copper lines along the root-mats with a listening horn clipped to a stake. She will tell you that the current has weather of its own. It thins before a pollen fall and thickens in the sweat. In the week after a child is born in one of the trunk-towers, it runs very slightly higher through that neighbourhood's taps. She has written this down and sent it up to the surface authority three times. She stopped expecting a reply years ago. She keeps writing it down anyway, in the same column-ruled books as the crack-counts, because down, that is what you do with things you don't understand. You record them patiently and wait to see what they turn into.

These are the people of the Reach: miners, refiners, rail-hands, wardens, clerks of a municipal authority that zones a valley which won't hold still. They live in hollowed trunks and felt-block rows and re-cut their windows every few years as the wood heals shut. They are tenants, and they know it. It is the first thing everyone down learns and the last thing they say aloud.

## The Second Answer: *The Born-Down*

The oldest child born in Stave is ten and a half. There are about six hundred of them now, and they are not quite like their parents.

It is nothing dramatic, and at first it was put down to upbringing. They don't shout. It isn't that they were taught not to. It simply never seems to occur to them. Their crying as infants is a thin, dry sound, like a leaf being turned. They wake with the Lid rather than before it. When the brightening moves up the valley, a born-down child opens their eyes at the moment it reaches their window, as though someone had touched their shoulder. They sleep deeply through crack-echoes that jolt their parents awake.

In the sweat, their skin takes a sheen. It is not sweat. The medical officer has sampled it. It is thin, clear, and faintly amber, and it smells of cold metal. It fades when the week ends. The children don't remark on it any more than surface children remark on getting sunburnt.

Their hair holds static the way everyone's does down here, but theirs lifts and leans, very gently, toward the nearest pillar-trunk. Mothers have watched a sleeping toddler's hair drift across the pillow over an hour as the child is carried from one side of the valley to the other.

When they're taken up to the surface, most of them are wretched within two days. The noise is part of it, but mostly it is the wet. Rain frightens them less than it bewilders them, the way a surface child might be bewildered by warm oil falling from the sky. They get headaches. They stop eating. Almost all of them ask to go home, and the word they use for home is *down*.

They play a game that none of their parents taught them. They call it *first*. A handful of them lie flat in the deep felt on their stomachs, arms out, ears pressed to the litter, and wait. When one of them feels a crack coming, they slap the felt. Then everyone listens for the sound, which arrives a few seconds later from the far end of the valley. Whoever slapped first wins. They are almost always right, and they are getting earlier. The best of them now call a crack twenty or thirty seconds before it comes.

They don't count echoes. Asked why, one eight-year-old said: *because you don't need to count where it is if you know where it is.*

About two years ago, a survey clerk named Ossery noticed that the born-down children draw maps. All children draw maps, but these showed the Reach with walls where no walls yet stood, trunks where there were no trunks, and a rail line strung between them. Ossery began collecting them. He now has more than four hundred, filed by date. Laid against the season-after surveys, the children's maps are wrong in the ordinary way children's maps are wrong: scale, proportion, a fondness for drawing cats on the cars. But the new walls are in the right places. Most of the new trunks are too. Ossery has started consulting them quietly when he plans survey routes. He hasn't told the authority. He isn't sure what he would say.

So the born-down live in the Reach as well, and they live in it differently. Their parents live *in* it. The children seem to live *with* it, the way you live with a house you grew up in. They know where the floor creaks before they step on it.

## The Third Answer: *The Ones With Oil for Blood*

Then there are the animals, and here it becomes harder to say who is a resident and who is furniture.

Nobody knows where the fauna came from. The founding crews found the first cavity already populated. There were hares in the litter, beetles on the trunks, and moths in slow drifts under a Lid that was then only two kilometres long. The obvious guess is that they came up with the plants from somewhere deeper. The less obvious guess, which is gaining ground among people who work at the front, is that they came up *as* the plants.

Cut a tap-beetle and it bleeds amber oil. Cut a pillar-trunk and it bleeds amber oil. Put the two side by side in a refinery test-vat and the fractions come out identical: the same light spirit, the same lubricant, the same black heavy base. Resin-moths are the same. Lattice-cats are the same. A felt-hare struck by a rail car smells, as it lies there, exactly like a crushed leaf of crust-scrub.

No one has seen a felt-hare give birth. No one has found a moth egg, or a beetle larva, or a kitten. The populations are simply larger each season, and the new animals always appear first at the growth front. They are found in their thousands on the pale, those soft white root-falls that pour out of the fresh stone. The frontier crews say the fauna *swarm* on the pale. A few of them, if you buy them a drink, will say that isn't quite the word. They'll say the animals *come off* it, the way fruit comes off a vine.

If that is true, then the animals of the Reach aren't really its inhabitants. They are its parts, moving about. The moths go to the tassels and the lantern-fern. The beetles go to the trunks, drink, and move on in herds that sound like rain. The hares graze the scrub and thump their soft signals through the felt. The cats hang in the lattice and drop, and they ride the rails. They learned the schedules in the first year. They sit on the benches of the open cars with their pale oil-slick sides pressed against the arms of commuters, and they get off at the same stops every day.

Watching them, it's difficult not to think of fingers. It's difficult not to think that something is using the cats to learn the shape of the rail line, the way you might trace a map with your hand in the dark.

## The Fourth Answer: *It*

Beneath all of it, holding all of it, is the one that actually lives here.

The surveyors confirmed it in the fourth year. They weren't looking for it, but the root-mats kept joining up. Every tap-line they laid eventually connected to every other tap-line through living tissue. Every pillar-trunk shared roots with its neighbours a kilometre off. The crust-scrub, which looks like a separate plant, grew up from rootlets that ran back to the same mats. The lantern-fern and the lattice did too. Even the Lid, three hundred metres overhead, fed down through the pillar-trunks into the same system that fed up from the stone.

There is one of it. It is twenty kilometres long and six across, and growing. What looks like a forest is the outside of a single body. That body lives by eating granite and slate, breathes out an electric current, and keeps a time nobody can find the source of.

It is not a mind in any sense a person would recognise. Or if it is, it thinks at the speed of wood healing around a window. Everything it does is slow. It takes seasons to decide, if deciding is the word, to thicken the lattice under a rail line. It takes years to push a pillar-trunk up another few metres and lift the roof a little further from the floor. It hears footsteps through the felt. It feels the rail vibration in its lattice. It tastes the copper taps in its roots and grows faster where they are. Where people gather, it thickens around them. It has been doing this for eleven years with the patience of stone. Its body is mostly stone, after all. Stone it has taken apart and made into itself.

Its hands, if it has hands, are the stonewhales.

People who work the front speak about the stonewhales differently from the rest of the fauna. They don't call them animals. They call them *the crew*, half as a joke. The stonewhales are blind and slow and slate-plated, as large as rail cars, and they do not come off the pale like the hares do. They were at the front when the front was two kilometres from the shaft. There have always been exactly nineteen of them. Nobody has seen more or fewer.

They follow the newest roots at a distance of a few metres, grinding the rock behind them to fine gravel. The roots split it and the whales finish it. Where they have passed, the stone is ready for the pale, and then for the scrub, and then for the fern and the lattice and the trunks. The frontier crews say it looks like farming. Some of the older ones have started to say it looks more like *building*: clearing a site, levelling the footing, laying out the rooms.

## The Fifth Answer: *Nobody Yet*

This is where the question turns over in your hand.

A root has no need of a room. Roots are happiest inside rock. They split it, they feed on it, they spread through it. A plant that eats stone would do perfectly well inside the stone, threading it with pale fibres and never opening a single hollow. There is no reason, in the plant's own economy, for the Reach to exist at all. There is no reason for a sky, or for daylight bright enough to read by. There is no reason for a lid held three hundred metres up by thousands of pillars that do nothing but hold it up.

A hollow is for something to live in. Light is for something with eyes.

The ecologists on the surface, the few who have read Hesper Valk's reports and Ossery's quiet memos, talk about succession. Crust-scrub arrives first on bare rock because it is hardy and can live anywhere. It makes the ground ready for the fern, and the fern for the lattice, and the lattice for the trunks. Each stage prepares the place for the next and is then partly replaced by it. The scrub isn't the point. It is the first step. Pioneer species, they're called, the first occupants of new ground.

It is possible, the ecologists say carefully, that Stave is a pioneer species.

The town arrived first on ground the Reach had just opened. Its taps taught the roots about current, and the roots grew toward them. Its rails taught the lattice where paths should go, and the lattice thickened to carry them. Its people's voices, pitched low, taught the felt what to muffle and what to let through. Its children were born into the smell of resin and the rhythm of the Lid, and they grew up leaning toward the trunks. In eleven years the town has been, without anyone intending it, a kind of teacher, or a sample, or a scaffold. And a room built with a scaffold is not built for the scaffold.

So the question the residents keep not asking aloud is not *what lives here*. It is *what is this being made ready for*.

## The Far End

Expeditions go out perhaps twice a year. They ride the rails to the last platform, walk a day past the last trunks, and cross the pale and the gravel fields where the stonewhales work. They go out to where the Lid thins to tassel-root without leaves and the daylight fades to a long grey dusk that never quite becomes night.

The reports are sparse, and they grow sparser the further out the expeditions go. People down have noticed that the members come back quieter even than when they left.

They report that it is warmer out there. Not much warmer, but steadily so. The heat that rises into the valley during the sweat seems to come from the far end, the way warmth comes from the far end of a house where a stove is lit. They report that the cracks, heard up close, are not really like rifle shots. They are rounder and deeper and more like a very large joint settling. They report that the stonewhales, out at the very edge, sometimes stop grinding. When they stop, all nineteen of them turn the same way, face into the dark, and lie still for hours, as though listening, or as though waiting to be told.

One report, filed in the ninth year, includes a sentence that has been copied onto the underside of several platform chalkboards by people who say they don't believe it. It reads: *The Lid over the far end is not dark. It is unlit. There is a difference, and when you stand under it you can feel it. It is like standing in a room where the lamps are all in place and the wiring is all done and nobody has come in yet to turn them on.*

## So: Who Lives Here

The people live here, the nine thousand of them, in their hollowed trunks and felt-block rows. They are careful tenants in the front rooms of a house much larger than they are. They keep it warm and speak softly in its hallways, and they write down every sound it makes.

The born-down live here too, and more thoroughly. They are the first residents who were ever fitted to the place, and they are growing more fitted to it with every season. They are a bridge, perhaps, or an introduction, or simply children who know, the way children always know, which rooms of the house are theirs and which are being kept for someone else.

The animals live here, if they are separate enough from the plant to count as living anywhere. The plant lives here, vast and single and slow, eating stone and breathing current, and building. The stonewhales live here, the nineteen of them, working the front like a crew that has seen the drawings.

And there is whoever the drawings are for. Whoever the light is being laid on for, room by room, toward the far end where it isn't lit yet. Whoever the hollow is being opened for, when the plant had no need of a hollow at all. Nobody knows what they are. Nobody knows whether they are coming up from below, or growing out of the Reach itself, or are simply what the Reach will be when it has finished learning. It is possible they are already here in some unready form, lying under the pale or behind the last stone, the way a sleeper lies in a house before waking.

On evenings in Stave, when the Lid dims from the far end and the brightening goes out of the valley like a tide, the born-down children often go out into the scrub to play *first*. More and more of them now just lie there with their ears in the felt and don't slap the ground at all, even when the crack comes. The parents have asked them why.

The children give the same answer, and they give it gently, the way you tell someone a thing they ought to have known.

*Because it isn't for us to call,* they say. *We're just listening for when it's ready.*

Then they lie still in the dark, and the whole town goes quiet around them, as everyone down always is. The roots keep splitting the stone, and far off the Reach settles another joint into place, and the room goes on being made.
