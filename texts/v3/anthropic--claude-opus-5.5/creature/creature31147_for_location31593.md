# Those Who Keep the Low Hall

## First, the obvious answer

If you asked the room who lives in it, the room would answer *mussels*.

They are the first thing you see and the last thing you would ever count. They are the black glaze on everything that faces upstream, so continuous that the pilasters look lacquered. Up close the glaze resolves into shells, each shell a pair of hinged lips, each pair of lips slightly open, and in every gap a pale, frilled mantle that tastes the water as it goes by. There is no individual. There is a surface that eats.

They are the room's kidneys. The jet comes out of the penstock slot carrying everything the reservoir holds: pollen, dead zooplankton, the fine grey flour of eroded hillsides, and whatever the lake has been dissolving from the drowned town at its bottom. The mussels take most of it. Behind every mat is a thin shadow of clearer water, like the lee of a rock in a stream, and the minnows prefer to swim in those shadows without knowing why.

In the quiet season, when the current slackens, the mussels close. Millions of them do it within a few seconds of each other, and the sound of it, those latches, travels down the hall as a single dry rustle. They open again when the flow returns. They are the room's most precise instrument for measuring the flow. Nobody reads it.

That is the first answer, and it is true, but it is the answer of someone who has only looked at the walls.

## The ones who walk at night

When the lamp goes dark, the crayfish come out of the iron.

They live in the rust-blossoms, which is to say they live inside the ghosts of the ladder rungs. Each blossom has been hollowed by generations of them into a chamber the exact size of a crayfish. The shaft wall is a tenement: one tenant per rung, stacked a forearm apart all the way up into the green. They are pale because they spend their days in the dark, and large because nothing but the catfish eats them, and the catfish has learned that they are mostly shell.

Their molts lie all over the silt at the edges of the room: perfect, empty, translucent copies of themselves, down to the hairs on the antennae. The silt slowly buries them. If you could dig down through the floor, you would find it layered with crayfish-shaped absences, a stratigraphy of outgrown selves.

On the nights the lamp is dark, one of them climbs the wall above the turbine pit.

She is old. You can tell because her carapace has taken on a faint green tint that the others lack, and because she is the only one who makes the climb every time. She goes up the scarred concrete, over the bronze cage, and out onto the glass dome, still faintly warm from the last time it burned. Then she grazes. She works the circular lawn of algae in slow arcs with her mouthparts, the way someone polishes a window with a cloth. She does not eat all of it. She never has. She eats the outer ring and leaves the centre, and by morning the dome has a clear margin and a green heart. The filament shows through the margin.

It would be too much to say she cleans the lamp. She eats. But the lamp is still visible because she eats there, and the lawn does not choke the glass because she eats there, and when the flow rises and the filament brightens, its light reaches the room through a clean ring she made in the dark. The builders hung the lamp so that crews could see to work. The crews are gone. Something still keeps the glass clear.

When she dies, one of the others will make the climb. They have done this before. The green tint comes with the job.

## The ones who have seen the lake

The eels are the only inhabitants of the Low Hall who know there is anything beyond it.

They were not born here. Nothing in the room could have told you this, but they came from the sea. They arrived as glass eels, transparent threads no longer than a finger, having crossed an ocean and found a river and climbed that river past every obstacle. Then, somewhere, they found the leaking face of the dam and the thin, wet seams of it, and they kept climbing through places nothing else could fit. Some of them came into the reservoir that way, and some came down the penstock from the reservoir, and some found the hall from below, up the tailrace, against the outflow. Nobody has seen which way. They go into the penstock in the quiet season and they come out of it in the loud season, and between those two moments they are somewhere in the dark of the pipe that none of the other animals have ever entered.

In the loud season they hang in the jet. There may be thirty of them, and they hang there like the strings of an instrument, heads upstream, bodies rippling, holding station against the full force of the reservoir with a kind of effortlessness that the drum-fish, straining behind their pilasters, have never achieved. They eat what the mussels miss. They have been doing this for a very long time. Eels can live for decades in a place that suits them.

Then one autumn, without warning, a few of them change. Their eyes swell. Their bellies go silver. They stop eating. And they leave, not up the penstock this time but down and out, following the outflow toward the sea they have not seen since they were threads of glass, to spawn somewhere in deep water and die.

So the room is connected, by the thinnest possible filament, to an ocean. Every eel in the jet is a message that came up from the sea and will one day go back down to it. What it says, nobody knows. The eels do not bring anything back. They only arrive, and wait, and go.

## The ones who talk

The drum-fish are the room's voices.

They hold the spaces behind the pilasters, one fish to each alcove, and they defend those spaces with sound. They make the sound by drumming a muscle against the swim-bladder, and the result is a deep, round croak that the barrel ceiling catches and throws back doubled. In the evenings the whole hall fills with it, a slow call-and-answer from alcove to alcove down both long walls.

If you listened long enough, you would notice two things.

The first is that their calls are pitched to the machine. The runner's *whum* sits at a particular low frequency, and the drum-fish grunt either just above it or just below it, never on it, as though the note belongs to something else and they are careful not to claim it. When the reservoir rises and the runner speeds up, the note climbs, and over the course of the loud season the drum-fish slowly shift their voices upward to keep clear of it. When the lake falls, they come down again. Their whole language breathes with the dam.

The second is that they go quiet before the surge. Some minutes before the valve upstream trips, before the roar climbs and the lamp flares and the casing starts to scream, the evening chorus stops. It does not fade. It stops, one alcove after another, down the length of the hall, and in the silence left behind you can hear the shrimp crackling in the silt and the runner knocking in its worn bearing. Then the surge arrives.

No one knows what they hear. Something in the penstock changes pitch before the pressure reaches the room, perhaps, or the gate upstream groans, and the sound travels faster through the water than the water itself. Whatever it is, the drum-fish hear it, and they fall silent, and every other creature in the room has learned what that silence means. The minnows tighten. The crayfish back into their rust. The eels, if it is their season, turn their heads a few degrees into the flow.

The builders would have had a siren for this: a klaxon on the wall, a light, a warning before the gates opened. The siren is long gone. The warning is still given. It is given by fish.

## The one body

The shoal lives in the shaft-light, and it is not several hundred fish. It is one animal made of several hundred fish.

It has a shape, and the shape holds a memory. It has a characteristic curl near the edge of the light where the water from the penstock first begins to pull downward toward the pit. No individual minnow remembers why. The minnows at that edge are always new; the ones who were there before are gone. But the shoal bends away from that spot the way a hand pulls back from a stove it touched long ago, and the bend has been there longer than any fish in it has been alive.

The bend is shaped exactly like the reach of the catfish.

## The one in the pit

Under the spiral casing, in the dark at the bottom of the turbine pit, where the light from the shaft never reaches and the lamp's amber only arrives as a faint warmth on the silt, the catfish lives.

It is very large and very still. Its skin is the grey-brown of the silt, and its barbels lie out across the floor of the pit like cables, and it does not appear to do anything. It feels. The whole of its body is an ear. Every thump of the runner arrives through the bronze of the bearing, through the steel of the casing, through the water and into the lateral line that runs the length of its flank, and it has been receiving that thump for longer than any drum-fish has been singing. If the catfish has a sense of time, the runner is its clock. If it has a sense of self, the runner is part of it.

It eats rarely. A minnow misjudges the current at the edge of the shoal's bend, drifts down into the pull of the pit, and then there is a movement too fast to see and the minnow is not there. This happens perhaps once in a week. The rest of the time the catfish waits.

But there is one thing the catfish does that no other animal in the room does, and only in one season.

In the lowest weeks of late summer, when the lake has fallen as far as it falls and the runner has slowed to a turn every few seconds, there comes a point in some revolutions when the runner hesitates. It reaches the top of its stroke, the worn flat of its shaft settling against the worn flat of its bearing, and for a moment it does not move. The whole room waits with it. The mussels are closed. The lamp is dark. The drum-fish do not call.

And the catfish comes up out of the pit.

It rises slowly, barbels trailing, until it hangs just beside the runner in the lace of the rusted casing, its great flat head a hand's breadth from the bronze. It stays there, motionless, while the runner does not turn. It is listening, in whatever way a catfish listens. It is doing what the crews would have done: laying a hand on the housing, feeling for the vibration, waiting to hear whether the thing will go on.

Then the water behind the gate finds its purchase, the runner lurches forward, *whum*, and the catfish sinks back into the dark.

It has done this every late summer of its life. The runner has always turned again.

## The one no one sees

There is one more inhabitant of the Low Hall, and it is not an animal.

Inside the glass dome of the lamp, around the filament, there is air.

It is not much, only the volume of the dome, a few cupped handfuls. But it is the only air in the room, and it has been sealed in there since the day the fitting was gasketed shut, which was before the hall was flooded, before the powerhouse was abandoned, before the town was drowned, before anyone who spoke the language on the brass plate had stopped speaking it. It is the builders' air. It is the atmosphere of the valley on a particular working day long ago, a day when the concrete was fresh and the rungs were iron and people came down the ladder with their breath held in their lungs and tools in their hands.

The filament burns in it. That is what the air is for, to keep the carbon from the water, to let it glow instead of dissolving. When the flow is high, the filament heats the air and the air presses outward against the glass, and the dome is warm to the crayfish's touch, and the green lawn grows on it because it is the only warm and lit thing in the dark. When the flow is low, the air cools and contracts and waits.

It is, in its way, breathing. It is the last breath the builders took down into the hall, still held.

## What lives here

So: who lives in the Low Hall?

The mussels live here, and they measure the flow. The crayfish live here, and one of them keeps the glass clear. The eels live here, and they carry the sea up the pipe and back. The drum-fish live here, and they give the warning before the surge. The shoal lives here, and it remembers the shape of danger in its body. The catfish lives here, and in the dry weeks it rises to listen at the machine. The builders' air lives here, in the lamp, around the filament, still held.

None of them know what the others are doing. None of them know what a crew is, or a shift, or maintenance, or a siren, or a lamp. But the room was built for people who would come down the shaft to watch the water, clean the light, listen to the bearing, and sound the alarm before the gates opened. Those people are gone. What lives in the Low Hall now is the shape they left behind, filled in by animals who fitted themselves into the noise and the current and the light. The room still wants its tasks done, and something is always doing them.

It is not a crew. It is closer to the way a riverbed keeps the shape of a river in a dry year. It is a vigil nobody chose to keep, kept anyway, in shells and scales and silence.

One late summer, the runner will hesitate at the top of its stroke and will not go on. The catfish will rise and hang beside the bronze and wait. It will wait a long time. The drum-fish will not call. The lamp will cool, the air inside it will settle, and the old crayfish, or whoever carries the green by then, will climb the wall in the dark and graze a clear ring around a filament that does not light again.

Then, slowly, each of them will find out, in its own way, that the room has finally gone quiet.

But not yet. Right now the eels are hanging in the jet. The drum-fish are calling, just above the note. *Whum.* The lamp warms to amber through a ring of clean glass. The shoal bends away from the dark, keeping a shape older than any fish in it. *Whum.* And under the casing, feeling every turn in its body, the oldest thing in the room lies still and listens.
