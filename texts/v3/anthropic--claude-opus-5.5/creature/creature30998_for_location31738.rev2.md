# Who Lives on the Grus Crown

## The First Thing You Would Notice

You would not hear them first. You would see a lamp.

In the pearl light of the afternoon cloud, walking up from the funicular with your ears still ringing from the silence of the ride, you would see a lamp come toward you along a catwalk twenty metres off. It would be dim, amber, swinging slightly. Only when it was close, ten metres, eight, would you see the person carrying it, and then all at once, the way corestones appear, as though they had been standing there the whole time and the fog had simply decided to show them to you.

The people of Kell are a pale people. Not unhealthy, exactly, but untouched. Most of them see direct sun for an hour a day at most, and in winter, when the dawn clear is late and short and often fails entirely, they may go weeks without a shadow. Their skin has the look of something kept wrapped. Their eyes are the part that surprises visitors. The pupils are wide, and they stay wide in the dimness, so that every face in Kell has a dark, attentive, slightly startled look, like someone listening hard to a sound you can't hear. In the clear hour they squint. Children born on the Crown cry the first few times they are carried out into the dawn, and many adults still turn their faces aside from it, or wear smoked spectacles on the rim, though they will tell you they love that hour more than anything.

They are deep-chested. The air up here is thin, and the body answers over generations. The oldest families have barrel ribs and slow, deep breath, and they can climb four flights of dripping stair without a pause while a lowlander stands gasping on the second landing. Their hands are grey. Kaolin gets into the creases of the knuckles and the beds of the nails and never quite washes out, so that even the town's clerks and schoolteachers, who haven't touched a clay trough in years, carry faint white lines in their palms like a map of rivers.

And their hair lifts. On the heavy days, when the charged cloud comes up, you can tell how much weather is coming by looking down a street. Every head in Kell rises into a faint soft halo. Nobody remarks on it. They smooth it down absently with wet palms, the way people elsewhere brush away a fly.

## The Way They Speak

They speak low, as the place demands, and they speak in weather.

No one in Kell says *morning*. They say *the clear*, or *before the tide*. The afternoon is *the pearl*, or just *in*, as in *we'll finish it in*, meaning under the cloud. A thin day is one with little charge; a heavy day is one when the hair lifts. The deepest part of the night, when the current sags and the lamps pulse, is *the ebb*, and it is the hour the town is most careful. People don't start things in the ebb. They don't argue. Babies born in the ebb are said to be quiet all their lives.

When they want to say someone is getting old, they say he's *going to grus*: still looks like stone, but press on him and he gives. It's affectionate. When they want to say something is lost for good, they say it *went over*, the way the scrapings go over, the way boulders go over, into the cloud.

They greet each other with a small lift of the lamp. Because a voice won't carry, the lamp does the talking. Raised high: I'm here, keep clear. Held low and still: stop, come close. Swung twice: trouble. There are older signals, more elaborate, that the pylon crews still use along the ranks of the Harp, lamp-codes flashed through the fog from pylon to pylon. Children learn them the way children elsewhere learn to whistle.

And they touch. It is a thing lowlanders find odd. In Kell, if you want someone's attention you don't call; you put a hand on their arm. A room full of Kell people talking looks like a room full of people consoling one another, hands on shoulders, on wrists, heads bent in close, because that is the only way to be heard.

## The Trades

Nearly everyone on the Crown belongs to one of a handful of crafts, and the crafts shape them almost as much as the weather does.

**The scrapers** are the town's oldest guild and its lowest, and they know it and do not care. Every street has a crew. They work in all hours, even the ebb, pushing long-handled iron blades along the lanes and steps, peeling the green back from the brick in soft wet rolls. Their arms are enormous and their shoulders are rounded forward. They are the ones who know the moss best, all dozen species of it, which grows back fastest, which one smells of pepper when it's cut, which one bleeds a faint rust stain that won't come out of anything. They cart the scrapings to the rim in barrows and tip them over, and they are the only people in Kell who regularly stand at the edge in the pearl, looking out at nothing. Many of them have a habit, when the barrow is empty, of standing a moment longer than they need to.

A scraper named Orla Tenn, forty years on the Weir Street crew, will tell you that you don't beat moss. You slow it. *It's like keeping a fire from going out, only backwards.* She is unmarried and lives alone at the top of a five-storey stack whose bottom two floors she has never seen, and on her windowsill she keeps a single cushion of the pale frosted moss in a porcelain dish, watered daily, unscraped. When asked why, she shrugs. *So it knows I don't hate it.*

**The wiremen and wirewomen** tend the Harp. They are the town's aristocracy in all but name: brave, a little vain, suntanned on the backs of their necks from working the pylons in the clear. They climb the lattice towers in harnesses of oiled leather and replace cracked insulators, scrape lichen beards from the strands, splice broken wire, clear the gutters. It is dangerous work, and not only for the height. On heavy days the Harp can kill. Every wireman carries a burn somewhere: a pale fern-shaped scar on the forearm, the back, the palm, where the charge once found them. They show these to each other the way sailors show tattoos. They are the ones who will not disturb a bird. They are also the ones who hear the Harp sing up close, and they claim they can tell the weather two days out by its chord.

**The clay people** are the most numerous. They are divided into castes so fine that outsiders can't follow them: diggers, who work the pits and shore the slumping walls; washers, who stand ankle-deep all day in the settling troughs and have white legs to the knee; pressers and agers, who work in the cellars and seem, to everyone else, to be a quieter, slower, more secretive people; throwers and moulders, who are the artists and know it. Each has its own saints, its own jokes, its own songs, sung very softly over the work. The throwers are the ones who invent the new patterns for cups and tile, and they argue about them for years. Every cup in Kell tells you when it was made, if you know how to read the foot ring.

**The kiln-keepers** live half in fire and half in fog. Their faces are red where everyone else's is pale. They sleep in shifts beside the kiln rows, where the brick is warm, and on winter nights half the town comes to sleep beside them, whole families bedded down along the kiln walls under the orange glow of the inspection ports. The kiln-keepers are the town's confessors in that sense. People talk in the warm. The keepers know more secrets than anyone on the Crown, and are known for keeping them, since keeping is their trade.

**The cable-house** is one family, and has been since the funicular was built. The Marrows wind the car up and let it down. They live in the winding-house itself, in rooms stacked around the great drum, and they have lived so long with the sound of the cable over the sheaves that they cannot sleep without it. When the funicular stops for repairs, the Marrows are said to lie awake all night. They are the only people in Kell who regularly go down, riding with the loads, and the only people who return with news, and so the town treats them with a certain wariness, the way you treat someone who has been somewhere you can't imagine.

## The Rooms

A household in Kell is a vertical thing.

A family lives in a stack, not a house: three or four rooms, one above the other, joined by a stair that also continues downward into floors nobody uses. The living rooms are always the top ones. Below, the rooms grow damper and darker the lower you go, and the family uses them the way you'd use a cellar: for storing, for aging clay, for cold meat, for the things you want kept and not looked at. Below those are the rooms you can still get into but don't. Below those, the sealed ones.

Most families have a granite wall. Somewhere in the stack, one side of a room is the flank of a corestone, rough and beaded and furred. It is the one surface in the house the scrapers never touch. Families let it green over. They hang things on it: lamps, porcelain, drawings. Children press their faces to it on heavy nights because the stone hums, faintly, through the moss. A great deal of Kell's domestic life is conducted with one hand resting on that wall. People will tell you it's the only part of their home that was there before them and will be there after, and that it's comforting to have one wall that doesn't need you.

Ownership in Kell is a strange idea. You own your rooms. You don't own the ground, because the ground is always coming up. The town's oldest laws say that a room belongs to whoever lives in it, and that when the moss and the silt take a floor, it passes back to the Crown. Deeds in the town registry record not addresses but levels: *third above the old Weir Street step*, *fifth over the corestone called the Leaning Sister*. The corestones have names, all of them, and they are the town's only fixed points. Everything else rises.

## The People Who Go Down

Most of Kell lives upward. A few live the other way.

They are called **the sinkers**, and they are not a guild or a family but a kind of calling. A sinker is someone who, at some point in their life, chooses the low floors. They move into the damp rooms at the bottom of the stacks, the rooms other families have given up, and they live there in lamplight and wet stone, and when the silt comes up over those, they move lower still, following the old stairwells down into the half-sealed floors where the air smells of iron and the walls weep. They clear passages through the packed moss. They know the buried streets. They know which stair in the Tallow quarter leads down through six floors into a room with a hearth still standing, and which crawlspace in the old market opens into a chamber where a whole table is set with porcelain of the first pattern, cups upside down on their saucers, waiting.

The sinkers are the ones who tend the dead.

Kell does not bury. There is nowhere to dig that isn't clay or grus or somebody's cellar. Instead, the dead are carried down. When someone dies, the sinkers come for them, and the family follows with lamps down the stairwells, past the storerooms, past the damp rooms, down into the lowest floor that can still be reached, and there the body is laid on a bench or a bed in a room that was once someone's kitchen or someone's parlour. A cup is left beside them, one they used. The family goes back up. The sinkers close the door.

And then the town does the burying itself. The moss grows. The scrapings fall. The silt rises. In a year or ten or thirty, that floor fills, and the dead are sealed in with the rooms they were laid in, kept in the cold, in the dark, in the slow green pressure, the way the old clocks and hearths are kept. When a pit crew breaks through into one of these rooms, as sometimes happens, they find the dead preserved almost past belief: skin gone brown and fine as paper, hair intact, the cup still beside them. The crews close the breach. They don't take the porcelain. It's the one thing on the Crown that isn't for sale.

So Kell is a town standing on its own ancestors, level upon level of them, and the people know it the way you know the floor is under you. Children in the high quarter are told, when they're afraid of the dark, that their great-grandmother is down there somewhere, holding the house up.

The sinkers themselves are a quiet, pale, faintly luminous people, paler even than the rest of Kell, their eyes so wide they seem all pupil. They come up for food and for the dead and otherwise not often. They are said to be able to hear the Crown in a way the upper town can't: a slow creak in the grus, a settling, a shift, the long, long motion of the mountain wearing itself away. Ask a sinker what's down there and they'll say *everything we've been*. Ask them what's below that and they'll say *the mountain*, and they'll say it the way you'd say the name of a person.

## The Neighbours

The people of Kell do not think of themselves as the only ones who live on the Crown. They are right.

**The pell** is what they call the slate-grey bird that nests in the insulators. The name is the sound it makes, or half of it: the call is two notes, a bright high *pe* and a falling *ell*, and in the muffled air it is so sudden and so clean that people stop in the street when they hear it. The pell is not afraid of the town. It is not tame, either. It moves along the wires in the fog, picking beetles from the lichen, and it looks at you with a round black eye that seems very old.

Nobody knows where it lived before the Harp. The geologists say there's no sign of it in the old records, which aren't very old. The wiremen say it was always here and simply waited, that it was a bird of bells and had to wait for someone to make the bells. The clay people, for their part, have started making insulators with a slightly deeper cup, a hair wider at the mouth, better for nesting. Nobody decided this. It just happened, over a generation, in the moulds. The pells took to them at once. There are now more pells in the Harp than anyone has ever counted, and on quiet nights you can hear them in the ranks, one calling and another answering, two notes and two notes, a long way off through the fog.

A pell nest is the one luck-charm everyone in Kell believes in. A pylon with a nest will not fall. A household whose window looks onto a nesting pylon will not lose a child. When a pell dies, and the wiremen find it, it is carried down with the dead.

**The slugs** come out in the ebb and cross the windowpanes, eating the lichen, and in the morning each window is traced with silver. Children read these the way children elsewhere read clouds. Some houses leave a lamp in the window for them, to see the trails better. The slugs are clear enough that the lamplight passes through them, and you can see, inside each one, the faint dark thread of what it has eaten.

**The spiders** hang their webs between the corestones, and by the end of the night the webs are so full of water they sag like nets, and in the clear hour they glitter. The throwers copy them. Half the tile patterns in Kell are spiderwebs full of dew.

And then there is the one they don't name easily.

## The Still Ones

The shaggy grazer that comes up from the flanks has half a dozen names in Kell, which is how you know it matters. The wiremen call it the **greycoat**. The scrapers call it the **old sheep**, though it is nothing like a sheep. The children call it the **standing one**. The sinkers, when they speak of it at all, call it by a word that in the old Kell dialect meant something like *the unhurried*.

It comes on the thickest nights, the nights when the lamps barely carry at all, and it moves through the outer ranks of the Harp on long legs, slow, grazing the cushion plants. Its coat is moss-coloured, and people who have been close to it say this is not a manner of speaking. Its coat is moss. Or has moss in it, living, the frosted pale kind and the rust-red kind together, the way the old walls of the town are banded. It smells of wet wool and green. Water runs off it in beads.

When a lamp is raised, it stops. It stands. And the longer it stands, the more it becomes a boulder, until the person holding the lamp can no longer say which of the grey shapes in front of them is stone and which is the animal. Then they lower the lamp and it is gone, and in the morning there is nothing there but corestones and the soft, deep prints of its feet already filling with water.

Here is what the people of Kell teach their children, though they would laugh if you called it a belief: *keep moving*.

It is the first rule of the Crown. You scrape your step or it grows over. You shake out your shoes or the lichen takes them. You don't leave a cart standing a week. You don't sit on a cushion plant in the pearl for too long, lost in thought, because when you stand up you'll find the green has begun on the hem of your coat. This is practical advice in a place where the moss grows the way it grows. But it has a second meaning, and everyone knows it.

Because the old ones sometimes stop.

It happens to maybe one in a generation. Someone who has lived long on the Crown, usually a scraper, sometimes a wireman, once, in a story the kiln-keepers still tell, a Marrow of the cable-house, one day just stops moving. Not dying. Sitting. At a window, on a step, at the rim. They sit very still and they stop scraping. And the moss finds them. It starts at the shoes. It moves up the cuffs. Their families bring them food and they eat it, slowly. They answer if you put a hand on their arm. But their eyes have gone somewhere else, and within a season the green is at their shoulders, and they are breathing slow and deep like the Crown breathes, and they smell of wet wool and green.

And then, on a thick night, they get up and walk out into the Harp, and are not seen again.

Nobody in Kell says that they become the grazers. Nobody says they don't. The scrapers will tell you, if you're a lowlander and ask with enough respect, that there are more of the greycoats now than there were in their grandparents' time. The wiremen will tell you that one of them once stopped beneath a pylon and looked up at a wireman on the lattice, and the wireman swore to the end of his life that it lifted its head in the way his father used to. The sinkers won't tell you anything, but they leave cups at the edge of the Harp on the thickest nights, full of cistern water, and in the morning the cups are empty.

Orla Tenn, with her dish of unscraped moss on the windowsill, will tell you plainly: *There's nothing bad in it. The moss isn't cruel. It just wants to keep things. Same as us.*

## What Else Lives There

Ask the people of Kell who lives on the Crown and they'll list themselves, the pells, the slugs, the spiders, the greycoats, the dead. Ask them the question differently, ask them on a heavy night by a kiln wall, with their hands on the warm brick and their hair lifting, and some of them will give you a different answer.

The Crown lives there.

They don't mean it the way a lowlander would mean it, as a poetic thing. They mean it as a plain description. Something breathes the cloud in twice a day and out twice a day. Something hums when the charge draws across it. Something swallows houses and keeps them, keeps the dead in the dark, keeps the cups on the tables, preserves everything it takes. Something grows a coat of green on anything that will hold still. Something has a voice, the long unresolved chord of the Harp in wind, and something has a heartbeat, the pulse of the lamps in the ebb, slowing and swelling with the weather. Something made a bird of bells wait until someone made the bells.

The geologists say the Crown is nearly finished, that it is the oldest thing in the world and has almost nothing left to do but fall apart. The people of Kell agree. They also say it is the most alive thing they know. They don't see a contradiction. They've watched their grandparents go to grus. Old things soften. Old things let go. That isn't the same as dying. That is the Crown teaching them how to be what it is.

And what it is, they would tell you, is not only here.

Every crate that goes down the funicular carries a piece of it. The insulators are strung on lines across lowland plains that no one in Kell will ever see, holding wires that hum in lowland storms. Porcelain cups made from the Crown's clay sit on tables in cities. Tile made from its rotten heart lines kitchens and hospitals and bathhouses. The pells don't nest in those far insulators, as far as anyone knows. But the throwers keep making the cups a hair deeper, a hair wider at the mouth. Just in case.

The Crown, the people say, is leaving. It is going down into the world a crate at a time, and they are how it goes. They are its hands. That is why they're here. Not to dig it out, not to live on it, but to carry it, the way the moss carries the town, the way the silt carries the dead, the way the cloud carries water up the flanks and lays it on the wires.

## In the Clear

On a clear morning, if you stand at the rim in the hour before the tide, you will see them all at once.

You'll see the pale people of Kell come out onto the catwalks with their smoked spectacles and their grey hands, faces turned half away from the sun, breathing slow. You'll see the scrapers at the edge with their empty barrows, standing a moment too long. You'll see the wiremen already high on the lattice, tanned necks gleaming, the Harp glittering all around them with the night's water, and in the bells of the insulators the small grey heads of the pells turning to the light. You'll see, far out at the edge of the ranks, a few grey boulders you don't remember seeing yesterday.

Somewhere below your feet, in the dark, the sinkers are sleeping beside the dead. Somewhere below them, the first Kell lies whole, cups on its tables. Below that, hundreds of metres of stone that has already become sand but has not yet been told.

Then the grey wall rises over the edge. The light goes soft. A lamp is lifted on a catwalk, high: *I'm here*. Another answers. The dripping begins, and the patter becomes silence, and the town that lives inside the mountain that lives inside the cloud goes back in, and keeps moving, and keeps, as everything here keeps, what it has been given.
