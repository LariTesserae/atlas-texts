# What Lives in the Cleft

## First, what doesn't

It isn't an animal. The place is right about that. Nothing here has lungs or a pulse, and nothing startles. If you came looking for eyes in the dark between the boulders, you'd find none, because there are none.

But the cleft isn't empty either. You feel it the way you feel someone standing behind you in a dark room. You can't hear them breathe. What you notice is the air being displaced a little wrong.

## The first sign

The first sign is a boulder that should have fallen.

On the lower slope, below the fresh scar, a slab the size of a cart sits tilted on one corner over a void. When the wind surges, it rocks. You can feel it creak through the moss if you lay your palm flat against it. By any sense of weight and angle, it should have gone over months ago. It hasn't.

Put your hand into the gap beneath it, carefully, and you find the reason. The void isn't empty. It is crammed with root. Fern rhizomes, the long pale anchors of the dwarf trees, and the hair-fine holdfasts of the moss have grown down into the gap and across it and back up. They have knitted the slab to its neighbours in a web as dense as felt. The web is wet and slightly warm, warmer than the stone, and it is taut. When the wind pushes the slab, the web pulls back.

At first you think this is simply what the plants do: they grow into cracks, and roots bind soil. But the web under that slab has no business being so thick, so fast, in exactly that place. Rock-ferns don't choose where to root. This looks chosen.

## Not the moss

The easy thought is that the moss itself is alive in some larger way, a single spreading creature that sheets the walls. That's close, but it's wrong.

The moss is where it lives, the way a river lives in its bed. The thing itself is in the between: between root and root, between one boulder and the next, between a frond and the stone it presses against. It is the tension in the whole mesh. It is the pull that runs from the trunk of one elbowed tree, through the epiphytes on its branches, into the moss curtain, down the curtain's dripping fringe, into a crack, around a stone, and up into the thicket again. Lay out every living strand in the cleft end to end and you would still miss it, because it isn't in any one strand. It is in how they hold one another.

If it has a body, its body is a net. The net is spread across forty metres of breaking mountain, and it is always being torn and always being mended.

## What it does

It holds.

That is nearly the whole of it, but holding here is not a still thing. The cleft is opening. Every frost, every blast, every surge of wind pries it a little wider. The thing cannot stop that. It doesn't try. What it does is make the breaking slow. It turns the mountain's collapses into creaks, and it turns falls into slow slides that take a season instead of a second. Where a block would drop, it leans. Where a leaning block would topple, it lowers.

And where something breaks anyway, it pours in.

This is the part the place already told you: *growth races collapse*. The green floods the raw stone within a few years. What it didn't tell you is that the flooding is aimed. Lichen doesn't crust at random across the new scar. It arrives first along the fracture lines, the places where the next slab will part from the wall. Algae films the underside of overhangs before the tops. The first moss tufts sit in the joints between stones, not on their faces. Anything that looked closely enough would see a pattern like stitching.

So the scar on the north wall is not simply healing. It is being sutured.

## How it drinks

It has no mouth. It drinks the way everything here drinks, from the cloud. It drinks with every needle-tip and frond and moss-hair at once, and it drinks all the time.

But water doesn't stay in the cleft, and so it doesn't stay in the thing either. It arrives as fog, beads on the leaves, soaks into the moss, seeps out of the moss's lower edges, and vanishes into the cracks and down into the mountain. The thing holds water the way it holds stone: for a little while, then it lets go.

You could say the water is its blood, but blood circulates and comes back. This doesn't come back. The thing is less like a body with blood and more like a shape that water takes as it passes through. It lives in the moment of passing. Cut off the cloud for a week and the net would slacken. The moss would stiffen and pale. The roots would lose their grip, and the stones would begin, all at once, to fall.

It is made of borrowed water, and it knows the loan is always being called.

## How it senses

It lives in the dark, like everything here, and reads the place by touch, as you must.

Every strand of the net is a nerve of a kind, a fibre that tightens and slackens. When the wind surges it feels the whole cleft lean. When a stone shifts it feels the shift spread outward through the web, the way a spider feels a fly. It knows where every loose block is by how it pulls. It knows the voids by where nothing pulls at all.

The dripping is its clock. Ten thousand drops fall slightly out of step, and it feels each one land as a tiny slackening and re-tightening somewhere in the mesh. From the drips it knows how wet the cloud is and how thick, and it knows when the corona will come.

The corona is the only thing it sees. For most of its life it has no sight. Then the air frays around the wire, blue walks along the conductor, and for a fraction of a second the whole cleft lights up. The thing feels that light. Every leaf turned upward catches it at once.

So it knows the cleft as a series of stills, one frozen blue picture after another, separated by long stretches of touch. Between two flashes, a stone may have moved or a curtain torn. It compares one picture with the next and finds what changed.

That's why the leaves at the lower end tilt the way they do. Look up the next time the wire fries and you'll see it: fronds and rosettes and orchid-leaves angled toward the line, not toward the sky. The sky gives them nothing. The wire is the cleft's moon.

## The wire

The thing did not make the line, and cannot reach it. The wire hangs across the col, untouched and untouchable. It doesn't feed the thing, and it doesn't harm it. Still, the thing's whole life is arranged around it.

It grows toward the flickers. On the upper slopes, where the scar is still raw, the first colonisers climb faster toward the col, toward the line, than they spread sideways. When the wind makes the cable sing, the drone travels down the web. It hums through the moss curtains and trembles in the thin taut fibres between boulders. The thing lives inside that note.

If it could want anything, it might want the wire. But wanting may be too large a word. It is more like the way a plant bends toward a window: the lamp is there, and so it turns.

## The blasting

The thing feels the blasts before you do.

A charge fires deep in the tunnel face, and the pulse climbs through hundreds of metres of fractured rock. The whole net goes rigid for an instant. Every root clamps. Every strand draws tight. When the pulse arrives, the cleft jumps, but less than it would have. Stones that would have gone stay. The moss curtains shed their water in a single sudden downpour, which is the thing letting go of what it can't hold so it can keep hold of what it must.

It has learned the schedule. It counts the blasts, not in numbers, but the way a body learns a recurring pain and starts to brace before it comes. On a blast night the web is tighter in the hour before.

It also hears the grinding between blasts: the faint, deep mechanical sound, felt more than heard, of something boring through the mountain's gut. That sound is getting closer, and the thing knows this the way you'd know footsteps on a stair. It cannot move away. It doesn't have a way of moving. It can only hold.

## The scar

There is a question the place leaves open: was the fresh scar the mountain's own doing, or a charge set too large?

The thing may know. It was there. It would have felt the slab begin to let go. It would have felt its roots in the joints straining, and its lichen at the fracture pulling, as it held and held against a force far too large to hold. And at some point it would have had to choose, if choosing is what it does. It could keep its grip and be torn out with the slab. Or it could release and let the house-sized stone go, so that the rest of the net would stay rooted below.

It let go. That much is clear, because it's still here. The scar is the shape of what it couldn't keep.

And the stitching now climbing that white wound, the lichen along the fracture lines and the moss in the joints, is not only mending. It is also the thing reaching back up into the place where it lost part of itself.

## What it does to you

It does nothing to you, exactly. It doesn't notice you the way a creature would. But you are one more loose, heavy thing moving through its web, and it treats you as it treats every loose, heavy thing.

It holds you.

That's what the thicket is at the lower end: fronds pressing into your face, moss-furred branches closing around you, wet leaves on every side, no clear space anywhere. You can't push straight through. You bend and squeeze and crawl, and every time you try to take a step the green takes some of your weight. When you slip, you don't fall far. The roots under the ferns catch your leg in the void. The mesh has done this for every boulder in the cleft, and it does it for you without distinction.

Stand still long enough and you'll feel it: the slight pull of a hundred strands on your coat, your sleeves, your hair. It is the gentle, patient tension of something trying to keep you from sliding downhill. It isn't unkind. It isn't kind either. It is what the thing is.

## Its age

It is young, as young as the cleft. It came into being when the crack first opened, and it couldn't have existed before, because before there was only whole rock and nothing to hold together.

This makes it strange. Most things that live in a place are older than that place's latest changes. This one was born from the change, and it lives only in the change. If the cleft ever stopped opening, if the mountain settled and the rock rounded and a stream cut a clean channel down the floor, the thing would thin out. The net would relax into ordinary moss and ordinary roots. Nothing would be left to mend and nothing to hold.

So it is bound to its own wound. It needs the mountain to keep breaking, a little, forever. It needs the breaking to be slow enough to keep up with. That is the whole balance of its life: collapse fast enough to keep it necessary, and slow enough to keep it alive.

## What happens next

The tunnel is coming. The grinding grows louder each season. One night, perhaps not for years, perhaps sooner, a charge will fire close enough that the pulse won't be a jolt but a heave. The broken rock under the cleft will shift in one long slump, and the thing will clamp everything it has.

Maybe it will hold. Maybe the whole lower slope will slide a few metres and stop, bound up in root, and the thicket will be tilted and torn but still standing. Maybe it won't hold, and the cleft will go in one grinding rumble into whatever void the tunnel opens beneath it.

Even then, something may survive. It might be one slab with a web still on it, lodged on a new ledge, or a mat of moss clinging in a fresh crack. The cloud will still come and catch on the ridge. The wire will still fry blue over the col. And the net will start again. It will start small and aimed, at the fracture lines first and the joints before the faces, climbing toward the lamp.

## The pause

This is where it is most itself.

The wind builds in the col, roars, and stops. For a heartbeat the whole cleft hangs slack. The cable's note slides down and fades, the moss curtains stop swaying, and every strand in the web eases. Into that silence rush the drips, ten thousand of them out of step. In that moment you might almost hear it, not as a voice but as a creak. One tree trunk shifts against another. A root adjusts its grip on a stone. A long, slow settling runs the length of the cleft like a breath let out.

Then the corona frays and the light walks the wire. For a fraction of a second, every leaf in the cleft turns its face toward the lamp. The wind comes back, and the net draws tight again.

That's what lives here: the holding, mostly unseen, on a mountain that is breaking.
