# The Loom Tarn

## The Height

Start with the mountain. It is past any mountain you know, so high the air around its shoulders is thin enough that the sky reads nearly black even at noon. The sun is a hard white coin with no halo, and the stars never fully leave. Nothing here is warm. Wind reaches the summit rarely, and when it does it arrives exhausted, having climbed too far.

At the top, where a peak should be, there is a hole. An ancient caldera, its rim worn down over some unmeasurable span into a smooth, low lip, like the rim of a bowl handled for a thousand years. Inside the lip is water, and the water goes down a very long way.

This is the Loom Tarn. Its surface is still, dark, and pewter-colored under the black sky. Nothing stirs it. No stream feeds it and none drains it. Its level has not changed within any record, and there is no evidence that it ever has. It was full when it began and it has stayed full, holding the cold of the altitude like a held breath.

The surface is only the lid, though. The place itself is below.

## Going Down

Beneath the first few meters the light fails quickly. The thin, colorless sunlight bends, greens, and is gone. Past thirty meters the Tarn is dark, and it stays dark for the next two or three thousand meters to its floor. The darkness is not total, because life here glows faintly in places, but it is the dark of a closed room at night, where shapes are guessed rather than seen.

Water fills everything. And as you sink, you realize that the walls of the basin are not rock.

They are woven.

## The Weave

The entire interior of the caldera, walls, ledges, galleries, and the floor far below, is made of plant matter, braided, knotted, plaited, and laced into structure. There is no exposed stone anywhere. If stone exists, it lies under uncountable layers of weaving, and nothing that lives here has ever touched it.

The plant is a kind of waterweed the lower-slope peoples call *cordgrass of the deep*, though it is neither cord nor grass. It grows as long, pliant, dark-olive strands, flat as ribbon and tough as sinew, sometimes forty meters long before it thins to a hair. It needs almost no light. It lives on the cold, mineral-heavy water and on the slow rain of organic debris that drifts through the Tarn forever. Left alone, it would grow as a loose, floating tangle. It is not left alone.

Everywhere you look, it has been worked.

Along the walls, the strands have been pulled taut and laced into vast mats, some large enough to cover a village, curving with the basin's slope. Over these, newer strands are threaded in herringbone, basketweave, and twill, patterns layered on patterns. In places the mats pull away from the wall to form shelves, and the shelves hold chambers: hollow spheres of tight wickerwork, like the nests of weaverbirds grown to the size of houses, each anchored by dozens of braided cables to the wall above and below.

Between the walls, spanning open water hundreds of meters across, run bridges and stays: thick ropes of plaited weed, some as wide as a tree trunk, strung from one side of the basin to the other at many depths. They do not sag. They are tensioned, and they are old.

## The Age of the Weave

Nothing in the Loom Tarn is new, and this is the key to how it feels.

The deepest layers of weave have been compressed by the ones above them, and by the cold and the weight of the water, into something that is no longer fiber. Near the floor, the woven mats have become a dense, dark, resilient substance, somewhere between peat, leather, and stone. The patterns are still visible in it, fossilized, as if someone had pressed a basket into clay and the clay had outlived them both. You can run your hand over a wall and feel ridges of twill a hundred thousand years old.

Above that, the weaving is softer and more legible, but it too has aged. Every edge is rounded. Every knot is smoothed and swollen into a rounded nub. The great chambers have settled on their cables and slumped slightly, like old furniture that has taken the shape of its use. The bridges carry a patina, a faint velvet of growth over their braids, and their lines have eased from geometric to organic, the way a path worn across a lawn loses its corners.

Nothing is crumbling, though. Nothing is ruined. The Tarn is not decaying. It is *finished*: it reached the shape it was always going to take, and it keeps that shape.

## The Weavers

The Tarn is full of animals, and some of them do the weaving.

The principal builders are long, slender, eel-like creatures. They are gray-violet, about the length of a person's arm, with narrow heads and two pairs of small, dexterous fins behind their gills. Each fin ends in a cluster of stiff, finger-like rays. The lower-slope people call them *shuttles*, because that is what they look like at work: darting through the weave, pulling a strand behind them, tucking it, looping it back.

Shuttles do not build anything new. Nothing new has been built in the Tarn in longer than anyone can reckon. The shuttles *maintain*. Each individual tends a territory, a patch of wall, a stretch of bridge, a single chamber, and spends its life inspecting the weave inch by inch. Where a strand has died, they pull it out. Where a gap has opened, they thread in living weed from the loose growth that sprouts at the edges of every mat. Where a knot has loosened, they retie it, in exactly the pattern of the knot that was there.

They do this perfectly. That is the Tarn's strangest quality. The shuttles repair at precisely the rate of decay. Every dead fiber is replaced by a living one laid in the same place, in the same way. The structure turns over its material slowly, strand by strand, and yet stays exactly what it is. A chamber abandoned by one shuttle is taken up by another before a single thread has frayed past mending. The weave is a standing wave: always being rebuilt, never changing.

No one knows if the shuttles began the weave or inherited it. No one knows if they understand what they tend or simply follow the pattern in front of them. Probably the distinction means nothing at their scale.

## The Rest of the Life

Shuttles are only the most visible of the Tarn's inhabitants. The open water between the structures, which is most of the Tarn, is full of life, all of it spread thin across enormous volume.

**Lantern-drifts** hang motionless in the dark: pale, gelatinous bells the size of a head, trailing threads, each glowing a faint, sickly blue-white from a ring around its rim. They do not move. They hang at their chosen depth for years. From a distance, the water between the great bridges is dotted with them like a sparse, drowned constellation. They give the Tarn most of what little light it has.

**Drumfish** patrol the lower galleries in loose schools of a dozen or so: broad, deep-bodied fish, dark bronze, with swim bladders like kettledrums. They are the loudest things in the Tarn, and they are never silent.

**Knotmice** are small, round, crablike creatures that live in the tight interstices of the weave, grazing on the film of bacteria and algae that grows on the fibers. There are billions of them. Brush any wall and a haze of them lifts off and settles again.

**Veil rays** are huge, dark, and very slow, their wings spanning ten meters or more. They glide along the walls at great depth, filtering the drifting debris. Some of them may be as old as the oldest bridges. They seem to follow circuits, the same routes year after year. The lower-slope people say a ray's path is itself a kind of weave, laid in water instead of weed.

At the floor there are **tenders of the pressed dark**: pale, blind, many-legged things like enormous shrimp. They live on the compacted ancient weave, scraping at it with delicate mouths, eating the oldest layers of the Tarn a grain at a time. Their slow consumption is balanced exactly by the settling of the weave from above. The floor never rises and never falls.

## Sound

The Tarn is dark and cold, but it is not quiet. It is loud, constantly, with a sound that has not changed its character in all the time the place has existed.

Water carries sound well, and the Tarn is a closed bowl. Every noise reverberates, echoing off woven walls that absorb high frequencies and return the low ones. The result is a constant, layered drone, felt in the chest as much as heard.

The lowest layer is the drumfish, a deep, irregular, rolling *thrum-thrum-thrum* from the galleries below, never stopping, as if something enormous were breathing in its sleep.

Above that is the hum of the bridges. The great braided cables are under tension, and the currents stirred by rays and schools and the Tarn's own imperceptible turnover set them vibrating. Each one sings at its own pitch, a long, sustained, organ-like tone. There are hundreds of them. Together they make a chord that fills the Tarn, dissonant and strangely calm.

Threaded through that is the clicking of the shuttles: dry, rapid, percussive, the sound of fin rays plucking and testing strands, knots being pulled tight, fibers snapped free. Millions of shuttles, all working at once, produce a sound like rain on a tin roof that never lets up.

At the top of the range is the whisper of the knotmice, a faint, pervasive crackle, like frying or like static, that rises when anything disturbs the walls and fades when it passes.

None of these sounds varies much. The chord of the bridges never resolves. The drumfish never pause. The clicking never swells or drops. You can float in the dark of the Tarn and hear exactly what anyone would have heard ten thousand years ago.

## Scale and Emptiness

The Tarn is huge, several kilometers across and several deep, and its structures are spread through that volume sparsely. The weave covers every surface, but the *open* water, the space inside the bowl, is mostly empty. A great bridge might hang alone for half a kilometer in every direction. A cluster of chambers might cling to a wall with nothing but dark water beneath them for a thousand meters.

This is what gives the place its peculiar feeling: totally built, yet mostly void. Every surface is made, but the made things are islands in a vast, cold, dark volume, connected by lines of braided weed and the paths of slow rays. The life in it is abundant but scattered, a lantern-drift here, a drumfish school there, a shuttle every few meters along a wall that runs for kilometers.

## The Rules

The Tarn keeps its own laws, and it keeps them absolutely.

**Nothing enters and nothing leaves.** No stream feeds the Tarn. No fish swim in or out. The shuttles, drumfish, rays, and all the rest were born here, descended from animals born here, as far back as the weave goes.

**Nothing changes.** There are no seasons, no tides, and no storms. The temperature is a flat, unvarying cold just above freezing, the same at the surface as at the floor. The only light is the faint, steady glow of the lantern-drifts.

**Everything is repaired.** Every broken strand is replaced, every loose knot retied, every abandoned chamber reclaimed. The weave loses material constantly and gains it constantly, and its shape does not move.

**Everything is old.** The pressed dark of the floor is older than any memory. The bridges are ancient, the patterns in the walls are ancient, and the circuits of the rays are ancient. Even the newest strand, laid this moment by a shuttle's fin, is laid into a pattern older than mountains.

The people of the lower slopes rarely come up. When they do, they stand on the worn rim, look down into the pewter water under the black sky, and listen. On very still days, they say, you can hear it even from the surface: the drone of the bridges, the thrum of the drumfish, the endless clicking of the shuttles at their work. It comes up through the water faintly, the sound of something that finished becoming what it is a very long time ago and has kept itself exactly so ever since.
