# The Combed Mountain

## First Sight

You come over the last rise and there is no mountain left, only masonry.

The whole upper face of the massif, from the saddle where you stand to a ridgeline perhaps two hours' walk distant, has been built over. It isn't built *on*; it's built *over*, so thoroughly that nowhere can you find a patch of the rock it once was. Terraces climb the slope in tight shelves, each one a row of shallow basins no wider than a man is tall. The basins press against each other like cells in a comb. Their rims are shared, one wall serving two pools. Between them run channels a hand's width across, cut into the rim-tops, so the whole slope is a lattice of standing water held in stone and threaded with small moving water.

It is very bright. You are above the cloud. The sun here is thin and white and has nothing to soften it, and it strikes ten thousand still surfaces at once. The slope does not glitter so much as it *glares*, a single sheet of light broken into a grid. You have to look at it through narrowed eyes, or look away at the sky, which is so deep a blue it seems almost dark by comparison.

Below the saddle, the cloud lies flat and white like a second floor. The built slope descends into it and does not come out.

## The Stone

Everything is made of a grey-blue slate that splits in clean planes, and of a white quartzite used for rims, sills and anything meant to be seen.

None of it is weathered. Every edge is sharp enough to cut a careless palm. The split faces of the slate still show the faint ripple of the break, the fine stepped lines where the stone parted along its own grain. Tool marks are everywhere: rows of small pecked hollows where a harder stone was struck, again and again, against a softer one. There is no metal mark anywhere, no clean chisel line, no drill spiral. This was done with stone against stone, with patience in place of edge.

The walls are dry-laid, with no mortar. Each piece is cut to key into its neighbours, a tongue into a notch, a wedge-face against a wedge-face, so tightly that water does not leak between them. It *weeps* instead. Along every wall face the joints darken and bead, and the beads run in thin lines down the stone, so that each wall looks as though it has been lightly drawn on in wet ink.

Nothing is loose. You can walk the whole morning and not find a pebble. Every fragment has been placed, and even the smallest chips are set as shims under larger stones. The ground underfoot, where there is ground at all, is paving: flags fitted edge to edge, sloped a finger's breadth toward the nearest channel.

## The Combs

Along the top of every seventh terrace, or so it seems, though you lose count, stand the combs.

They are thin fins of slate set upright in rows, each fin taller than you and no thicker than your hand, spaced a finger apart. From a distance they look like the teeth of enormous combs laid tooth-up across the mountain. Up close they are cold and dripping.

In the early morning the cloud below breathes upward. It rises in slow, soundless swells, pours over the lip of the lowest terraces and climbs the slope as a fog so fine you cannot see it until it is around you. When it passes through the combs it stays behind. Every fin furs over with droplets, then sheets with water, and the water runs down grooves pecked into each face. The grooves lead to a quartzite gutter at the base, the gutter to a channel, the channel to a basin.

By mid-morning the fog is gone. The sky is clear, the sun is up, and the combs stand wet and shining in their rows while the water they took from the air moves quietly downhill through the lattice for the rest of the day.

It is the only harvest the mountain has.

## Water

The water is very clear and very cold, cold enough to ache in the wrist. It tastes of almost nothing, faintly of stone.

It moves slowly. Each basin fills to the lip of its outflow notch, and only then does the next take its share. Nothing rushes. The channels carry a skin of water so thin it seems to lie on the stone rather than run through it. You notice it only as a trembling of reflected light. The whole terraced face drains downward at the pace of a slow breath, fed from above by the combs and emptied below into the cloud.

Some basins are deeper than others. Every few terraces there is a cistern, a square well-mouth with a quartzite coping, dropping into darkness. If you lean over one you see the sky reflected at the bottom, far down, a small blue square with your own head in it. The walls of the shafts are wet the whole way.

At night, in the cold hours, a skin of ice forms across the basins. The whole slope goes from mirror to milk-glass under the stars. In the morning the sun takes the ice back within an hour.

## The Unfinished Edge

At the ridgeline the building stops, but it does not end cleanly.

Here the last terrace is half-made. There are rims without basins, and basins without channels. Above them the living rock of the mountain finally shows, a raw grey face, and in it you can see how the work is done.

Along the rock face runs a line of small holes, each pecked by hand to the depth of a finger, spaced a palm apart. Some are dry. Some hold water. In some the rock around the hole has cracked, a hairline running from hole to hole, and in a few places a whole slab has come free and leans outward, ready to be lifted away.

The makers used the cold. They cut the holes, filled them with water, and let the night freeze it. Ice widens as it forms, and the rock opened along the line they had chosen. It is a slow tool, one night's push at a time, but at this height the nights are always cold enough.

Lying on a ledge beside the holes are the hammerstones: rounded cobbles of some dark, heavy rock that is not found anywhere else on the slope. Each has a worn flat on one side and a polished hollow on the other, shaped exactly to the ball of a thumb. There are perhaps twenty of them, set in a neat row.

The split faces of the freshest slabs are pale and unweathered. Frost dust lies in the cracks. It looks like work abandoned yesterday.

## The Absence

There is no one here.

There are no huts or hearths, no midden or bones, no footworn paths. The paving shows no wear at all. The hammerstones in their row have their thumb-hollows polished, but nothing else about them looks handled. You see no tracks, no hair, no insects, no birds overhead, nothing in the basins but water.

You look for the green that should be growing somewhere, and find almost none: no moss on the walls, no lichen on the combs. Only in the very deepest cisterns, when you lower your face close to the wet stone of the shaft, is there a faint, dark, velvet film where the light barely reaches. It is too thin to name and too slight to be sure of.

Over the day you begin to notice other things that don't fit. A basin you passed in the morning has, by evening, a channel notch that you are almost certain was not cut before. Its edge is sharp, and a small cone of stone dust lies in the water beneath it. On the unfinished terrace, a slab that was leaning is now lifted clear and laid flat, a pace from where it stood. You did not hear it move.

The work continues. It is just never seen continuing.

## Sound

It is quiet in the way high places are quiet: the air is thin and carries little.

What there is comes from the water. You hear a single drip from a comb-fin, then another farther off, then a scatter of them like the first slow rain. A lip of basin spills with the faintest *tsst*. Once or twice in the afternoon, from deep inside one of the cisterns, comes a single hollow note, a large drop landing on still water far down, rising up the shaft and then gone.

At night there is the ice. It does not crack loudly. It ticks: a small dry sound here, another there, across the whole slope, as the basins seal over one by one. It sounds like the mountain counting to itself.

Sometimes, just before dawn, from the direction of the ridge, you think you hear a sharper sound: stone struck on stone, twice. Then nothing for a long while.

## The Second Building

The water carries something out of the rock, and it leaves it behind.

Along every rim where the flow is steadiest, a white crust is forming. It is a mineral skin, thin as an eggshell, laid down grain by grain as the water spreads and the sun takes it. On the oldest-looking terraces, the lowest, those nearest the cloud, the crust has thickened into small travertine lips that overhang each basin's edge by a fingernail. In a few places it has begun to fill the joints between the slates, gluing the dry-laid walls together from outside.

The mountain is being built twice. First by hands, or something like hands, in cut stone. Second by water, in deposited stone, far slower, a hair's breadth in a lifetime, rounding every sharp edge the first builders left.

If you stand at the lowest terrace you can see where this leads. The basins that dip into the cloud are softened, almost fleshy, their rims swollen white, the grid blurring. Somewhere down there, lost in the fog, the terraces must stop looking made at all.

Up here, in the bright, everything is still new and sharp and wet, and the frost holes along the ridge are waiting for tonight's cold to open them a little further.
