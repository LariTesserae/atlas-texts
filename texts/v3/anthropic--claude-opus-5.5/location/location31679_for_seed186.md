# The Shelf of Standing Current

## The Height

The plateau sits so high that the sky above it has stopped being sky. Air here is a rumor, thin enough that the blue scattering of lower worlds never happens, so the heavens are black at every hour and the stars hold still, hard and unwinking, because there is too little atmosphere to make them shimmer. The plateau lies near the pole, and for most of the year the sun only skims below the southern rim. It leaves a bruise-colored seam along one edge of the horizon that brightens and fades like a slow pulse but never breaks. On the few weeks it does rise, it is a small white coin that throws shadows as sharp as cut paper and gives almost no warmth.

The Shelf is wide. From any rise you can see thirty or forty miles of it: long shallow swells of ground, basins, low ridges like the backs of buried animals, and at the far edge the sudden nothing where the plateau ends. Beyond that edge, miles below, lies the cloud sea. It is a floor of weather so far down that its storms look like frost forming on glass. Nothing on the Shelf is tall except a few isolated spires of old rock, worn round-shouldered and smooth by ages of wind and ice. They have stopped eroding in any way that matters. They have settled into their shapes the way an old face settles, and they will keep those shapes longer than anything alive will notice.

It is cold. The cold is not violent. It is steady, patient, and near the freezing line, and it rarely plunges. The ground is not frozen solid. It is a spongy black peat, cold and wet at the surface, and it gives underfoot. Your breath does not steam so much as vanish, because the dry thin air takes moisture almost before it forms.

And yet the Shelf is green. In the dark it is a deep, near-black green, but green.

## The Breathing

The moisture comes from below. Every few hours the Shelf "breathes." Pressure changes in the cloud sea push a tide of fog up the cliffs. It pours over the rim in slow white falls, like a waterfall running backward, and spreads across the plateau in a knee-deep layer. The fog is so cold it is half ice. It does not rain. It deposits. Everything it touches grows a fur of rime, and when the pressure reverses and the fog drains back over the edge, the rime begins to weep. For an hour after each breath the whole Shelf drips with a sound too faint to hear, and the peat drinks it all.

This is the only water. There are no rivers, only threads of meltwater that run a few hundred yards and sink. There are no lakes, only shallow black pools in the basins that fill after the breaths and empty through the peat within a day. The Shelf is dry air laid over wet ground and fed by a tide of cloud.

## The Green

The plants grow low, dense, and cushioned against the wind, and they are everywhere.

The most common is **wirewort**, a rounded mound plant the size of a sleeping dog. It is made of thousands of tight leaves, each with a single dark vein running down its center. The veins are copper. The plant pulls metal ions out of the peat and lays them down as living wire, and the wire carries charge. Wirewort cushions are faintly warm to the touch, a degree or two above the air, and if you set your palm against one in perfect stillness you can feel a prickle like a limb waking.

Between the cushions grows **creepmoss**, which holds static the way wool does. When the fog drains and the air turns dry again, the moss discharges. Slow blue coronas crawl across its surface like the ghost of fire, moving at a walking pace, splitting and rejoining, going out and relighting. On a dry night the basins are threaded with these wandering lights, and they are the brightest thing on the Shelf except the stars.

Along the fog-lines, where the breaths reach first and linger longest, stand the **rime-reeds**. These are pale hollow stalks, some twice the height of a person, grouped in stands of hundreds. They exist to comb the fog. Every stalk is ridged in fine spirals that catch ice, and the ice runs down the spirals to the roots. In the thin air they make no music, though the shape of them seems designed for it: open-topped tubes arranged like organ pipes, standing silent in a place too empty for sound.

## The Works

This is where the Shelf becomes strange.

Nothing here was built. There is no wall, no road, no made object anywhere on the plateau, and nothing suggests there ever was one. But the land behaves like a factory. The longer you look, the harder it is to see it any other way.

**The Fulgurite Fields.** Storms do not reach the Shelf, but their charge does. The cloud sea below builds enormous electrical potential, and the plateau is its highest point. Several times a season the tension breaks. The strike does not fall from the sky. It rises from the edge, a column of violet light lifting silently from the rim and forking inland across the ground. It follows the copper veins of the wirewort, and where it enters the soil it fuses sand into glass. Over thousands of years the plants have turned these strikes to use. Their roots grow into the fulgurite tubes and use the glass as conduits, and the tubes branch into networks. In places where the peat has worn thin you can see them exposed: grids of pale glass pipe, root-furred, running in parallel lines for a mile at a time, sorted as if by a surveyor. The strikes are drawn to them now. The fields are a lightning-harvest tended by nothing.

**The Sorting Belts.** Wind across the Shelf is weak but ceaseless, and it carries grit. Over the ages it has sorted the grit by weight into long stripes. Black magnetite runs in one band, garnet-red in another, pale quartz in a third, laid out in parallel belts across the open ground like the product of a mill. The belts are magnetized. They align the wandering coronas of the creepmoss, so on dry nights the blue lights travel in columns, all moving the same direction, like freight.

**The Stroke Vents.** In three basins the ground breathes on its own schedule. These are geothermal vents, but instead of steaming they pulse. They push a column of warm air and mineral vapor every eleven minutes, regular as a piston, then fall still. The rhythm has not varied in any way the land records. Around each vent the plants grow in precise concentric rings, each ring a different species, and each ring blooms in turn as the warmth passes outward. From above, a vent looks like an engine's flywheel carved in green.

**The Mill.** At the north end of the Shelf, a small tired glacier grinds endlessly against a floor of dark rock. It moves only a few feet a year, but it moves, and what it produces is rock-flour, pale and finer than dust. The flour drifts downslope in the breaths and settles into a long fan of mineral silt. Nearly everything on the Shelf eats from that fan, directly or indirectly.

**The Output.** The current does not stay. Charge gathered by the wirewort, drawn up through the fulgurite, and carried in the moss drifts slowly downhill toward the rim. There, on the stillest nights, it pours off the edge. Sheets of faint green-violet light hang over the drop like curtains and fall toward the cloud sea, flickering, for hours. It is an aurora that goes down instead of up. The Shelf produces something and ships it out, to whom or what no one could say.

## The Living

Life here is abundant, but it is spread thin across a vast emptiness. You can walk an hour and meet nothing, then crest a ridge and find a basin full.

**Lampbacks** graze the silt fan and the wirewort margins. They are low, wide, long-haired animals whose coats are so thick they look like walking cushions themselves. Their wool holds static, and when they brush against one another, which they do constantly in their slow crowded herds, their coats spark with a dim blue glow along the contact line. A grazing herd at night is a slow lake of lights that brighten and fade as the animals shift. They move in shifts. One herd grazes a stripe of silt for a set span of hours, then drifts off, and another arrives. The same stripe is never grazed by two herds at once, and none of them seems to decide this.

**Rime-moths** are the most numerous things on the Shelf. They hatch from the fog itself, or seem to. Their pupae hang in the rime-reeds, and each breath brings a hatch. At the height of a breath the fog is thick with them: pale, heavy-bodied, moving in slow spirals. Their wings carry a faint charge, and in their thousands they make the fog glimmer. They feed on the dew as the rime weeps, lay their eggs in the reeds, and die within days. The creepmoss is fertilized by their bodies.

**Stilt-hunters** move among the herds. They are pale, tall, and thin-legged. They cannot see well in the dark and do not need to, because they sense the electric fields of living things. They walk through the creepmoss lights without disturbing them. They hunt lampback calves, which carry less charge than the adults and so stand out as small cold shapes in a warm field. A stilt-hunter can stand motionless for most of a night, then take three long steps. That is the whole hunt.

There are birds, but they do not fly, because the air will not hold them. **Cinder-walkers** are small, round, and black-feathered, and they hurry over the ground in pairs, probing the peat for larvae. Their feathers hold warmth so well that frost does not form on them, so you see them as small dark holes moving through a field of rime.

There are snails, slow and shelled in metal. They graze the copper out of dead wirewort, so their shells are laid down in layers of green-bronze, and old snails are heavy and bright. When they die the shells sink into the peat, and in the deep layers, where the ground has been cut open by meltwater, there are seams of them, a coinage of millennia pressed in black.

## The Silence

The Shelf is almost perfectly quiet, and the quiet has a physical cause. The air is too thin to carry sound. A lampback herd moving across a basin makes no noise you can hear from fifty yards away. A lightning strike rising from the rim, bright enough to cast shadows for miles, arrives as a faint dry tick, like a fingernail on glass, several seconds late. The wind makes no sound because there is nothing dense enough for it to press against.

What remains are close sounds, the ones that happen within arm's reach: the crackle of creepmoss when a corona passes near your foot, the faint wet collapse of rime shedding from a reed, your own heartbeat, too loud, and the hiss of your blood in your ears. Standing still on the Shelf feels like standing inside a held breath.

## The Rules

A few things are true here that are not true elsewhere.

Current is a substance. It gathers, flows, pools, and drains the way water does in other places. Plants and animals are shaped by it as much as by cold or light.

Everything alive carries charge, and everything alive can feel the charge of everything else. Nothing on the Shelf is truly hidden. Predators and prey know exactly where each other are at all times, and survival is a matter of timing, never of concealment.

Order arises without anyone ordering it. The belts, the rings, the grazing shifts, the parallel glass: the Shelf tends toward the regular and the repeated, toward pattern that looks planned and is not.

The breaths do not stop. The vents do not skip a stroke. The mill does not pause. The Shelf is always working.

## Standing There

If you stood at the edge on a still night, you would see the curtains of light falling off the rim beneath your feet, dropping toward a storm-floor so far below it looks like frost. Behind you the basins would be full of the drifting blue of the moss and the herds, and the stars would stand motionless above you in a sky with no color at all. There would be no sound but your blood. And you would have the sense, impossible to shake, that you were standing in a vast working plant: a place of engines, conduits, mills, and output, all running, all green, all alive, all without an owner, producing something no one has ever collected, in the dark, forever.
