# The Held Sea of Ammaret

## The Rim

The mountain has no summit. You climb for days through thinning cloud-forest, past the last goat paths and scrub pines. You expect a peak. What you find is a lip of black glass, and beyond it a sea.

It fills the crown of the mountain the way water fills a cupped hand. It is miles across, and the far shore is lost in steam. No river feeds it and no river leaves it. It rises from beneath, from the mountain's own heat, and it sits there, high above the world, warm as blood.

The rim is young. Everyone who arrives notices this first, and they notice it through their feet. The stone underfoot has barely finished being stone. It is glossy basalt, ropy and wrinkled like cooled porridge, and in places still faintly warm. It has no soil, no lichen, no rounding of edges. The rock has not had time to be weathered. It has only had time to exist. Cracks are sharp enough to cut. Here and there a bubble in the glass has burst, leaving a hollow with a razor rim, and someone has placed a small stone inside each one, though no one remembers why.

Mist lies on the water all day. The sun at this altitude should be fierce, but it reaches the surface as a pale, even pewter glow, and below the surface it becomes something dimmer still.

## Going Under

The city is beneath the water.

You go down on a weighted rope of twisted weed-fiber, a stone the size of a child's head tied at the bottom. The water closes over you like a warm bath. It is the temperature of a body, so after a moment you can barely tell where you end. It carries a mineral taste: iron, faintly sulfur, something almost sweet. It is slightly thicker than ordinary water. You sink slowly, and your hair drifts around you like smoke.

The light fades as you descend, but it never goes fully dark. Somewhere around forty feet the growth begins, and the growth gives off its own light.

## The Weed Forests

The vegetation of the Held Sea grows on stone that is only decades old, sometimes only years. It has no soil to root in. It clings by pale, sucker-like holdfasts to the glass itself and draws its nourishment from the warm, mineral water that seeps upward through every crack.

**Tallstem** is the most common. It is a ribbon-weed that rises in columns sixty or eighty feet high and sways so slowly you have to watch a single frond for a minute to see it move. Its color is deep bronze-green. Its fronds are wide as a hand and patterned with fine veins that glow a faint amber where the water is warmest. Walking through a stand of tallstem is like moving through a cathedral of banners in a wind that almost isn't there.

**Lampmoss** carpets the flat terraces. It is short, dense, and velvet-soft, and it gives off a cold blue-white light that brightens when touched and fades over an hour. The paths of the city are laid in lampmoss, and you can trace where people have walked recently by the lingering glow of their footsteps, a map of the last hour's movement.

**Bellflower** grows only near the vents. It is a fleshy, cup-shaped thing that traps rising gas bubbles in its throat and slowly swells, until it detaches and floats upward like a pale balloon. It bursts at the surface with a small, wet sound. The inhabitants call this sound *the sea letting go*.

The forests are dense. The fronds brush your arms. The growth crowds every ledge, climbs every wall, and fills every gap between buildings. The sea is lush the way a hothouse is lush: warm, still, overgrown. Yet it is quiet, and it feels less like a jungle than a garden someone left alone for a long time.

## The City of Bells

The people of Ammaret live in air.

On the floor of the Held Sea, the mountain is still being born. From vents deep in the caldera, lava extrudes slowly, a finger's width a day. It meets the warm water and cools into pillows: round lobes of black rock, glassy-skinned, that stack and slump against each other like loaves in an oven. Sometimes a pillow cools with a hollow inside, a dome of stone with an open bottom. Gas rising from the vents catches in these hollows.

These are the bells, and the bells are the city.

There are thousands of them, clustered in terraces across the inner slopes of the caldera, some small enough for one sleeper, some as large as a meeting hall. They crowd together, wall against wall. Where natural bells were too few, the people made more. They chip and shape the cooling pillows with obsidian adzes and basalt hammers, coaxing hollows into the rock while it is still soft enough to work, and guiding the new stone into domes. This is delicate, patient labor. A mason of Ammaret might work a single bell for eleven years, returning each week to shape the new growth as it creeps upward.

You enter a bell from below. You swim up into it and your head breaks the surface into a pocket of warm, thin, faintly sour-smelling air. The floor of each bell is water, a still, glowing pool of the sea itself. The people sit on ledges carved around the inner walls, feet dangling in the water. They sleep in woven weed-hammocks slung between stone pegs. Their light is lampmoss grown in shallow trays, and they wake it by brushing it with their palms.

The bells connect to one another by **passages**: short submerged tunnels, sometimes only a body-length, that let people move from air to air with a single breath. In the dense heart of the city, you can travel a mile without ever being more than a few seconds from a breath. At the edges the gaps grow longer, and only the young and strong go there.

## The Breath-Carriers

The air in the bells does not last. It seeps slowly through hairline cracks in the stone, and the people breathe it down. Each bell must be refilled.

This work belongs to the **breath-carriers**, a guild whose members are respected, envied, and a little feared. They descend to the vents on the deep floor, where the new lava glows dull red through the water and the sea shimmers with heat. They carry bladders of cured eel-skin, open them over the rising streams of gas, and let them fill. Then they swim the bladders up through the forests and the passages and release them into the bells, one by one.

A breath-carrier knows the city by its air. They know which bells leak fast and which hold for months. They know the taste of vent-gas from different fissures, and some are said to prefer one vent over another, the way a drinker prefers a spring. Their lungs are enormous. Their skin is often mottled with small burns from the heat of the deep floor.

The gas from the vents is breathable but thin, and it carries a faint drowsiness. People in Ammaret move slowly, speak softly, and sleep a great deal. Visitors from the lowlands often feel as though they are dreaming the whole time they are there.

## The Quiet

Sound is strange in the Held Sea.

Underwater, there is almost nothing: the slow brush of fronds, the rare clack of a hammer on stone traveling for miles as a small clean *tock*, and the deep ticking and creaking of new rock as it cools and contracts. This last sound is constant and almost subliminal, like a house settling at night, forever. Children fall asleep to it.

Inside the bells, sound pools and softens. The air is close and the walls are curved, so voices come back slightly delayed and blurred. People speak in low murmurs. Some bells are known for their resonance. In these, singers hum single long notes that hang in the air and fade, and listeners in the adjacent bells hear them through the stone as a warm, faint vibration.

There is no music with rhythm in Ammaret. Rhythm belongs to things that are going somewhere. Here, songs are held tones: drones that swell and fade like the glow of lampmoss.

## The Rare Animals

Animal life in the Held Sea is scarce, and each creature is known.

**The lamp-eel** is the largest. It is long and slow, the color of wet slate, and it drifts through the tallstem forests grazing on fronds. There are perhaps a few hundred in the whole sea. They live for longer than people do, and the people of Ammaret know them individually. They give them names, track their paths, and mourn when one dies. Its skin, taken only from the dead, becomes breath-bladders.

**Glass snails** move across the newest rock, grazing a fine film of mineral growth from the surface. Their shells are translucent and faintly amber, and in the lampmoss light you can see their bodies pulsing slowly inside.

Occasionally a **diving bird** comes down from the misty surface above. It is black, long-necked, and silent, a creature from the world outside. It plunges into the upper forests after something no one has identified, then rises again and vanishes. The people consider its appearance an omen, but they disagree about what it means.

There are no fish. No one knows why.

## The Growing Floor

The floor is growing.

Everything in Ammaret is built on stone that did not exist a generation ago. The lava creeps. The pillows stack. Slowly, over lifetimes, the whole sea floor rises, and the city rises with it. The oldest bells lie deep in the rock now, sealed and buried, their openings closed by new flows. The living city occupies a thin, shifting band of the newest stone, always climbing upward.

The people measure time by this. A child's age is counted in *hands of stone*, the width of new rock laid down since their birth. The masons keep marker-stones set into certain walls, and the elders can read the history of the city in the layers beneath them: a flow that came fast in a hot year, a gap where the vents quieted for a decade.

The dead are not buried. They are carried to the deep floor, near the vents, and laid in the path of the slow lava. Over weeks the stone reaches them and closes over them. They become part of the floor, and then part of the foundation. The people of Ammaret say, without sorrow, that the city is built on its ancestors, and they mean it literally.

## What the Sea Is

No one knows why the sea sits on a mountain. The lowlanders say the mountain is a vessel someone is filling. The people of Ammaret say the mountain is still being made, and the sea is the water it was made in, the way a child is made in water. When the mountain is finished, they say, the sea will drain away, and the new world will stand in the open air, dry and complete.

They do not seem to fear this. It is very far off. The rock grows a finger's width a day, the city grows a few hands of stone per life, and the rim of the caldera is still hundreds of feet above the highest bells.

Until then, they live in the warm, dim, glowing water, in pockets of borrowed air, on stone that is still soft. They shape it by hand as it grows, and they wait without hurry for a world that is not yet finished.
