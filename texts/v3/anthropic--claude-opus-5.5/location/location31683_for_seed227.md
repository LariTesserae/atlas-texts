# The Throat

You come to it by the sound before anything else. From a quarter mile inland, across heath gone flat and brown with cold, there is a pressure in the air that the ear can't place. It isn't thunder, because it doesn't stop. It isn't wind, because the grass is barely moving. It comes up through the soles of the feet. Closer, the ground seems to *breathe*: a long inhale you feel more than hear, then a detonation, then a hiss that runs on and on.

The opening is small. It is a slot in a low hummock of turf and grey limestone, barely higher than the surrounding moor, no wider than a door turned on its side. Ferns crowd its lip. The turf around it is wet in a ring, beaded and dripping on a day when no rain has fallen, and the ring's edge is sharp, as if drawn. Past that line the ground is dry and wiry. Inside it everything glistens.

## The Chamber

Below the slot the world opens with shocking suddenness into a hollow. It is not a tunnel but a dome, a bubble in the rock, and your eye can't find its far side. The little light that falls through the slot runs down a single column of air, grey-green and full of drifting mist, and touches the floor forty feet below in a pale smudge. Everything outside that column is dark, deep and total. The volume is enormous for how hidden it is. The whole thing sits beneath a rise of moor you could walk over without breaking stride.

The roar lives here.

At the chamber's seaward end, somewhere in the black, is the throat: a passage worn by the sea through a quarter mile of rock, opening to the coast below the cliffs. It slopes up from the tide. When a wave enters the far mouth, it drives a piston of air ahead of it through the narrowing stone, and that air arrives in the chamber as a single enormous boom. It shoves at the mist and slaps against the ear hard enough to hurt. Then the wave falls back and the chamber inhales, drawing cold air down through the slot with a long, falling moan. Then another boom. On calm days the booms come maybe every twelve seconds, heavy and patient. In winter storms they merge into one continuous bellow, and the air in the column shudders visibly. Moss fronds stream toward the slot and then flatten back, over and over.

Nothing can be said aloud here. You can't hear your own shout. You feel your voice only as a buzz in the jaw. After an hour the body stops hearing the roar as sound and takes it as weather, a force pressing on the chest and eyelids. Hours after leaving, the ears still ring with it, and the quiet of the moor feels like a hand over the mouth.

## Stone

The rock has been here a very long time and has settled into what it is. Every surface the moving air touches is smoothed, not polished like river stone but softened, the way a step is worn by feet. Ridges run in long parallel flutes pointing toward the throat, the record of ten thousand years of exhalation. The dome's ceiling, where the light grazes it, is pocked with shallow scallops, each the size of a cupped palm, each holding a bead of water that swells, trembles with every boom, and falls.

That is most of the water here. There is no stream and no pool of any size. There is only the mist carried up from the sea and condensed on cold stone, a slow, endless sweat. A few hollows in the floor hold a finger's depth of it, fresh-tasting and faintly salt at the edges. The whole chamber is damp without being wet: a cellar, not a well.

It is cold, a steady cold that doesn't change much between summer and winter. The chamber holds its own temperature the way a body does. On frosted mornings the slot steams, and the moor above sees a thin white breath rising from the ground, pulsing.

## The Green

Where the light falls, and in a widening halo around it, the chamber is lush. It shouldn't surprise anyone, and it still does.

Moss grows thick enough to swallow a hand to the wrist. There are several kinds, layered: a coarse golden-green on the floor, a feathery dark species on the walls that combs itself in the draught, and a pale, almost silver cushion-moss in the scallops of the ceiling. Filmy ferns grow among them, the thin kind whose fronds are a single cell thick and translucent as wet paper. They can only live where the air never dries, and here it never does. Liverworts overlap on the lower walls like green roof tiles, and the booming has shaped them too: every lobe points the same way.

The green doesn't spread evenly. It follows the light down the walls and fades with it, grading into a band of brownish algae, then a bloom of whitish mineral crust, then bare wet stone, then dark. The chamber is a gradient from living to not, laid out on a curve of rock. The halo has grown outward over centuries, a few inches in a lifetime, and the old moss at its heart has built itself a sponge of peat nearly a foot deep, all its own dead.

Hart's-tongue fern crowds the slot itself, long straps of glossy leaf that shiver and rattle in the inhale. From below, against the light, they look black.

## Things That Live Here

Almost nothing, and that is part of the character of the place. There is space between presences here.

A pair of rock doves nest on a ledge high in the halo. They may be the same bloodline that nested there when the first people came. They go out through the slot at dawn and come back at dusk, and they don't seem to notice the noise at all.

A wren appears sometimes, working the moss for insects. It is a tiny brown thing, and its song is obviously very loud because its whole body shakes with it. Here it is completely silent.

In the dark part of the chamber are the long-legged cave spiders. They are pale, patient, and few, and their webs are strung across the flutes of stone where the air runs, catching whatever the wind brings in. Each web is set to the boom and bellies out with every breath.

There are small grey woodlice in the peat. There is a slug, very occasionally, fat and amber.

And once in a long while, at a spring tide, a seal comes up the throat on the surge as far as the water reaches, out of sight, down in the black. Nobody sees it. They know it by the prints and the smell of fish afterward on the lowest rock.

## Marks

People have been here, but not many, and not lately.

Cut into the landward wall below the slot is a stair. It is really just a series of notches pecked into the stone with harder stone, each one worn into a shallow bowl by feet and by the long patience of the damp. Moss has claimed the inner half of each step. A wooden peg, black and soft as cork, is still wedged in a crack halfway down. A rope once hung from it.

On the floor, at the edge of the light, is a ring of hearthstones. They are blackened on one side. The soot has held for a long time in air that never quite dries, and moss has grown up to the edge of the black and stopped, as if it had manners.

Deeper, at the very last reach of the grey light, three cairns stand. They are waist-high piles of flat stones set carefully, the top stone of each laid with its long axis pointing at the throat. They could be graves, or markers, or just the work of someone with a long time to wait. They are coated in the white mineral crust, which is slowly cementing them to the floor. In another few thousand years they will be rock.

Scratched on the wall beside the cairns, visible only if you bring your own flame, are marks: rows of short strokes in groups, hundreds of them. One reading is that they are a tally of days. Another is that they are a count of booms. A third is that they are a way of talking in a place where talk was impossible. Maybe someone came here and waited and counted and marked, and then left, or didn't.

Among the moss near the hearth, if you pull it back, are a few things: a bone needle, a shell bead, and a hooked flint scraper whose edge is still sharp.

## How It Moves

Nothing here happens fast except the sound.

The boom is the heartbeat, and under it are slower pulses. There is the tide, which changes the rhythm twice a day, from the lazy thud of the ebb to the rapid hammering of the flood. There is the season, with summer's slower breaths and winter's roaring. Under those is the growth of moss, the building of peat, the thickening of crust on the cairns, and the lengthening of the throat itself as the sea eats a little more rock each storm. Very slowly the throat is climbing toward the chamber, and the chamber is climbing toward the moor.

Someday the dome will fall in. The slot will become a pit, then a gorge, and the sea will come all the way up, and the boom will become a wave breaking in open air. It won't be soon. For now the place holds all of it at once in a pocket under the turf: the dark, the cold, the green halo, the patient drip, the scattered stones of whoever waited here, and a roar so large and constant that it has become a kind of silence of its own.
