# The Tableland of Held Breath

## The Ground

The plateau was a mountain range once, maybe several in sequence, each worn down and rebuilt and worn down again. What remains is the last stage of that wearing, a surface planed almost flat and lifted high into thin, warm air. It stretches in every direction farther than the eye can resolve. The horizon is less a line than a gradual failure of detail, where the ochre ground and the pale sky trade colors until neither is sure which it is.

Nothing here has an edge. The bedrock broke into boulders so long ago that the boulders have rounded into loaves, then into low domes, and finally into soft swellings of stone like the backs of sleepers under a blanket. Run a hand across one and it feels almost warm-waxy, polished by an age of grit and wind until the crystals inside show through as faint speckled constellations: rose quartz gone milky, feldspar gone to chalk. Between the domes lies a crust of coarse sand, the rock's own debris, and in the hollows that sand has settled into fine, pale flour that holds a footprint for years.

Here and there the old mountains still show themselves as tors, clusters of stone pillars stacked like worn coins. They are the roots of summits, the hardest parts, the last to go. From a distance they look like assemblies of figures. Up close they are mostly shelter, and they are never empty.

## The Air and the Water

The altitude should make the place cold, but the plateau sits under a low-latitude sun, and the stone drinks that heat all day and releases it all night. The air is thin, warm, and dry to the lungs. Breathing here is a conscious act. You take the air in the way you would sip something rare.

Water arrives from below rather than above. Each night the lowlands far beneath the plateau's rim exhale moisture, and before dawn it climbs the escarpments as a slow white fog. The fog does not roll. It rises, filling the hollows first, then lapping over the domes, until for an hour or two the tableland is a pale sea with stone islands and the tops of tors standing out of it. Then the sun finds it, and the fog condenses onto everything cooler than itself: stone, leaf, shell, wing, fur. For a short while the whole plateau beads. Every surface carries a skin of droplets, and the light through them turns the ground briefly jeweled. By mid-morning it is gone, pulled back into the air or into the bodies of the living.

Rain comes rarely, perhaps a few times a season, in warm heavy drops from clouds that seem to form out of nothing overhead. When it does, the flour-sand in the hollows darkens and swells, and for a day or two shallow pans of water lie in the lowest places, mirroring the sky so perfectly that the land looks perforated. These pans are the plateau's only open water, and they dry from the edges inward, leaving concentric rings of salt and silt like the growth lines of some flat, patient tree.

## What Grows

Very little grows here, and all of it is slow. Lichens are the main vegetation: rust-orange, sulfur-yellow, a grey-green like old copper. They spread across the domes in rosettes that may be older than any forest in the lowlands. Some rosettes have overlapped so many times that the rock beneath them wears a map of their disputes.

In the damp hollows there are cushion plants, tight, hard mounds of tiny leaves packed so densely you could sit on one and it would not dent. They are velvet-green on top and dead-brown within, living only on their outer skin and building themselves on their own remains. A few grow as large as a sleeping animal and are probably centuries old.

At the feet of the tors, where fog lingers longest in shadow, grow the only things tall enough to cast real shade: sparse, silver-haired shrubs with leaves turned edge-on to the sun. They grow in slanted postures, all leaning the same way. Nobody could say whether wind taught them to lean or the fog, arriving always from the same rim.

## What Lives

Then there are the animals, which is where the plateau stops being a desert and becomes something else.

At first glance the ground seems dappled, as if the sand had a pattern. On a second look the pattern is moving, very slowly. It is creatures, and they cover the tableland at a density that is hard to hold in the mind.

The most numerous are beetles, broad and flattened, their wing-cases ridged and dusty ochre so they match the sand almost exactly. They stand in the dawn fog with their backs tilted toward the rising moisture, and droplets gather along the ridges and roll down channels in their shells into their mouths. There are thousands in every hollow, millions across what the eye can see, standing nearly shell to shell, each in its own tilted posture of drinking. When the fog lifts they lower themselves and move off at a pace you would need an afternoon to notice.

On the domes lie lizards, stacked. They drape themselves over each other in layered heaps, sometimes five or six deep, a basking arrangement that seems to share warmth downward and shade upward. The lizards at the bottom of a heap are pressed against warm stone and do not move for days. The heaps shift through the day like slow tides, the top lizards sliding off into shade and the bottom ones climbing out toward light. A single dome can carry hundreds. They blink rarely, and when they do, it ripples across the heap like wind through grass that isn't there.

Snails cling to the tors in unbroken sheets. Their shells are thin and translucent, the color of weak tea, and in the early light, beaded with fog, a pillar covered in snails glows faintly amber, as if lit from inside. They graze the lichen and leave faint silver trails that crisscross the stone in ways that, seen from far enough back, look almost like writing.

Birds stand in lines, and this is one of the plateau's stranger habits. They are small and long-legged, grey as the domes, and they do not flock in the air. They gather on the ground in rows hundreds long, spaced a wingspan apart, all facing the same direction, and stand motionless for hours. Then, without any visible signal, the whole row turns a few degrees, and stands again. Over a day the lines rotate slowly around the plateau, as if tracking something below the horizon: the sun, the lowland weather, or something that doesn't show.

Above, sometimes, a broad-winged scavenger circles without flapping, riding the heat off the stone. Its shadow slides over the domes and the lizard heaps, but nothing below scatters. Either nothing here fears it, or the fear was used up long ago and never replaced.

Throughout it all there are the smaller presences: mites in the cushion plants, pale moths in the tors' shadows that rise only in the hour after the fog, ants moving in threads between the hollows. The whole surface of the plateau is animal. Anywhere you set a foot, you have to choose where.

## The Quiet

For all that life, the place is quiet.

Thin air carries sound poorly, and the creatures seem to have given sound up. The beetles do not click, the lizards do not hiss, and the standing birds do not call. What you hear, if you stay long enough, is a sort of texture rather than a noise: the soft friction of countless shells shifting against sand, the tick of stone cooling at dusk, the faint papery sigh when a lizard heap reorganizes. When the fog comes in there is a moment of true silence, a damping so complete that your own heartbeat becomes loud.

Occasionally, after rain, the drying pans make a sound. As the water pulls back, the salt crust cracks in tiny reports, and across the plateau these small pops arrive from every direction at once, sparse as the first drops of a shower. It is the loudest thing the land does.

## The Marks

There are structures here, though no tool made them.

Scattered across the tableland are low walls of stacked pebbles, curving arcs knee-high and sometimes a hundred paces long, open on one side. Their builder is probably one of the grazers, a slow, heavy-shelled creature seen only rarely, which seems to push stones as it feeds, nosing them aside to reach the flour-sand beneath. Over generations the pushed stones accumulate into these arcs. They are always open to the east, always curving the same way. Fog pools inside them longer than elsewhere, and the cushion plants grow thickest there. The builders may be farming, or the walls may simply be the shape of an old habit.

There are other marks that are harder to explain: rings of upright stones worn to stubs, older than the arcs and more precise. They are so ancient their stones have rounded like everything else, so they look less built than grown, like the fairy-rings mushrooms make, only in rock. The lizards favor them. Birds' rows, as they rotate, seem to align with them now and then, standing for a while along a ring's diameter, before turning away again.

There are termite chimneys too, tall and fluted and the color of the local sand, clustered near the tors. They are the tallest artificial things on the plateau, and some have outlasted their colonies and stand empty, eroding with the same patience as the mountains, becoming domes themselves.

## The Pace

Everything happens slowly. The fog is the only daily hurry. A cloud's shadow takes most of an afternoon to cross the visible land. A lizard heap reshapes itself over a day. The birds' rows complete their rotations over seasons. The cushion plants add a finger's width in a lifetime. The domes lose a grain here, a grain there, and the plateau as a whole lowers so gradually that no generation of any creature on it could perceive the loss, though they are all standing on what is left of summits.

This gives the place its particular strangeness. It is not empty, and it is not dead. It is full to crowding, every surface occupied and every hollow breathing, but it is full the way a held breath is full. The creatures seem to be waiting, and the land seems to have finished. Standing among the silent rows and the stacked sleepers with the fog beading on your sleeves, you begin to feel that the plateau reached its final form long ago and is now simply keeping it, with an unhurried care that includes you for as long as you stand still enough to belong.
