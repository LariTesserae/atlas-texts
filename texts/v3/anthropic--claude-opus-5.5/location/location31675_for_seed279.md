# The Chimney Fen

It is heard before it is seen. Coming down off the last ridge of black rock, still a mile or more away, you notice a pressure in the air before you recognize it as sound. It is a low, many-throated hum, like wind over a thousand bottle mouths, and under it runs a ceaseless glassy patter. By the time the ridge gives way to the lowland, the sound has become something you stand inside rather than listen to.

## The Ground

The basin is young. It is a stretch of seabed that rose within the memory of the old songs, and it still has the rawness of a thing just uncovered. Nothing here has been softened. The rock underfoot is dark, fine-grained basalt, broken into plates and ledges whose edges are still sharp enough to cut a palm. Where the plates meet there are cracks, and from the cracks water seeps up. It is faintly warm, faintly milky, and heavy with dissolved stone.

That water is what built the fen.

## The Chimneys

Wherever the seep meets cool air, the minerals come out of solution and harden. Over time each seep has grown a tube around itself, a hollow chimney of pale stone, and the chimneys stand everywhere. They are packed as close as reeds in a marsh, as trees in a forest too crowded to walk through, as bristles on a brush. Some reach only to the knee. Some rise three times a person's height, their tops lost in the mist. Most are the width of a forearm, though a few are wide enough to put your head inside, and some are thin as fingers, clustered in bundles.

They are not smooth. Because they are grown rather than worn, their surfaces are crusted with tiny crystal facets, frills, and ledges, the record of every slight change in the water's chemistry. Break one and you find rings inside, like the rings of a tree: pale bands and grey bands, thousands of them, each one a season. The chimneys are white where fresh, bone-yellow where older, and grey-green at the base where they stand in water. They lean at small angles, following the cracks, so that looking down any lane between them is like looking into a crowd of pale figures who have all just turned their heads.

There is no vegetation among them. No moss holds on the crystal, and no grass roots in the cracks. The chemistry is too harsh, or the land too young, or both. The fen is a forest in which every trunk is stone.

## The Water

Everything here is wet, but there is never much standing water. It lies in films and threads. The seeps well up at the chimney bases and spread into shallow channels a few fingers deep, milky and faintly steaming in the cold. The channels braid and rejoin across the basalt and eventually drain toward the sea, which lies somewhere past the southern edge of the fen, never quite visible.

The air is always close to saturation. Warm water rising into cool air makes a permanent low mist. It condenses on the chimneys and runs down their outsides in beads, then drips from every frill and ledge. It also condenses *inside* them, on the cooler upper walls, and falls back down the hollow tubes in single drops.

Every chimney, then, is always dripping, both inside and out. There are uncountable numbers of them, and this is where the sound begins.

## The Sound

Each chimney is a pipe, and each pipe has a pitch determined by its length and bore. A drop falling inside one strikes the water at the bottom with a plink that the tube amplifies and rings out as a note. Wind moving across the open tops adds a breathy hooting. The tall chimneys give deep, slow tones you feel in your sternum, and the finger-thin bundles chitter high and bright.

Multiply that by the chimneys in a single stretch of fen, then by the fen reaching to the horizon, and the result is overwhelming. There is a roar made of notes: a chord too dense to separate, always shimmering, always dripping. Up close, one chimney beside your ear can be picked out clearly, *tink . . . tonk . . . tink*. A few paces away it is swallowed into the mass.

It is loud enough that speech fails. People who come here learn to talk by touch: taps on the forearm, pressure on the shoulder, a grip on the wrist. Two travelers walking the lanes will keep one hand on each other, and over days a whole vocabulary passes through their fingers.

**The chord changes.** This is the first strangeness, and it is so slow that it takes a long stay to notice. The fen's overall tone rises and falls, not with the wind but with something deeper, perhaps the far tide or the pressure of water in the rock below. Over about eleven days the great hum climbs gradually through a set of intervals and then sinks back. Listeners have named the stages. There is a stretch they call *the Held Breath*, when the chord settles on something nearly consonant and the whole basin seems to hover. There is another called *the Grinding*, when the tones pull against each other and the air feels rough, and animals and people alike grow uneasy.

**There are quiet places.** This is the second strangeness. The chimneys stand so close, and their tones overlap in such complex ways, that the sound waves cancel one another at certain points in the fen. These are nodes of near-silence, some no larger than a person's body, a few big enough for a small camp. Stepping into one is shocking. The roar drops away as if a door has shut, and suddenly you can hear your own breath, your heartbeat, the drip from your own sleeve. Step out and the noise returns like a wave breaking over you.

The nodes shift as the chord shifts. A quiet place in the Held Breath may be deafening in the Grinding, and the reverse. Knowing where the silences lie in each stage of the cycle is the fen's real geography, and it is harder won than any map of rock.

## The Light

The light is always dim. The mist never lifts entirely, and the sky above it is overcast most days, a flat, pewter lid. The sun is at most a pale smear, and some days it cannot be found at all.

The chimneys do something odd with what light there is. Their fresh crystal walls are faintly translucent, and they gather the diffuse grey glow and hold it. On the darkest days the white chimneys seem to shine faintly from within, so the fen at dusk is a forest of dim pale columns standing in black water, brighter than the sky. At true night they fade, but slowly, and for an hour after dark the tallest chimneys still carry a ghost of light at their tips.

What little color exists is at the seeps. The water around the warmest vents carries thin films of microbial life in concentric bands: rust-orange nearest the heat, then ochre, then a faint bruised green at the cool edge. The films are paper-thin, and they are the only living color in the basin, like stains on a cloth.

## The Creatures

Animals are rare, but they exist, and all of them live by the chimneys.

**Drip-snails** are small and soft-shelled, the shell nearly clear, and they graze the microbial films at the seeps. They move so slowly that a snail watched through a morning seems not to have moved at all. Their shells take up the minerals of the water and gradually harden, so an old snail is heavy as a pebble and nearly as rigid.

**Tube-dwellers** are the fen's secret, and few who visit ever see one. They are pale, long-bodied, many-legged things about the length of a hand. They live inside the chimneys, clinging to the inner walls above the waterline and eating whatever falls in. A dweller's body partly blocks the bore, and this changes the chimney's pitch. When a dweller climbs or descends, its chimney's note bends. Listeners say that in the deep of night, during the Held Breath, they can sometimes hear a single tone sliding upward in the chord, the sound of one creature making its slow way toward the open air. No one knows why they climb.

**The grey stalker** is a tall, thin wading bird the color of wet slate. It is seen perhaps once in a season. It stands motionless among the chimneys on legs so thin they blend in, and it hunts the tube-dwellers. It tilts its head beside chimney after chimney, listening for the bend in a note. When it finds one it drives its long bill down the tube. The fen's listeners watch it with something between respect and resentment.

## The Marks of People

No one lives in the fen, but people have come to it for a very long time, and they have left traces.

**Stone circles** are rings of flat basalt slabs laid in the shallow water, marking quiet places. Each is carved with small notches along one edge, a tally that seems to record the stages of the chord in which that node falls silent. Some circles have notches for every stage, and those are the precious ones, places where it is always quiet. There are said to be only three.

**Plugged chimneys** appear here and there. Someone long ago stopped the top of a chimney with a cap of grey clay, silencing it. Where many chimneys have been plugged in a line, there is a *quiet lane*: a path along which the roar is noticeably lessened, deliberately tuned. The oldest lanes are nearly lost. The plugged chimneys kept growing around their caps, and the clay now sits sealed inside the stone like a pit inside a fruit.

**Cairns** of stacked plates stand at the fen's edge, where the lanes begin.

**A single hut** stands near the center, or what may be the center. It is round and low, walled with broken chimney-stone and roofed with slabs. It sits at the heart of one of the three always-silent places. Inside, the silence is total and almost frightening. Along the walls are rows of small chimney fragments, each broken to a specific length. Tapped with a knuckle, each one rings a note of the fen's chord. It is a tuning set, or a record, or an instrument. The ashes in the hearth are old, but not ancient.

The people who built these things had no tools more complex than a stone, a hand, and clay. They made no machines. Everything they did was done by listening, and by adding or taking away small amounts of silence.

## The Slowness

Every process in the fen is loud, and none of them is fast.

A chimney grows about the thickness of a fingernail in a year. A tall one is centuries old. The basin itself is still rising, by perhaps a hand's width in a lifetime, and as it rises the seeps shift. Old chimneys lose their water and go dry, and they fall silent one by one. New ones start as small white crusts around fresh cracks. The fen is moving very slowly inland, toward the ridge, and falling slowly silent behind itself toward the sea. At the southern edge stand fields of dead chimneys, dry, cracked, and yellowed, with the wind whistling faintly across their tops. It is a thin ghost of the music, the sound of a place that used to sing.

In some thousands of years the whole basin will be dry, and the chord will have moved somewhere else.

For now it drips and roars in the grey light. The pale columns stand close enough to touch on either side, the milky water threads between them, and somewhere a tube-dweller climbs, so that one note among ten thousand bends slowly upward and nobody hears it but the bird.
