# The Stump at Hollow Coin

## I. Approach

From the dust plains below it looks like a mesa: a vast flat-topped mass rising three kilometres out of a dry grey basin, its flanks rounded by time, its summit an unbroken horizon. The cliffs have the fluted, slumped look of very old stone, as if they had once been sharp and forgotten how.

As you climb, the texture changes. The flutes are too regular. They run in long vertical bundles, braided and twisting, and between them are seams that look like tendon more than strata. Halfway up, where a buttress has sheared away, you can see a cross-section, and it is not rock. It is grain: dense, dark, tight as horn, streaked amber and umber and a deep bruised violet.

It is wood. The whole mesa is wood. You are climbing the side of a stump.

## II. The Ground

The summit is a plain some sixty kilometres across, and it is nearly flat. The erosion that softened the cliffs has had even longer up here. Whatever this was when it stood, whatever it was when it fell or was felled, has been sanded by wind for so long that the top has become a polished floor. It has the dull sheen of a banister worn by generations of hands, except the hands were air and dust and the generations were geological.

The growth rings are visible across the whole surface. They are enormous, concentric bands of lighter and darker grain that sweep past the horizon in slow arcs. Some bands are a few metres wide. Some are a kilometre. Walking across the Stump means walking across its history, and the history is uneven. There are rings of plenty, pale and broad, and rings of starvation so narrow and dark they look like cracks. Near the centre is a ring nearly black, pitted and blistered. The inhabitants call it the Burn, and nobody knows what burned.

Where the rings meet the wind at a low angle, the softer latewood has worn away faster than the hard earlywood, so the floor ripples. There are low parallel ridges a hand high, running for kilometres, that throw faint shadows in the dim light and make the plain look combed.

The Stump is not dead. That is the first thing you learn and the last thing you get used to.

## III. Dry Life

There is no water here, and there never was. The air is thin, cold, and dry enough to split lips in an hour. The dust of the basin below is mineral and powder-fine. No rain has fallen on this world in any history anyone has kept.

The Stump lives anyway. Its chemistry has nothing to do with sap. It moves its substance by solid-state creep. Molecules migrate through the lattice of the wood itself, shouldered along grain boundaries, passed cell to cell the way heat passes through iron. This would be impossibly slow except that the Stump pushes the migration along with sound.

The whole plateau vibrates. Its circulation is acoustic, and so is its breathing and feeding. Deep in the heartwood there are chambers the inhabitants call bells: hollows kilometres long, shaped by the growth of ages into resonators. The Stump pulses them. The pulse travels outward through the grain as pressure waves, and each wave nudges nutrients, resin, and fixed carbon a fraction of a micron further along. Sound is the Stump's metabolism.

So the place is loud, and it is loud all the time.

## IV. The Sound

At first it is too low to hear as sound. It arrives through the soles of your feet and the long bones of your legs, a pressure more than a tone, and you think it is your own heartbeat until you notice it isn't keeping time with you. Then your ears adjust and you begin to pick out the layers.

**The drone.** The fundamental: a note so deep it has a period of several seconds. It swells and recedes like breath. When it peaks, dust lifts off the floor in a faint shimmering skin and settles again.

**The chords.** Above the drone are the voices of the individual bells, dozens of them, each at its own pitch. They are not harmonious in any way human music would recognise. They beat against each other, and where they beat, the air wobbles. There are places on the plain where two bells interfere so precisely that the wind seems to stall, and a standing wave hangs over the ground like heat shimmer that never moves.

**The cracks.** At dusk the temperature falls fast, and the surface of the Stump contracts. It does not creak. It *reports*: sharp, cannon-loud splits that echo off nothing, because there is nothing out here to echo off except the air itself. They come in cascades, a volley running along one ring for ten kilometres, then silence, then another. In the first hours of night the plain sounds like a distant war.

**The rasping.** Everywhere, under everything, is the sound of mouths on wood. That is the fauna.

## V. What Lives on the Stump

Density up here is low. The plain is mostly open floor, and in any direction you can see perhaps three or four things standing up from it before the horizon. But the animal life is thick. It is simply flat, or small, or moving.

**Rasps** are the grazers. They are low, many-legged, shell-backed things about the size of a dog, with jaws like wood files. They move in loose herds of a few hundred across the rings and eat the surface a fraction of a millimetre at a time. They prefer the soft latewood, and over millions of years they have sculpted the combed ridges as much as the wind has. A rasp herd feeding sounds like a sawmill heard from across a valley, a continuous, grinding hiss punctuated by the click of carapace on carapace. They follow the drone. When a bell swells, they turn toward it like a field of heads.

**Chaff swifts** live in the air in flocks of tens of thousands. They are tiny and fast, with bodies of lightweight fibre, and they feed on the pollen-dust the Stump sheds into the wind. That dust is fine, bronze-coloured, and faintly sweet-smelling. A flock moves like smoke. When it turns, the combined snap of its wings is a single loud crack, like a sail filling. When several flocks turn at once in the evening, the sound blends with the cracking of the ground and it becomes impossible to tell which is which.

**Knellbirds** are larger, solitary, and heavy, with hollow chest cavities shaped almost exactly like the Stump's bells. They sit on the few upright structures and call at the bells' pitches, amplifying them. Some of the inhabitants believe the knellbirds are tuning the Stump, and that without them the bells would drift and the grain would starve. Others believe the Stump grows the knellbirds for exactly that purpose. There may be no difference.

**Sawyers** are the predators. They live *inside* the wood. They are long, segmented, and armoured, and they tunnel through the grain by vibrating their bodies at the frequency of the local bells. At that frequency the wood briefly loosens and flows around them, and it hardens again behind them without a seam. You cannot see a sawyer coming. You can only hear it, a change in the pitch of the ground under a rasp herd, a sour note, and then the herd scatters, screaming like gears stripping, and one of them is pulled down into the floor, which closes over it smooth as a lake.

The lake image is the only water metaphor anyone here has. They got it from old records.

**Grainmoss** is the closest thing to undergrowth. It is not a separate plant. It is the Stump growing outward in fine filaments where the surface is cracked, a velvet of violet-black threads that cluster in the dark rings. It hums faintly when you brush it. Rasps won't eat it. Swifts nest in it.

Everything that grows on the Stump is the Stump. There are no other plants. There is no soil. The ground, the moss, and the few upright shoots are all one continuous living mass.

## VI. The Shoots

At long intervals, perhaps one every few kilometres, the Stump sends up a shoot.

The shoots are slender by the Stump's standard and enormous by any other. They are fifty to four hundred metres tall, smooth, straight, and nearly branchless, like the masts of ships. Their bark is pale grey. Near their tops they flare into fans of stiff, thin, dark leaves that do not photosynthesise in any ordinary sense. They are antennae. They catch the faint light, and they also catch the wind, and they vibrate, and the vibration is fed down the shoot into the plateau. The shoots are how the Stump listens to the sky.

When wind comes from the basin at evening, the shoots all sing at once. Their note is a high, glassy wail that rises over the drone. It is the loudest sound on the plateau, and it can be heard from the dust plains three kilometres below.

The shoots never last. A few centuries, and the wind and the rasps and the cracking cold take them down, and they lie across the rings like fallen pillars until they are worn flat and the floor has absorbed them. The plain is streaked with their ghosts: long pale stripes cutting across the rings at every angle.

## VII. Light

The sky is dim. It is not dark, but always dim.

The Stump sheds pollen-dust constantly, and the thin air holds it in a high bronze veil that never clears. The sun is a pale coin behind it, bright enough to look at directly, casting shadows that are soft and brown instead of sharp. The inhabitants call the sun Hollow Coin, and the name has stuck to the plateau itself.

Daylight has the colour of weak tea. Dawn and dusk go on for hours as the coin slides along the rim of the veil, and in those hours the whole plain turns a deep copper, and the combed ridges throw long parallel shadows that make the floor look ploughed.

At night there are no stars. There is only the faint glow of the veil, lit from somewhere far away, and small points of cold violet light in the dark rings where the grainmoss grows. Where a sawyer has passed recently, the wood glows faintly along its track for an hour or two, a line of ember-light under the surface, curving, forking, and fading.

## VIII. The City

It is not obvious that there is a city. The density is too low for that to register at first. What you notice is that some of the upright things on the plain are not shoots.

They are towers, built of the Stump's own wood and grown from it rather than carved. Their walls are grain that continues seamlessly into the floor. They stand kilometres apart. From the plain's centre on a clear evening you might count thirty of them before the veil swallows the horizon. They are all tall, narrow, and hollow. Every one of them is an instrument.

The builders, whoever they were, did not cut the Stump. They *tuned* it. They learned the frequencies at which the wood flows, the ones the sawyers use, and they used those frequencies to shape the grain. They sang the towers up out of the plateau, and the method they worked out is not described by physics as anyone outside the Stump would understand it. The towers hold sound the way a vessel holds liquid. Inside them, the Stump's drone is gathered, focused, and held.

This is where the rules of the place start to change.

**Held rooms.** Some chambers inside the towers have no walls at the upper levels. They have *tones*: sustained chords so precisely shaped that the air itself has become rigid in the pattern of their nodes. You can lean against a held wall. It is cool, slightly yielding, and it hums against your skin. If the chord falters, the wall is gone. The Tuners who live here watch the chords the way sailors once watched weather.

**Bridge-songs.** Between some of the nearer towers run bridges. They are not built of wood. They are standing waves, each a single long note sung between two tower-throats and held so steadily that its nodes have gone solid. They are walkable, slightly bouncing ribbons of pressure, faintly visible as a line where the pollen-dust refuses to settle. To cross one you have to walk in time. Step on an antinode and your foot goes through.

**Doors of pitch.** Some doors exist only at certain frequencies. At the right note the grain in the doorway loosens and you pass through as a sawyer would, wood flowing around you like cold honey. At any other note the doorway is solid wall. The Tuners carry small resonant tokens, discs of heartwood shaped to ring at personal pitches, and they carry them with the care that people elsewhere give to keys.

**Recording.** The builders discovered that the Stump remembers. Each growth ring is laid down under the influence of the sound that was playing when it formed, and the grain holds the pattern. With the right instruments, a ring can be played back. The Burn, the black ring near the centre, plays back as a roar. Nobody who has listened to it all the way through will describe it.

The Tuners have come to believe the builders wrote themselves into the rings on purpose. They think that somewhere in the kilometres of heartwood below the city there is a ring that is a voice, or a library, or a whole population encoded in grain, waiting for the right note. They spend their lives searching the rings with listening-spikes, in silence, while the plain roars around them.

## IX. The Tuners

There are very few of them: perhaps a few thousand, spread across thirty towers and sixty kilometres of plain. The city was built for many more. Most of the towers are empty, their held rooms long since collapsed into silence, their bridges gone. Some still sing on their own, the chords sustained by the Stump's drone without anyone to tend them, and knellbirds nest in their throats.

The Tuners are quiet in speech, because they have to be. They communicate largely by touch and by small tones pitched just above the drone. They have enormous regard for silence, which is the one thing the plateau never offers. Each tower has a single room at its core where the chords cancel each other perfectly, a null. It is the only silent place on the Stump. People go there to be born, to marry, and to die. Visitors who enter a null for the first time often weep, not from any emotion they can name, but because their bodies have been braced against sound so long that the absence feels like falling.

## X. Cycles

The plateau is never still.

**The daily pitch.** As the air warms through the dim day, the wood expands, and every bell drops in pitch by a fraction of a tone. By afternoon the drone is noticeably lower, and the rasps shift their grazing to the rings that resonate best at the new frequency. At dusk the cold comes, the wood contracts, the pitch climbs, and the cracks begin. The pitch rises all night. By dawn the plain rings high and bright and brittle, and then the sun warms it and the cycle turns.

**Dust-storms.** Every few weeks the Stump sheds a heavy pulse of pollen, and the veil drops to the surface. Visibility falls to metres. The air turns bronze and sweet and faintly electric. The chaff swifts gorge and multiply. Everything becomes a sound-world, with direction told only by pitch. The Tuners stay inside. Rasps huddle in their herds and grind in unison, a sound like surf, which is another thing nobody here has heard but which the records describe.

**Ringing season.** Every hundred years or so, the Stump lays down a new ring at its outer edge. The whole plateau changes key. It happens over weeks: a slow slide in the drone, the bells shifting against one another, old standing waves collapsing and new ones forming in new places. Bridges fall. Held rooms fail. Doors that were open for a century seal shut. The knellbirds go silent, then learn the new notes one by one. The Tuners spend these weeks retuning everything, frantically, and they lose towers every time. Their histories are measured in keys, not years.

**The sawyer swarms.** Sometimes, for reasons nobody understands, the sawyers come up. Thousands of them surface in a single night and move across the plain in a front, the floor softening and flowing in their wake, the rasp herds screaming ahead of them. By morning they have gone back down, and a band of the plateau kilometres wide has been reworked, its grain churned into new patterns, its combed ridges erased and its old shoot-ghosts gone. The next ring laid down over that band will record it. Some Tuners think the sawyers are not predators at all, but the Stump's own editing process, scouring old records to make room for new ones.

## XI. The Edge

At the rim of the plateau the wood gives out into air. There is no railing, only the rounded shoulder of the cliff, sloping away into haze. From here the dust basin is invisible, three kilometres down through the bronze veil. The sound is different at the edge. The drone thins. You can hear the wind, which is otherwise drowned, and under it, very faint, something else.

It is another drone, from far away across the basin, pitched differently. It is lower and slower, and it is not in any key the Stump knows.

The Tuners don't talk about it. Some of the oldest records describe the Stump as one of many, a forest, a long time ago. Whether the others are still standing, whether they are singing back, and whether the Burn was something that happened to all of them at once are questions they leave in the null rooms, where there is silence enough to think.

Behind you, the plain rings on. A rasp herd grinds past in the copper evening. The shoots begin their wail. Somewhere, a volley of cracks runs out across the rings like a fuse. The Stump, which has not seen water in all its long life, keeps singing itself into existence one micron at a time.
