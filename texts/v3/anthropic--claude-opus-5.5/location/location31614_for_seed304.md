# Weepstone

The first thing you learn about the place is that it is still being made.

It sits on a low spur of grey ground at the edge of a cold estuary marsh. It is a knuckle of rock barely higher than the reed beds around it, perhaps a few hundred paces end to end. From the marsh it looks less like a town than a single swollen outcrop, pale and wet, heaped with bumps and hollows, veiled most mornings in a mist that hangs at roof height and will not rise. Get closer and the bumps become roofs, the hollows become doorways, and the whole mass turns out to be a settlement so tightly packed that there is no seeing where one house stops and the next begins. That is because, in the sense that matters, they don't stop. They are one stone.

## The springs

Under the spur, water moves through old limestone and picks up more lime than it can carry. It comes up in dozens of small springs, cold and faintly fizzing, tasting of chalk and iron. The moment it meets air it starts to give its burden back. Wherever it runs, it lays down a skin of new stone. At first this is a soft grey paste, then a crisp crust, and within a few seasons a hard, pale, finely layered rock the people call weepstone.

It forms fast. A reed left lying in a runnel for a month comes out as a stone tube. A basket set under a drip for a year becomes a stone basket, then a stone lump with a basket-shaped hollow inside. A sleeping dog, the old people say, would wake up wearing armor, though no one has seen it done, and dogs are rare here anyway.

This is why the land is young. Nothing here has had time to wear down. Every edge is sharp. The newest crusts are sugary and glittering, rimmed with tiny crystal teeth that will open a careless palm. Older surfaces, a century or two, have gone smooth and slightly greasy with moss, but even they keep the crisp profiles of whatever they grew over. The ridges of the spur are not weathered hills. They are frozen overflow, lobes and terraces and rimmed pools stepping down toward the marsh like a staircase poured from cream.

## How the town is built

No one quarries weepstone. They grow it.

The builders work with water the way other people work with clay. They cut channels and lay flumes of split alder and hollowed reed-bundle, all by hand, with flint adzes, bone chisels, and the occasional precious blade of hammered copper. They stretch mats of woven rush over wooden frames as formwork, then lead a thin sheet of spring water across the mat, day after day, turning the flow a little each morning with a stone plug or a pinch of clay. In three or four winters the mat is a wall. Take out the frame and you have a room.

So the town is layered like a reef. Each generation's rooms are grown onto the last. Old roofs become new floors. Old streets, left under the water too long, become tunnels, then are chosen as foundations and sealed entirely. Every wall is shared. Every roof is somebody's path. Alleys are roofed over with grown arches so thin the daylight comes through them milky and pale. In places a stair climbs straight through the corner of a family's sleeping room, because the stair was there first and the room grew around it, and everyone has agreed not to mind.

There are perhaps six or seven hundred people. That is enough to make it a town rather than a village, and more than the spur has any right to hold. There are no gardens, no yards, no gaps. The only open spaces are the cistern courts: small wells of air, open to the sky, each with a pool at the bottom where the spring gathers before being sent out through the flumes. These courts are the closest thing the town has to squares. People meet there, wash there, argue about water there.

## The rule of standing water

The single law everyone obeys: **water must keep moving.**

Moving water builds slowly and where it is told. Still water seals. A spill left pooling on a floor overnight leaves a gritty film by morning. A doorway nobody walks through for a season begins to narrow, its sill thickening, its jambs budding with glassy knobs. There are rooms deep in the old core of the town that have closed completely, their doors grown shut like healed wounds. No one knows what was left inside. Some families claim their ancestors are in there, sitting at tables, sheathed.

That last part is not entirely a story. The dead are laid in niches under chosen drips, wrapped in rush, and left. Over years they take on the stone. The oldest are smooth pale shapes you can only just tell were people. Children touch them for luck on the way to the cisterns.

Because water builds, water rights are building rights. Whoever controls a spring's flow decides what grows where. The flume-wardens, a handful of old men and women with scarred hands and wooden rakes, walk the channels every dawn. They scrape back the crust, clear the clogs, and settle disputes by the simple method of turning the water away from whoever is wrong. A family out of favor watches its walls stop growing while its neighbors' swell toward it.

## Atmosphere

The air is cool and always damp. There is no hard frost in the town itself, because the springs run a little warmer than the winter air. Instead they breathe. On cold mornings every channel, pool, and weeping wall gives off a low mist that fills the alleys and pours slowly over the roof-edges into the marsh. By midday it usually burns thin, but it never quite leaves. The stone is wet to the touch in every season. Walls bead and run. Ceilings drip in slow, steady rhythms that people learn the way you learn the breathing of someone sleeping beside you.

The light is soft and even, the ordinary grey-white of overcast northern skies. Here it is doubled by the pale stone, which takes the light and gives it back, so that shadows are faint and nothing in the town is ever truly dark or truly bright. Inside, rooms are lit by what seeps through the thinner walls and by small oil lamps of seal fat, whose flames tremble in the drafts that move along the flumes.

## Sound

It is a quiet place, and the quiet has a texture.

Drips, mostly: hundreds of them, each with its own pitch depending on the pool it falls into. There is a constant low trickle along the flumes, half-heard. There is the scrape of a warden's rake, a mat being beaten, a voice two walls away that carries oddly through the stone and seems to come from directly beside you. Children shout sometimes, and the sound flattens and vanishes against the wet walls almost at once.

And there is one sound that belongs to nowhere else. When the tide goes out and the lower terraces are exposed to the wind, the fresh crusts dry and tighten. As they do, they crackle, very faintly, a fine continuous ticking like frost forming on a window, or like something small eating. On still evenings the whole seaward edge of the town makes this sound at once. People call it "the town settling." They say you can hear it growing.

## What lives here

Almost nothing, and what does is tough.

Plants struggle on new stone. It is too alkaline, too fast-changing, too wet. What grows is low and green-black: mats of liverwort on the older walls, cushions of a stiff grey moss in the cistern courts, a slick of algae in every channel that the wardens scrape and dry and burn. A few families keep sea-beet or scurvy-grass in wooden troughs on the rooftops and guard them jealously. Out past the edge of the stone, the marsh begins abruptly: brown reeds, black mud, pools that go iridescent with iron. The town gets its thatch, its rush, its fuel, and most of its food from out there.

Animals are few. Each cistern keeps a single eel, placed there as an elver and fed on scraps. It eats the fly larvae that would otherwise foul the water, and it is treated as a kind of quiet citizen. Some are said to be older than the families who keep them. Small grey crabs work the lowest terraces at low tide. A heron sometimes stands on a roof-ridge in the mist, perfectly still, and the town's children dare each other to walk close. Gulls come and go. There are a few cats and fewer dogs. Rats cannot seem to settle; burrows seal. The absence of vermin is the one thing visitors from the marsh villages envy.

## Motion

For all its quiet, nothing here holds still.

The tide comes and goes twice a day across the lower terraces, wetting and drying and wetting the crust. The mist rises every morning and thins every afternoon. Rain, which is frequent, dilutes the springs and briefly slows the building. After a hard storm the wardens run from channel to channel, because the flow will have shifted, and water always goes somewhere new when no one is watching. Walls grow. Doors narrow and are cut wide again. Flumes clog and are cleared. Old rooms are sacrificed, flooded deliberately and sealed, to become foundations for new ones above.

The whole spur is slowly getting taller, and slowly spreading into the marsh. Every generation the town stands a little higher above the reeds than its grandparents' town did. Every generation the oldest parts sink a little deeper into the stone's own body, closed, sheathed, remembered only as solidity underfoot.

Stand in a cistern court at dusk, when the mist is coming back and the lamps are being lit and the seaward terraces are beginning to tick. Put your hand flat on a wall. It is cold and wet and very slightly rough, and if you leave it there long enough, you will feel it: not movement exactly, but patience. The sense of a thing that is, very carefully and without hurry, making room for itself by closing around everything it touches.
