# The Warm Basin

The first thing is the air. It sits against the skin like breath from very close, faintly sulfurous with a mineral sweetness underneath, the smell of a kettle that has boiled hard water for a hundred years. You feel it before your eyes adjust, because there isn't much to adjust to.

## The Hollow

It is a hollow in low ground, a collapsed chamber where the roof of a small cave fell in long ago and never finished falling. Only a ragged oval of it is open to the sky, off-center and to one side, and through that gap comes a grey, diluted light that seems tired by the time it reaches the floor. The rest of the roof still arches over, a low dome of pale stone beaded everywhere with water. The whole space is about the size of a threshing floor or a small chapel. You could cross it in twenty steps if there were anywhere to step.

There isn't. Every hand-span of the floor is pool.

## The Pools

The spring rises somewhere at the back, under a lip of rock you can't see beneath, and it has spent a very long time building the room it lives in. The water is warm, blood-warm at the edges and hotter near the source, and heavy with dissolved stone. Where it spills, it lays down a skin of travertine as fine as eggshell. Over centuries those skins have become rims, and the rims have become basins, and the basins have filled the entire floor.

They are crowded together like cells in a comb or scales on a fish. Each pool is bounded by a scalloped lip no wider than a finger, and each lip is shared with its neighbors. Some pools hold a cupful. Some are deep enough to sit in. They step down from the back of the chamber toward the open side in shallow terraces, each tier overflowing into the next along a thousand tiny spillways. Nothing between them is dry or flat. The floor is all edges and brimming.

The stone the water makes is the color of old ivory, cream shading to ochre where iron has come up with it, and in the hottest channels to a faint rust-pink. Where water runs constantly, the surface is glossy and gelatinous-looking, though it is hard as bone. Where it only seeps, it grows into soft-looking cauliflower bosses, knuckles of stone budding on stone. The walls weep too. Thin sheets of water slide down them without dripping, so the rock looks lacquered, and at the bottom of each weeping face a curtain of flowstone hangs in stiff folds like drapery someone wet and then forgot.

The erosion here runs backwards: the water is building rather than cutting. But the building is so old and so even that the place feels finished. Every rim is the height the water wants it to be. Every spillway has found its level. The basin has reached a kind of agreement with itself.

## Stillness

Nothing moves quickly. The flow into the chamber equals the flow out, and has for longer than anyone could measure. The water level in each pool holds true to the thickness of a hair. The temperature doesn't vary between morning and night or between seasons, because the source is deep and indifferent to weather. Steam doesn't billow. It hangs in a thin, level layer a hand's height above the surface, and in the dimness it looks like the pools are breathing out very slowly and never finishing the exhalation.

Stone grows here, but you would have to return after a lifetime to notice a rim a fingernail higher. To any single visit the place is fixed, a held note.

## What Lives Here

Almost nothing. No moss survives the minerals, and no fern roots in the crust. The light is too thin for anything green, and the water too alkaline and too full of stone. The only thing resembling growth is a slick of bacterial film in the hottest runnels near the source, in faint bands of orange and olive and a sickly cream, like watercolor wash laid on glass. It doesn't ripple or spread. It stays in its bands, each one exactly where its preferred temperature sits.

Animals are rare. In a few of the deeper, cooler pools near the mouth live tiny colorless snails, each the size of a lentil. They move so slowly they seem to be part of the stone, and some of them *are* becoming part of it, their shells frosted over with the same white crust as everything else. Once in a long while a single small bat shifts on the ceiling near the opening. It is the only warm-blooded thing here, and it makes a dry leathery rustle that seems enormous in the quiet. Otherwise you are alone with the water.

## Sound

It is mostly quiet, a quiet made of small layered noises. There is the tick of drops falling from the roof into pools, each pitched slightly differently by the depth of the water it lands in, so the ceiling plays a slow, patternless, faintly musical sequence. There is the whisper of thin sheets of water slipping over rims, a sound that never rises or falls. Now and then something gurgles far back at the source, a single deep bubble surfacing like a word spoken in another room. The walls are close and the ceiling low, so every sound comes back softened, as though the stone were padded.

## The Traces of People

People found this place, used it, and left long ago. You notice their work only slowly, because the water has been claiming it.

Near the mouth, three steps have been cut into the old rock, worn into shallow dishes by feet. The travertine has crept over their edges, rounding them, so they look less carved than grown.

Along one wall runs a low bench of fitted stones, unmortared and chosen for flatness. Its seat is filmed with mineral, and the gaps between the stones have filled with white crust until the bench is nearly a single piece. It is clearly for sitting in the warm water up to the waist. Someone came here to soak, to ease joints, or to wash, or perhaps for something more ceremonial.

In the wall above the bench is a small niche chipped into the rock at head height, its ceiling black with ancient soot from a lamp. The soot is the one truly dark mark in the whole pale chamber, and the water has left it alone. The lamp is gone.

Down in one of the lower pools, wedged against a rim, lies a clay cup: handmade, thumb-pressed, lopsided. It has been there long enough to be cemented in place, its lip grown thick with stone and its bowl half-filled with a smooth white deposit, like a cup of milk turned to rock.

At the back, someone once laid a trough of hollowed wood to lead the hottest water toward the bench. The wood is long gone, but the travertine that coated it remains: a hollow stone channel in the exact shape of the vanished log, bark texture and knots faithfully cast on its inner surface, now carrying water on its own as if it had always been part of the rock.

No one has come in a long time. The marks aren't ruins so much as fossils in progress. The basin is gently, without hurry, making them its own.

## The Nature of the Place

The spring builds the pools, and the pools shape the flow, and the flow holds the temperature. The dimness keeps out green life, and the absence of life keeps the water clear and the stone pure. Each part holds the others steady. The place isn't waiting for anything. It has no season to arrive at and no flood to fear. It is warm and wet and full, every inch occupied by water resting in stone of its own making, and it will look tomorrow exactly as it looks now, and as it did when the lamp in the niche was still burning.

If you sat on the bench and let the warmth come up around you, you would feel how completely the place holds itself: full to its rims, spilling exactly as much as it receives.
