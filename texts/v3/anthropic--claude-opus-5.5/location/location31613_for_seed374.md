# The Clamour

## Stone

The ground is new. You can tell this before anything else, before the noise even, because the rock under your feet has edges. Nothing has worn it down. It is black basalt, poured out not long ago in the reckoning of stone, and it still holds the shapes it had when it cooled. There are ropy coils where the surface skinned over and kept flowing underneath. There are plates heaved up and tilted like broken slate. There are blisters the size of rooms whose roofs caved in, leaving round pits with glassy lips. Run your palm across almost any surface and it comes back scored. The rock is full of bubbles frozen mid-rise, so the whole landscape has the texture of a sponge made of iron.

It lies low, barely above the sea, a vast shelf of lava that spilled out of some unseen vent inland and ran down into the water until it stopped. The shelf goes on for miles. From any high point, and there are few, you see it spread flat and black toward a horizon lost in haze. Its seaward edge is ragged, a coast of fingers and bays where lava tongues pushed out into the surf and froze. Here and there on the plain stand low domes and spatter cones, shoulder-high to house-high, like cairns that nobody built.

Nothing has softened any of it. There is no soil, not a handful. There are no sand beaches, because the sea has not yet had time to grind any. The waves strike the black edge and fall back white. Rain runs off the rock or vanishes into it. Where it vanishes, it does not stay.

## Heat

The shelf is warm. It isn't hot enough to burn, but it is warm the way a hearthstone is warm hours after the fire. The heat comes up through the rock from somewhere far beneath, steadily and without change. Barefoot, you would feel it at once. In the cracks it is warmer. In the collapsed blister-pits it is warmer still, and the rain that gathers there steams.

Steam is everywhere. Every fissure breathes a thin white thread. Low pools sit in the hollows of the rock, some clear and some milky with dissolved minerals, and all of them smoke gently into the air. The seepage never stops. Rainwater soaks in, meets the heat, and rises again as vapour. The lower air of the whole plain is therefore full of moisture. It condenses on every cool surface, beads on the undersides of overhangs, and drips. The rocks weep, not with springs but with this endless recycled breath.

Above the shelf, the vapour gathers into a ceiling. The sky is never blue here. It is a pale, luminous grey, bright where the sun is and dim everywhere else, a light that seems to come from all directions at once and casts almost no shadows. Day is a long, soft dusk. Night is an orange murk, lit from below by the few places where the rock still glows faintly in its deepest cracks, and by lamps.

## The Near-Absence of Green

There are no trees, no grass, no ferns. Nothing has rooted, because there is nothing to root in.

Life has found the water, though. In the warm pools there are mats: slick, layered films of bacteria in rust, olive, and a startling sulphur yellow. They coat the pool floors and drape over the lips where overflow trickles down, building up in thin crusted terraces. They are the only colour on the plain besides black and white and grey. In the cooler seeps, a faint dark slime clings to the rock. That is all. Anyone who has lived here all their life has never seen a leaf.

## Birds

Then there is the reason for the noise.

The shelf is a nesting ground, and the birds come in numbers that do not fit into the mind. The warmth draws them, since eggs laid on warm stone need less brooding. So does the sea, which is cold offshore and teeming with fish where currents rise against the new coast. And so does the rock itself: all those bubbles and blisters, ledges and pits and overhangs are nest sites past counting.

They are seabirds of several kinds:

- **Gannet-like divers**, white with gold-washed heads, packed onto the flat plates at pecking distance, each pair defending a disc of rock exactly as wide as two necks can reach.
- **Stubby auk-like birds**, black and white, crammed into crevices in their thousands, whirring in and out of the cracks.
- **Grey gulls**, which own the domes and cones and rob everyone.
- **Small dark petrels**, which nest deep in the lava tubes and come and go by night, crooning underground.

Every horizontal surface that is not a pool is a bird. Every cavity has a bird or an egg or a chick. The air above the shelf is a slow storm of wings, tens of thousands aloft at any moment, circling out to sea and back. Offshore the water is pocked white where divers strike it like thrown spears.

The rock beneath the colonies is not black. It is caked grey-white with guano, layer on layer, so the plain from above is mottled like a gull's back. The smell is enormous: ammonia, fish, salt, and sulphur from the pools. Newcomers weep with it. Residents stopped noticing generations ago.

## The Sound

It is deafening, and it never stops.

Tens of thousands of gannet-birds bark and grate at their neighbours without pause. Gulls laugh in waves that roll across the colony. The auk-birds make a low buzzing growl that, massed, becomes a physical pressure in the chest. Beneath all of it, surf thunders against a coast with no sand to cushion it. The waves drive into lava tubes that open at the waterline and boom back out as blasts of spray. Every few breaths, somewhere along the shore, one of these blowholes fires with a cough like a cliff falling.

Closer at hand, the pools hiss and bubble. Steam vents whistle. Rain, when it comes, rattles on rock and on roofs.

There is no quiet anywhere on the shelf. You cannot hear a voice beyond arm's length. At night the gulls settle a little, but then the petrels start their moaning songs from beneath the ground, and the surf is louder in the dark. The noise has been here as long as anyone remembers. It is the air itself.

## The City

People live here, and there are a great many of them.

They built a city on the shelf, and it is a city in every sense. There are thousands of dwellings, streets, districts, and a harbour. Almost everything in it is made of the same black rock the ground is made of.

There is no wood on the shelf. There is no clay, because there is no soil. So the people build with stone, and the stone is everywhere, already broken into slabs by the cooling and cracking of the flows. They pry plates free with bone levers and stone wedges. They stack them without mortar, fitting each piece by eye and by long practice, the way you would build a field wall. The walls are thick and rough and bristle with protruding edges, since the rock cannot be smoothed with the tools they have. They roof with long slabs laid across narrow gaps, which is why every room is narrow. Where a span is too wide, they corbel inward, course over course, until the walls meet in a pointed vault.

Better still, they use what the lava made. The collapsed blister-pits become sunken courtyards, their curved walls already built, needing only a roof of slabs. The lava tubes, natural tunnels running under the shelf, become passages, storerooms, and whole underground lanes. Parts of the city are more hollowed than constructed. Houses crowd around every pit. Walls lean on walls. There is almost no open ground, because open ground is where the birds are, and the birds are wealth.

The city and the colony are therefore not separate. They interleave. The rooftops are nesting ledges. Gannet-birds sit in pairs on the ridgelines of houses, and gulls own the tops of chimneys that have never held a fire. The narrow lanes run between walls lined with auk-birds nesting in the chinks of the stonework, deliberately left open for them. In some districts the people have built walls for the birds alone, honeycombed stacks of rough slab riddled with nest cavities, taller than the houses. These bird-towers loom everywhere in the haze like dark hives, streaked white and roaring.

Seen from a height, the whole thing is a single dense texture: black walls, grey guano, white birds, and rising threads of steam, all packed together and spreading out across the plain further than you can see.

## How the People Live

Everything comes from the birds, the sea, and the heat.

**Food.** They eat eggs, taken by the basket in season from ledges they know like their own houses. They eat young birds, taken fat from the nest. They eat fish, caught with bone hooks on lines of twisted bird gut, or in nets of sinew from boats of stretched skin over frames of whale rib and driftbone. Drifted timber sometimes washes in, and it is the most precious thing on the shelf, hoarded, carved, and passed down.

**Cooking.** There is no fire to speak of. Nothing burns but bird fat and dried guano, and those are for lamps. Instead, the people cook with the ground. Food is lowered in skin bags or stone pots into the hot pools, or buried in the warm gravel at the bottoms of fissures. Each district has its cooking pits, communal and constant, where pots simmer all day.

**Light.** Lamps burn rendered bird oil in hollowed stone bowls, with wicks of twisted feather-down. Their smoke is greasy and fishy, and the insides of the stone houses are black with it. The tiny orange flames through doorways are the city's only warm light in the grey.

**Clothing.** Bird skins sewn feather-side in. It is never cold, but it is always damp, and the feathers shed water. Needles are bird bone. Thread is sinew.

**Trade and wealth.** Guano is the city's great export, scraped from the ledges in slabs and loaded onto skin boats for coasts beyond the haze where there is soil to feed. Feathers, oil, and dried birds go the same way. In return come timber, fibre, and metal: a few knives of soft bronze, owned by the rich and passed down as heirlooms. Most blades are still ground from the glassy black rims of the lava pits, which flakes to an edge sharper than bronze.

**Speech.** Because nobody can hear, much of the city's talk is done with the hands. There is a full sign language, fast and precise, used across the lanes and from roof to roof. Speech is for close range only: mouth to ear, or foreheads pressed together. Children learn to sign before they talk. People who leave the shelf for the quiet coasts find the silence frightening, and they find themselves shouting.

## The Rule of Stillness

Here is the strangest thing about the place, if anything is strange: nothing changes.

It isn't that nothing happens. Birds come and go endlessly, waves strike, steam rises. But the whole of it is a single held state. The heat never rises or fades. The vapour ceiling never lifts or thickens. The temperature of each pool is the same as it was in a grandmother's time. The colony does not grow or shrink. The birds arrive in the same numbers, take the same ledges, raise the same count of chicks, and are taken in the same proportion by the people and the gulls, year after year. The city does not grow either. It has reached the size the birds can feed, and it holds there.

And the rock does not wear. With no soil, no roots, no frost, and weather that is only ever warm rain and steam, there is nothing to grind the basalt down. Guano accumulates and is scraped away. The edges stay sharp. The shelf looks this morning as it looked when it first cooled, only with more walls on it.

The people describe their world in terms of this steadiness. Their word for the place, in sign, is a flat hand held level and pressed slightly downward: *held*. Their idea of time is not a road but a ledge that is always occupied. Their stories are not about change but about keeping. They keep the count of eggs, keep the ledge-rights of families, and keep the cooking pits clear. The worst thing a person can do, in their tellings, is take more than the birds give, and tip the balance. It has never happened, as far as anyone knows. It is simply the thing that is not done.

## Walking Through

Walk the city at midday and it is like this:

Grey light, shadowless. Warm damp air on your face, smelling of ammonia and sulphur and fish. Black walls pressing close on both sides, beaded with condensed water and alive with the heads of auk-birds peering from the cracks. A gannet-bird on the lintel of a doorway lunges at your hair. Underfoot, rough rock and a skin of guano. Ahead, a lane opens onto the rim of a sunken pit, its walls ringed with houses, its floor a milky pool steaming with a dozen cooking bags hung in it on sinew lines. Two women sign across the pit to each other, their hands quick in the haze. Beyond, a bird-tower rises, streaked white, its every hole a nest, the air around it thick with wings.

And over everything, the sound, unbroken: barking, laughing, buzzing, hissing, and from the coast, the long boom of the sea driving into the rock, again, again, as it has since the shelf was laid down and as it will, as far as anyone can tell, for good.
