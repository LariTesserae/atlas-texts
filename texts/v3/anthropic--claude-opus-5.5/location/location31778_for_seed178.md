# The Rising Country

It is dark here for most of the year. The first thing to understand is that it is never quiet.

---

## The Ground

The land is a broad, shallow basin about forty kilometres across, opening northward to a grey sea that is moving away from it. Twelve thousand years ago this was the floor of an ice sheet. Four hundred years ago it was the floor of a fjord. Today it is a lowland of raw gravel terraces, cracked basalt shelves, and outwash plains, and it rises out of the water by about a hand's width each month.

That is far too fast to be natural, and it isn't. It is also far too fast for the land to wear smooth. Nothing here has had time to round off. Beach ridges run in stacked parallel lines across the lowland like a written record: each one is a former shoreline lifted out of the sea, still sharp at the crest and still full of unworn cobbles and shells. The oldest, far inland, are only a few decades old. The newest are still wet. Ice-scoured bedrock shows through between them in whalebacks, striated and polished on the upstream side and plucked into jagged steps on the downstream side, the way the glacier left them. The rock sweats constantly. Meltwater and groundwater move through every fissure, so every rock face is a weeping face. In the cold the seeps freeze into curtains of grey ice, and those curtains break off with a sound like shattering crockery.

The ground cracks as it rises. Rebound is uneven, so long fractures open across the terraces overnight, a few centimetres wide and sometimes kilometres long. Water fills them at once. Sand boils bubble up where pressurised groundwater finds a new path, pushing out grey slurry in low domes that ripple and pulse. One morning a meadow can be split by a clean black line with water running at the bottom. By the next week the line has become a creek, and in a month the creek has a name.

## The Water

Rivers come down from the dwindling ice cap to the south, three large ones and hundreds of small ones, and none of them has a settled bed. They braid across the outwash in shifting sheets, splitting and rejoining around gravel bars that did not exist the previous season. Because the land is rising under them, they are always cutting down. Every channel has fresh, steep, crumbling banks where the turf hangs over like the lip of a loaf.

Every few weeks one of them floods catastrophically. A meltwater lake dammed under the ice breaks out, and a wall of brown water, ice blocks, and uprooted willow comes down the plain in the dark. People know one is coming about twenty minutes ahead. The birds leave first. Then the pitch of the river changes and deepens, and a vibration comes up through the ground into the soles of the feet. After it passes, the plain has been redrawn. Stranded icebergs the size of houses sit in the new gravel and melt for months, and small lakes form in the hollows they leave.

The air carries almost as much water as the ground. Onshore wind brings sea fog, which meets cold land-breath flowing down off the ice. The result is a nearly permanent condensing murk: drizzle in the warmer weeks, and in the colder ones rime that builds on every twig and stone as feathered white crystals growing into the wind. Mosses carry beads of it. So do the fur of foxes, the wool of anyone standing still for long, and the cables of the machines.

## The Growing Things

Given the cold and the dark, the vegetation is shocking.

The rising land is being colonised as fast as it appears, by plants that seem to have been told there is no time. Mosses come first: deep, spongy, saturated carpets of green, bronze, and rust-red, knee-deep in places. They swallow the gravel ridges within a few years. Sedge and cotton-grass follow into the wet hollows, then crowberry and bog bilberry, then willow and dwarf birch. On the older terraces inland, alder thickets close in so densely that people can only get through along stream channels or the trails that animals have trampled open.

The growth is in layers, everything close against everything else. Under the willow there is heath. Under the heath there is moss. Under the moss is a black, sodden peat that is already metres deep in places where it has no business being more than centimetres. Lichens crust every stone, and liverworts coat every seep. Where the rock weeps, the water supports hanging gardens of saxifrage, mountain sorrel, and ferns, all dripping.

In the warmest season the plain runs uphill in bands of colour, grey, green, bronze, then rust, following the terraces. The older the ground, the taller and darker the growth, so the vegetation reads like tree rings laid out flat. In the dark season nobody can see it at all. They smell it instead: rot and resin, sweet cold peat, the iodine tang coming off the kelp-choked shallows.

The reason for the growth is partly nutrients and partly design. The glacial flour is rich. And the machines, described below, leak something into the ground. Engineers once called it heat, and that was only partly true.

## The Animals

The animals came for the new land and stayed for the crowding.

Seabirds are the loudest and most numerous. Millions of auks, kittiwakes, fulmars, and gannets nest on the fresh sea cliffs, which are simply the edges of the most recently lifted rock. Because the cliffs keep rising and the sea keeps retreating, the colonies migrate outward every few years. The abandoned ledges above them turn bright green from guano, and turf rapidly overruns them. Geese graze the new marshes in flocks that cover whole terraces. Waders crowd the mudflats at the retreating tide-line: knots, dunlin, purple sandpipers, turnstones. When a raptor passes over, they lift together in dense twisting clouds, and the roar of their wings is like surf.

On the ground there are voles in huge numbers, tunnelling through the moss. Arctic foxes hunt them, and so do stoats, owls, and skuas. Reindeer move through the willow in winter, digging through the rime-crust for lichen with the steam rising off their backs. Seals haul out on the gravel bars in the river mouths, and the braided channels sometimes strand them far inland. Their barking carries for kilometres. Salmon and char run up rivers that change course between one spawning season and the next, so the fish arrive at gravel beds that have moved, or dried, or become lakes.

None of these animals is strange. They are ordinary northern animals. There are simply a great many of them, packed tightly, all under pressure from a country that won't stay still long enough to be learned.

## The Sound

Visitors arrive expecting the cold and the dark. They are rarely ready for the noise.

It comes in layers, and no layer ever fully stops.

At the bottom is the river: a continuous low roar of water and moving gravel. Boulders knock together on the riverbed with a sound like distant machinery, felt as much as heard. Above that is the wind, which pours down off the ice cap most nights as a katabatic gale, screaming through the willow scrub and around the stones. Above the wind are the birds. The colonies make a solid wall of noise, a shrieking, grinding, laughing roar that never pauses, even in the dark, because in the dark the auks are still coming and going to sea.

Then there is the ice. Out at the glacier front and in the stranded bergs, it cracks, groans, and calves. The larger calvings arrive as a flat boom a few seconds after the flash of spray, if there is any light to see the spray by. The ground joins in as well. When a new fracture opens, it goes with a sharp report like a rifle shot, and the long ones sound like a sheet tearing for several seconds. Freezing seeps shatter. Sand boils gurgle. Hail comes in off the sea and drums on the moss and the stones.

Over and under all of it are the machines.

People who live here long enough begin to speak in short, loud phrases and to rely on gesture. They sleep with wadding in their ears. Their children are born into the noise and seem not to mind it, and they find other places, when they visit, eerie and hard to sleep in.

## The Machines

They are scattered across the plain at intervals of a few kilometres, and they are the only real built presence here. There are perhaps sixty of them over the whole basin. People call them *keels*, because their visible part looks like the keel of an upturned ship driven vertically into the earth. Each is a tall, narrow blade of dark, faintly iridescent material. Some stand ten metres high and some fifty. They taper to a point and are ribbed along the edges. No one living knows how deep they go. They were placed three or four generations ago by a project whose records are incomplete, for a purpose that everyone agrees on in outline.

In outline, the keels are releasing the land. The weight of the old ice sheet pressed the crust down into the mantle. When the ice left, the crust began to rebound, but on its own it would have taken thousands of years. The keels accelerate the rebound. By some means current physics describes poorly, they act on the viscosity of the deep rock, or on stress across the whole regional crust, or on both. Some old documents describe them as "unclenching" the mantle. The land rises because the keels let it rise. They also redistribute the strain as they go, so the ground cracks where they choose and not where it otherwise would. A person standing close to one can feel it working as a slow pulse, too deep to be heard as sound, a pressure against the chest roughly every eleven seconds, coming up through the ground.

When they work harder, they sing. It is a long, rising, many-layered hum at a pitch that makes the teeth ache, and it carries farther than any sound on the plain, over the river, the wind, and the birds. Keel-song usually means a lift is coming: an hour later, the ground under one district will jump a few centimetres all at once. Water sloshes out of the pools, birds scream up off the ledges, and fresh cracks open in a spreading lattice.

They also give off light, and in this country that matters more than anything else about them. At the tip of each keel is a faint, steady, violet-white glow, dim enough to look at directly. Over the dark months these points are the only fixed lights on the plain, and people navigate by them the way sailors navigate by stars. The ground around each keel is warmer than the ground elsewhere, by a few degrees. It isn't much, but in each keel's neighbourhood the moss grows thicker and the willow grows taller than anywhere else on the terrace. Snow melts first there, and animals gather. Seen from above, each keel sits at the centre of a darker, denser circle of growth, like a bruise in the vegetation, a few hundred metres across.

The keels do not break down, and they do not respond to anything people do. Some have been swallowed by floods and sit in the middle of new lakes, still glowing under the water, with fish circling them. One has been tilted by uneven rebound to lean at thirty degrees, and it goes on working. Another sits on a fresh sea cliff with a gannet colony packed against its warm flanks, so that the whole blade is ringed in birds, feathers, and noise.

## The Other Structures

Apart from the keels, there is very little building. Along the older, higher terraces there are a few hundred people in scattered single steadings: low stone-and-turf houses dug half into the ground with thick walls against the wind and the noise, their roofs grown over with the same moss as everything else. Some huts are made of glacier-cobble. Some shelters are old cargo containers, and some of those have now been lifted many metres above the shoreline they were put down on. There are drying racks for fish and a few wind-scoured fences for reindeer. Here and there a stone cairn marks an old channel that no longer exists, or the height of the sea in some remembered year.

There is one harbour, and it has had to be abandoned and rebuilt four times as the sea withdrew. Its earlier piers stand in a line across the plain. The oldest is now high and dry among the alder, still ringed in dead barnacles, and willow grows up through its planks.

## Weather

The weather is always changing. It comes off the sea in fronts that arrive every few days, bringing sleet, freezing rain, and hail, and turning the fog to rime. It comes down off the ice as katabatic wind, cold, dry, and violent, peaking in the small hours. When the two meet over the plain, the air churns, and the fog tears apart in fast grey rags. Squalls cross the lowland in minutes. In the gaps the aurora sometimes shows: a green and violet sheet over the black country, briefly lighting up the rime on every branch, then gone again behind the next wall of cloud.

The polar night lasts about three months. For the rest of the winter there is a short, grey noon twilight, a few hours of light the colour of pewter. The summers bring the midnight sun and the only real light of the year, and they are brief, crowded, and frantic. Everything breeds, grows, and flowers at once, and the birds are loudest. People say even the keels seem to work harder in summer, though no one can prove it.

## How It Works

The rules of this place are simple and physical, and the people who live here know them in their bodies:

- **The land rises.** Nothing built stays at the level it was built on. Wells go dry and new springs open. Any path will eventually become a ridge, and any shore a slope.
- **The water finds new ways.** No river channel is permanent. People keep lightweight things they can carry. The best houses are built on the old, stable, high terraces, where the rivers have already finished cutting.
- **Listen to the keels.** Keel-song means a lift is coming and the ground will crack. The pulse is the land's heartbeat. If it stops, and people say it has stopped only twice in living memory, then something very large is about to change.
- **Follow the birds.** They hear floods before people do, and they feel lifts before people do.
- **Close to a keel is warm, dense, and crowded.** It has the best forage, the most predators, and the most noise.
- **In the dark, steer by the keel-lights.** They are the one fixed thing in the country, though even they are rising with it.

---

Someday, according to the old documents, the rebound will be finished. The land will have risen to where it would eventually have come on its own. The keels will fall silent and their glow will go out, and the country will begin to age normally. The rivers will settle, the cliffs will round off, and the peat will thicken slowly over centuries instead of decades.

No one living here thinks that day is close. For now it is a place of wet, black, crowded growth and endless change, lit only by violet points in the dark, where the ground cracks under the moss and the water roars under the birds, and the country pushes up out of the sea a hand's width at a time.
