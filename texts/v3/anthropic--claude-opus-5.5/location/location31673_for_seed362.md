# The Dry Works

## Approach

From a distance it reads as a plain, and it is one: low, gently undulating, never rising more than a few hundred meters above whatever lies beneath it. No one has found what lies beneath it. Up close the plain turns out to be machinery. Walk another hour and the machinery turns out to be alive. Walk a day and you stop being able to tell the two apart, because here they were never separate.

The whole surface, a region large enough to have its own climate and to curve visibly away at the edges when the air is clear, is one continuous growth that has been shaped, or has shaped itself, into a factory. Pale timber gantries run to the horizon in parallel ranks. Between them lie conveyor beds of woven root, flat and taut as canvas, moving at the pace of a slow walk. Stems as thick as chimneys rise in regular rows, each capped with a flared crown of thin translucent leaves angled precisely toward the sky. Nothing here is wild and nothing is inert. Every surface is bark, fiber, husk, resin, or leaf, and every surface is doing a job.

## The Light

The sky is white. It isn't overcast. It is lit evenly from horizon to horizon, as though the whole dome were one enormous diffuse lamp. There is no sun to point to, and no night. The brightness is cool, the flat clean light of a north-facing studio, and it is the only fuel the Works uses.

The leaf-crowns drink it. You can see it happen. Where the light falls on a crown, the leaf tissue goes faintly darker and faintly warmer, and a tremor runs down the stem beneath it, a peristaltic ripple that turns into rotation lower down, where the stem ends in a knuckle of dense wood seated in a socket of woven root. The knuckle turns. It turns the axle it is grown onto. The axle turns a spindle, a belt, a press, a hundred meters of slowly revolving drum. Light goes in at the top and comes out at the bottom as torque. There is no intermediate stage, no sap, no reservoir. The plants do not store anything. They transmit.

Because the light never stops, the Works never stops.

## No Water

There is no water, and there never was any. The wood is bone-dry, dry enough that a fingernail drawn across bark leaves a pale scratch of powder. The plants have no liquid metabolism. What moves inside them is starch dust: fine, cold, white, finer than flour. It flows through hollow stems and open channels the way liquid would flow elsewhere, pouring, pooling, finding its level. Where a channel has cracked, a thin white stream spills out and runs downslope in a silent rivulet that fans into a delta of powder on the belt below. The belt carries it off. Nothing is wasted, and nothing is ever wet.

The cold matters. At any real warmth this much suspended starch would be a bomb waiting for a spark. The Works runs cool, a steady chill a few degrees above frost, and the air always tastes faintly of cereal and dust. Breath doesn't fog. There is no moisture in it to condense.

## The Weather

Over a surface this large, the dust makes its own weather.

The leaf-crowns shed. Every crown sloughs its outermost layer on a cycle of some hours, and the layers come away as sheets of tissue finer than paper, which the gentle updrafts over the drive-stems catch and lift. They accumulate high up into something like cloud: vast slow banks of drifting leaf-skin, faintly translucent, dimming the white sky to pearl as they pass. When a bank grows heavy enough it comes down, not as rain but as a dry snowfall of flakes that settle on the belts and are carried to the reclamation mills and pressed back into stock.

Starch-dust squalls roll across the lower levels, low white curtains a few meters high that smell sweet and leave every surface powdered. Pollen fronts come too. Somewhere in the Works, on some schedule no one has decoded, entire districts flower at once. The pollen is gold, and it moves across the plain in a single hazy wall a hundred kilometers long, turning the light amber for a day. Where it passes, new growth follows within the week: fresh gantries, new belts, a line of drive-stems where yesterday there was a flat bed of root.

The weather is the Works maintaining itself. The Works is also its own weather.

## Sound

It is almost silent.

A machine this size ought to roar. It doesn't, because nothing in it is hard enough to clatter. Wood turns in root sockets packed with starch powder, which lubricates like graphite. Belts of woven fiber slide over rollers of polished heartwood with a sound like a sleeve brushed against a coat. Big presses close with a dull *hff* of compressed air and nothing more. The loudest sustained sound is the dust: a soft continuous hiss, like sand in an hourglass magnified to the size of a country.

Under that, close to the stems, you can hear the light going in. It is a thin creak, the sound of green wood flexing, repeated in every stem in slightly different rhythm, so that standing in a grove of drive-stems is like standing in a very quiet rigging. Now and then a seedpod ticks as it seals. That is the whole soundscape. People who come here talk in whispers without deciding to.

## The Wear

The Works is not new. It is old in the way a well-used staircase is old.

Every surface that moves against another has been polished to a satin gloss. Rollers are grooved with shallow channels worn by millions of revolutions. Gantry rungs, the ones something climbs, and it is unclear what, are hollowed in the middle into smooth saddles. Everywhere there are abandoned lines: whole avenues of machinery that have stopped, their drive-stems gone grey and leafless and their belts slackened and silvered by light. Newer growth has grown over and through them without removing them. So the Works is layered, stratified. Newer mechanism rides on the backs of older, and older rides on older still, and cross-sections where something has split show dozens of generations of factory compressed into bands like tree rings.

It has settled into itself. Nothing looks raw, and nothing looks like it is being built for the first time. It looks like a place that has been running so long it has worn into the exact shape of its own function.

## Density

Everything is close. The gantries stand a few meters apart. Belts run beneath belts beneath belts, and you can lie on one and see three more crossing overhead at different angles, each carrying its own cargo in its own direction. Drive-stems stand thick as a stand of bamboo. There are no clearings, no plazas, no open ground. There is only the narrow space between mechanisms, and that space is itself usually a mechanism: a channel, a chute, a slowly turning spiral stair of grown wood that exists to move dust from one level to another.

To travel you ride. You step onto a belt and go where it goes, then step off onto a crossing belt, and so on. The Works is a map drawn in motion. It has no roads, only routes, and the routes are changing.

## What It Makes

At the end of every line, whatever feeds in, the Works makes the same thing: seedpods.

They are hard ovoid husks of dense brown wood, from the size of a fist to the size of a house. They come off the final presses sealed, with a seam around the middle like a walnut. The belts carry them outward toward the edges of the Works and set them down in the grey buffer zones where the machinery thins out. There they sit.

Break one open, and it has taken people days with saws, and the inside is bigger than the outside.

The difference isn't subtle. A pod the size of a melon can hold a room. A pod the size of a cart can hold a gallery with a vaulted ceiling you can't see the top of. The interiors are always the same: pale wood, empty, lit by the same white sourceless light as the sky, and cool. Sometimes, far in, there are the beginnings of machinery, a single drive-stem or a short length of belt that isn't moving yet.

This is the rule of the world, and once you understand it, everything else follows. The Works manufactures interior. It grows space inward and packs it into husks. The whole plain is a mill for making *more room*.

## Where Reality Gives Way

Because it makes space, the space here doesn't hold still.

Distances don't add up. You can ride a belt east for an hour, turn north for an hour, west for an hour, south for an hour, and arrive somewhere you've never been. You can ride straight and arrive where you started. The Works is full of old seedpods, some of them enormous, long since grown over and incorporated. Every so often the route you're on passes through one without your noticing. You step off into a district far larger than the plain could possibly contain. This is why a low, bounded region has weather like a continent's. Much of its area is folded up inside itself.

Shadows are wrong. With light from every direction there should be almost none, and mostly there aren't. But in certain districts objects cast faint shadows *toward* the brightest part of the sky, as though the light were being pulled out of them rather than falling on them. Those districts run fastest. Their drive-stems turn so quickly the leaves blur.

The growth rings are wrong. Where old timber has split, the rings can be counted, and the count keeps going past the outermost bark. More rings, faint and pale, extend out into the empty air around the cut, visible only at a slant. Some of the people who have studied them believe they record years that have not happened yet. Others believe the Works isn't growing forward in time at all, only outward, and the rings are simply a record of how much more room it intends to take.

Sounds sometimes arrive early. The soft *hff* of a press closing is heard a second, two seconds, before the press moves. Nobody has found a pattern in it. It is unnerving the first few times and then becomes just another texture of the place, like the dust.

And at the far edges, where the finished pods are set down, the land is not land. The buffer zones thin into a pale, level nothing, a floor without material that is somehow supported. The pods rest on it. Past a certain point, it is simply the inside of a pod: that same pale wood and cool light, stretching away. It is not clear whether the Works sits on a surface or inside a husk, or whether that question means anything here.

## Life That Isn't Machinery

Almost everything alive here is the Works. Almost.

There are moths. They are grey, broad-winged, dusted with starch so heavily they look like they're made of it, and they are the only thing that eats. They feed on the drift of shed leaf-skin in the high cloudbanks and come down to lay eggs in the cracks of abandoned lines. Their larvae chew the old grey wood to powder, which spills onto the belts and is carried away and reused. They are the Works' decomposers, if it needs any. At low density they're easy to miss. You see one, pale against the pale sky, and then not another for a day.

There are tallymen: long, jointed, stick-thin beetles, bark-colored, that walk the belts against the direction of motion at exactly belt speed, so that they appear to stand perfectly still while the Works slides beneath them. Occasionally one stops and taps a roller or a socket with its forelegs, three times, and moves on. Wherever a tallyman has tapped, a new stem sprouts within the month. Nobody knows whether they inspect the Works, instruct it, or are simply a part of it that happens to have legs.

And there is, reportedly, something larger. Very rarely, a traveler finds tracks in fresh starch dust: broad, soft, four-toed prints spaced far apart, leading into a district and not out. The few who claim to have seen it describe something the size of an ox, pale and slow, that stops beside the largest seedpods and lays its head against them, as though listening. Then it is gone, and the pod it was leaning on is open, and empty, and bigger inside than any pod has been before.

## Being There

What it is like, finally, is this: cool air that smells of bread and dust; a white sky with no source; everywhere the slow, silent, polished motion of wood against wood; belts beneath you carrying you somewhere you didn't quite choose; the soft creak of stems drinking light; and underneath everything, the sense that the place is enormous in a way that has nothing to do with how far away the horizon is. The Works is not large because it covers a large area. It is large because it is *making* itself larger, quietly and patiently, one sealed husk at a time. It has been doing so long enough that the wear has set into grooves, and it shows no sign at all of stopping.
