# The Hollow of Many Mouths

It is not large. You could cross the floor in forty steps, and the dome closes perhaps three times a tall man's height overhead. But it feels enormous when you first stand in it, because it is almost entirely empty. It is a made space, a single breath of air held inside a shell. Nothing grows in the middle of it and nothing stands there. The floor is bare and faintly concave, and the walls lean inward until they meet.

Everything here was made by hand. There is no bedrock underfoot, no native soil, not a single stone that arrived without being carried. The floor is fired brick laid in a spiral, each brick thumb-marked on its underside. The walls, and this is the first thing that makes the place strange, are built of jars.

## The Walls

Tens of thousands of unglazed clay jars are stacked on their sides, mouths facing inward, packed in a mortar of lime and crushed shell. From inside, the wall is a honeycomb of round dark openings, each about the width of a fist, rising in ranked courses up and over into the dome. The jars are not uniform. Some are squat and wide-bellied, some long-necked. Some were thrown on a slow wheel, and some were coiled and smoothed with a wet palm. The ones near the floor are old enough that their rims have softened into rounded lips. The ones high in the dome are sharper, as if the builders learned their craft while climbing.

Every jar is a small cave, and every cave is occupied.

## The Water

The hollow sits at the edge of a marsh, low enough that the ground outside is always sodden. Air comes off the reed flats cool and heavy and seeps through the jar-wall itself, because unfired lime and porous clay breathe. Water condenses constantly. Where the outer air meets the cooler inner surface, beads gather on every jar lip and slide inward and down. The walls weep. They are never dry and never streaming. Each surface holds a skin of moving water about as thick as a fingernail.

This water has shaped the place for a very long time. The mortar between the lowest courses has been carved into shallow grooves, and the grooves lead downward in branching patterns like the veins of a leaf. At the base of the wall a channel runs all the way around the room, cut into the brick. Its edges are worn so smooth they look melted. The channel slopes, almost imperceptibly, toward a single drain on the eastern side, which is a large jar sunk mouth-up into the floor. Water enters it with a sound like a held note.

The builders understood the water, and I think they built for it. Each jar is tilted a few degrees downward, just enough that condensation gathering inside will run out rather than pool. Drips fall from the rims of the upper jars onto the jars below, and from those onto the ones below them. The whole wall is a slow cascade.

## The Sound

The hollow is loud. It is never silent and never quiet.

The drips come first. There are thousands of them, falling from lip to lip, and each strikes a jar of a slightly different size and wall thickness. So each drip rings at its own pitch: a high tick, a deep *tunk*, a hollow bell-like *toom*. Individually the notes are small. Together, heard from the center of the floor where the dome gathers them, they make a continuous shimmering roar, like rain on a lake heard from underneath the surface. It shifts constantly. When the marsh air grows wetter, the rhythm quickens and the pitch climbs. On dry days, which are rare, the sound thins to scattered notes and the room seems to listen for itself.

The jars resonate. Speak a word in the middle of the floor and dozens of mouths answer. Each one takes the part of your voice that matches its size and holds it a moment too long. A single hum becomes a chord. A shout comes back as a crowd. I suspect the builders made it this way on purpose. The spiral in the floor bricks ends at the precise point of greatest resonance, marked by one brick darker than the rest and worn into a hollow by feet. Stand there and the whole building speaks your name back to you with ten thousand throats.

The animals are loud too.

## The Inhabitants

Life here is abundant, and nearly all of it lives in the jars. Vegetation is sparse. Plants have little to hold onto in a place without soil. Moss rims some of the lower jar mouths in soft green collars, fed by the constant damp. A few small ferns have rooted in cracked mortar near the drain, their fronds curled tight. A pale, hair-thin lichen grows in rings on the upper walls where a little light reaches. That is nearly all. Everything else here eats, calls, flies, or crawls.

**Swiftlets** nest in the high jars of the dome, hundreds of them, gluing cups of spit and marsh fiber to the curved inner walls. At dawn and dusk they pour out through the opening at the crown, a ragged hole where the builders either stopped or deliberately left the top unfinished. When they return they come in all at once, a dark rush, shrieking. Their calls hit the jars and multiply until the room is a screaming storm of small voices. They also click in the dark, a dry rapid ticking by which they find their own nests among thousands of identical openings.

**Bats** hold the middle courses. They are small leaf-nosed creatures who hang inside the jars by day. At night their clicks thread through the swiftlets' silence, too high to hear but not too high to feel. It comes as a pressure, a faint itch at the edge of hearing, and the jars pick it up and half-ring with it.

**Frogs** live in the lowest jars and in the channel. They are small and grey-green, and they cling to the wet clay with wide toe pads. Their calling begins at dusk and does not end until dawn: a chorus of *gronk* and *peep* and a strange rising trill. The jars amplify it into something almost mechanical. The frogs seem to know which jars suit their voices. Each one calls from its own mouth, the same one every night, and old jars with especially rich resonance are fought over. The losers call from worse jars and are heard less.

**Beetles, crickets, and pale cave-spiders** fill the gaps. The crickets sing in the dry-ish middle courses, and their stridulation rings in the clay like tapping on glass. The spiders string webs across jar mouths, and the webs catch drops as well as insects. In the dim light they glitter like beaded curtains.

**A single owl**, grey and ancient-looking, occupies one large jar near the western wall, set at eye height, wider than the others. Perhaps it was built for her ancestors. She hunts the frogs and the dropped swiftlet chicks. When she calls, a low doubled hoot, the whole building takes it up and turns it over and over until it sounds like the hollow itself is breathing.

Predators and prey move constantly through the honeycomb. A snake, thin and brown, travels through the wall in a way that looks impossible. It slides into one jar and out of another several feet away, because behind the visible mouths the jars touch, and old cracks connect their bellies. The inside of the wall is a labyrinth. Things live in there that are never seen, only heard, scraping and chittering between one jar and the next.

## The Light

Light is dim and comes from above. The crown opening admits a grey column on overcast days, and most days are overcast. It never reaches the walls directly. It lands on the center of the floor as a soft pale disc and scatters, weak and wet, into the room. The jar mouths remain dark. You see their rims as pale rings, hundreds of them, lit faintly from the front, each with a black center. It is like standing inside an eye made entirely of pupils.

At midday, if the sky thins, a brighter shaft sometimes drops through and catches the falling drips. For a moment the air is full of tiny slow sparks, and then the clouds close again.

## The Weathering

This place has stood a long time. It is not a ruin, but it has settled into itself. The lowest jars have fused with their mortar into a single porous grey mass, and you can barely tell where clay ends and lime begins. Mineral deposits have grown from the upper rims in short stubby fingers of white. These are thin stalactites of dissolved lime, and on some of them the drip no longer falls onto the jar below but onto the tip of a stone that the water itself made. These have their own sound, a sharper, glassier *tick*.

The floor spiral has worn unevenly. Its outer turns are almost pristine, but the inner turns, near the resonant center, are hollowed by feet. The builders walked the spiral inward, again and again, over generations. That wear is the only direct evidence that anyone ever lived here. No tools remain, no hearth, no bones. There are only the thumbprints under the bricks, the worn path, and the jars.

Some jars have cracked and stopped ringing. Some have been colonized so thoroughly by swiftlet nests that their mouths are nearly sealed. Many have been lost, and none have been replaced. Still, the place holds its balance. The water carries away lime from the lower courses and deposits it on the upper ones. Bat and bird droppings wash into the channel and feed the frogs' insect prey. The frogs feed the owl and the snakes. The dead feed the beetles. Nothing here is growing, and nothing is collapsing. The system turns over and over, like the sound.

## The Strange Part

Here is the thing it took me longest to understand.

The sound and the water are not separate. When the room is loud, with the frog chorus at its height, the swiftlets returning, and the owl calling, the walls weep more. The drips quicken. You can see it: a shout in the center of the floor sends a visible shiver down the wet faces of the jars, and moments later the drip-roar swells. Vibration seems to shake condensation loose, or draw it out of the air, or both. The louder the room, the wetter it becomes. The wetter it becomes, the louder it rings.

On nights when every voice is raised at once, the hollow enters a kind of feedback. The dripping becomes a downpour from the walls, the channel fills and runs audibly, and the drain jar at the east side begins to sing. It holds a long, low, unwavering note that rises out of the ground and fills the whole space. The animals fall silent one by one, frogs first, then crickets, then even the swiftlets in their nests. For a while there is nothing but that single enormous tone, the water, and the dark rings of the jar-mouths watching.

Then it fades. The channel slows. A frog starts again, then another.

I do not know what the builders intended. Perhaps it was a cistern that learned to sing, or an instrument built to be played by rain and animals, since they knew they would not always be there to play it. Perhaps the spiral path and the dark brick at the center were for standing in that note when it came, to be filled by it. What remains is a small, hollow, made place at the edge of a cold marsh. It is dim and wet and crowded with voices, and it is louder than anything built by hands that could only shape clay has any right to be.
