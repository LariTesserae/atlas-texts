# The Settling Ground

## The Bowl

You come to it from above, because there is no other way to come to it. The plateau of black spruce and lichen runs flat for days in every direction, and then the land simply lets go. A scarp, raw and angular, drops fifteen, twenty, in places forty metres into a basin so wide that its far rim is a grey suggestion under the cloud. The edge is fresh. You can see it in the soil profile along the break: the pale sand, the rust-banded clay, the dark ribbon of old peat, all cut clean as a slice of cake, with spruce roots hanging out of the face like torn wiring. Some of the trees along the lip lean outward over nothing. One or two have already gone over and lie head-down on the talus, still green.

The whole basin is sinking. It has been sinking for sixty years, since the seams beneath it were worked out and the pillars were robbed and the roof was allowed to come down behind the retreating face. The land above followed the roof. It does not fall. It settles, a few centimetres a year, sometimes a hand's width after a wet autumn, and the ground surface of an entire valley now sits well below the country around it. Standing on the rim, you are looking down at a place that is slowly going underground.

## The Ground Is Plant

There is no bare earth in the bowl. Where the old surface subsided, water that used to drain away now lingers, never deep, never standing for long. The land is not a lake or a marsh. It is damp in the way a sponge left on a cold windowsill is damp. Moss took the chance. Sphagnum first, then feather moss over it, then hummock after hummock of peat building itself from its own dead lower half. The basin floor is a continuous living mat, a metre, two metres, in the older hollows perhaps three, laid over the sunken mineral soil like a quilt over a body.

Everything grows out of the moss or into it. Dwarf birch, knee-high and wiry. Labrador tea with its rusty undersides. Crowberry, bog rosemary, cloudberry leaves the size of a palm. Where the old spruce forest dropped with the ground, many trees drowned slowly at the roots and now stand as grey poles furred with beard lichen, so that parts of the basin look like a forest of masts. Others adapted. They lean and layer, sending lower branches down into the moss, where the branches root and send up new stems, and a single tree becomes a ring of trees becomes a thicket. Lichen covers everything that is not moss: the dead poles, the fallen trunks, the concrete, the steel. Reindeer lichen in pale mounds, map lichen on the stones, orange *Xanthoria* on anything a bird has perched on.

Walk across it and your boot sinks to the ankle, then finds something, then sinks again. The surface is springy, cold through the sole, and wherever you step a faint dark water rises around the print and slowly withdraws. There is no path. Paths close. A track driven through in summer is a green seam by the next year and gone the year after.

## The Cold

It is always cold here, colder than on the plateau. The bowl collects cold air the way it collects water. On still nights the air slides down off the rim and pools, dense and motionless, and by morning every twig in the lowest hollows wears a sleeve of hoarfrost. Frost comes early and stays late. Even in the brief summer the moss a hand's depth down is near freezing, and in the oldest hummocks there are lenses of ice that never fully thaw. Permafrost is being born here, in miniature, under the insulation of the peat. The ground is making itself colder.

Breath hangs. Fingers stiffen within minutes. The light never quite warms anything.

## The Light

The light is dim, and it is dim for several reasons at once. The latitude is high, so for much of the year the sun runs low along the southern horizon and never clears the rim of the bowl. The basin floor lives in the long shadow of its own edge. There is also cloud, the usual low stratus of the north, and there is the plume.

What light there is, is grey-blue and sourceless. Colours are muted to olive, rust, bone. Distances flatten. You cannot tell whether a dark shape across the moss is a thicket two hundred metres away or a boulder twenty metres away until you are almost upon it.

## The Sound

Before you see the fans you hear them. Before you reach the rim you hear them. On the plateau, kilometres out, there is a pressure in the air that you take at first for weather, a wind that never gusts or drops. Closer, it becomes a roar, and in the bowl itself it becomes the world.

The mine is not dead. The seams are exhausted, but the workings go on beneath the basin for kilometres, galleries and roadways and collapsed goafs full of gas, and methane will not leave on its own. So the ventilation continues. Three main shafts remain open, spaced far apart across the basin, and at each one a great axial fan draws air up out of the earth, day and night, winter and summer, without pause, as it has for decades. The fans are the only reason the bowl is not slowly filling with firedamp. The company that dug the mine is long gone. A small authority keeps the fans running now, because the alternative is worse.

Each fan sits in a housing of riveted steel and concrete the size of a barn, and each discharges through an evasé, a flared stack like an upturned trumpet, angled toward the sky. The noise they make is not a hum. It is a vast, broadband, grinding exhalation, a jet engine that has been idling since before your parents were born. Close to a shaft it is physically painful. It enters the chest and the teeth. You cannot speak to anyone beside you, cannot hear your own footsteps, cannot hear your own thoughts in words. Hearing protection is not optional. Without it you are deaf within the hour.

The bowl makes it worse. The sunken ground and the steep raw scarps act as a reflector. The sound comes back off the rim and off the far rim and meets itself, so that nowhere on the basin floor is truly quiet. Between the shafts, in the middle distance, the three roars overlap into a single oceanic noise with slow beats in it, a throb where two frequencies drift in and out of phase. People who worked here say the beat has a period of about eleven seconds and that after enough years you breathe to it.

Under the roar, if you stand in exactly the right place, you can sometimes feel more than hear the deep machinery: the motor, the bearings, a knock every revolution in the oldest of the three that has been there so long it is on no one's list anymore.

## The Breath of the Mine

Mine air comes up at a steady temperature, the temperature of rock a few hundred metres down, eight or nine degrees whatever the season. Up here, in this cold, that is warmth. And it is wet, saturated from the seepage in the galleries. The moment it leaves the evasé it meets the freezing air of the bowl and condenses. Each shaft wears a plume, a column of white vapour leaning downwind, sometimes rising straight up for a hundred metres on a still day, sometimes flattened along the moss in a long grey river.

This is where the water in this dry-cold place mostly comes from. Downwind of each shaft there is a tongue of land, a few hundred metres long, that lives inside the plume. It is the dampest ground in the basin and the most luxuriant. The moss is deeper and greener. Ferns grow here that grow nowhere else for a hundred kilometres. The dead spruce are clad an inch thick in lichen. In winter everything in the plume path is encased in rime, feathered ice that grows windward from every twig and wire and fence post until the whole tongue of land is white and bristling, a frozen breath laid across the dark moss. When the fan's direction of discharge shifts even slightly with the season's winds, the rime shifts with it, and over decades each shaft has painted a broad pale fan-shaped stain of rich growth onto the landscape. From the rim, on a clear day, you can see all three: three soft green comets pointing east.

## What Was Built

The structures are few and far apart. Between them is a great deal of moss.

The three fan houses. Concrete bases, cracked where the ground has settled unevenly beneath them, shimmed and re-levelled many times, the steel housings patched in several generations of paint: company green, then municipal grey, then a primer red that nobody got around to covering. Each has a transformer yard fenced in chain-link, the fence leaning, the gravel inside it slowly vanishing under moss.

The power line. It comes in over the rim from the plateau on wooden poles, crosses the basin in long sagging spans, and branches to each shaft. The poles stand at odd angles now, some tilted by the settling ground, a few propped with timber braces and a few with newer steel ones. The line itself sings faintly in wind, though you will never hear it over the fans.

A headframe, long disused, over a shaft that was capped. Steel lattice, a wheel still at the top, the whole thing slightly out of plumb. Ravens use it.

The ruin of a pithead bath building, roofless, its concrete floor a garden of moss and birch. Rows of rusted locker frames stand in it like the ribs of something.

A narrow-gauge railbed runs part way across the basin and then dips, gently and completely, beneath the peat. The rails can be followed by probing with a stick. In one place the ground has settled so that a short section of track rises out of the moss in an arch, sprung up by the uneven sinking, like a staple pulled half out of wood.

Survey monuments. Dozens of them, scattered across the whole bowl: steel pins in concrete, each numbered, each once level with the ground, each now standing proud of the moss on a little pedestal of exposed concrete or sunk out of sight beneath it, depending on how the ground around it has moved. Someone comes once a year and measures them. That is how the sinking is known.

And the odd artefact the peat has not yet swallowed: a drum, a coil of cable, a cast-iron cover plate, a car door, a boot.

## What Lives Here

Not much, and not often. The noise sees to that. Most animals avoid the bowl, and those that stay have learned to live half-deaf or to live at the quiet edges.

Voles live in the moss by the thousand, but you will never see them. They run in tunnels under the living surface.

Willow ptarmigan come down into the basin in winter, drawn by the dwarf birch buds, sitting in white clusters in the rime fields where they are nearly invisible.

Ravens own the headframe and the fan housings. They seem indifferent to the roar. They tumble in the warm plume on cold days, riding the rising mine air, and they have been seen landing on the evasé lips to warm their feet.

Once in a long while a moose crosses, belly-deep in the birch scrub, ears flat.

There are no songbirds to speak of. Nothing that depends on hearing a mate's call can hold territory here.

In the damp plume-lands, in summer, there are mosquitoes, fewer than you would expect, and the tiny moss mites and springtails that make up most of the animal life of any bog, in numbers beyond counting, unbothered by any sound.

## How It Changes

Slowly. Everything here is slow except the air.

The ground goes down a finger's width a year. The moss grows up a finger's width a year, more in the plumes, less in the cold hollows. In some places the two rates match and the surface stays where it is, a living thing treading water. In others the sinking wins and a new hollow opens and fills with dark water and then, over a decade, with moss again. In still others, over the older workings where the collapse has finished, the peat is slowly winning, and the bowl is filling from the bottom up.

The scarps stay sharp because the edge keeps moving outward. Every few years a new crack opens in the plateau a little beyond the old rim, and a new strip of spruce forest begins its slow tilt into the bowl. The basin is young and it keeps renewing its youth by growing.

The fans will run until the authority decides the gas is spent or until the money is, and no one expects either soon. When they finally stop, the roar will go out of the bowl all at once, and for the first time in a century you will be able to hear what the place actually sounds like: wind in dwarf birch, the creak of the frost, water moving under moss. People who worked here say they cannot imagine it. Some say they would not want to hear it.

For now the bowl breathes out through its three iron throats, its breath freezes on the trees downwind, the moss grows toward the light, the ground goes down toward the dark, and the noise goes on and on over all of it like weather.
