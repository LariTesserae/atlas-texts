# The Drowned Kiln

*a lowland without light*

---

You know you have arrived because the air changes texture before anything else does. It thickens. It becomes warm the way a mouth is warm, humid enough that the skin of your face grows wet without rain, and the darkness, which was merely darkness a moment ago, gains a grain. It has a nap like velvet brushed the wrong way. Move your hand through it and it resists slightly and then gives, and you feel the direction it was lying.

There is no light here. No starlight or glow, not even the phosphenes the eye invents when it is starved. People who have walked out of the Kiln say the worst part is not the blindness but the discovery that their eyes had been making things up all their lives, and here they stop. The eye gives up on this place. You learn it through your feet, your skin, your ears, and the long patient attention of your body to warmth.

---

## The Ground

The land is new. You can feel this before anyone tells you. The rock underfoot has no softness of age. It is glassy, a black obsidian-like slag broken into plates and ridges with edges that still cut. Nothing has rounded them yet. Walk carefully and the soles of your boots will learn the country: long shallow shelves that tilt a few degrees toward some unseen low point, fields of brittle blisters that pop underfoot like thin ice, sudden seams a hand's width across where warm water stands a finger below the lip.

The shelves are warm, not hot, about the temperature of a body. The warmth comes up from below in slow pulses that last hours, so that a stretch of ground you crossed at blood-warm might be merely tepid when you return. The rock sweats constantly. Condensation beads on every surface, gathers in the fractures, and threads downhill in rivulets you can hear only if you kneel and put your ear near the ground. The whole region drains toward something by degrees so gentle that you would need a lifetime to find where it all ends up.

Water is never far away. The seams hold it. Hollows in the slag hold it in warm shallow basins, some no larger than a washbasin and some that your hands cannot find the far edge of. The water is faintly mineral, faintly metallic, like licking a coin. Sometimes it is fizzy against the skin. In the larger basins it is stratified: warm on top, cooler a forearm down, and then, if you reach far enough, suddenly warm again, as if the pool had a second surface beneath the first.

Nobody knows how deep the deepest pools go. A weighted line dropped into one of the big basins near the eastern engines was reeled out for an hour and came back dry and dusty at the end, as if it had been lowered into a cellar.

---

## The Engines

The land was made by machines, or it was uncovered by them, or both. The accounts disagree.

Scattered across the flats, far apart, stand the pumping engines. Nobody walks to one on purpose. You find them the way you find a warm stove in a dark house, by drifting toward heat. First the air becomes drier and smells of hot oil and old iron. Then your outstretched hand meets a railing, riveted and furred with condensation, and beyond the railing is a massive body of cast metal, too large to embrace, radiating a heat that is dry and pleasant against the wet air.

They are beam engines, or something like them. You can trace by touch the great iron walking-beam overhead, the cylinder as broad as a cottage, the brick housing crumbling at its corners and patched in places with sheet metal. Thick cables run away from each engine along the ground, sheathed in something like tarred cloth. They lie across the slag for miles, and if you lay a palm on one you feel a faint current in it, less a shock than a tingling persuasion.

The engines still run. They run *slowly*. One stroke of the great beam, down and then up, can take a day. You can stand beside an engine for hours and feel nothing but the heat and a vibration too deep to call a sound, and then gradually become aware that the iron above your head has moved. It has dropped a hand's breadth since you arrived. Somewhere inside, a valve sighs, and the sigh takes twenty minutes to complete.

What they pump is not certain. The intake pipes go down into the rock, and the outflow pipes, where you can find them, are dry. They exhale nothing. The oldest story is that the engines were built to drain a sea, and they did, and the land rose black and raw out of where the water had been. But the engines were never told to stop. They went on pumping, and when the sea was gone they began drawing out whatever came next in the order of things. First the water, then the light.

Put your ear to an outflow pipe and you hear nothing at all. It is a different nothing from the ordinary quiet, the way a closed door is different from a wall.

---

## Things That Live Here

For a place without light, the Kiln is crowded. This is the second thing everyone notices.

You are never alone for more than a few breaths. Something brushes your ankle, then your wrist. Small soft bodies land on your shoulders and walk across your collar and lift away. The air over the warm shelves is thick with moths. They have no reason to be moths, since there is no light to draw them, but they are, powdery and feathery-antennaed. They drift toward warmth, so they drift toward you, and within an hour of walking you will be wearing a shifting coat of them, a dry soft weight that lifts and resettles with every step.

In the seams and pools there are frogs, thousands of them. They are silent almost all of the time. Their skins are slick and hot, and when you kneel at a pool's edge you can feel them bumping against your fingers, curious, pressing their throats to your skin as though listening to your pulse. Once in a great while, perhaps once in several days, they all sing together. It is a single low pulse across the entire flat, lasting a few seconds and then gone. People who have heard it say it arrives from every direction at once, and that afterward the ground feels slightly further away than it was.

There are eels in the larger basins, long enough that one passing your hand takes a full minute to finish passing. There are crabs with glass-thin shells that click faintly over the slag in migrations you only notice when you sit down and discover you are sitting in their river. There are bats, or things that behave like bats, that never make any sound you can hear but leave a pressure on the eardrum as they pass. Near the cables there are warm-blooded creatures the size of cats with fine short fur and a great many legs, which gather on the tarred sheathing in rows, bellies pressed to the current, dozing. They do not mind being touched. They have the same faint tingle as the cables.

The animals are unafraid of you and not especially interested. You are one warm presence among many. The density is what stays with you: the sense that every cubic foot of this air, every hand's width of pool, is occupied by something breathing, feeding, pressing close, layered upon itself in the dark like leaves in a compost heap.

---

## What Grows

Very little. The slag is too young and too clean for soil, and there is no light to feed anything green.

What does grow grows along the cables and around the bases of the engines, where warmth and current seep into the rock. It is a kind of moss, or felt, damp and springy, clinging in patches that seem to follow the buried paths of wires. Where it grows thickest it forms cushions you could lie on. It tastes of salt and pennies. Rumor says it feeds on the current itself, and that if you follow a moss trail far enough you will reach an engine even when you cannot feel any heat.

In the seams there are pale filaments like root hairs without any plant attached, trailing in the warm water, knotting slowly into mats. They seem to be reaching toward something. Over years, those who return say, the mats have been drawing long lines across the flats, faint and fibrous, all bending the same direction.

---

## Sound

It is quiet here, mostly. The quiet is warm and close, like being under a heavy blanket.

The sounds you do hear are small: the tick of condensation, the whisper of moth wings against your ear, the creak of a slag plate settling as it cools another fraction of a degree, a single frog's throat-click, water moving somewhere below in the fractures. The engines' long sighs are felt more than heard.

But sound misbehaves. Your footsteps sometimes return to you from ahead instead of behind, a few paces in front, as if someone were walking your route before you. If you call out, the echo comes back from a place where there is no wall, and occasionally it comes back altered: your voice saying a word you did not say, or saying yours in a tone of mild surprise. Two travelers speaking side by side can find their voices arriving in the wrong order, the answer before the question. The old hands in the Kiln learn to speak rarely and briefly, and to stay touching someone's sleeve.

---

## How the Place Behaves

In the Kiln the darkness is more than an absence. It has behaviors.

**Distance is unreliable.** A basin you crossed in twenty paces takes two hundred on the way back. A cable you were holding a moment ago is no longer within reach, though you never let go. The land does not seem hostile about this. It is closer to how a dream rearranges a house. Maps drawn by touch contradict each other, and every map is accurate for the person who drew it.

**The shape of the land follows your memory of it.** This is the strangest rule, and the one travelers argue about most. Some say the ground forms itself just ahead of your foot out of what you expect to find, and that if you walk with confidence you walk on firm slag, but if you hesitate the next step may meet warm water or nothing at all. The engines are the exception. They are always exactly where they are, stubborn and unforgetting. For this reason everyone who walks the Kiln navigates between them, engine to engine, the way sailors once went from lighthouse to lighthouse.

**The dark accumulates.** In the low places where the land drains, it seems to pool. Walk downhill and the grain of it grows thicker until it is like wading through warm cloth, then like wading through fur. The moths do not go there. Neither do the frogs. Nobody who has pushed far into the deepest low ground has described what lies at the bottom, only that at a certain point they found they had been turned gently around and were walking uphill again without remembering the turn.

**Heat is the only compass.** You go toward warmth and away from cold. The warmth moves in long slow tides beneath the slag, across months and years, and the animals follow it. When the warmth shifts east, the whole living crowd of the Kiln slowly drifts east with it, a migration so gradual that someone who lived here all their life might see it happen only twice.

---

## The Remains

Besides the engines, there is little that was built. You might find, by stumbling on it, a length of narrow-gauge rail with no ties beneath it, half sunk in glass. You might find a shed of corrugated iron, its door open, a stool inside and a tin cup on a nail, both beaded with water. Survey stakes stand in lines across the flats, each capped with a brass plate engraved with numbers you can read with your fingertips. The numbers do not increase in sequence. They count down, toward some zero that has not been reached.

At one place, it is said, there is a gauge on a post beside an old engine, glass-faced, with a needle you can feel through a crack in the glass. It measures something in units no one recognizes. The needle has been moving, very slowly, toward the end of the dial for as long as anyone has been coming here. When it arrives, the stories disagree about what happens. Maybe the engines will stop. Maybe they will reverse, and whatever they have drawn out will come back, and this black raw country will fill again with water or with light, or with something else entirely that the pumps have been holding.

For now, the beam comes down. Hours pass. The frogs press their throats to your fingers. The moths settle on your shoulders like a second coat. The slag cools a fraction of a degree, sharp-edged and sweating. And the Kiln goes on being what it is: warm, crowded, patient, newly made, and without any light at all.
