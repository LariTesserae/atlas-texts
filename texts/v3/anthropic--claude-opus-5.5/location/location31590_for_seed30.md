# The Singing Saddle

## The Crossing

You climb for most of a day through ordinary mountain country. The granite is old and rounded, grey with lichen, split by frost into boulder fields. Heather grows between the stones, and here and there a stunted rowan. Everything has the softness of a place that has been weathered for a very long time.

Then, just below the col, you cross a line.

It is almost exactly a line. On one side the granite is pitted and rust-stained and crusted with map lichen. On the other the rock is new. It is black-green and faintly glassy, with sharp edges nothing has touched yet. It is warm, like a flank. When you press your palm to it, you feel a fine continuous ticking, the sound of stone still settling into itself.

This is the Saddle. It is not large. From the lip you could throw a stone most of the way across the bowl, perhaps two hundred paces from wall to wall. The whole of it would fit inside a cathedral with room left over. But it is filled, always, with cloud and with noise.

## The Cloud

The cloud lives here. It pours over the eastern rim in slow grey falls and pools in the bowl like milk in a cupped hand. It rarely lifts. The light that reaches the floor is pearl-coloured and sourceless, as if you stood inside a lamp with its wick turned very low. The sun, when you can find it, is a pale coin you could look at directly. Shadows are soft. Distances beyond forty paces go to rumour.

Nothing in the Saddle is dry. The cloud condenses on every surface, and the water runs. It beads on the glassy stone, gathers into threads, and threads join into rivulets that pour down the walls and across the floor in a thousand small braids. The rivulets are warm, about blood-warm, and they leave something behind as they go.

Watch any wet surface for an hour and you will see it. A skin forms, pale grey at first, then darkening. Ribs and drapes build up like flowstone in a cave, but at a pace you can follow with your eyes if you are patient. A pebble set in a rivulet at dawn is fused to the floor by noon. By dusk it is a smooth bump. By morning you cannot tell where it was.

This is how the Saddle is made. The cloud is carrying stone.

## The Masts

There are five of them, though in the fog you rarely see more than two at once.

They rise from the floor of the bowl at uneven intervals, tall and very thin. At the base each is the width of a man's thigh. At the top it narrows to a finger's width, then to a thread, then to something the eye cannot hold, so you cannot say where the mast ends and the sky begins. They are not metal. They have no seams, bolts, or joins. Their surface is a deep oily darkness with faint iridescence moving in it, like the sheen on a beetle's wing seen through smoke.

They hum. Close up, the hum is below hearing, a pressure in the sternum and the back teeth. Farther away it becomes a note, one steady tone the whole bowl is built around.

The cloud moves toward them. You can see it happen: grey tendrils curl inward, drawn and combed, spiralling slowly down each mast and dispersing at the base in a fine warm drizzle. Whatever the masts do to the cloud, they load it with mineral, or perhaps they unfold the mineral out of it. Around each mast's foot the fastest growth happens. The stone there rises in fluted skirts, still soft enough to take a thumbprint if you are quick.

No one living remembers who raised them. They look like they were placed here recently, and they look like they will never fall.

## The Rule of the Place

The stone listens.

The deposition is not random. It follows pressure, and pressure means sound. Where sound waves meet and reinforce each other, stone is laid down. Where they cancel, the water runs clear and leaves nothing. The whole bowl is an instrument that records, very slowly, whatever is sung in it.

You can see this in the floor. It is not flat but patterned in concentric ridges and interlocking curves, like sand on a drumhead or frost on a pane. The long, low swells are the masts' own hum, laid down over years. The finer ripples are something else.

Speak loudly on the floor of the Saddle and, within an hour, a faint crescent of new stone will have risen around the place you stood. Shout and the crescent will be a ridge. The few people who come here learn to whisper. They walk softly, and they do not sleep on the bare floor, for fear of what their own snoring might build around them.

Near the southern wall is a flight of steps. They are uneven and oddly proportioned, each riser a slightly different height, and they climb the wall to a ledge where the view opens on clear days. Someone sang those steps into existence, one note per stair. You can still hear the melody if you walk them in order and listen to how your footfalls ring.

## The Birds

Most of the singing is not human.

The Saddle is loud, relentlessly loud, and the noise is swifts. They are small, dark, and sickle-winged, with pale throats, and there are thousands of them. They nest in the new stone of the walls, in cavities that pock the upper slopes like the holes in a honeycomb. They stream through the fog in shrieking parties, invisible until they are suddenly all around you, a rush of wind and cries, and then gone into the grey again.

The swifts have learned the rule of the place.

A particular call, a rapid, rising trill repeated by many birds together, grows ledges. Where a colony trills in chorus at dusk, a lip of stone forms beneath them overnight, just wide enough to nest on. Over generations they have tuned the walls to their own voices. The upper bowl is terraced with thin shelves and pocked with round hollows sized exactly for a swift's body, arranged in patterns that echo the shapes of their songs.

At dawn the whole colony wakes together, and the sound is enormous. It is a high, sawing, many-voiced roar that fills the bowl to the brim and makes the fog shiver. In the half-hour after the dawn chorus, the stone grows fastest. The walls creak and pop audibly as new layers set. Steam rises off the fresh rock. The rivulets run thick and milky.

In the middle of the day the swifts rest, and a strange hush falls. The ticking of the stone becomes audible again, along with the drip and trickle of a thousand rivulets and the masts' low tone underneath it all. It is the only quiet time. Then, in the afternoon, the hunting begins.

## The Hunters

Falcons work the Saddle. They are slate-backed and heavy-shouldered, and they appear out of the fog without warning, stooping through the swift flocks in steep dives.

When a falcon is sighted, the swifts give their alarm call. It is harsh and grating, a sound like tearing cloth, taken up by hundreds of throats at once. That sound has its own architecture. Wherever the alarm has rung out repeatedly, the stone grows spines: thin needles and jagged combs of rock that jut from the walls at odd angles. The swifts can thread them at speed. The falcons cannot.

So the geology of the Saddle is also a record of predation. The eastern wall, where the falcons most often come over the rim, bristles with thickets of stone thorns, some as long as a forearm and fine as glass. Walk beneath them and they ring faintly in the wind. The western wall, sheltered and seldom hunted, is smooth and softly terraced, all ledges and hollows.

Other lives cluster in the warmth.

Small black salamanders, no longer than a finger, live in the rivulets. Their skins are speckled with the same grey mineral that coats everything, and they seem to eat the film itself, grazing the new flowstone before it hardens.

Pale moths are drawn to the warm walls at dusk. They settle in dense furred patches that twitch and resettle. The swifts feast on them in the last light.

Spiders string webs between the stone thorns. The webs catch the condensing cloud and gather mineral too, so the oldest are no longer silk but lace of stone, brittle and beautiful. If you brush one it shatters with a sound like a dropped wineglass.

Snails, colourless and translucent, drag across the damp floor leaving trails that briefly shine.

## What Grows

Very little grows here. The stone is too young for soil.

In the wettest rivulets there are cushions of moss, a startling electric green, the brightest colour in the Saddle. Lichen appears only as a faint bloom on the oldest surfaces near the rim, thin as a coat of paint, pale orange and sage. In a few cracks on the western wall grows a single kind of fern, small and silvery-fronded, that seems to have come from nowhere. Its roots grip the new rock directly. The swifts line their nests with its fronds.

That is all. Mostly the bowl is bare stone, water, fog, and birds.

## The Hut

Near the base of the northernmost mast stands a hut, or what was one.

It was built of the old granite, dressed and carried in from below the line. It had a slate roof and a heavy door. The new stone has been growing up its walls for a long time now. It has swallowed the doorway entirely and climbs in smooth grey waves to just under the eaves, so the building looks half-sunk in a frozen sea. One window remains clear, a small square of wavy old glass. Through it, in the dimness inside, you can make out a table, a chair, and on the wall a grid of chalked markings.

Beside the hut, on a pedestal of new rock that has grown up to cradle it, is a slab of polished grey stone set at an angle like a lectern. It is carved with notation: rows of marks that might be musical, might be mathematical, might be both. Some of the marks have been partly filled in by the creeping flowstone, their edges softened. Others are crisp. Along one edge, in a smaller hand, someone has scratched a later addition. It is a transcription, surprisingly accurate, of the swifts' ledge-trill.

The builders came here to tune a mountain. It seems they meant to make something specific: a shape, a place, perhaps a shelter or a tower or a road. The masts would lay it down according to their voices. Then they left, or stopped, or were overtaken. The birds took up the work.

## Storms

When weather comes over the ridge in earnest, the Saddle changes.

Thunder is the loudest sound the bowl knows. A close strike rolls between the walls and hangs there, and the masts flare with a sheen that shivers along their lengths. In the hours after a storm, walls grow. Whole new ribs of stone sweep across the floor in great curving bands, following the paths the thunder took as it rang around the bowl. They are low at first, knee-high, still steaming, and they will be hip-high by the end of the week. There is one near the western edge from a storm the swifts still remember. They avoid its shadow.

After a big storm the rivulets run so thick with mineral that they look like milk. The salamanders gorge. The moss glows. The birds are silent for a full day, and the silence is so strange that the few people who live below the line say they can feel it from the valley.

## Becoming

The Saddle is growing. Slowly, but you can measure it.

The walls rise perhaps a hand's breadth a year, faster where the swifts are thickest. The bowl deepens as its rim lifts. The line where old granite meets new stone creeps outward and downhill, a finger's width at a time, swallowing heather. In a century or two there may be a peak here, a new summit taller than its neighbours. It will be ribbed and terraced and bristling with thorns, carrying in its layers the whole history of what was sung over it: the masts' single tone, the dawn chorus, the alarm calls, the thunder, a few whispered human words, and one flight of uneven stairs.

Nothing here has been worn down. Nothing has had time. Every edge is sharp, every surface is wet and warm and faintly ticking, and the air is full of voices shaping the ground they stand on.

Stand very still on the floor of the Saddle at dawn, when the swifts begin. Keep your mouth closed. Feel the stone under your boots shift and swell, answering. You are standing on a song that has not finished being written.
