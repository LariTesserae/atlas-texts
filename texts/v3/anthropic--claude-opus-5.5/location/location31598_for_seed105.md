# The Held Plateau

It takes a while to understand that you are underwater, because nothing here behaves as though it is drowned.

The ground is a high tableland of broken slate and old granite. It rises in long shelves toward a rim of peaks you can only half make out, dark shapes against a darker dark. Hollows fold into it, low saddles open between the ridges, and a valley slopes gently away to the east. It has the look of mountain country above the treeline, the kind of place where wind should be hissing through dry grass. But what fills this country is water, and it fills it completely. There is no surface above you, or none near enough to matter. The water goes up past the peaks and keeps going. Somewhere very far overhead, a faint grey pallor suggests that light exists and has chosen not to come down.

The water is fresh, and it is cold, a few degrees above freezing. It is the particular cold of meltwater, and it gets into the joints of the stones and holds there. It is also heavy. Moving through it feels less like swimming than like walking in a dream where the air has thickened. Everything that falls here falls slowly, turning end over end as it goes.

## The Forests of Standing Stems

The plateau is green, or it would be green if there were light to prove it. In the dimness the plants read as shades of slate and bruise, with here and there a filament giving off its own faint cold glow.

The dominant growth is a plant the place seems to have invented for itself. Call them tallstems. They root in the cracks between slabs, where old soil has packed in over centuries, and send up single hollow stalks. Each stalk is ringed every arm's length with a small translucent bladder full of gas, and the bladders hold it upright in the water column like a string of lanterns with nothing in them. On the sheltered shelves the tallstems stand in groves. Some reach forty or sixty feet, so high that their upper reaches vanish into the murk, still climbing toward a light they will never reach. Near the top they unfurl ribbon-leaves, long and flat and faintly ribbed, which stream in whatever direction the current pulls.

Between the groves, the rock is carpeted with a dense, spongy growth: mosses, or something that took the place of moss. It grows in cushions that are cold to the touch and spring back slowly after being pressed. Beneath them run pale threadlike roots that knit the loose stones together. This is why the slopes do not slide. The plateau's slow erosion has been negotiated over a long time, and the living mats hold the scree in place, stone by stone, in a truce that has lasted longer than any structure here.

In the deepest hollows, where the water is stillest, grow the lampweeds. They are low, frond-like, and branching, and along their edges runs a thin bioluminescence the colour of weak moonlight on snow. They are the only real light on the plateau. Seen from a ridge, the hollows glow faintly, like the embers of fires that went out a long time ago.

## Nothing That Moves on Its Own

There are no animals. No fish hang in the groves and no crabs pick along the slabs. No worms tunnel the mats, and no small drifting creatures cloud the water. It is not that they are hiding. Nothing here was ever eaten, and the plants carry no marks of grazing. Their leaves are whole and unchewed, edged only by the slow fraying of current.

This absence shapes everything. Seeds are not carried by anything, so they rise in the gas bladders. When a tallstem's upper bladder ripens, it detaches and floats upward, and seeds drift out of it as it climbs. Whole seasons of seeds go up into the dark and come down elsewhere, and the plateau reseeds itself from above like snowfall in reverse. Dead growth is not eaten either. It settles and mats down into a dense brown peat that slowly fills the lowest basins, so the ground is gradually made higher by what dies.

The water is so clean of life that it has an odd clarity. In the rare places where there is light, you can see a very long way, and what you see is completely still apart from the plants. Every motion here is current, or growth, or falling.

## The Breathing

The plateau is not quiet, and it is not still.

Twice a day, perhaps (it is hard to count days here), the whole column of water breathes. A pressure-swell comes down from far above, and the water grows heavier and colder and presses into the land. Every tallstem in every grove bows under it together, their bladders compressing, their stalks leaning over like grass in a gust. The mats flatten. Then the swell eases, the pressure lifts, and the plants rise again all at once.

The bladders are loud as this happens. Under compression they creak, and as the pressure releases some of them pop and vent small bursts of gas that rise in silver chains. A grove in the release sounds like distant applause, or like rain on a tent, a crackling that spreads out across the plateau in waves as the swell moves through.

Currents follow the breathing. Cold water pours down through the saddles between the peaks in real rivers of current, fast enough to hum. The passes have been scoured smooth by it. In the narrowest gaps the water moves hard enough to make the loose stones knock against each other, a deep rattling clack that carries. Sound travels far and fast underwater, so the plateau is always full of these noises from somewhere: the rattle of stones in some distant pass, the groan of a slab settling, the crackle of the groves. Beneath all of it, too low to hear so much as feel, is a hum coming up from the rock itself, as if the mountains were resonating with the weight above them.

When the big swells come, which happens every few weeks, the whole plateau rings. The pressure change is sharp enough that the granite shelves sing: long bell-tones that roll out across the valley and slowly fade. After a great swell, the lampweeds in the hollows flare briefly brighter, all at once, then dim again.

## The Marks

Someone was here, or is here, or was here in a way that has not entirely stopped.

Across the plateau, far apart and never grouped, there are made things. They are simple: a ring of stones set upright on a shelf, each one chosen for its shape and wedged firmly into the ground. A low wall of stacked slate curves along the lip of a hollow, dry-laid with no mortar, the kind of wall a shepherd might build to break the wind. Here and there a cairn stands, stones piled carefully so the largest is at the bottom and the smallest on top, though the water should have made balancing easy and so they did not need to be built with such care.

The strangest are the lines. Between some of the upright stones, and in places strung across the mouths of the passes, there are cords. They are twisted from plant fibre, tallstem fibre by the look of it, stripped and rolled into rope, and knotted at intervals in patterns that repeat but do not quite match. The cords run taut in the current. Where they cross the passes they thrum when the water moves, a note that changes with the speed of the flow. Some of the plateau's sounds come from them: low, steady, almost musical drones from the strung gaps.

There are tools, too. A flaked stone blade lies on a flat rock beside a heap of stripped stalks, as though whoever was stripping them had set it down meaning to come back. A shallow bowl ground into the top of a boulder holds a few hard seeds. A flat stone near one of the walls has been worn smooth and scored with fine parallel lines, as if something had been sharpened or counted there many times.

None of this is ruined. Nothing is broken, collapsed, or buried under peak. The cords are not rotten; some of them look recently twisted, pale and fresh. And the knots change. A line strung across a pass will have seven knots one visit and nine the next, and a stone in the ring will have turned slightly to face a different way. There are no footprints, no bodies, no bones, and nothing that could make any of it. The absence of fauna holds everywhere, and still the work goes on.

The mats grow up around the bases of the stones and stop just short of them, leaving a clean margin of bare rock, as if something keeps them trimmed back. Around the cairns, the tallstems lean slightly inward, even in still water.

## The Shape of It

Standing on the high shelf near the rim, you can see the plateau as a whole, or as much of it as the darkness allows. The glowing hollows are scattered like a thin constellation. The groves are dark and rise and fall with the breathing. The passes rattle and hum, and the strung cords drone in them. Far below, the valley falls away east into deeper, colder dark, where the peat is thickest and the lampweeds give out. Above, the water climbs without end to that faint grey that might be sky or might be ice.

It is a mountain country drowned so long ago that it no longer knows it is drowned. It has grown its own forests, its own seasons of upward-falling seed, its own weather of pressure and current. It is loud with the sounds of stone and gas and rope. It is empty of every kind of animal, and still, somehow, tended.

The cold does not change. The dark does not lift. Each time the swell comes, the groves bow and rise, the seeds go up, the old stones sing, and somewhere on the plateau another knot has been tied.
