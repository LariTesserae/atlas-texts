# The Threshing Highland

## The Floor

Begin with the ground, because there isn't any. There hasn't been bare soil here for longer than there have been continents elsewhere.

The highland is a plateau so broad that its edges are a theory. Its surface sits at about the height where the sky turns from blue to something harder and whiter. What covers it, what *is* it, is a dry living mat called the sward. It is a felted, interlocking mass of wiry stems, papery sheaths, and root-cable, compressed by its own weight into a surface that gives slightly underfoot, like walking on a mattress stuffed with dry grass that has been pressed for a thousand years. Cut into it with a blade and you find the layers. At the top is a hand's depth of living growth, pale gold and silver-green. Below that is a brown, fibrous stratum where last century's growth is still half alive. Below that it grows darker and denser until, a few meters down, the sward becomes something like lignite, dense and black and glossy, the fossil record of the plant eating its own past and building on it. No one has dug to the bottom. It may not have one. The plateau may be vegetation all the way down to whatever rock it first colonized, which the plants long ago dissolved, drank, and forgot.

There is no water on the surface, and almost none in the air. Nothing is wet. Dew never forms; the air is too thin and too thirsty. The plants here do not run on water the way ordinary plants do. Their sap is a thin amber resin, oily and slow, which does not freeze in the cold and does not evaporate in the dry. What little true water exists is held inside living bodies, sealed away in waxed cells and hoarded like coin. Crush a stem and you smell turpentine, dust, and a sharp electric tang, never green wetness.

The erosion here is old erosion. The plateau is not young and raw. Wind has been scouring it for so long that the sward has learned the wind's shape. The whole surface is combed into long, low parallel ridges, kilometers long and knee-high, all running the same direction, like the grain in wood or the nap on velvet. From altitude the highland looks brushed. Where the wind splits around some obstacle the ridges part and rejoin in slow, perfect curves, and these patterns have not changed in ten thousand years. The land is violently active and completely settled at once: every storm moves across it the way a hand moves through fur, disturbing everything and changing nothing.

## The Engines

The sward is continuous, but the things that rise out of it are few and far apart. That is the first thing the eye learns here: vast, bright, flat distance, and then, at intervals of ten or twenty kilometers, a *presence*.

The locals, if there were any, would call them engines, because that is what they are. They are single plants, or colonies that behave like single plants, and they are industrial in every sense except that nobody built them.

**The bellows-trees** are the most common. Each is a squat, barrel-shaped trunk as wide as a cathedral and perhaps sixty meters tall, crowned with a ring of enormous pleated leaves that fold and unfold like an accordion. They breathe. With a sound like a foundry furnace being stoked, a vast roaring *whoom*, the pleats contract and drive air down through the hollow trunk and out through vents at the base. The air goes into the sward, pressurizing the root-mat, pushing gases through kilometers of fibrous channels. Then the pleats open again with a long, dry, rattling inhale. A single bellows-tree cycles about once every forty seconds, forever. You can hear one from fifteen kilometers away. Standing within a kilometer of one, you feel each exhale in your sternum and the ground pulses beneath you like a drumhead.

**The rotor-lilies** grow in scattered stands of three or four. Each is a tall, rigid stalk topped by a flower with long, twisted, stiff petals that catch the wind and spin. They are fast, fast enough that the petals blur and scream, a sustained metallic shriek that rises and falls with the gusts. As they spin they rub against a collar of fine hairs at the top of the stalk, and the friction builds charge. The rotor-lilies are generators. The whole stalk carries current down into the sward through fibers slick with conductive resin. On a gusty day a stand of rotor-lilies crackles with blue coronas, and the air around them smells of ozone so strong it stings the eyes.

**The threshers** are the rarest and the loudest. Nobody would mistake one for a tree. A thresher is a low dome, a kilometer across, ridged and ribbed like the back of a great beetle, and it is covered in shutters: vast bark-plates that open and slam closed in rolling waves across its surface. The sound is artillery, a continuous rolling bombardment that goes on for hours, *crack-crack-crack-crack*, echoing off nothing because there is nothing out here to echo off. Nobody is certain what the threshers do. They seem to take in dust, chaff, dead sward, dried insect bodies, and the fine silica grit that blows in from wherever the plateau ends, and they process it. What comes out of their downwind vents are seeds: hard, dense, pearl-white pellets that pour out in rivers and are carried off by the wind across the whole highland. The threshers are factories. They are the reason the sward is endless.

Between these presences there is only the sward, gold and silver, brushed flat, running to the horizon under a white sky. The spacing is exact. No two bellows-trees grow closer than about twelve kilometers apart. The rotor-lily stands never cluster. The engines are spread across the highland the way pieces sit on an enormous board, each with its territory, each linked to the others by roots that run so deep and so far that the whole plateau may be one machine with scattered moving parts.

## The Light

It is bright here, brutally and constantly bright. The air is thin, the sky is pale, and the sward reflects almost everything. The mature leaf-sheaths are coated in a fine silica bloom, a white powder, like the frost on a plum, that shields them from the ultraviolet. At noon the highland glares. The ridges gleam in long parallel stripes of light, and the horizon dissolves into a shimmer that is not heat haze but the dazzle of a billion tiny glassy surfaces catching the sun.

It stays cool despite the light. The temperature rarely climbs above the kind of crisp chill you get on a clear high mountain morning. The brightness brings no warmth, only clarity. Shadows are hard-edged and blue-black. The bellows-trees cast shadows kilometers long in the morning, and these sweep across the sward like the hands of great clocks.

There is also the other light. The rotor-lilies build charge all day, and the sward carries it, and the charge has to go somewhere. In the afternoons, when the wind rises, the highland begins to spark. Tiny blue discharges crawl across the tops of the ridges. Coronas flicker around the tips of the tallest stems. By evening the discharges get bigger, and then the lightning starts.

## The Weather

This is a highland with weather of its own, and the weather is made by the plants.

The bellows-trees drive air down into the sward and push it out again kilometers away, through vent-fields of hollow reeds. The expelled air is warmer than the surrounding atmosphere, and dusty, and charged. It rises. Every bellows-tree has a slow column of rising air above it, and every vent-field has a thermal. Between them the atmosphere churns. Above the highland, dust-clouds boil up into white towers that never rain because there is nothing to rain. They are made of pollen, silica grit, and seed-dust, lit from inside by lightning.

The storms are dry storms. They come nearly every day, sweeping across the plateau in walls of pale dust hundreds of kilometers wide. They are loud beyond description: wind screaming through the reed-fields, the rotor-lilies howling at full speed, thunder that never stops, and under all of it the bellows breathing and the threshers firing in their endless barrage. The lightning here is not a flash and then darkness. It is a continuous nervous flicker, a web of discharge spreading from cloud to sward and sward to cloud, so constant that on storm nights the highland is almost as bright as it is at noon, a flickering white-violet glare.

The sward does not burn. There's too little oxygen at this height, and the resin is fire-resistant, and the silica bloom insulates. Lightning strikes the ridges ten thousand times a night and leaves only faint glassy scars, fulgurite threads fused into the living mat. Over time these accumulate. The deep layers of the sward are shot through with glass veins, like the old strata are threaded with frozen lightning.

The storms rearrange nothing. They blow themselves out by dawn, the dust settles, and the ridges lie exactly as they were, combed in the same direction, gleaming. Everything is violent and nothing is broken. The plateau is in storm-state as its normal state.

## The Sound

Nothing here is quiet, ever. The deafness is the first and last thing anyone would know about this place.

Even on the stillest morning there is the breath of the bellows-trees, a pulse felt through the ground from dozens of kilometers away. Every tree cycles at its own slightly different rhythm, and they drift in and out of phase, so the whole highland throbs with a slow irregular heartbeat, surging when several trees exhale together, fading when they fall apart. Over that, the whir and shriek of rotor-lilies. The dry rustling hiss of the sward itself, billions of stiff stems ticking against each other in the wind, a sound like static or like a huge crowd whispering. The distant hammering of a thresher at the edge of hearing. Then the fauna, which add their own layer.

When the storms come, all of it rises to a roar that has no gaps. Sound becomes a physical environment, a pressure you move through. The animals here are mostly deaf, or hear only through their feet, or communicate with light and vibration instead. The ones that do call scream at frequencies that cut through the storm: high, piercing whistles and clicks, like metal on glass.

## The Fauna

For a place so bare to the eye, it swarms.

The sward is thick with small life. There are **chaff-beetles**, flat and wedge-shaped, bronze and dusted white, living in their millions in the upper fibrous layer, grazing on dead sheaths, and emerging in shimmering waves when the storms stir the mat. There are **spark-moths** with wings dusted in conductive scales, which feed on the charge itself. On storm nights they gather at the rotor-lilies in clouds, lit up blue by coronas, flickering, and the lightning that strikes the stands sometimes kills thousands of them at once. Their bodies fall like ash and are fed to the threshers.

There are grazers: the **drovers**, long-legged six-footed animals the size of large deer, with wide splayed feet for walking on the springy sward and heads that are mostly jaw. They move in loose herds, hundreds at a time, but always widely spaced, each animal twenty or thirty meters from the next, as if they obey the same spacing law as the engines. They crop the sward in long rows parallel to the wind ridges. They have no ears, only huge flat pads on their feet that pick up the ground's vibration. They talk to each other by stamping. A herd communicating is a rolling percussion that joins the rest of the noise. Their water, all of it, comes from the sealed cells in the sward. They are dry animals. Their dung is dust.

Above, in the thermals, there are **gliders**. These are huge, paper-winged creatures with spans of twenty meters, bodies little more than a keel and a mouth, which ride the columns of rising air above the bellows-trees and skim the pollen from the dust-clouds. They circle for days without landing. When a storm builds they rise with it, up into the lightning, and some of them are always burned out of the sky and fall, spinning slowly, to the sward.

There are **thresher-mites**, tiny and red and in their trillions, living inside the threshers' shutters. They clean the processing surfaces and are periodically expelled with the seed-rivers, so that each flow of white pellets is flecked with red.

The highland's one great predator is rarely seen. It is called the **stilt**. It is tall and thin and grey, almost invisible against the glare, standing motionless on legs like reeds among the reed-fields. It hunts drovers by feeling their stamping through the ground and walking toward them, slowly, in perfect silence against all that noise. It is the only quiet thing here. Some say the drovers can feel its silence as a gap in the vibration, and that is how they know to run.

## The Rules

A few things are true here that are not true elsewhere.

Nothing rots. Decay requires water, and there is none, so everything that dies simply dries and is either compacted into the sward or fed to the threshers. The highland recycles by grinding, not by decomposing. It is a mill, not a compost heap.

Charge is currency. The rotor-lilies generate it, the sward conducts it, and it seems to drive the pumping of the bellows-trees and the shutters of the threshers. Energy flows through the living mat like power through a grid. Where lightning strikes, the sward grows faster. The storms are not a disaster for the plants but their harvest.

Spacing is law. Every large thing keeps its distance. The engines, the herds, even the stilts. No one knows whether this is a matter of roots, or of the charge fields, or of something the sward itself decides. But the highland is always sparse, always spread out across its enormous brightness, and it will not tolerate crowding.

The whole plateau breathes. If you could see it from far enough up, the bellows-trees' rhythms would resolve into waves, slow pressure-pulses spreading out across the sward and interfering with each other. Some people believe the highland is one organism, the engines its organs, the root-mat its body, its storms its thoughts. It has been thinking, loudly, in lightning, for a very long time.

## The Horizon

Stand on a ridge at dawn, after a storm has cleared. The sky is white-blue and enormous. The air is cold and thin and tastes of ozone and resin. The sward runs gleaming to every horizon, brushed into its long parallel lines, glittering with silica and with the fresh glass scars of the night's lightning. A bellows-tree breathes somewhere to your left, and you feel it in your chest. Far off, a thresher starts up its bombardment. A herd of drovers stamps a greeting. A rotor-lily begins to spin, slowly, then faster, then screaming. Above, a glider turns in a thermal, lit gold by the early sun.

Nothing here was built. All of it is machinery. It is dry and bright and cold, vast and nearly empty, and the whole highland roars.
