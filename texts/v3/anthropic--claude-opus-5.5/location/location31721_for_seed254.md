# The Culvert Undercroft at Tanners' Fall

It is a single vault, and it is full.

From one end to the other it runs perhaps sixty paces. The brick barrel arches maybe nine meters overhead at the crown. Everything the city above once needed to keep underneath itself has been packed into that space. There is a river, or what is left of one after it was bricked over and told where to go. There is a steam main, a pumping station and a sluice-house. There are eleven small buildings, walls touching, lining a single lane the width of a handcart. And there is a quantity of green so complete that the brick is only a guess in most places. It comes back in patches, where the heat is too strong for anything to hold.

It is warm down here in the way a laundry is warm. The air is thick and faintly mineral, heavy enough that it seems to have weight on the tongue. Nothing is dry. Nothing has been dry for a very long time.

---

## The Vault

The ceiling is old brick, laid in English bond, gone the color of wet liver. The mortar between the courses has mostly dissolved and been replaced by moss. Seen from below, the whole arch looks upholstered, a dark quilted green stitched in brick-colored lines.

Every few meters the vault is pierced by a pavement light. These are iron grids set into the street above, each holding dozens of thick glass prisms, the kind once used to throw daylight into coal cellars. The glass has turned violet with age and is crusted on the underside with calcite and algae. What comes through is not sunlight so much as a rumor of it: small grey-lilac cones that descend and dissolve long before they reach the floor. The footsteps and wheels of the street above pass across them as brief eclipses. There are no people down here, but the light flickers constantly with people somewhere else.

Between the prisms hang the lamps. They are industrial sodium fixtures in wire cages, wired on a circuit that nobody seems to have told to stop. About a third of them still burn. Their orange is low and syrupy, and it pools rather than spreads. Each working lamp has gathered a fern-beard around itself, a hanging mass of fronds all tilted toward the bulb like a crowd around a fire. The dead lamps have nothing. You can map the whole electrical history of the place by where the green is thickest.

So the light is two colors that never quite mix: cold violet from above, warm amber from the side. In between is a dimness you adjust to slowly, over the course of perhaps half an hour. After that you begin to see how much is really here.

---

## The Water

The river enters at the high end through an arched culvert mouth. It is fast, brown-black, and loud, and it drops almost two meters over a stepped stone weir. That weir is the Fall the place is named for. Below it the water runs in a channel down the full length of the vault, confined between granite kerbs polished to the texture of soap by a century and a half of flow. At the far end it goes through the sluice-house and away into the dark.

That is the obvious water. The rest is everywhere.

The vault weeps. Rain from the city above percolates through soil, cobbles and brick, and arrives here hours or days later, hard with dissolved lime. It seeps from every mortar joint. It leaves soda straws hanging from the ceiling, hollow calcite tubes as thin as pencil lead, some of them a meter long, each with a single drop trembling at its tip. Where the straws hang over the lane, the ground beneath them has built little white rimstone cups. The dripping never stops, and it is never quite in rhythm.

The steam main adds more. It runs the length of the north wall, an iron pipe as thick as a man, lagged once in asbestos and canvas, now lagged in moss. It is hot to the touch at its joints. At each flanged coupling it leaks a thin, persistent plume, and the plumes condense on the cooler brick nearby into sheets of running water. Along that wall the brick is streaked like a cheek that has been crying for decades.

And the floor is not really a floor. Under a crust of living plants lies a mat of dead ones, fronds and leaves and roots laid down year upon year. It is saturated, springy, black, and slightly warm. Step on it and water rises around your boot and recedes when you lift it. The cobbles are down there somewhere. You would have to dig to prove it.

---

## The Sound

It is loud, and the loudness has layers.

The deepest is the Fall itself. A river forced through a stone tube and dropped onto stone steps makes a sound that is less a noise than a pressure: a continuous, chest-level roar that the barrel vault catches and returns and catches again. Conversation would have to be shouted. Most of the time you would not bother.

On top of the roar sits the pump. In the sluice-house at the low end, a steam-driven beam engine still works. It has been converted at some point, badly and permanently, to take its heat off the main. It runs on the water level. When the sump behind the sluice fills past a mark, a float valve opens and the engine begins to move. Its stroke is slow, about six beats a minute. Each stroke is a sequence: a hiss of admission, the long groan of the beam, a heavy wooden thud as the pump bucket bottoms out, then the gush of lifted water pouring into a higher channel. Then a pause, and again. It will run like this for an hour or two, sometimes most of a day after heavy rain upstairs, and then the float drops and it stops. The silence afterward is only relative, because the Fall never stops. But the absence of the pump is so marked that it feels like a held breath.

Then there is the steam main, which knocks. Condensate collects in its low sections, and when the pressure pulses, the water hammers. There is a sharp iron bang, sometimes three in a row, walking down the length of the pipe like someone rapping to be let in.

Then there is the ringing. There are fixtures down here nobody has removed. They include chains, iron galleries, a hanging hoist hook, and the corrugated tin roofs of the lane's buildings. The roar sets them all faintly singing, a high metallic whine you only notice once you stop noticing everything else.

And beneath all of it, fine as sand, is the dripping. Ten thousand drips on stone, on tin, on leaves, on water.

What you do not hear is anything alive. No rustle, no scurry, no squeak, no buzz, no wingbeat. Nothing in all that green moves except by drip, draught or growth. It takes a while to notice this, because the place is so loud. Once noticed, it cannot be un-noticed. There are no rats in the lane. There are no spiders in the lamp cages. There are no midges over the channel, though every condition seems perfect for them. Perhaps it is the constant vibration, which comes up through every surface. Perhaps the steam lines were once dosed with something against pests, and the residue never left. Perhaps it is simply that the only doors are sealed, and nothing ever found its way in. Whatever the cause, the Undercroft is botanical and nothing else. It is a machine and a garden and a street, and no one is home.

---

## The Lane

The buildings are small and old and built hard against one another. They were never separate things. They are rooms of a single underground works that happened to be given roofs. They stand in a row along the south side of the channel with the lane between them and the water.

From the Fall end: a **gauge-house**, barely larger than a wardrobe. Its window is still full of glass, and behind the glass a brass water-level dial sits frozen with its needle at a number that must once have mattered. Its roof is a single sheet of lead, and on that roof grows a perfect cushion of liverwort, flat green lobes overlapping like scales.

Next, a **smithy**, doorless, its hearth cold. The anvil is still on its block. Over the years the block has sprouted, or rather the moss on it has thickened enough to root seedlings. A small fig, pale from lack of light, has come up beside the anvil's horn and wrapped a root around its waist.

Then three **cottages**, or what the works called cottages. They were bunks for the engine-men, two up and two down, their upper floors reached by iron ladders fixed to the outside wall. Their doors are open. Inside, maidenhair ferns have taken the walls from floor to ceiling in the lower rooms, where the vault drip comes through. In the upper rooms, closer to the lamps, grow the larger ferns: hart's-tongue, lady fern, shield fern. They sprout from beds, chairs, a washstand, a small cast-iron stove whose flue now carries nothing but roots. Every surface has been reinterpreted as ground.

Then a **store** with a timber loading gallery projecting over the lane. The gallery's beams have rotted and been replaced, cell by cell, by the plants growing inside them. It still holds its shape, though if you pushed on it your hand might go through.

Then the **lamp room**, where the electrics come in. It is the warmest room in the lane after the engine-house. A transformer the size of a wardrobe hums inside it, and the air around it is dry for half a meter in every direction. That is the only dry air anywhere. The plants stop exactly at that line. A bare halo surrounds the machine.

Then a **pay office** with a counter and a grille. Beyond the grille a trailing vine, some kind of shade-tolerant ivy, has come down from a crack in the vault. It has filled the whole back room in a single dark-leaved curtain, so dense that the office appears to have a green wall where its back wall should be.

Then two more buildings whose purpose is no longer legible, so completely are they wrapped. Then the **engine-house** itself, taller than the rest, its roof rising into the vault. It is the source of the beat.

Above them all, across the channel, run iron catwalks bolted to the north wall. These are ladders, galleries and railings that once gave access to valves on the steam main. They form a second, higher street, a metal one, and it is where the plants have done their most architectural work. The mosses and ferns climbed the railings and filled in the mesh floors. Seedlings germinated in the moss. Over decades some of those have become small woody things, figs and ivies and, improbably, a single buddleia. They reach nothing near sunlight, but they persist. Their roots and the ironwork are now intergrown. The catwalk has become a hanging bank, and where the railing ends, the green simply continues along the steam main as a single long living cornice.

## The Plants

It is worth saying clearly: there is no bare ground here except where heat forbids it. Plants are not *in* the Undercroft. They *are* its surfaces.

The zones follow the physics exactly.

**Where the drip is heaviest and the light least**, under the dark lamps and far from the prisms, there are mosses and liverworts. They grow in thick pelts, a finger deep or more, so saturated they hold water like a sponge. Press them and a clear trickle runs out. On the vault they grow hanging, in pendant ropes and beards. On the floor they grow in hummocks, forming the living upper layer of the peat.

**Where the steam leaks**, along the north wall, the conditions are something like a tropical cloud forest compressed into a strip half a meter wide. It is hot, dripping and constant. Here the filmy ferns grow, their fronds only one cell thick, translucent in the lamplight, like green tissue paper. They need air that never, ever dries, and this is the only place they could live. They grow in shingled rows down the wet brick below each flange, and each colony is shaped like the plume of warm air that feeds it.

**Around the lamps**, as said, there are the larger ferns. Each lamp is a sun, and each supports a small system: ferns in the nearest ring, mosses behind them, liverworts in the shadowed far side.

**Under the prisms**, where violet daylight comes down, grow the few flowering plants. There are pale begonias, some trailing campanula, a mat of mind-your-own-business. These are all old horticultural escapes, perhaps descendants of window boxes that sat in the pay office or the cottages generations ago. They flower sparsely and without pollinators. Some may self; most simply spread by rooting stems. Beneath each prism grows a small, slightly paler garden, roughly the shape of the light that falls there.

**From above** come the roots. There is a whole world of roots overhead, where the city's trees and gardens push down through the soil and find the vault. Where the mortar is gone they come through: pale fig roots, elm roots, the roots of things that may be long dead upstairs. They hang in tangles and curtains from the crown of the vault, white and ropey. The oldest have reached the floor, thickened and anchored, and now stand as pillars. Several of these root-pillars stand in the lane, and one rises from the channel's kerb itself. In a sense they hold the ceiling up, and in a sense they are slowly pulling it apart.

And **along the channel**, just at the waterline, grows a fringe of emergent green. There are rushes and a watercress-like plant, pale and leggy, trailing their roots in the current. The Fall's spray keeps everything within a few meters of it permanently beaded and glistening. There the moss grows so long and loose that it streams sideways in the draught the waterfall throws. It is the only thing in the vault that could be said to wave.

## How It Changes

Slowly. Almost everything here happens slowly.

The water level in the sump rises and falls with rain that fell on the city days ago. The pump starts and stops accordingly. A heavy week upstairs becomes, down here, a week of the beam engine's beat. A dry summer becomes weeks of only the Fall, the drip and the knocking pipe.

The lamps fail one at a time, at intervals of years. When one goes dark, the ferns around it do not die at once. They yellow over a season or two, and mosses crawl up into the space they held. The whole map of green slowly redraws itself around the lights that remain.

The calcite grows downward at the pace of a fingernail over a lifetime. The mortar goes on dissolving. The roots go on thickening. At some point one of the root-pillars will be strong enough, or the vault weak enough, that something gives. But that point is not visible from here, and there is no sign that it is close. The whole place has the feel of a system that settled into its current arrangement long ago and now merely maintains itself. The river runs, the pump pumps, the steam leaks, the plants take what each leak and lamp and drip offers them, and nothing is in a hurry.

The one rhythm faster than a season is the beat of the engine: hiss, groan, thud, gush. Six strokes a minute, on and on, until the sump is empty.

---

## What It Is

It is a works, and it is still working. It is a street, and it is still a street, just not one for people. It is a garden no one planted. It is a room small enough to cross in a minute. It is under a city that has probably forgotten it, though the city is still here in the flickering light of its footsteps, in the rain it sends down days late, in the roots of its trees.

It is full. There is nowhere in it to stand that is not already occupied by water, iron, brick or leaf. The noise fills whatever the plants have not. And it is warm, dim, dripping and loud, a green machine beating slowly under the pavement, doing exactly what it has always done.
