# The Breathing Shelf

## The Shape of It

The shelf sits two-thirds of the way up a mountain that has not finished becoming a mountain. Everything about the rock says *recent*. The ridgelines are knife-cut, not rounded. The faces are pale grey andesite that fractures into plates and wedges, and the plates have edges that will open a palm if you grip them carelessly. The scree fans below the cliffs are angular and loose, and none of their stones has had time to wear smooth. In places the rock is still lightly glazed, a dull glassiness where something hot once cooled fast. There are no deep gullies and no long, patient valleys. The mountain has not yet been carved. It has only been lifted and cracked.

Pressed into its eastern flank is a hollow, roughly the size of a village green. You can cross it in four hundred paces, maybe fewer. It is a cupped shelf, tilted slightly back into the mountain, as if a great scoop had been taken out of the slope and the lip left standing. Behind it the headwall rises in raw steps toward a summit you cannot see from inside. In front, the lip is a low rim of shattered stone, knee-high in places, waist-high in others. Beyond the lip there is nothing. The ground simply ends, and below lies the cloud-sea.

Most mornings the cloud-sea is unbroken: a white plain stretching to every horizon, lumpy and slow, lit so brightly from above that looking at it hurts. Far out, one or two other peaks break through it like islands. They are just as sharp and just as young, and none has a name that has come up this far.

## The Light

The light here is not warm. It is intense. The sky above the shelf is a dark, saturated blue that deepens toward indigo at the zenith, and on the clearest days you can almost see the black behind it. There is very little air between you and the sun, and nothing softens it. Every shadow is hard-edged and faintly blue. Every rock casts a shadow that looks cut out with a blade. The snow patches that linger in the shaded corners throw light back up so fiercely that the undersides of things are lit: the belly of a bird, the underside of your chin, the inner curl of a leaf. Up here light comes from below as much as from above.

The cloud-sea is the brightest thing of all. It turns the entire lower half of the world into a lamp. Anyone who spends a day on the shelf without covering their eyes will spend the night seeing it anyway, burned in white behind their eyelids.

## The Cold

It is cold in the plain, relentless way of height. Even at midday, in full glare, the air stays sharp enough to ache the teeth. Stone in the sun grows warm to the touch while the air a hand's breadth above it stays frigid. The shelf is a place of microclimates: the sunlit face of a boulder, the lee of the rim, the dark underside of a leaf-rosette. Each is its own small country of temperature, and every living thing here knows exactly where those countries are.

Nights are brutal. Ice forms in any cupped hollow within minutes of the sun leaving. By dawn the entire shelf is rimed: every blade, every stone edge, every strand of moss furred with white needles that point into the wind.

## The Water That Arrives as Air

There are no streams on the shelf. There is no spring. Rain, when it comes, comes rarely and hard and is gone within the hour, sheeting off the fresh rock with almost nothing to hold it. And yet the place is wet, in a particular way.

The water comes from the cloud-sea.

Each day, as the sun heats the slopes below, the cloud-sea begins to breathe. Its surface lifts. By mid-morning a lobe of white rises against the mountain, and there is one low notch in the lip of the shelf, a V-shaped gap in the rim, where the cloud finds its way over. What follows is the strangest thing about the place, though it happens every day: the cloud *pours in*. It moves like a river flowing uphill, a slow white cataract spilling over the notch and flooding across the floor of the hollow. It fills the basin knee-deep, then waist-deep, then entirely, until the shelf becomes a room of white where you cannot see your own feet.

The cloud stays for an hour, sometimes two. During that time everything drinks. Droplets bead on every surface, gather, and run. Moss swells visibly. Leaves that were curled open flat. The rock darkens. Then the sun burns through, and the cloud drains back out the notch the way it came, or simply thins to nothing. The shelf steams for a little while, and then it is dry and blazing again.

This happens most days. On some days the cloud never rises, and the shelf goes thirsty. On some days it rises twice. Life here runs on this pulse: dawn rime, mid-morning flood of fog, afternoon glare, evening freeze. A handful of tiny tarns hold the leftovers. They are shallow pools in rock hollows no bigger than a table, skinned with ice every night and thawed every noon, their bottoms carpeted with a dark green slime that is also alive.

## The Green

Against all that grey rock and white glare, the vegetation is shockingly lush. It does not grow tall, but it is dense and deep and saturated with color. Where the fog lingers longest, in the low lee of the rim and the hollows beneath the headwall, the ground is completely covered.

The moss grows in hummocks: rounded, springy mounds a forearm deep, so thick that a foot sinks into them to the ankle. Their surfaces are a green so vivid it looks painted. Cut into one and the inside is layered, gold, then brown, then black at the base: years of growth stacked on each other, the living skin on top of its own compressed history. The moss holds water like a sponge. It is the reason the shelf is lush at all. After the fog drains out, the moss keeps drinking for hours, and everything that grows in it drinks from it.

Among the moss grow the cushion plants: tight domes of tiny leaves packed so close that the dome is hard to the touch, like a fist of green stone. They are warmer inside than out. Put your hand flat on one in the evening and you will feel it, a faint warmth held in the tangle of stems. Insects shelter in them overnight. Frost forms on their surfaces but not in their hearts.

Then there are the tall ones. Scattered across the shelf, never close together, stand rosette plants on single thick stems, chest-high to a person. Their leaves are long and silver-furred, arranged in a tight spiral crown, and the whole plant wears a skirt of its own dead leaves hanging down the stem, dry and thick as thatch. At night the living leaves fold inward over the central bud, closing like hands cupped around a candle. In the morning, as the light hits them, they open slowly. You can watch it happen over the course of an hour. The fur on their leaves catches the fog droplets and channels them down into the crown. On a fog-flooded morning each rosette holds a small cup of clear water at its center, and the birds know it.

Low grasses grow between everything in stiff tufts, tawny at the tips. Lichens cover every rock face that isn't covered by something else, in orange, sulphur yellow, black, and a strange pale mint. On the youngest rock, the scarp faces that fell only a season or two ago, there is nothing yet. The rock is clean and pale, and the first lichen spots are just beginning, faint as freckles. You can read the age of a rock face by how colorful it is.

## The Animals, Who Are Many and Quiet

The shelf is full of animals. You notice this slowly, because almost none of them make noise.

The ecology begins with the wind. The same daily heating that lifts the cloud-sea also lifts the air, and the updraft carries things. Every afternoon insects arrive from the forests far below the cloud: moths, aphids, gnats, tiny beetles, spiders drifting on silk threads. They are swept up the mountainside, far higher than they ever meant to go, and deposited on the shelf, where the cold stuns them. On the snow patches in the shaded corners they lie scattered like pepper, some still twitching, most not. It is a daily fallout of food, delivered from a world the shelf never sees.

The residents harvest it. Springtails swarm the snow patches by the thousand, tiny and dark, so numerous that the snow looks faintly grey and moving. Wolf spiders patrol the edges, black and fast. There are ground beetles that live their entire lives under the flakes of scree, emerging only in the afternoon glare to scavenge the fallen.

Above the beetles are the birds. A species of small finch lives here in huge numbers, brown-grey with white wing-flashes. They move in flocks of hundreds that pour across the shelf low and fast like blown smoke, settle on a snow patch, strip it, and are gone. They do not sing. Their calls, when they make any, are thin, high, and brief, pitched so high they barely register. Mostly what you hear is their wings, a soft collective hush as the flock turns. They nest in the cracks of the headwall, and in the breeding season every crack has a pair.

Swifts come in the afternoons: sickle-winged, dark, cutting the updraft to snatch insects out of the air before they even land. They never touch down on the shelf. They are the fastest thing here, and the most silent.

The mammals are small and round. A kind of rock-hare lives in the scree, grey-brown and short-eared, the size of two fists. They are everywhere once you learn to see them: sitting motionless on stones, sunning, watching. In other ranges their kind whistles. These don't, or almost don't. They drag grass and rosette leaves into the gaps between rocks and pile them into little haystacks to dry in the sun, for winter. They also build low walls of pebbles across the openings of their burrows, a curious habit, and the walls are neat, almost deliberate. They look disconcertingly like the human cairns.

High above everything, most days, there is a single raptor. It is large and dark and turns in slow circles in the blue, riding the same updraft that feeds everything else. When it drops, the finch flock explodes and the rock-hares vanish into the scree, and for a moment the shelf is completely empty of visible life. Then, slowly, it fills back up.

## The Silence

The quiet here is not an absence. It is a texture.

The thin air carries sound poorly. The cloud-sea below absorbs it. There is no running water, no rustle of trees, because there are no trees. On a still afternoon the loudest sound is your own pulse. Under that, if you listen, there is a layer of small things: the tick of ice contracting in a tarn, the faint crackle of rime needles breaking off in the sun, the hush of finch wings, the dry whisper of rosette-skirts when a gust comes over the rim.

When the fog floods in, even these go. Fog swallows sound entirely. Inside it the shelf becomes a padded white room where a stone dropped at your feet lands with a muffled thud, and your voice stops at arm's length.

Then, occasionally, the mountain speaks. The rock is young and restless, and each freeze-thaw cycle levers it apart a little more. In the afternoon, when the sun has warmed the headwall and loosened the ice in its cracks, stones fall. You hear a sharp crack high up, then a clattering that grows and multiplies and pours down a gully, then silence again, with a faint dust cloud hanging against the blue. Once or twice a season a whole slab comes away, and the new scar is pale and clean and uncolored for years afterward.

## The People Who Come and Go

No one lives on the shelf. People come, though, and they have left marks.

The approach from below is marked with cairns: stacked piles of flat stones, knee-high, set on ridge-crests at intervals so that from each cairn the next is just visible. Some have fallen. Some have been rebuilt so many times that their lower stones are bright with lichen while their top stones are bare and new.

On the shelf itself there is one structure. It is a low hut in the lee of the headwall, built of the mountain's own fractured slabs, fitted without mortar, the gaps packed with moss. Its roof is turf cut from the hummocks, laid over a frame of bent rosette stems, and the turf has kept growing: the roof is green and alive and indistinguishable from the ground behind it. A person has to stoop to enter. Inside is a stone hearth blackened by old fires, a sleeping ledge padded with dried grass, and a niche in the wall holding a few objects: a bone needle, a scraper of dark stone chipped to an edge, a small flute made from the wing bone of the raptor, with three holes.

Stretched between two boulders near the fog notch is the most important thing the visitors have made: a net. It is woven from twisted hair, perhaps goat hair, and hangs vertically, taut, about the size of a door. When the fog pours in through the notch, it passes through the net, and the droplets catch on the fibers, gather, and run down into a trough carved into the stone beneath. The trough drains into a basin hollowed out of the rock, clearly by hand, patiently, over many seasons. On a good fog morning the basin fills. This is the only reliable drinking water on the shelf, and someone learned how to pull it out of the air.

Tied to the top cord of the net, and to a few of the rosette stems, are strips of cloth, dyed in colors taken from the lichens: sulphur yellow, rust orange. They have been bleached almost white by the light. They snap faintly in the wind.

Why do people come? The evidence suggests several reasons. In the hut's niche there is a small bundle of finch down, gathered from abandoned nests after the breeding season, the warmest filling there is. There is a scraped patch of rock where orange lichen has been harvested, presumably for dye. There are notches cut into the doorframe, rows of them, grouped in fives, and no one who isn't the one who carved them knows what they're counting. Seasons, perhaps. Visits. Days of fog.

Perhaps people also come for the light. There is a flat stone at the edge of the lip, worn a little smoother than the rest, where someone clearly sat, often, facing out over the cloud-sea.

## A Day, Passing

Dawn: every surface white with rime. The rosettes closed tight. The tarns frozen. The first light hits the high peaks across the cloud-sea long before it reaches the shelf, turning them pink, then gold, then white. The shelf waits in blue shadow. The rock-hares come out and sit hunched on stones, facing east.

Sunrise: the light reaches the headwall and slides down it. The rime begins to glitter, then to fall, needles dropping with the faintest ticking sound. The rosettes begin to open. Steam rises off the moss hummocks where the sun first touches them.

Mid-morning: the cloud-sea lifts. The white lobe climbs the slope, reaches the notch, and pours in. For an hour the shelf is a submerged world, silent, wet, dripping. Water collects in the basin beneath the net. The finches wait it out in the cracks of the headwall.

Noon: the fog drains away. The glare returns, doubled by all the wet surfaces. Everything glistens. The rosette crowns hold their cups of water, and the finches descend to drink from them one by one, perching on the silver leaf-tips.

Afternoon: the updraft rises. Insects begin arriving on the wind, falling onto the snow patches. The springtails swarm. The swifts arrive and slice the air. The finch flocks work the snow in waves. The raptor circles. Somewhere on the headwall a stone cracks loose and clatters down, and every animal on the shelf freezes for a moment, then carries on.

Evening: the sun drops toward the cloud-sea, which catches fire. The whole shelf is lit gold from below. Shadows stretch impossibly long across the moss. The rosettes begin to close. The rock-hares drag their last mouthfuls of grass into the scree. The finches go into the cracks.

Dusk: the cold arrives like a door closing. The tarns skin over within minutes. The sky above goes violet, then black, and the stars come out in numbers that seem almost false, dense and steady and unblinking in the thin air. The cloud-sea glows faintly grey under them. The shelf is absolutely still.

Night: rime begins to grow again, needle by needle, in the dark.

## What the Place Is

The shelf is a small place held in an enormous one: a green room built into the side of a new and restless mountain, above a sea of cloud, under a sky that is almost space. It is full of life and full of emptiness at once. There are thousands of animals, each one small, each one separate, spread across a hollow that feels vast because nothing in it is taller than a person and nothing around it is close. It is wet without having any water of its own. It is bright without being warm. It is quiet, except when the mountain moves.

Its entire economy runs on things arriving from below: fog each morning, insects each afternoon, people once in a long while. Everything on the shelf has learned to catch what the rising air brings, whether with moss, with silver fur, with fast wings, or with a net of twisted hair stretched across a gap in the stone.
