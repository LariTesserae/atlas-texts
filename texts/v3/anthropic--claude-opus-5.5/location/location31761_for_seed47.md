# The Shelf

The first thing to understand is that you are standing near the top of the sky.

The ground is a plain. It is a truly flat one, the kind of flatness that only comes from time, and it runs to every horizon without a hill or a gully. The horizon sits oddly low, a knife-line of pale ochre against a sky that is blue only near the ground. Overhead the blue thickens into indigo, and at the zenith, even at noon, it is nearly black. The sun is ordinary and sharp. It throws hard shadows, and because there is almost nothing on the plain to cast them, each shadow feels like an event. A pebble at midday has a shadow as crisp as a coin.

This is the Shelf. It is a tableland the size of a continent, lifted so far above the rest of the world that the air here is thin enough to make lungs ache and blood slow. Nothing on it is taller than a person's knee, except the few things that were put here on purpose.

## The Ground

The surface is stone ground down past the point of being any particular kind of stone. It was granite once, or basalt, or the bed of some sea that dried out before anything lived in it. Now it is a pavement of worn, interlocking plates, each polished to a dull satin, with seams between them packed tight with grit. Walk on it and the plates don't shift. They have had so long to settle that they fit together like the scales of an animal.

In places the pavement gives way to *reg*, fields of small rounded stones of nearly identical size, sorted by wind over so many ages that it looks like a craftsman's work. Farther out there are pale pans where the stone has weathered into a fine, faintly luminous powder, soft as flour. If you walk across one, your footprints will not last the hour.

There is no water. There was once. You can find the ghost of it in the stone: faint banding, old ripple-marks preserved in the harder layers and then worn so thin that you see them only when the sun is low and raking. The Shelf's only liquid now is the dust, and the dust behaves more like water than it should.

## The Wind

The Shelf is never still. The plain rises so high that it pushes into the fast, cold rivers of air that circle the world at altitude, and those rivers scrape across it. The weather is less something that happens on the Shelf than something the Shelf is constantly standing in.

The storms come without warning and without sound.

That is the strangest part for anyone who comes here. The air is too thin to carry noise well. A storm the height of a mountain range can cross the plain toward you, a wall of ochre and bone-white dust with its leading edge curling like a breaking wave a hundred kilometers long, and you will hear nothing. You might hear the faint hiss of grit against your own sleeve. You will feel the pressure change in your ears. Then the light goes amber, then brown, then a dim, grainy dusk, and the wind leans against you like a hand. The whole time, the only sound is your own breathing.

The storms come in several kinds. People who spend long enough here learn to name them.

**Sheets** are the most common. They are low, fast layers of dust only a meter or two deep, pouring across the plain like floodwater. Stand in one and you are waist-deep in a silent brown river, with clear sky above your head. Sheets carve nothing. They polish.

**Towers** are rotating columns that rise from the pans, draw the fine powder straight up into the thin air, and hold it there, spinning, for days. From a distance they look like pale trees. There are sometimes dozens at once, scattered across the horizon, slowly migrating.

**Fronts** are rare, perhaps once or twice a season. A front is the jet stream itself touching down, and the whole sky moves. Everything loose on the surface of the Shelf goes into the air at once and travels hundreds of kilometers before it settles. After a front, the pans have moved. The reg-fields have shifted their edges. The plain looks the same but has been completely rearranged, like a table wiped and reset.

The turbulence is enormous and constant, and it has done its work. This is why the Shelf is flat. Any ridge, peak, or crag that ever stood here has been sanded away. What remains is the shape erosion reaches when it has nothing left to take: a surface of perfect, stubborn smoothness, offering the wind no purchase.

That raises the question of why the Shelf is still so high.

## The Risers

Scattered across the plain, very far apart, stand the Risers.

You might walk for a week between them. A person could cross the Shelf and see only three or four. They are slender columns of something like dark glass, perhaps thirty meters tall, standing perfectly vertical in a landscape without any other verticals. Each one is faintly iridescent, oil-on-water colors that shift when you move. They are unweathered. In a place where the wind has erased mountains, they show not one scratch. The dust flows around their bases in clean, curving wakes, like a stream around a post, and never touches them.

They are machines. Nobody alive knows exactly what they do, though the shape of it can be worked out.

Lay your palm on a Riser and you feel, very faintly, a slow pulse, one beat every several minutes. Lie on the pavement near one with your ear against the stone and you feel it there too: a deep, patient pressure moving *upward* through the rock. The pavement around a Riser is fractionally higher than the pavement elsewhere. It is only a few centimeters, but measurable. Slowly, very slowly, the Risers are pushing the Shelf up from underneath.

This is the rule of the place, and once you understand it, everything else follows. The wind wears the Shelf down, and the Risers lift it. The two are almost exactly balanced, and have been for so long that the balance has become the landscape. The rock you stand on was, a long time ago, kilometers below you. It has been pressed up through the crust, raised into the wind, and planed away grain by grain. The dust of the storms is the Shelf itself, eaten from the top as it is fed from the bottom. The whole continent is a slow conveyor, and the surface is the place where it ends.

That is why the Shelf is simultaneously the oldest and the youngest ground in the world. Its shape has not changed in an age. Its substance is renewed constantly, rising like a tide too slow to see.

Some of the Risers have failed. You find them occasionally: a column snapped off at the base, the stump still gleaming, the glassy shards scattered and half-buried and, strangely, still unscratched. Around a dead Riser there is always a shallow depression, a bowl a few kilometers wide where the lift stopped and the wind won. The bowls are the only low places on the Shelf. Dust pools in them, and the strangest of the plants live there.

## Other Marks

The Risers are not the only built things, though they are the only ones that still work.

There are **waystones**: squat, knee-high blocks of the same dark glass, laid out in lines that run arrow-straight for hundreds of kilometers. Their spacing suggests someone once walked or traveled between the Risers and needed to find their way through the storms. When a sheet passes over a waystone, the dust around it glows faintly for a moment, a green-white shimmer, as though the stone is still trying to signal.

There are **shelters**, a handful of them across the entire Shelf. They are low domes half-sunk into the pavement, doors sealed with no visible seam. One or two have cracked open over the ages. Inside is a single round room, smooth-walled and empty except for fine dust that has somehow gotten in, and a strange, lingering warmth in the floor. They are warm even now. Whatever heats them has not stopped.

And there are **the lines**. From high enough, perhaps from the rim of a tower-storm if you could ride one, you would see them: faint, perfectly straight scorings in the pavement, crossing the plain in a vast, slightly irregular grid. The wind has nearly erased them. They may be the traces of whatever originally placed the Risers, or foundations of something long gone. They may be roads. They may be something that has no word.

No one lives here now. Whoever built the Risers either left or became something that no longer needs to stand on the ground.

## What Grows

Life on the Shelf is sparse, flat, and patient.

The most common plant is **keel-moss**, a grey-green crust that grows only in the lee of stones, in the thumb-sized pocket of stillness each pebble makes behind itself. Because the wind comes mostly from one direction, all the keel-moss on a given stretch of plain grows on the same side of the same stones. Walk into the wind and the plain looks bare. Turn around and it is faintly green, thousands of small tails of moss all pointing downwind like iron filings around a magnet. After a front the stones rearrange, and the moss must slowly regrow on their new lee sides. It knows how. It has been doing it forever.

In the pans grow **puffballs**, hard, rounded cushions the size of a fist and the color of old bone. They are rooted so deeply into the fine powder that the storms cannot pull them loose. Over long years, the dust drifts up around each puffball and the plant grows upward with it, so that a very old one is a slender stalk reaching a meter down into the powder, its living head still barely above the surface.

In the bowls around dead Risers, where the dust lies deepest and some residual warmth still seeps up from below, there are the **lanterns**. These are small translucent plants, pale as frost, with leaves like folded glass. At night they glow very faintly, the same green-white as the waystones in a storm. No one knows whether they are drawing on the dying machine beneath them or whether they are something the builders left behind on purpose. They are the most beautiful things on the Shelf, and they grow nowhere else.

## What Moves

Animals are rare. You can spend days here and see nothing alive but the moss.

The animal you are most likely to see is a **skate**. It is flat, the size of a large dinner plate, the same ochre as the dust, and so thin it is nearly two-dimensional. Skates spend most of their lives pressed against the pavement, unmoving, feeding on the moss and the fine organic matter the wind brings up from the lowlands far below. When a storm comes they peel themselves off the ground, and the wind takes them. They fly flat and fast, tilting to steer, riding the leading edges of sheets for hundreds of kilometers, and then drop, sudden and precise, back to the pavement when the storm has passed. A sheet full of skates is an eerie thing: dozens of flat shapes flickering through the brown air, all silent, all angling the same way like a school of fish.

Beneath the pans live the **sifters**: small, blind, soft-bodied things that swim through the fine powder the way other animals swim through water. You never see them. You see only the faint dimples they leave on the surface of a pan in the stillness between storms, and, very occasionally, a single ring of disturbance spreading slowly outward, as though something had surfaced and dived again.

And there is one other animal, the one people who know the Shelf talk about only carefully. Every so often, at a great distance, at the edge of a tower-storm, a traveler will see something moving that is not dust: a long, pale shape, much larger than a skate, gliding within the rotating column. It rises with the spiral, drops, and rises again. It never comes close. Nobody has ever found one on the ground. It might live in the storms, feeding on whatever the towers lift. It might be a machine. It might be one of the builders, grown strange.

## Night

At night the Shelf is very cold. It is not deadly, but it is the deep, dry cold of high places, where the ground gives up its warmth to the black sky almost at once.

The stars are extraordinary. There is so little air above you that they barely twinkle; they burn steady and hard, and there are more of them than seems reasonable. The Risers catch starlight in their iridescent surfaces and glow very faintly, like pillars of dark oil. In the bowls, the lanterns shine.

Often the storms continue through the night. Then you can lie on the pavement and watch the stars vanish and reappear as silent brown sheets pour across the sky just above you, faster than a galloping horse, making no sound at all. You can feel the ground underneath you, cold, immovable, pressed up from below by the patient pulse of something that has not stopped working in ten thousand ages.

The Shelf is being destroyed every moment, and it is being made every moment, and it has been exactly like this for longer than there have been people to see it. It does not feel like a place that is ending. It feels like a place that found the one shape it can hold forever, and is holding it.
