# The Wicker

## I. The Approach That Never Quite Arrives

There is no ground here. Or rather, the ground is also made.

You would begin, if you could begin anywhere, on a strand. It is as wide as a road and woven from forty or fifty living stems, each thick as a thigh, twisted together in a slow rope-lay and bound every few paces with a lashing of thinner vine. The lashings were tied by hand. You can see the hand in them: the knots are not quite regular, each tightened with a slightly different pressure, and some are finished with a small decorative tuck that serves no structural purpose. The stems are alive. Their bark is smooth, grey-green, and faintly warm to the touch, like the flank of a sleeping animal. Where the lashings bite into them, the wood has swollen around the cord and half-swallowed it, the way an old fence-wire vanishes into a tree. Some lashings are so old that only a raised seam in the bark shows where they once were.

The strand runs out ahead of you and curves away, and when you look over its edge there is nothing beneath it except more strands, very far down, crossing one another at long diagonals. They are thin as hairs with distance and dim in a green-brown haze. Beneath those are more, fainter still, and beneath those there is only a warm, damp dark that breathes up at you.

You are high. The air is thin enough that you notice your breath, but it is not cold. It is warm and wet, like the inside of a mouth. Mist condenses on everything: on the bark, on your skin, on the fine hairs of the leaves. Every surface beads and drips. The water runs along the undersides of the strands, gathers at the knots, and falls in long silent threads that break apart into nothing before they reach anything below.

This is the Wicker: a basketwork the size of a continent, grown and plaited and grafted from living plants, hung in the high air above a lowland no one has seen. Or perhaps there is no lowland, and it hangs from nothing at all.

## II. The Mesh

The Wicker is not a solid. It is mostly hole.

Its strands cross and recross at enormous intervals. Between one crossing and the next there may be a day's walk of open air, and the gaps between strands are often wider than the strands are long. The mesh has gauges. There are regions of *tight weave*, where the strands pass close enough to leap between and small shelters have been plaited into the angles. There are regions of *open weave*, where a single strand is the only solid thing in sight in any direction and you walk it like a thread across a canyon of mist. Then there are the *hollows*, rounded voids enclosed by curving walls of mesh so distant that they read as a vague darkening of the air, like the far side of a lung.

The hollows are large enough to have weather. Warm air rises through them in slow columns. Clouds form in their upper curves and drift, and it rains inside them, softly, for hours. The rain falls through the hollow and wets the lower mesh, and the lower mesh steams, and the steam rises. Each hollow keeps its own small cycle of breath. Some are always raining. Some are always clear and full of a still, amber dimness. Travelers name them by their weather: *the Sighing Hollow*, *the Hollow That Drips From One Place*, *Long Fog*.

The walls of the hollows are made in the same way as everything else, woven and lashed, but at that scale the weave becomes a kind of terrain. A single over-under of two master-strands can make a ridge you must climb for an afternoon. The plaited crossings of the great lattice have become, over ages, *knots* in the sense of mountain knots: places where many strands converge and bind, grown together into swollen masses of wood with valleys and gullies in them, soft with moss, running with seep-water, rooted through with smaller plants living on the larger.

Plants live on plants live on plants. Epiphytes crowd every crotch. Ferns hang in skirts from the lashings. Mosses coat the upper faces of the strands so thickly that you walk on a sodden green carpet that squeezes water up around your feet. Bladder-vines trail in the gaps, their pale sacs filled with rainwater. Tendrils reach from one strand toward the next across impossible distances and are sometimes caught and tied off, extending the weave, though it is never clear by whom. Nothing here touches stone or soil. The roots go into other roots. It is vegetation all the way down, and all of it has been shaped.

## III. The Hands

Everything here was made, and the making was done with very little.

Along the strands you find the evidence: a bone awl pushed into a crack in the bark and left there so long that the wood has grown a lip over its handle. Fiber twine, spun thumb-thick from bark-bast, twisted with a drop-spindle, the whorl a disc of dried gourd. Grafting cuts, the careful slanting wounds where two living stems were split, fitted together, and bound with clay-and-sap poultice until they healed into one. Charring marks where someone used fire to bend green wood. Adzes of ground shell. Wedges of hardwood burnished black by grip.

There is no metal, no wheel, no mechanism. The Wicker was made the way one makes a basket: by hand, one stroke at a time, over-under, pulling tight. The only difference is that it never stopped. The whole continental structure is the accumulated work of hands that each tied a few knots, grafted a few stems, and then set down their tools.

Some of the makers are still here, a few. They are quiet people, sparse across the vastness. You might walk three days without seeing one, and then find someone sitting in a knot-hollow patiently splicing a fraying lashing. They work very slowly. They will not tell you where they came from, or rather they tell you in a way that doesn't answer: *I came from the part I'm tying.* They carry small bundles of cord and a bone needle. They mend. They extend. They say the Wicker is not finished. When you ask what it will be when it is finished, they look at you with a mild, puzzled patience, as if you had asked when a river will be done flowing.

## IV. What Lives in the Gaps

The Wicker is very empty, and it is also full of animals. Both are true, because the space is so large. The creatures are abundant but diluted, scattered through a volume so immense that each encounter feels solitary, and yet once you learn to see them they are everywhere.

**Driftmoths** are the most common. They are palm-wide, soft-bodied, and dusted a lichen-grey that vanishes against bark. They ride the thermals of the hollows in slow spirals, thousands at once, so that a hollow's rising column of air is visible as a faint shimmering pillar, like dust in a sunbeam, though there is no sunbeam. They feed on the nectar of hanging flowers and are fed on in turn.

**Lashers** are long, legless, and emerald. They wind themselves around strands exactly as a cord wraps a stem, and hold still so long that they are mistaken for lashings. When a driftmoth settles within reach they uncoil and strike, and then wrap again, tighter. The old makers claim some lashings in the Wicker are lashers that never moved again. They hold with the same fidelity as twine, and after enough years the bark closes over them too.

**Cratch** are small, many-legged, and clicking, though the click is so faint you mostly feel it through the wood. They live in the mosses of the strand-tops in colonies and graze the moss in slow waves, leaving behind neat furrows that rain fills and the moss regrows.

**Weaverbirds**, or what are called weaverbirds, are dark and heavy-beaked. They build hanging nests in the tight-weave regions, and their nests follow the same over-under pattern as the Wicker itself, perfectly scaled down. Some of the older nests have been incorporated into the mesh, with tendrils of living vine threaded through them. Whether the birds learned the pattern from the Wicker or the Wicker learned it from the birds is the kind of question that here has no fixed answer.

**Hollow-whales** are the largest. No one sees one whole. In the biggest hollows, in the rain, something vast and pale moves slowly through the mist, and for a moment a flank the size of a hillside is visible as a slightly paler grey. Then it is gone. They are thought to be filter-feeders, sweeping driftmoths from the thermal columns. They are thought to be lighter than air. They are thought to be woven. The makers say simply that the hollow-whales are *where the Wicker keeps its breath*, and will not elaborate.

Predator and prey move at the slow pace of the whole place. A lasher may wait for a month. A driftmoth swarm takes a season to rise from the bottom of a hollow to its top. Everything happens, and nothing happens quickly.

## V. Light and Sound

It is always dim. There is no sun, or no sun visible. The light seems to come from everywhere at once: a diffuse green-gold glow that filters through countless layers of leaf and mesh and mist, reaching you already used and softened. It has the quality of light at the bottom of a forest pool. Shadows are vague. Colors are muted, all moss and bark and wet bronze. At long intervals the glow slowly dims further toward a heavy amber-brown, and then slowly brightens again. The makers call this *the breathing*. It may be day and night, or it may not. Its period is not constant.

Some of the fungi on the older knots give off a faint cold phosphorescence, pale blue in the gullies, and in the darkest parts of the breathing the knots become visible from far away as faint blue smudges, like distant towns.

And it is quiet, profoundly so. The weave absorbs sound. Moss swallows footsteps. Mist dampens voices. Distance does the rest. A shout does not echo; it simply ends, a few strides out, as if the air has taken it. What you hear is small and close: water dripping from a knot onto moss, the faint dry rasp of a lasher uncoiling, the clicking of cratch through the wood, your own breath. Sometimes, very faintly, there is a long low creak of the whole structure shifting in its hang, the sound a basket makes when you set something heavy in it, stretched across hours. It may be the loudest thing in the world, and you barely hear it.

## VI. The Rules

Here the Wicker becomes stranger, and it is the part the quiet makers speak of least.

**It is larger inside than out.** If you walk a strand toward the edge of a hollow, the hollow's wall recedes. Travelers have walked the outer skin of the Wicker, the places where the mesh thins and you can look *outward* rather than *inward*, and seen, beyond it, only more Wicker, curving the other way. There is no outside. Every outer surface is the inner surface of something larger.

**Distance follows the weave, not the air.** Two strands may hang side by side a stone's throw apart, and yet walking from one to the other along the mesh takes a week. Leaping the gap does not work. People have tried. They fall, and the mist takes them, and sometimes they are found days later, dazed, sitting on a strand in some far region, clutching the moss and saying they fell for a very long time and then were *caught*. The Wicker does not allow shortcuts. The only way to go anywhere is the made way.

**Knots remember.** Every lashing holds something of the moment it was tied. Put your hand on a very old knot and you may feel it: a flicker of someone's patience, a hum of a song they were humming, the specific ache of their fingers. The oldest knot-masses, where thousands of lashings have fused, are so dense with this that walking into them is like walking into a crowd of quiet people all concentrating. The makers go there to rest. They say it helps them remember what they are doing.

**The pattern repeats at every scale.** The over-under of two stems in a lashing, the weaverbird's nest, the crossing of master-strands, the shape of a hollow, the curve of the whole: all are the same gesture. Look at the bark closely and its fibers are plaited. Look at a moss frond and its cells are plaited. The makers say that if you could see far enough in either direction you would find the same over-under, and nothing that was not made.

**You will find your own knots.** This is the strangest thing, and every traveler who stays long enough reports it. Walking some remote strand, you come on a lashing tied in exactly the way you tie. It has your particular over-tightened second loop and your habitual tuck. It is old, half-swallowed by bark. You have never been here. Then, a little way on, there is another. The makers are not surprised when you tell them. *Of course,* they say. *You've been working on it.* They seem to believe that everyone who enters the Wicker has always been part of it: that the hands which made it include yours, tying knots you have not yet tied in places you have not yet reached, and that the structure is being made from both ends of time at once. That would be why it holds. That would be why it is not finished.

**Nothing falls out.** Water falls, but it rises again as mist. Driftmoths die and fall, but the mesh catches them and the moss eats them. Leaves drop and are caught. People fall and are caught. The makers say the bottom of the Wicker is the top of it, and that the falling rain in the lowest hollows is simply rain falling *up* into the next. Whether that is literal or a way of speaking is, again, not a question that has a fixed answer here.

## VII. What It Is

After enough days walking the strands you stop asking what holds it up.

It holds itself up the way a basket holds its shape: by tension, by every strand pressing against every other, by the accumulated care of an uncountable number of small acts. It is enormous and nearly empty. It is warm and wet and dim and almost silent. It is alive in every fiber and made in every fiber, and after a while the difference between those two things stops meaning anything.

On one of the old knots, in a gully blue with fungal light, a maker once showed a traveler how to splice a fraying cord: unlay the ends, marry the strands, tuck each under its neighbor, roll it tight against the thigh. The traveler did it clumsily. The maker nodded and said it would hold. Then they both sat and listened to the drip of water, and to the faint slow creak of the whole world settling a fraction deeper into its own weave.

The next morning, or what passed for morning, the traveler walked on and found, many strands later and very far away, a splice exactly like that clumsy one. It was grey with age, grown over with moss, and holding.
