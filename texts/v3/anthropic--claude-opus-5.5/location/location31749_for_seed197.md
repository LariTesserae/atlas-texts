# The Standing Town

## Approach

It sits in a shallow basin barely above the river flats, where the land dips as if pressed by a thumb. From the rim it looks like an overgrown hill, a green dome maybe four hundred paces across. It shines wetly in the morning and dulls by noon. It is not a hill.

You hear it before the dome has fully risen out of the reeds. At first it is a pressure behind the ears, the way weather announces itself. Then it becomes a drone that the ground carries up through your heels, so you feel it in the long bones of your legs. At the edge of the basin the drone is enough to make conversation difficult. At the outer wall, speech is impossible. Your own voice leaves your mouth and is taken, absorbed into the larger sound like a drop into a river.

The dome is a town. No one built it with hands.

## The Walls

The outer skin is a lattice of living stems, thick as a wrist, woven so tightly they have grafted into one another over centuries. Where two stems cross they have fused into a smooth, knuckled node, and the whole surface is a net of these knots. The bark is pale green-grey and slightly waxy, and under the fingers it vibrates constantly, like a cat's flank or a struck bell that never stops being struck. Moss fills the gaps between stems in the lower courses. Higher up, where the lattice is thinner, the gaps are open and light comes through in thousands of small lozenges.

The walls are hollow. Every stem is a tube, and inside the tubes live the singers.

## The Singers

They are insects, roughly: something between a cicada and a cricket, the length of a finger, bronze-backed, with tymbal membranes along their sides that flex several hundred times a second. There are millions of them, perhaps more. None is ever seen in the open for long. They live their whole lives inside the hollow stems, eating the soft inner pith, mating in the dark, laying eggs in the grain. Their song goes into the tube, and the tube is tuned.

This is the first rule of the place. The plant grows to the song. Where the singers' combined sound makes a stem resonate strongly, the stem thickens and lengthens. Where the sound is weak, the stem starves and sloughs away. Over generations the plant has shaped itself into an instrument for the chorus, and the chorus has settled into the pitches the plant amplifies best. Neither leads. They grew into each other until the question stopped meaning anything.

The result is a single chord, held for longer than anyone can reckon. It has three tones you can pick out and several more you only feel. It never stops. At dusk it swells as the night-singers join. At dawn it thins slightly as some fall quiet and others wake, but it never drops below a roar.

## Inside

You enter through one of the low arches where the lattice lifts off the ground, and the space inside opens up enormously.

The dome is almost entirely empty. There is no undergrowth on the floor, no crowding trunks, no clutter. There is only a vast, bright hollow, and threaded through it at intervals are the structures that make it a town. Far more air than matter. Your eyes keep reaching across open distance and finding almost nothing until, suddenly, something.

What is there is the architecture of a settlement. Its shape was set by physics rather than by anyone's plan.

The singers' chord fills the dome as a standing wave. Sound bouncing off the curved inner walls sets up a fixed interference pattern of loud zones and quiet zones, called antinodes and nodes, hanging in the air like invisible scaffolding. The plant, which grows toward resonance, has sent stems inward along the lines of greatest vibration. So the interior is crossed by long, arching galleries of living lattice. Streets run through midair, ribbed walkways hang between the curved walls, and there are rooms: spherical or lens-shaped chambers of woven stem suspended where several loud zones intersect.

The layout is geometric, but not in any human way. It follows curves that repeat at fixed intervals, nesting and mirroring each other. There are avenues and cross-streets. There is something like a central plaza, a broad woven platform at the dome's focal point where the three main tones converge. There are districts where the rooms cluster tightly and districts where a single chamber hangs alone in a great volume of air. A cartographer could map it, and the map would also be a picture of a chord.

The galleries are wide enough to walk. Their floors are the flattened upper surfaces of fused stems, worn smooth and faintly hollowed by long use. The builders use them, and the other animals do too. So, sometimes, do people.

## The Silences

Between the loud zones are the nodes, where the sound waves cancel.

These are the strangest places in the town. You can be walking along a gallery in noise so enormous it has become texture. It presses on your chest, blurs your vision slightly, and makes your teeth ache. Then you step through some invisible membrane into complete stillness. You hear your own heartbeat, then the small wet sound of your eyes blinking. A pace later the roar takes you back.

Nothing grows in the nodes, because there is no vibration to grow toward. They are pockets of pure empty air, shaped like lenses, spheres, or long thin threads, suspended throughout the dome. They are also where the water goes.

The chord keeps the air in constant motion, and the air is warm and humid, rising off the damp river flats. Moisture condenses on the vibrating stems but cannot rest there; it is shaken off as fine mist. In the loud zones the mist stays suspended and glittering. In the nodes, where nothing moves, it slowly settles and gathers. Every node holds a little water: a floating bead, a lens-shaped pool hanging in midair, a trembling sphere of clear water the size of a fist or a head or, in the oldest and largest nodes, a small room.

The town's water hangs in its silences. It does not fall, because the pressure of sound around the node holds it in place like a hand cupped around a flame. If you reach into a node and touch the water, it is cool and very still, and it tastes faintly green. If you disturb it enough to push it out of the node, the noise instantly shatters it into mist.

Rain does fall sometimes, in warm sudden bursts off the flats, drumming through the upper lattice. In the loud zones the drops are knocked into spray before they reach the floor. The dome's interior becomes a slow bright fog, and the nodes swell. After a heavy rain some of the floating pools grow large enough to swim in.

## The Other Animals

The singers are the town's builders. They are not its only residents.

Swifts nest in the upper lattice in enormous numbers and hunt the singers that emerge to fly at dusk. They fly through the dome's empty volume in long curving streams, perfectly silent to the ear because their calls are lost in the chord. They seem to navigate by the loud and quiet zones, banking along invisible edges. When a flock passes through a node, you briefly hear a thousand wings at once, a single dry rush, before the chord closes over them again.

Pale frogs, translucent enough that you can see the dark knot of their hearts, live in the floating pools. They are born in the water, grow in the water, and spend their whole lives suspended in a sphere of silence in the middle of the air. Some have never touched a solid surface. When they call, the sound stays inside the node, and a person standing in the same silence hears a small, clear, bell-like voice that no one outside can hear.

Long-legged spiders spin enormous sparse webs across the open volumes between galleries. The webs vibrate constantly in the chord, and the spiders seem to read them like a map, feeling where the air thickens or thins. Geckos patrol the galleries. Moths come in at night by the thousands, drawn by something in the sound. Bats come for the moths and seem half-mad in the noise, their own voices drowned out, flying by memory and by the feel of the pressure on their skin.

Everything here is abundant but spread thin through the enormous hollow. You might watch a gallery for an hour and see only a gecko. Then the dusk swifts come, and for ten minutes the air seems more bird than air.

## The Breakdowns

This is where the place stops being a strange ecosystem and becomes something else.

The sound is so dense, and so old, that it has begun to carry more than sound.

**The echoes that come first.** In certain galleries, especially near the central plaza, sound arrives before its source. You hear a flock of swifts pass, and three seconds later the flock passes. You hear rain begin, and a little later the rain begins. Those who have spent time here say that if you stand in the right place you can hear your own footsteps approaching from ahead, and then you take them.

**The rooms that aren't there.** The standing wave is not perfectly stable. Tiny shifts in the chorus, when a generation of singers dies or the temperature changes or a great rain fills the dome with mist, make the loud zones flicker. For moments the wave shows the shape it would make if the chord were slightly different. In those moments rooms and galleries seem to appear in the air, faint as mist and exactly as solid-seeming as the real ones. They are the town as it might have grown. You can see into them. Some have things inside.

**The voices.** The chord has been sounding for so long, and the plant has grown so deeply into it, that the tone seems to hold traces of every sound that has ever entered it. In the loudest zones, if you stop resisting the noise and let it take you, it resolves into speech. The words are in no language you know, and seem to come from many directions and many times at once. They sound like people who once lived in the hanging rooms, or will, or might.

**The silences that hold more than water.** The nodes are not always empty. Sometimes a node holds a moment of stillness from somewhere else. You step into the silence and find it smells of a different season or holds a different temperature. Or the light inside is the light of another hour: dusk at midday, or a bright noon deep in the night. Once in a while a node holds a sound from outside the chord entirely: a dog barking, a door closing, someone laughing. Nobody knows whether those sounds are from the past, the future, or somewhere that isn't here.

## Time

Nothing here is new. The galleries are worn smooth, the stems have grafted into shapes that look carved, and the chord has held so long that the fundamental tone has worn a faint groove through the hollow of every stem. The balance between plant and singer, sound and silence, mist and pool has long since settled. The town has stopped growing in any direction that matters. It renews itself steadily, stem by stem and generation by generation, and holds its shape.

Yet everything in it is moving. The mist circulates, the pools swell and shrink, and the swifts stream through at dusk and dawn. Spiders rebuild their webs every night, frogs call in their floating silences, and the bats hunt in their confusion. The chord breathes, swelling and ebbing, and the light shifts through ten thousand small windows. The echoes arrive early, the ghost-rooms flicker, and the voices speak.

The Standing Town is small enough to walk across in a few minutes and full enough of empty air to swallow a mountain's worth of silence. Built without a single tool, it is a city of one chord. Its water hangs where the sound cancels, and its walls sing.

When you leave, the drone follows you out across the flats and fades slowly behind you. For days afterward the ordinary world seems very quiet. You find yourself pausing in rooms, listening for the place where the sound cancels out, half expecting to find water floating there.
