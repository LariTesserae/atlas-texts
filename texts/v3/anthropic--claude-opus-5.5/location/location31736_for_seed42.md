# The Drowned Terraces of the Upper Tarn

The lake sits in a cirque high above the treeline, a bowl of grey rock scooped out by ice that left long ago. From the rim it looks like any mountain tarn: small, cold, dark at the center and green-gold at the edges, with scree running down to the waterline and a single outflow that trickles away through boulders. You could walk around it in an hour. The wind up here is steady and thin, and there is snow in the north-facing gullies even in late summer.

Look down through the water on a clear day, though, and the shapes resolve. There are walls under there. There are streets.

---

## The Town

It was built in terraces, the way mountain settlements are, stepping down the slope of the old valley floor toward a stream that no longer exists. The masonry is drystone: flat slabs of local schist, fitted without mortar and chinked with smaller stones. The builders had hand tools, hammerstones and perhaps a few soft iron wedges, and nothing else. The doorways are low and the lintels are single long slabs. Some houses are round and some are rectangular, and a few larger buildings near the center might have been byres or halls or granaries. Narrow lanes run between them, stepped where the slope steepens, and in places they are paved with stones worn smooth by feet that stopped walking a long time ago.

Nobody knows exactly when it drowned. At some point a rockfall came down from the eastern wall of the cirque and sealed the valley's mouth, and the stream backed up behind it. The water rose slowly, over a season or a few years. The houses show no sign of hurried abandonment. The hearths are swept, and there are no bones in the lanes. The people seem to have watched the water climb their terraces, packed what they could carry, and walked up and out over the pass. They left the town to the lake.

It is not a large place. There may be forty or fifty structures, spaced generously, with the kind of open ground between them that suggests garden plots or animal pens. It covers perhaps three terraces' worth of hillside. Its whole extent could be seen in a single long glance if the water were air. But it is unmistakably a town, planned, with lanes that meet at a small open place in the middle where a flat standing stone still rises out of the silt.

---

## The Green

The water is the reason you can see the town. The vegetation is the reason it has lasted.

Every surface is grown over. It isn't draped in weed the way a shipwreck is. It is *made of* plant now, the stone carried inside a living skin. The lowest terraces, in the deepest water, are carpeted in stoneworts: charophytes, algae built like tiny horsetails, whorled and jointed and faintly gritty to the touch. They grow in dense meadows knee-high to a person, pale green shading to grey. Over centuries they have done something strange and patient. Stoneworts pull calcium out of the water and deposit it on their own stems as a crust of lime. When they die they fall and are buried, and the next generation grows on top of them. The result is marl, a soft white limestone precipitated by plants.

The marl has filled in the lower lanes almost to the lintels. It has flowed into the chinks between stones and hardened there, so the drystone walls, which were never mortared, now are, by algae. Some low walls have been entirely overtaken and survive only as long ridges in the white-green meadow. The town is slowly turning from built stone into grown stone, and the process is so slow that a person diving here in their youth and again in old age might notice a door that had been open is now half-closed.

Higher up, in the brighter water, the plants change. Pondweeds and water milfoil rise in tall feathery columns from the roofless houses, each building a planter, each doorway a gap in a green curtain. Long ribbons of weed trail from the tops of walls and sway so slightly that they look still unless you watch for a full minute. Mosses coat the north faces of everything in thick dark cushions, fontinalis and its cousins, the water moss that grows where the water is cold and clean. Filamentary algae hazes the open ground between houses like mist frozen in place.

There is almost no bare stone anywhere. Put your hand on a wall and you touch moss, or marl, or the stiff bristle of stonewort, or the slippery film of diatoms that coats everything else. The town has become the frame for a garden that no one planted.

---

## The Water

It is cold. It is not frigid, since in summer the top few meters warm enough to swim briefly, but below that it holds steady at the temperature of a spring, a few degrees above freezing, year round. Cold water holds oxygen well, and the plants pour more into it. On bright afternoons the stonewort meadows are silvered with tiny bubbles clinging to every whorl, and they break loose and rise in slow streams.

The light comes down clean. There is little silt in suspension, because the plants hold everything in place and the inflow is snowmelt filtered through scree. At noon the sun reaches the deepest terrace as a soft green-gold, bright enough to see color, dim enough that the edges of things go blue. Near the surface the light is ordinary daylight, broken into moving nets by the ripples. Nothing about it is strange or luminous. It is simply clear water in a high, bright place.

Twice a year the lake turns over. In autumn the surface cools until it is denser than the depths, and it sinks, and the whole tarn mixes. In spring it happens again after the ice goes out. These are the town's two seasons of change. The turnover stirs up a faint haze, the weeds lean, and a slow shiver passes through the green. Otherwise the water barely moves. Currents here are measured in the drift of a single particle across a street over an afternoon.

In winter the lake freezes over completely. The ice is thick and often clear before the snow comes, and in that brief window, if you lie on the ice and shade your eyes, you can look straight down into the town as if through a window pane. Then the snow falls and the lake goes dark for months. The plants slow, and the town waits under its lid.

---

## The Inhabitants

The town is full of life, though in the way of all high cold places it is spread out. There are long quiet stretches between the busy places.

**Caddisfly larvae** are everywhere. They build portable cases from whatever is at hand, and what is at hand here is the town: flakes of schist, grains of marl, fragments of old hearth charcoal. Their cases are tiny drystone constructions, and they crawl across the walls of the houses carrying miniature versions of those walls on their backs. In some sheltered doorways the empty cases of past generations have accumulated in drifts like rubble.

**Freshwater shrimp**, amphipods, swarm in the moss. Disturb a cushion and a hundred of them spill out, curled and hurrying.

**Char** live here, the cold-water fish of glacial lakes, dark-backed and spotted, their bellies red in spawning season. They hold station in the lanes, facing into water that barely flows, and they patrol the open square around the standing stone. In autumn they spawn on the gravel of the upper terraces, where the old paving has been kept clear by the currents of the inflow.

**Newts** walk the walls, slow and deliberate, and rise to the surface to breathe before sinking back down into the green.

**Water beetles**, great diving beetles and smaller whirligigs at the surface, hunt among the milfoil. **Leeches** ripple along the floor of the deeper houses. **Freshwater mussels**, some of them decades old, sit half-buried in the marl of what might once have been a garden, filtering the clear water.

And there are **frogs**. That is where the noise comes from.

---

## The Sound

Put your head underwater here in the spring and you will pull it back out, startled.

The tarn is loud. Water carries sound far better than air, and the lake is small and enclosed by rock. Every sound made in it bounces from wall to wall of the cirque and through every lane of the town.

In spring the common frogs come down from the scree slopes by the hundreds to breed. They gather in the shallows of the upper terraces and call, a low purring chorus in air. Beneath the surface it is something else. The frogs dive and call from inside the drowned houses. The small stone rooms, roofless but still walled, with low doorways, act as resonating chambers. Each house has its own pitch, set by its size, its shape, and the depth of marl in its floor. A frog calling from inside the round house on the second terrace produces a deep, hollow thrumming. One in the narrow rectangular building by the square makes a higher, buzzing note. With a hundred frogs in thirty houses, the whole town hums like a crude instrument, a stone organ played by amphibians.

The effect is unsettling, and hard to account for until you understand it. For a few weeks each spring the drowned town is louder than it ever could have been when it was alive.

The frogs are not the only sound. Diving beetles stridulate, rasping their wings. Water boatmen, tiny insects in the shallows, sing by rubbing parts of their bodies together. For their size they are among the loudest animals on earth, and their chirring fills the upper water like cicadas in a summer forest. Oxygen bubbles pop off the stonewort in a constant faint crackle, like a fire far away. When the ice forms in autumn it booms and groans and sings in long descending whistles that travel down through the lanes. When it breaks up in spring it cracks like thunder.

Even in the quiet months there is sound. Snowmelt rattles the scree on the slopes above and runs into the tarn with a continuous hiss. Stones occasionally let go from the cirque walls and plunge in. The water magnifies the smallest click. A mussel closing its shell is audible across a street.

---

## The Rule of the Place

Everything here happens slowly, and everything accretes.

The stoneworts precipitate lime, and the lime is filling the town. The caddis larvae take the town apart grain by grain and rebuild it on their backs, and their cases fall and are buried and become part of the marl. The moss thickens by fractions of a millimeter a year. The water rises and falls a hand's width with the seasons. The lake turns over twice a year and freezes once.

In a few thousand more years the town will be gone, not collapsed but entombed. It will be a white limestone hill under green water, with the ghosts of walls inside it and a standing stone buried to its tip. The frogs will still sing, but the rooms will have closed around them. Their song will change as each chamber fills, going higher, then thinner, then silent. The town's voice is on a clock set by algae, and it has been rising in pitch for as long as anyone has been listening.

For now it is at a strange balance point. It is still legibly a town and already a garden, still stone and already becoming plant. It is a place people built and then gave away, and the animals and weeds have taken it up without changing its shape. It sits high and cold in its bowl of rock, clear as glass, loud in the spring and silent under the winter ice, getting smaller and whiter by the century.

At the rim of the cirque, where the old path comes over the pass, there is a cairn. It might have been put there by the people who left, looking back one last time. It is the only part of the town that stayed dry.
