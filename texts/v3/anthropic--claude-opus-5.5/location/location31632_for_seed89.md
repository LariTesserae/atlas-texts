# The Roost Below the Summit

## The Approach

The mountain ends in a ridge of pale, fluted limestone. The wind moves over it constantly and the air is too thin to hold any warmth. There are no trees here, and the last shrubs gave out a thousand meters below, where the cloud deck lies. From this height the cloud looks like a floor you could walk on. Above it there is only rock and sky, and the rock is scored into knife-edges and runnels by a very long history of rain.

Just beneath the crest, on the lee side, there is a slot in the ridge. It is about as tall as a person and about as wide as two. Warm air leaves it.

You feel this before you see anything. Up here the air bites, but standing at the lip of the slot you are breathed on by the mountain. The breath is humid and sour, sharp with ammonia, and at the back of it is something sweet like overripe fruit. You can also hear the slot. It is a pressure of sound more than a noise: a dense, many-throated chittering, broken by clicks, and under it the sound of wings, so many and so continuous that it becomes a single texture, like surf heard from inside a shell.

## The Mouth

The first few meters are the only green in the place. The warm, wet exhalation of the cave meets the cold outside air here, and the difference condenses on the walls as a permanent sweat. Liverworts grow on it, flat and lobed, a dark bottle-green, packed into every hollow of the stone. Mosses hang in small wet beards. A single fern, no longer than a hand, grows from a crack at head height. It is the highest fern on the mountain, and it grows only because the cave keeps it warm.

After three more steps the light turns grey. After three more there is no light at all.

## The Room

The chamber beyond is not large. Walking straight you would cross it in a minute. Its ceiling rises into a dome perhaps thirty meters high, and the whole room is a single rounded hollow, like the inside of a skull or a bell. It is the kind of room that feels bigger in the dark than it is. Sound moves through it strangely, slapping back from the curved walls, and the shape of the space is something you learn with your ears.

The room is full of bodies.

The ceiling is not stone. It is fur. Bats hang shoulder to shoulder across the entire dome in a living pelt that shifts and ripples. Where they are thickest, near the apex, they hang in layers: bats clinging to bats clinging to rock, a mat a hand's depth thick. The temperature rises as you look upward. The floor is warm, like a room with a fire in it. The apex, where tens of thousands of small bodies cluster, would feel like bathwater. The colony heats its own room. The mountain supplies the walls, and the animals supply the summer.

There are several kinds of bats, sorted by habit rather than by any visible boundary:

- **The small insect-eaters** crowd the dome in their hundreds of thousands. They leave at dusk in a single roaring column.
- **A larger, fruit-eating kind** hangs in looser clusters near the mouth. Their wings are wrapped around them like umbrellas. They are the source of the sweetness in the air: they fly down below the clouds to the orchards and figs of the valleys, eat, and fly back up to digest. The mountain has no fruit of its own. It is fed from below.
- **Swiftlets** own the walls. They are not bats but small, dark, sickle-winged birds. Their nests are cups built from their own hardened saliva, pale and translucent like old wax, stuck to the stone in rows so dense they overlap. Each wall is scaled with thousands of these cups. The swiftlets navigate the dark by clicking, a dry, rapid ticking like fingernails on a table. Their clicking is the only sound in the chamber that a human ear can resolve into individual notes. Everything the bats say is too high to hear, except the chittering of their social arguments, which never stops.

## The Floor

Below all this, the floor is a landscape of its own. It is made entirely of what falls.

Guano has been accumulating here for longer than any living tree has stood anywhere. It forms dunes, soft ridges and hummocks several meters deep. The fresh layer is dark and damp. The older layers underneath have compacted into something like peat, and the oldest have turned to stone. Where the floor slopes you can see strata in the cut faces, banded like sediment in a canyon wall: thin dark lines from wet years, thick pale lines from dry ones. The floor is a calendar that no one reads.

The dunes are alive. The surface moves:

- **Beetles**, small and black, in densities that make the guano seem to boil.
- **Cockroaches**, pale brown and soft, which scatter from any disturbance with a sound like rain on dry leaves.
- **Millipedes**, coiled in the damper hollows.
- **Mites and springtails**, too small to see individually, which give the surface a faint, crawling grain.
- **Cave crickets**, colorless and long-legged, with antennae three times their body length. They stand motionless on the slopes, feeling for the air currents of anything larger.
- **Centipedes**, the predators of this layer, as long as a forearm. They are rust-colored and many-legged. They hunt the beetles and crickets, and on some ledges they climb the walls, hang from the rock by their hindmost legs, and pluck bats out of the air.

Snakes live here too, lean, pale racers that coil on ledges near the mouth. At dusk, when the column of bats pours out, the snakes stretch their heads into the stream and strike blind, catching bats by feel. Almost every night, almost every snake eats. The colony is so vast that these losses are invisible.

## The Plants That Cannot Live

The fruit bats drop seeds. They fall into the guano, which is the richest soil imaginable, warm and wet and full of nitrogen, and they germinate. Scattered across the floor of the chamber are seedlings: fig, guava, things from the valleys that have never grown at this altitude and never will.

They are white. They grow toward a sun that does not exist, pushing up pale, thread-thin stems with tiny yellowish leaves that never unfold. Some reach a hand's height before they fail. Each is a small, doomed forest, sprouting and dying in continuous succession, so there are always seedlings on the floor and they are always the same age.

Fungi do better. Mold furs the older guano in grey and faint mustard. In the dampest hollows, small, stalked, brown-capped mushrooms rise from the dunes. They are eaten by beetles almost as fast as they grow.

## Water

There is no stream here and no spring. This close to the summit, all water arrives from the air.

The colony breathes out water. The warm, wet exhalation of a million small bodies rises to the dome, meets the stone, and condenses. It gathers on the rock, on the fur, and on the tips of hanging wings, and it falls. There is a slow, perpetual indoor rain in the chamber. The drops are warm, and they fall from the living ceiling onto the living floor. The rain is heaviest at dawn, when the colony returns, crowds together, and settles. It is lightest at dusk, when the room empties.

The drops collect at the lowest point of the chamber, in a shallow basin at the back. The pool is perhaps three meters across, dark and tea-colored, and faintly warm. Its surface is never still, pocked always by the drip from above. Tiny white shrimp live in it, and the larvae of some fly, and a film of bacteria that gives the water a slick, greasy sheen. The pool does not overflow. It seeps away into the mountain through cracks in its floor. A kilometer below, where it emerges from the cliff as a trickle, the valley people say the water tastes of the sky.

## The Stone

The mountain was once the floor of a sea. Pressed into the walls of the chamber, wherever the bats and swiftlets have not covered them, are the ghosts of shells: spirals, ribbed fans, fragments of coral, the stone equivalents of creatures dead for a hundred million years. Here in the room the old sea is coming apart.

Guano is acidic. The ammonia rising from the floor, the phosphates, and the damp all eat the limestone. The room is slowly dissolving from the inside. The colony has, very gradually, been digging its own chamber. Where the dome now curves there was once a narrow crack. Over a span of time without a clear beginning, generations of bodies and their breath and their waste widened it into this bell-shaped hollow. The walls bear a crust where the stone has reacted with the guano, a pale, chalky, phosphatic rind, soft enough to mark with a fingernail.

In places the walls are polished. Near the mouth, where the exodus is narrowest, the rock has been rubbed smooth and faintly glossy. Nothing did this but the brush of wing-membrane and fur, a million times a night, over some unimaginable number of nights. It is the smoothness of a stair worn by feet, except that the feet were wings.

## Night

At dusk the room turns itself inside out.

There is no signal a human could detect. Perhaps it is a drop in the pressure of the outside air through the slot, or a faint cooling. The chittering rises in pitch and intensity until the room is almost unbearably loud. Then the first bats drop from the ceiling, and the rest follow, and the dome empties from the top down like water draining from an upturned bowl.

The exodus through the slot takes nearly an hour. It is a dark river pouring out over the ridge and down toward the clouds, and it roars. The air inside the room gets colder as the living heat leaves. The drip slows. The snakes feed. The floor goes on, indifferent: beetles, crickets, centipedes, the pale dying seedlings.

For the hours of the night the chamber is almost quiet. Only the swiftlets remain, clicking in their nests, along with the drip, and a sound almost too faint to notice, which may be the stone contracting as it cools.

At dawn the colony returns and pours back in through the slot. The warmth returns, the rain returns, and the noise returns.

## The One Human Trace

People have been here. Not many, not often.

High on one wall, reachable only by climbing, the swiftlet nests have been harvested. In one patch the nests are younger and more sparsely spaced than elsewhere, as though the birds are still slowly reclaiming ground. The nest-gatherers of the valleys came up the mountain, climbed into the dark with ladders of lashed cane, and cut the saliva-cups from the stone with blades. They came once a generation, perhaps, and perhaps they have stopped.

What they left is small. On a ledge near the mouth there is a shallow stone bowl, a lamp, its lip blackened, the fat long since gone. Above it, the ceiling is faintly sooted, the only dark smudge on the pale rind. And in the flowstone of one wall, half-engulfed, is a length of knotted fiber cord. The stone has grown over it, slowly and without effort, the way it grows over everything. In a few more centuries it will be a fossil too, a shell in the wall of a room that eats itself.

## What the Place Is

It is a small, warm, loud, living room at the top of a cold, empty mountain. It is made by animals and heated by them. They feed it from the valleys, rain on it with their breath, and carve it slowly larger with their waste. The mountain gives it walls and height. Everything else, including the weather, is made by the bodies hanging in the dark.
