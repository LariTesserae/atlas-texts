# The Seam

## The Crack

High on the mountain, above the last trees that can stand upright in the wind, the ground has opened. It opened recently, by the measure of stone. The edges are still sharp enough to cut a careless hand. The rock is black and glassy, a kind of basalt that has not yet weathered to grey. It fractures in clean planes, and light slides across those planes the way it slides across oiled wood.

The crack is not large. You could walk its whole length in the time it takes to eat a meal slowly, perhaps four hundred paces from where it begins as a hairline in the scree to where it ends in a tangle of fallen blocks. At its widest it is twenty paces across. It is deep, though. Look down and the walls go on into a blue dimness that never quite resolves into a floor. Warm air rises out of that dimness. It is not hot, only a little warmer than the mountain, and it smells faintly of wet iron.

Up on the open slopes, everything is cold wind, bare stone, and thin grass flattened sideways. Inside the crack the air is still. That stillness is the first thing you notice when you climb down. The second is the green.

## The Green

Everything the rift has touched, it has grown over. The warm breath from below meets the cold air from above, and the meeting makes fog. Most evenings the fog pours into the crack like milk into a cup. By morning it has condensed on every surface and dripped away, leaving the walls beaded and glistening. It almost never rains here in any proper sense. The water arrives as mist, clings, and falls in single drops. Stand still long enough and you will hear them, one every few breaths, landing on leaves somewhere below you.

This slow, patient water has made the walls impossibly lush. The moss is so thick that pressing a hand into it sinks you to the wrist, and it is layered in colours: gold-green on top, a deep bottle colour beneath, a pale almost-white root mat against the stone. Ferns grow out of the moss in whorls, their fronds unrolling from tight fists. Some are as small as a fingernail. Others lean out over the drop with blades longer than a person is tall, their undersides rusted with spores. Pale-stemmed plants with no leaves at all send up translucent shoots, like grey glass, that feed on the rot of everything else. There are flowers, but they are the small, secretive kind: tiny cups of dull violet hidden under fern fronds, and white stars that open only in fog.

The green is young, as the rock is young. You can see it happening. Bare fractures made by last season's small tremors are already skinned with the first moss. Older ledges carry soil a hand deep. The oldest shelves, near the top, hold trees: stunted, twisted things with black bark and tiny leaves, rooted sideways into cracks, their trunks bent in shapes that follow the fog rather than the sun.

The light is always dim. The sun reaches into the rift for perhaps an hour at midday, a single slanting blade that crawls down one wall and back up again. The rest of the time it is fog-light, twilight, or darkness. The colours down here are the colours of dusk: greens that verge on black, greys, the soft sheen of wet stone.

## The Town

People live in the crack. At first you do not see the town, because it is built of the same stone as the walls and has grown over as thoroughly as everything else. Then your eyes adjust, and it appears everywhere at once.

The dwellings are cut into both walls: rooms hollowed out of the black rock with hand-picks of harder stone. Their doorways are low and rounded and their sills are worn smooth. They are stacked in tiers, ten or twelve levels high on the steeper face, connected by stairs carved into the rock itself. There are terraces with low walls, where someone once grew something and still does, in places. Cisterns are cut into the ledges to catch the drip, their surfaces perfectly still and black. Channels are chiselled along the walls to lead water from one basin to the next, so the whole town is threaded with thin grooves where condensation creeps slowly downhill. Cords are strung between pegs everywhere, carrying tools, baskets, and messages.

And there are bridges. Dozens of them span the gap between the two walls at every level: walkways of lashed poles, woven fibre, and flat stones slung in nets. Some are broad enough for two people to pass. Some are a single rope with another above it for the hand.

It is a town. It has a plan, an order, layers of making. There is a lower quarter and an upper quarter, a place where the stairs converge into something like a square, and a long gallery with carved pillars that might be a meeting hall. The size and care of it suggest it was built for hundreds. Very few are left.

Most of the rooms are empty. You can look into doorway after doorway and see only moss creeping across a floor, a fern growing from a sleeping-niche, a stone bowl filled with rainwater and drowned moths. Here and there a doorway is curtained with woven grass, and behind it is warmth: a small fire of dried moss, a pallet, a figure moving slowly. Perhaps forty people live here. Perhaps fewer. They are spread through the town like the last seeds in an old pod, each household with empty rooms on every side.

## The Widening

This is why they left, the ones who left. **The crack is still opening.**

It widens by about a handspan each year. It is not violent. There are small tremors, felt more than heard, a shiver through the stone every few days. But over a generation the town has been pulled apart by the width of a room. The old bridges show it. The oldest are short, built of short poles, and they no longer reach; they hang broken down one wall, trailing moss like beards. The newer bridges are longer, and the newest are longer still, pieced together from many poles, sagging in the middle.

The whole town is a record of the widening. Staircases that once met in the middle of a crossing now end in air. On opposite walls there are carvings that were clearly once a single image, split down the middle, a crack of darkness between their two halves. A figure's outstretched hand is on one wall; what it reaches for is on the other, twenty paces away and still receding.

The people who remain spend much of their lives lengthening bridges. It is their main labour, their craft, their civic duty. A bridge is a living thing to them. It must be let out a little each season, its cords loosened and new lengths spliced in, or the widening will snap it. They measure time by it. A child is born "when the long bridge had seven poles"; an elder remembers when it had three.

## The Fauna

The people are sparse. The creatures are not.

The crack is so full of animal life that at times it seems the whole rift is breathing. Nearly all of it is silent.

**Moths.** The dominant presences are moths, and there are more of them than there are drops of fog. They are soft, broad-bodied things, grey-brown and dusty, with wings as wide as a palm and densely furred. By day they cling to every surface, to the walls, the undersides of ferns, the ceilings of empty rooms, packed wing against wing in sheets that look like lichen until one shifts. Touch a wall that seems to be stone and it may shiver and peel away in a hundred pieces.

At dusk, when the fog comes, they rise, all of them at once. The air of the crack fills from wall to wall with wings. It is like standing inside falling snow, except the snow is warm and brushes your face and eyelids and lips, and it moves upward. The moths climb through the fog in a slow spiral toward the open sky, feed through the night on the grasses of the high slopes, and come back down before dawn. The residents move through the evening flight with cloths over their mouths and eyes half-closed.

**The quiet.** The moths are not merely silent. The fur of their wings eats sound. When they are aloft in their millions, the crack becomes almost perfectly quiet. A dropped stone makes no clatter. A voice, even a shout, dies within a few paces, smothered as though spoken into a pillow. Even by day, with the moths settled in their sheets across the walls, the town is deadened and hushed. Footsteps are faint. The drip of water is the loudest sound there is.

The people have adapted to this. They rarely speak aloud, and when they do it is close, mouth to ear. Mostly they talk with their hands, or with touch: a squeeze of the forearm, a tap on the shoulder, a pattern pressed into a palm. For longer messages they use cords. A knotted length of fibre is hung from a peg beside a doorway or tied to a bridge-rail, and the knots are read by touch in the dark. Every bridge has knotted cords along its railings. Some are instructions for its tending. Some are names. Some are older than anyone can read.

**Other creatures.** Where there are moths, there is everything that eats moths.

There are spiders, of course, large and pale, with bodies like peeled almonds. Their webs span the smaller gaps between ledges, and they are rebuilt every dusk only to be torn apart by the flight and rebuilt again at dawn. There are small lizards with translucent skin through which their organs show as faint shadows. They live in the moss and flick out at resting moths, and they are so numerous that the moss seems to shimmer with them. There are bats that hunt the flight, but they hunt in silence, because a bat's cry is useless here. Instead they have enormous eyes, like wet black beads, and they drift through the moth-snow slowly, taking what they please.

And there are owls. There are only a few, which makes them the only thing besides the people that is sparse. They are pale, heavy birds that sit on the highest ledges with their faces turned toward the dark below. When the moths rise, the owls fall into the rising, and you can watch them from above: a white shape descending against the upward current of grey, wings folded, dropping into the depths and not coming back up for a long time.

Down in the moss live snails, uncounted, with shells of dull red. The trails they leave harden as they dry, turning from slime into fine, tough threads like pulled glass. The residents gather these threads, roll them, and twist them into cord. The newest bridges are strung partly from snail-thread. It is strong and resists rot, and a new length of it shines faintly in the dim, like a spider's line catching light. The people keep the snails the way other people keep bees: moss-beds tended on the terraces, damp and shaded, where the snails graze, lay their trails, and are left alone.

## Below

No one knows what is at the bottom of the crack.

The warm air rises from it. The moths do not go down into it; their sheets thin out a few tiers below the lowest dwellings, and beneath that the walls are bare, wet, and black. The owls go down. The residents lower things on cords sometimes. The cords come back up damp and warm to the touch, and occasionally with something tangled at the end: a white feather, a scrap of leaf from no plant that grows above, and once, the story goes, a knotted cord that none of them had tied.

The oldest carvings, the split ones, face downward. The figures on them are bent over, looking into the depths, as though the first builders came here not to live in the crack but to watch it.

## One Day in the Seam

Dawn is not light but the moths coming home. They settle in waves across the walls, each wave dimming the stone a shade further. The fog lifts slowly, leaving everything wet. In the pale grey hour, the few residents emerge. They walk the bridges and test the cords with their palms. Where the widening has pulled something taut, they loosen it, splice in snail-thread, and tie a new knot to record the change.

Midday brings the blade of sun, crawling down one wall. Where it passes, the moss steams and the lizards come out to bask, a scatter of glassy bodies on every ledge. For that hour there are almost colours: a green bright enough to hurt, the red of the snail shells, the rust of fern spores. Then the light slides away and the dimness returns.

In the afternoon, the tremor comes. It is a small shiver through the stone. A few pebbles fall and make no sound. Somewhere a cord creaks and holds. The moths on the walls ripple all at once, a wave of disturbance running the length of the crack, then settle.

Then dusk and the fog. The cool, heavy mist pours over the lip of the rift and sinks. The drip begins. The moths rise, the air fills with wings, the owls drop, and the quiet becomes total. The last residents are behind their grass curtains, hands on each other's arms, speaking without speaking. In the deep dark below, the warm breath keeps rising. The two walls lean, very slightly, farther apart.
