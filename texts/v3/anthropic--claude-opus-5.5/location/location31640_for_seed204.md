# The Seam

## From Above

From the ridge you would not know it was there. At a little over five thousand meters the mountain is a long spine of pale granodiorite, snow in its hollows, a glacier resting against its northern shoulder. The wind is steady and comes from the west. Nothing grows. Walking the crest, you come to a place where the rock simply parts: a dark slot, perhaps forty meters across at the lip, running a few hundred meters along the ridge before it pinches shut at both ends like a healed cut.

Look down into it and the city is there.

The slot is new. Fifty-odd years ago, as the inhabitants count, a deep adjustment in the mountain opened it in a single night. The walls still carry the sharpness of that moment. The broken crystal faces of feldspar and quartz catch the light and have not dulled. Edges are knife-clean. Loose blocks sit where they fell, unrounded, their fracture surfaces pale as bone. No lichen has had time to arrive. No soil has formed. The rock is as fresh as a broken plate.

People came because it was fresh. A new fracture tells you exactly how it wants to move.

## The Shape of It

The Seam is about three hundred meters deep, narrowing as it goes, so that the bottom is little more than a corridor a few meters wide, choked with fallen blocks and ice. Between lip and floor, the city clings to both walls. It does not rest on anything, and it does not bridge the gap in any obvious way. It holds.

The buildings are built into the rock. Their substance is a pale, faintly translucent ceramic, spun from the granodiorite itself and fused back onto it, so each dwelling seems to grow from the wall like a mineral bloom. The shapes are narrow and vertical. Stacks of rooms follow the natural jointing of the rock, and terraces are cut where the stone already wanted to break. Where one wall's structures face the other's, thin threads cross the gap: walkways no wider than a person's shoulders, lines carrying water and power, and a few filament bridges so fine they are visible only when frost collects on them.

The whole city is anchored by what the inhabitants call listening. Deep in the walls, threads of engineered material run through every crack, sensing the rock's stresses the way a hand on a horse's neck senses its mood. When the mountain shifts, and it shifts constantly by tiny degrees, the anchors answer. They are not quite pushing and not quite holding. They distribute. They soften a stress here and brace a surface there. Engineers from the lowlands have tried to describe the mechanism in the language of physics they know, and their descriptions keep coming out incomplete. The anchors seem to move force through stone faster and more selectively than stone should allow. The city works regardless.

The Seam is widening at about four millimeters a year. The city is designed to widen with it. Every crossing line has slack, every walkway has a sliding joint, and every building on the east wall is slowly, imperceptibly, parting from its partner on the west. The inhabitants measure the years by it. A child's growth is marked on a doorframe, and the doorframe's distance from the opposite wall is marked beside it.

## Water

The cleft is always wet.

The glacier on the north shoulder feeds meltwater down through the fracture network. It emerges from the walls as seeps, as dark streaks, as slow beads that gather on crystal edges and fall. The walls weep. In the lower city the weeping never stops. Every surface is filmed and glistening, and the air there is saturated and cold enough to feel on the teeth.

Cloud does the rest. When moist air crosses the ridge from the west, the slot catches it. Fog pours over the lip and slides down the walls like milk poured into a glass, filling the Seam from the top. On those days the city vanishes into grey, and the filament bridges become visible all at once, pearled with droplets.

The inhabitants harvest it. Strung across the upper reaches is a lattice of charged mesh, fine enough to pass light and wind but not water. The droplets it pulls from the passing fog run along channels into cisterns cut in the rock. The cisterns are the one thing in the Seam built on a generous scale: deep chambers of black water, perfectly still, so clear you can see the fracture faces of the rock beneath the surface going down and down.

Water is also the enemy. Every night it freezes.

## The Daily War

What the inhabitants fear is not a great earthquake, which the anchors can absorb, but the ordinary cycle of freeze and thaw.

Water enters a crack by day. At night it freezes and expands. The crack opens a hair's breadth wider. The next day more water enters. This is how mountains are dismantled: by patience, one night at a time, until one morning a block the size of a house lets go.

Because the Seam is young, its cracks are sharp and tight, and this is exactly when frost wedging is most dangerous and most effective. So the city fights it every night.

At dusk the temperature falls fast. The sun leaves the slot early. The narrow sky above turns violet while the upper walls are still lit gold, and then the gold climbs off the rock and is gone. As the cold comes on, the maintenance systems wake. Small crawling units, pale, many-legged and about the size of a hand, emerge from ports in the walls and spread across the rock in their hundreds. Each one follows the web of cracks, reads the water in them, and warms, drains, or seals as it judges best. They move slowly and deliberately, pausing often. At night, if the city lights are low, you can see their faint internal warmth as a slow scatter of dim points across both walls, creeping like stars that cannot hold still.

They cannot reach everywhere. By morning, ice has formed in the places they missed: glazes on the overhangs, beards of icicles from the weeping seams, frozen cascades in the lower city.

Then the sun reaches the upper walls, and the Seam's morning rain begins.

The ice lets go all at once as the light descends. First come small ticks and cracks from above, then a pattering, then a real shower of ice fragments and meltwater falling the full depth of the cleft, glittering in the slanting light. It lasts perhaps twenty minutes and moves downward with the sunlight. The inhabitants stay under cover. The walkways have hoods. Then it is over, and the walls stream, and the day's work of watching the rock begins again.

Sometimes a real block falls. There are nets for that: nearly invisible sheets strung at intervals down the depth, which catch stone and lower it slowly to the floor. The floor of the Seam is a slowly rising bed of caught rubble, sharp-edged, waiting.

## What Grows

Nothing, and deliberately.

The inhabitants understand roots the way they understand ice. A seed lodged in a crack becomes, in time, a wedge. So the city keeps itself sterile of green. There are no gardens, no potted herbs, not even lichen. The crawling units scrub any spore that settles. Food comes up from the valleys in sealed containers along a cable running down the mountain's southern face, and whatever organic waste the city produces is sent back down the same way.

This gives the Seam a particular austerity. Every surface is stone, ceramic, water, or ice. The only color comes from the rock itself: grey-white granodiorite flecked with black mica and pink feldspar, the dark streaks where water runs, the blue of old ice, and the pale glow of the ceramic when light passes through it.

## What Lives

Life gets in anyway, at its smallest and most stubborn.

In the meltwater films on the lower walls live springtails, tiny and dark, gathered in patches like spilled pepper. They graze on airborne dust and on microbial films the scrubbers miss. Glacier fleas travel down in the melt. Mites work the dry crystal faces higher up. The inhabitants tolerate these because they are too small to pry at anything.

There are birds, but rarely. A pair of snow finches nests somewhere in the upper north end, where the Seam narrows. They are seen perhaps once a week, darting across the gap in sudden flashes of white wing-patch. Once in a long while a wallcreeper appears, with slate-grey body and crimson wings, working its way up a wall in fluttering jumps and probing cracks for the springtails. When one arrives, people come out on their terraces to watch. It is considered good luck, or at least good news. It means the rock has enough life in its cracks to feed something.

Once, the old inhabitants say, a lammergeier dropped a bone into the Seam. They did not see where it fell.

## The People

The city was built for perhaps eight thousand. About six hundred live there now.

This is not decline. The Seam was designed for the cleft it would become, not the cleft it is. Most of the dwellings are empty and maintained, their rooms kept dry and warm, their anchors listening, waiting for a population that may or may not come. Walking the walkways, you pass door after closed door, terrace after vacant terrace. The inhabited rooms are scattered widely, a lit window here and another fifty meters along, so that at night the city looks less like a city than a scattering of watchfires on a cliff.

The people who live there are quiet by temperament and by necessity. The work is watching. There are rock-listeners, who sit in chambers deep in the walls reading the anchor network, and water-keepers, who tend the cisterns and the mesh. There are those who repair the crawlers, and those who work the cable to the valley. There are a few who seem to have come simply because they wanted to live in a place that was being made, a mountain still in the act of breaking.

## Sound

The Seam is almost silent, and its quiet has texture.

There is dripping: constant, everywhere, at a thousand different rhythms. Rock ticks as it warms and cools. Wind passes over the lip and makes a faint, low note, almost below hearing, that rises and falls with the gusts. Sometimes a crawler clicks as it moves. At dawn comes the rain of ice, and in the lower city the cisterns sometimes emit a single deep sound, like a drop falling into a very large jar, when water moves in the rock around them.

And there is a hum, faint and steady, that you stop noticing within an hour of arriving. It is the anchors. You only hear it when it changes. Twice a year or so the mountain makes a larger movement, and the hum shifts pitch for a few seconds while the whole city takes the stress. Every conversation stops. Everyone waits. Then the note settles, and the dripping goes on, and someone somewhere laughs a little, and the day continues.

## The Seam at Rest

Stand on a middle terrace at midday, the one hour when the sun reaches all the way to the floor. The light comes straight down the slot, and for a little while everything is lit at once: the wet crystal walls blazing, the ceramic dwellings glowing from within, the filament bridges drawn in bright lines across the gap, the black mirror of an open cistern holding a narrow strip of sky.

Far below, water runs over broken stone that has never been worn smooth. Far above, fog is gathering at the western lip, preparing to pour in. The crawlers are sleeping in their ports. A springtail patch shifts across a wet wall like a shadow. The rock is widening by the smallest measurable amount, and the city is widening with it.

In a few thousand years the edges will round. Lichen will win. The Seam will become an ordinary gully on an ordinary mountain, and whatever lives here then will not know that for a while, at the beginning, someone kept it sharp.
