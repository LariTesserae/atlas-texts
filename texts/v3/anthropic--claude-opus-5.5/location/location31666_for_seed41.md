# The Warm Throat

You come to it from above, though you won't see it until you're almost in it. On the surface there is dry scrub and pale limestone, cracked and hot, and one fig tree that grows too green for where it stands. Its roots don't spread across the ground the way the other trees' do. They go down.

Follow them. Between two slabs of rock is a gap barely wider than a person's shoulders. It is a throat in the stone, and the roots pour into it like rope let down a well. The air coming up out of that gap is warmer than the afternoon around it. It is wet and thick, and it smells of earth and rot and something faintly sweet, like fruit left too long in a closed room.

## The Descent

The throat bends twice. The walls are smooth, rounded by water that hasn't run here in a long time. Where your hands go, the stone is slick with condensation that is almost warm, the temperature of skin. The fig roots stay with you. They press against the walls, split into finer and finer threads, and grip every seam in the rock. In places they have swallowed the stone entirely, so that you're climbing down through a basket of wood with limestone showing through its gaps.

Then the throat opens and you are in the room.

It isn't large. You could cross it in twenty paces, and the ceiling is perhaps three times your height at its highest. But it is so full that it feels larger, the way a forest feels larger than the field it stands in. Your eyes need a long while. The only direct light is the thin column that follows you down the throat, bent and weakened by the time it arrives. It lands in a pale smear on the far wall and moves across the room through the day like the hand of a slow clock.

## The Hanging Forest

The first thing you understand is that the ceiling is alive.

The fig's roots have reached the room and let go of the stone. They hang free, hundreds of them, from the thickness of a wrist down to the thickness of a hair. Some have reached the floor and rooted again, thickened into pillars, and fused with their neighbors, so that one side of the chamber is a colonnade of pale, twisted wood. Others still hang, their tips swollen and wet and searching, hovering a hand's width above the ground as if they haven't yet decided where to land.

Everything else grows on the roots. There are mosses in greens so dark they are nearly black, ferns no longer than a finger, and pale fleshy plants with no leaves at all. Those last are some kind of root parasite, colorless as boiled bone, and they bloom in small waxy bells. In the throat-light a few true plants manage real green: a spray of maidenhair on the lit wall, a vine with broad soft leaves that faces the light the way a sunflower would, following the column of light across the stone each day and never quite catching it.

Below the light's path the floor is soft and deep. Leaf-litter drifts down the throat, along with seeds, dead insects, and droppings from above. The bats add the rest. Walking on it is like walking on a mattress. Your feet make no sound. That is the first hint of something you will keep noticing.

## The Heat

The room is hot, and the heat does not come from above.

At the lowest point of the chamber is a pool, and the pool is the source. It is shallow and roughly kidney-shaped, and its water is so clear that in the dimness it reads as a hole in the floor rather than a surface. You only believe it's there when something moves in it. The water is almost too warm to hold your hand in. It rises from a crack deeper down, where the limestone meets some older, hotter rock, and it seeps in slowly. There's no bubbling and no spring-surge. The pool fills by a finger's depth over a day and drains back into the floor over a night.

Its warmth is what makes the room. The fig above drinks from the damp that climbs the throat. The hanging forest lives in the steam. Over centuries the heat has laid down a rim of pale mineral around the pool, like the lip of a bowl. Shelves of flowstone, cream-colored and smooth, step down from the walls wherever the water once ran, rounded and settled, all their sharp edges long since softened into curves.

## The Rain

Once a day the room rains.

It happens in the late afternoon, when the surface above begins to cool. The column of warm, wet air rising up the throat meets cooler air coming down, and the moisture in it gives up. Condensation beads on every root, gathers, and falls. For perhaps half an hour the whole hanging forest drips. It isn't a downpour. It is a fine, dense, steady fall of drops, each one warm, each one landing softly in the moss or the litter or the pool.

Nearly everything in the room is timed to this. The pale bell-flowers open just before it comes. The moss swells visibly. Small translucent snails that spent the day sealed in the crevices come out and climb the roots to meet the water. After the rain the room steams more heavily than before, and for an hour the air is so thick you can see it moving in slow curls through the throat-light.

At dawn a smaller, fainter version happens, less a rain than a sweating. It has a smaller following.

## The Crowd

The room is crowded, and it is quiet, and it takes a long time to reconcile those two facts.

Start with the bats. They hang in the upper reaches where the roots are thickest, so many that the ceiling in places is more fur than stone. Hundreds, maybe more, folded and packed in clusters. They leave at dusk and return before dawn, pouring through the throat in a stream that lasts nearly an hour each way. When they go, you feel it: a pressure change, a warm stirring of the air, a papery shiver of wings. But you barely hear it. The bats are calling the whole time. The room is full of their voices, shrieking and clicking, mapping every root and every other bat in a roar of sound. It is all pitched above what a human ear can catch. The noisiest place for miles is, to you, nearly silent.

Then the smaller things. The roots are traveled constantly. Long-legged pale crickets, the size of a thumb, move along them with the slow deliberation of creatures that have no reason to hurry. Centipedes the red-brown of old blood hunt the crickets. Beetles work the floor litter, dozens per handful, turning the bat-droppings back into soil. There are moths everywhere: dusty brown ones on the walls, and a small white species that rests on the roots in such numbers the roots look frosted.

Geckos hunt the moths. They are nearly transparent, pinkish, with enormous dark eyes. They move in short silent bursts and then hold absolutely still, and there are more of them than you would think. Once you've seen one, you see twenty.

The pool has its own population. Small fish live in it, pale and eyeless, their bodies faintly pink with blood showing through the skin. They are adapted to water as warm as a bath and barely move except to drift toward anything that falls in. Shrimp as clear as glass cling to the flowstone rim. In the warm shallows at the pool's edge sit frogs, small, dun-colored, and damp. They don't call. No frog in this room has ever called, as far as anyone could tell you. They sit and swallow and, once a year after the heaviest rains, lay strings of eggs among the roots trailing in the water.

And the room is dense with the small predators of all of this: spiders in the crevices, a few thin brown snakes in the colonnade that take geckos and the occasional bat, and a kind of small, secretive rat that lives in the drifts under the ceiling and comes out to forage in the dark hours when the bats are away.

## The Glow

When the throat-light fades at dusk, there is still something to see by, a little.

The root-tips glow. Not all of them, and not brightly, but the swollen wet tips of the hanging roots that haven't reached the ground carry a faint green-white luminescence. It isn't the root itself. It is a film of something living on it, a fungus or a slime, feeding on what the tips exude. The glow is so faint you have to let your eyes forget the day entirely before you can see it. When you do, the whole ceiling becomes a hanging field of dim points, some of them swaying slightly in the warm draft, like a sky that has been lowered on strings.

The white moths come to it. They circle the glowing tips in the dark in slow soundless orbits. What they get from it isn't clear. The geckos come to the moths.

## The Marks

Someone was here, a long time ago. Not often, and not for long, but more than once.

Around the pool there is a low wall of stacked stones, knee-high, following the flowstone rim on the side nearest the throat. It was built carefully, flat stones chosen and fitted, but it has been here long enough that the mineral water has cemented it. It is no longer a wall of separate stones but a single ridge of rock with the ghosts of joints in it. Roots have grown over it. Moss has grown over the roots.

On the lit wall, the one the throat-light crosses, there are hands. They are ochre handprints, a dozen or so, some large and some very small, pressed and sprayed onto the stone at the height of a standing person. The flowstone has been growing over them ever since. Some are clear. Some are faint shapes under a skin of translucent cream mineral, like hands pressed against the inside of frosted glass. One is almost entirely covered, just the tips of four fingers still showing above the flow, as if someone is sinking.

There are pots. Three of them, or what's left of three, set in a niche by the colonnade. They are rough, undecorated, the clay dark. One is whole and full to the brim with a mass of fine root, the fig having found it, filled it, and cracked it outward without breaking it. Nobody knows what was in them. Whatever it was, it's roots now.

And there is one stone by the pool, a flat, broad one, worn smooth on top in a way that stone doesn't wear by itself. It was a place to sit. It still is, if you want it. From there you can see the whole room: the pool, the wall of hands, the light moving on the stone, the hanging forest, and the throat going up into brightness.

## The Rules of the Place

Some things are true here that are true nowhere else nearby.

Sound doesn't carry. The soft floor, the hanging roots, and the heavy wet air drink it. A word spoken at normal volume at one end of the room reaches the other end as a murmur. A shout comes back as nothing, not even an echo. The loudest sound you're likely to hear is the rain, and even that is only a soft continuous hush, like breathing through cloth. Everything that lives here has made its peace with this. They hunt by touch and scent and, for the bats, by a voice nobody else can hear.

Nothing is still for long, but nothing moves fast. The day is a slow machine: the light crossing the wall, the pool filling and emptying, the rain, the bats leaving and returning, the glow coming up in the dark. Under that is the slower machine of the fig, which is always growing, always reaching, always slightly more of the room than it was the year before. Under that is the slowest one of all, the mineral water, which is patiently turning everything it touches into stone, the wall, the hands, the edges of the pool, grain by grain.

The room is not getting bigger and it is not collapsing. It found its shape a long time ago. Now it keeps it, and fills it, and breathes.

When you climb back out through the throat, the dry air of the surface feels thin and harsh and loud. The scrub buzzes with insects that suddenly seem deafening. And for a while afterward, when the afternoon starts to cool, you will half-expect it to rain.
