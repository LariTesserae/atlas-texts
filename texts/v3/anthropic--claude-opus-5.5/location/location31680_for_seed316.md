# The Mill

## The Approach

You hear it before you understand that you are hearing anything. For a long time it seems like a pressure in the sinuses, a fullness behind the eyes. Then the pressure resolves into sound. It isn't rising. It has been there the whole time at the same enormous volume, and you have only now noticed it.

Then the ground runs out of relief. The last low ridges, already worn to the height of a knee, sink into the plain the way a wave sinks back into the sea. After that there is nothing higher than a stone, and the stones go on for a distance that the mind first measures in days, then in seasons, and finally stops measuring.

This is the Mill. It is a basin the size of a continent, the oldest exposed surface in the world, worn to within a few arm-spans of perfect level across its whole breadth. It is not dead. It is still being finished.

## The Stones

The whole plain is paved in rounded stone. There is no soil and no sand at the surface, only cobbles and pebbles lying shoulder to shoulder to shoulder, packed so completely that you cannot see the ground beneath them, because there is no ground beneath them. Below the stones are more stones, down past any depth that has been guessed at.

Every one of them is moving.

A sheet of warm water, ankle-deep and rarely deeper than the shin, slides across the entire plain. It is pushed by a wind that has not changed direction in longer than there have been directions to name. The water does not pour or rush. It leans. Under that constant lean, the stones rock against their neighbors, turn a fraction, settle, rock again. Each individual motion is tiny: a pebble tilting a hair's breadth, a cobble shrugging a quarter-turn. But the plain holds more stones than there are stars, and every one of them is grinding against six or seven others at once.

That is the sound. It is the noise of the planet being rubbed smooth.

Up close the stones are beautiful in the way very old things are beautiful. There are basalts gone soft grey, quartzites polished to the look of wet sugar, banded stones whose layers were laid down in seas that no longer exist. None of them has an edge. Many are so evenly rounded that picking one up feels like being handed an egg. The Mill is slowly making its stones all the same, the same size, the same curve, as though it is working toward a single ideal stone and producing it in impossible numbers.

## The Sound

Deafening isn't quite the word, because deafening implies you will eventually be deaf. The Mill doesn't take your hearing. It replaces it.

The roar is continuous, with no rhythm and no breath in it. It resembles surf that never breaks, or a waterfall that is everywhere instead of in one place. It has depth, though. Beneath the high clatter of pebbles lies the lower groan of cobbles, and beneath that a sub-sound felt only in the sternum and the teeth: the whole stone-mass shifting as one body, meters down.

Shouting does nothing. Your voice leaves your mouth and is ground up within a hand's width. Travelers learn to speak by touch: palm to shoulder, fingers pressed in patterns on the back of a hand.

Those who stay long enough begin to hear things inside the roar. Voices seem to be in it, and bird calls, and something like rain on leaves, though there are few leaves here. Some say the Mill is a kind of grinding memory. Every sound ever made on the plain fell into the stone-noise and was rolled and worn along with everything else, until it lost its edges and became part of the general roar. You are not hearing voices, they say. You are hearing what voices become after ten million years of tumbling.

## The Water and the Air

The water is warm, bath-warm in most places, and no sun heats it. The warmth comes from the friction, from all those stones endlessly rubbing. The plain heats itself, and the heat rises.

The grinding also makes a fine pale silt, rock flour so small it barely counts as solid. The water carries it everywhere and turns milky, the color of weak tea with cream. You cannot see your own feet. Walking is done by feel, toes finding the rolling cobbles, ankles taking the endless small shoves.

The warmth lifts the silt. Above the whole Mill hangs a permanent mist, thick, warm, and faintly gritty. It is less vapor than suspended stone. Breathing it leaves a chalky taste. Skin grows a pale film within hours. The light that reaches the ground has passed through a sky full of powdered rock, which is why the Mill is always dim: a pearled, sourceless dimness, as though the day is being viewed through a bowl of milk. Noon and dusk differ only in shade. The sun, when it can be located at all, is a warm smear with no disc.

## The Weather

A place this large and this uniform makes its own weather, and the Mill's weather is a single storm.

The friction-heat rises everywhere at once. The steady wind shears it. Across a thousand leagues the rising warmth and the leaning air braid themselves into a vast, slow, permanent rotation, a storm too broad to see as a storm. From inside, it appears only as local events. Sheets of warm rain fall thick with silt and leave a film of mud on everything, then pass. Squall lines advance across the flatness like grey walls, and the walls have visible height because there is nothing else on the whole plain to give scale. Sudden gusts spin the mist into standing columns that wander for an hour and collapse. Sometimes the water-sheet tilts under a squall and the whole plain flows sideways for a while, faster, and the roar deepens a register.

It never truly calms. Calm here would mean the stones stopping, and the stones do not stop.

## Where the World Wears Thin

The Mill is old past the point of being merely old. It has been eroding for so long that erosion has begun to act on things erosion should not reach.

**Edges fail.** Shadows here have no edges; they fade out gradually, even in what little direct light there is. After some days, travelers notice that their thoughts lose their edges too. They find it hard to say where one idea ended and the next began, or when they started walking. The start of a sentence gets worn off before the end arrives. People who return from deep in the Mill speak in a smooth, continuous, beginning-less way that others find unsettling.

**Finished stones.** Now and then a stone is worn so perfectly round that the plain has nothing left to take from it. These stones stop behaving like stones. They lift out of the water and drift at knee height in the mist, turning slowly, faintly luminous with the diffused light. Friction no longer seems to know how to hold them. They wander downwind by the dozens, sometimes by the thousands, a slow herd of grey spheres over the roar. Touching one is a strange experience. It feels like touching nothing, a smooth absence of texture, a temperature exactly your own.

**Holes.** Sometimes a stone wears all the way out. It does not shrink to a grain. It wears out of existence, and where it was there is a small hole in the world. The holes look like dark pebbles, but they have no surface. The mist does not enter them, and the roar has a small silent notch where they sit. The other stones roll around them without touching. Over time the holes drift too, and in places they gather in shallow drifts. There, the plain looks as if it is fraying.

**Distance.** Because the Mill is so flat, the only relief left is the curve of the world itself. In the milky light the horizon behaves oddly. It sometimes appears closer than your own feet. Sometimes a squall line three days off is heard arriving before it is seen, and seen before it is under you. Sometimes it passes through you without the rain ever falling. Echoes are common here, even though there is nothing to echo from. Travelers report hearing a cobble turn and then seeing it turn a breath later.

**Down.** Deep in the interior, far from any edge, the plain has been level for so long that it has begun to forget which way is down. Water there flows in slow rings instead of downwind. Stones stack briefly and fall sideways. Rain sometimes rises. A traveler's inner ear, trying to find the slope in a place with none, delivers a steady nausea and the conviction that the horizon is above them in every direction, like the rim of a bowl they have been set down in.

## What Grows

Very little lives here, and what does has made peace with being ground.

The commonest life is a dark green-black mat, thin as paint, that grows on the upturned faces of stones. When a stone rolls, the mat is scraped away. It regrows on whatever face is now uppermost, never older than a few days, never thicker than a fingernail. Seen from a standing height, it gives the plain a shifting mottled pattern. Tracts of it fade and darken as the stones turn, the way a flock's wings darken all at once when it banks.

In the lee of the rare fixed things, which the cairns described below, the water eases for an arm's length. There grow reeds, pale and slender, their roots gripping many stones at once so that the stones can no longer roll. Reed-patches are islands of fixity. They are also, briefly, islands of quiet. Nowhere else on the plain is the roar even slightly lessened.

## What Moves

Animals are few and far between, and each seems to have arrived at a different answer to the Mill.

There is a wading bird, tall, grey as the mist, with legs too long for its body. It stands for hours on one leg in the milky water. The water parts around that single leg, the stones at its foot go still, and the bird makes around itself a tiny pocket of stillness. What it eats is unclear. Some believe it eats the quiet, and that this is why the roar seems thinner near one.

Beneath the stones live pale crabs, blind and flattened, riding the slow churn meter after meter down. They surface occasionally, flat as coins. Their shells are worn smooth, mottled and polished like the stones themselves. When they die, they are indistinguishable from the stones and are ground along with them.

Rarest of all is something travelers call the Slow One. It is a vast domed creature the size of a small hill, moving across the plain at the pace of a season. Its shell is always being worn down and always growing back from beneath, so it never has the same surface for long. It is the tallest thing on the Mill, and in the dim light it looks exactly like the last worn hill that should have vanished ages ago and simply refused. Perhaps it is. The stones grind around it, and the mist parts around it. Its passage leaves a long wake of faintly stiller water that lasts a day before the roar closes over it.

## What Has Been Made

Someone has been here. Not many, and not recently, but the marks remain.

Scattered across the plain, usually a day's walk or more apart, are **cairns**. They are small stacks of three, four, or five rounded stones balanced one on another. By every rule of the Mill they should topple within minutes. They do not. The water parts around their base. The stones at their foot stop rolling, and the mat grows thick and old on them, sometimes years old. A little reed-patch often grows up alongside. Within an arm's length of a cairn, the roar drops by some fraction. It is still deafening, but perceptibly less. Standing beside one after days on the open plain is like stepping from a gale into a doorway.

No one knows who built them or how. The most common belief is that the builders understood something about stillness: that three stones set in balance, placed rightly, make a place the Mill cannot finish. Perhaps they were trying to build quiet.

There are other marks. There is a low curved wall, knee-high, running half a league and then ending. No mortar holds it, only the patience of placement. There are shallow grooves in a few large stones, worn by some repeated hand-motion, as though someone sat in the same spot for a lifetime rubbing. In one place stands a single doorframe. It is two upright stones and a lintel, standing alone in the flow. The water on one side of it runs very slightly differently from the water on the other. Those who pass through say the roar on the far side sounds a fraction older.

There are no tools and no metal, nothing that is not stone or hand or patience. The Mill would grind anything else into silt.

## The Interior

Travelers who have gone deep, and some have gone very deep, report that the plain grows stranger by degrees rather than suddenly.

The finished stones grow more common until they fill the mist like slow grey snow. The holes gather into wide dark drifts. The water circles rather than flows. The roar does not diminish, but it smooths. It loses its textures one by one: first the clatter of pebbles, then the groan of cobbles, until only a single vast even tone remains, with no grain in it at all, as seamless as the stones.

Some believe that at the heart of the Mill the work is almost done. There, every stone is perfectly round and identical to every other. The roar has become one unbroken note. Every edge has been removed from the land, the light, the sound, and time. What remains is a place where nothing has a beginning or an end, only continuation, warm and dim and full.

No one who has walked that far has come back able to say where their walk began.

## Standing In It

On the open plain, in the dim warm roar, with the milky water leaning against your shins and the stones shrugging endlessly under your soles, the Mill does not feel like a ruin, and it does not feel like wilderness. It feels like a workshop at the end of a very long task.

The whole world is packed tight here, stone against stone against stone, with no gap anywhere. Every surface is being touched at every moment by every other. The storm turns overhead too slowly to see. A grey bird stands on one leg in a pocket of stillness you can almost hear. Somewhere downwind, a cairn is holding its small quiet against the entire continent.

And the Mill keeps grinding. It is patient and warm and tireless, wearing the oldest land in the world down toward whatever the world becomes when nothing about it has an edge left.
