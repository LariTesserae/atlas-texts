# The Header Tank

It begins with a sound, because you would hear the place long before you could see it.

Every eleven seconds, something enormous moves. It isn't a crash. It's a long, loaded *push*, like a door the size of a hillside easing open against a tide, and it ends in a deep iron note that goes on ringing after the motion stops. Then there is silence, or what passes for silence here: the fizz of bubbles, a clicking of small shells, the tick of metal cooling by a single degree. Then the push comes again.

This is the heart of the mountain, and the mountain is made.

---

## The Mountain Outside

From a distance, if anyone were at a distance, the mountain would look almost natural. It rises out of cloud and keeps rising, its flanks the grey-brown of weathered stone, its ridges softened and slumped. Close up, the ridges turn out to be conduits. The scree slopes are the scaled plating of some vast housing, each plate once bolted down and now fused to its neighbors by rust, lichen, and time. The "strata" are courses of masonry, riveted girders, and poured slag, laid down by builders so long gone that their work has eroded the way real mountains erode: rounded, slumped, gullied by rain, polished by wind.

Nothing here was ever bare rock. Dig anywhere and you find brick, then iron, then older brick, then something glassy and black that might once have been a floor. It is construction all the way down.

At the very top, where the air is thin and cold and the sky goes violet at noon, the mountain ends in a squat dome with a single round window in its crown. Frost grows on the outside of that dome every night. Inside it is warm, full to the ceiling with water, and alive.

---

## The Room

The Header Tank is small, about the size of a village chapel. You could swim from wall to wall in a dozen strokes, if swimming were allowed here, and it would take you under a minute to reach the domed ceiling from the floor. For something sitting on top of the largest made thing in the world, it feels startlingly intimate, like the last room at the end of a very long corridor.

The walls curve inward. They were iron plate once, and they still are, in the way a riverbed stone is still the boulder it came from. Every edge has been rounded. The rivets that held the plates together have worn down to smooth blisters, like knuckles under skin. Seams that were once sharp are now shallow valleys, and the valleys are where the plants grow.

The water fills everything. There is no air pocket at the top of the dome and no surface to break through. The round window in the crown sits fully underwater, a lens of thick greenish glass, and the high-altitude sun comes through it as a clean, moving column. The light down here is ordinary, ordinary daylight. It is simply filtered through water and glass, and it sways a little with every stroke of the engine.

The water is warm, bath-warm near the floor and a little cooler at the dome. The heat comes up from below, through the floor, from whatever the pump is doing down there in the dark.

---

## The Pump

In the center of the floor, a single piston sleeve rises out of the metal like a well-head. It is wide enough for a person to climb into, and its lip is worn to a soft, glossy curve. Inside it, something enormous travels up and down.

This is the rhythm of the whole place. On the upstroke, the piston's crown rises almost to the lip of the sleeve and pushes a slow bulge of warm water up into the room. On the downstroke it withdraws, and the room's water is drawn down after it with a long sucking pull, through grilles in the floor and out into the conduits that run all the way down the mountain.

So the tank is never still. Every eleven seconds, it breathes out and breathes in. Water comes up from the far lowlands, lifted through miles of pipe by engines no one tends, rests here for a few strokes, warms, and is released back down the mountain's thousand channels. The Header Tank is the high point of a circulation older than anything that remembers it. It is less a reservoir than a pause at the top of a very long breath.

The noise is immense. Sound carries through water better than through air, and the tank is an iron bell with its clapper built in. Each stroke rings through the walls and the floor and every shell and body in the room. Up close, you would feel it in your teeth before you heard it. And beneath the stroke runs a constant lower hum, which is the mountain itself: every other engine, every other pump in every other chamber, vibrating up through the structure as one far-off, unending thunder.

---

## What Grows

The plants arrived sometime after the builders left, and they took to the place completely.

Long ribbons of green weed root in the wall seams and stream out into the room. They lean toward the piston on the downstroke and away from it on the upstroke, so the whole tank seems to sway in unison, like a crowd listening to music. Near the window, where the light is strongest, the weed grows almost translucent, and fine silvery bubbles of oxygen cling to its blades until the next stroke shakes them loose to drift up and collect under the glass like mercury.

Around the rim of the piston sleeve, where the water is warmest and moves hardest, grows a denser, stranger thing: a velvet of rust-red moss that feeds on the metal itself. It is slowly and patiently eating the pump, and has been for longer than can be counted. Somewhere far below, the lip of the sleeve was once sharp. The moss is why it isn't anymore.

On the lee side of the old rivets, sheltered from the pull, grow small round-leaved plants like water-lilies that never found a surface. They hang in place instead, their leaves turned up to the window, their pale roots gripping the metal blisters.

The room is lush, but it isn't crowded. Wide channels of open water run between the growths. The piston's pull keeps them clear, sweeping the floor clean on every downstroke, so the green gathers only in the seams, the sheltered corners, and the window's halo of light. The space between is as much a part of the place as the growth.

---

## What Lives

Everything here lives by the stroke.

The shrimp are the most numerous: tiny, clear-bodied, flecked with orange. They cluster in the weed and let the upstroke loft them into the open water, where they feed on whatever the pump brings up from below. When the downstroke comes they flick back into cover with a sound like a handful of sand thrown at glass. Thousands of them do this at once, every eleven seconds, and the clicking of their tails is its own small weather beneath the engine's ringing.

Snails graze the walls. Their shells are made from the rust they eat and share its deep oxide red. Over generations they have worn faint paths in the metal, polished lanes that curve from seam to seam, and in the oldest parts of the tank these lanes cut deeper than the rivet valleys. The snails are, very slowly, the architects now.

In the piston sleeve itself, in the narrow gap between the moving crown and the ancient wall, live the eels. They are long, pale, and nearly blind, and they have learned the stroke so well that they never get crushed. They ride the crown up and slip down the gap on the return, eating the moss and the shrimp that tumble in. Now and then one emerges at the top of the upstroke, drapes itself over the lip of the sleeve like a loose rope, tastes the room, and is gone again as the piston falls.

High under the dome, in the light, there is a single large fish. It is dark green, heavy-bodied, and old. It holds its place just below the window, fins barely moving, and it does not seem to feed. It may be very patient, or it may be something else. The other animals give it room.

---

## The Rules of the Place

Certain things are always true here.

**Nothing rests on the floor.** The downstroke takes everything loose (shed shells, dead leaves, the bodies of old shrimp) and pulls it through the grilles into the conduits. Every discarded thing in the Header Tank begins a journey down the mountain, into pipes that branch and branch until they reach the lowlands. Somewhere far below, perhaps, a red snail shell washes up in a gutter and no one knows it came from the top of the world.

**Everything rises on the upstroke.** Warmth, food, silt, and sometimes creatures that have never been seen here before come up from the dark below the piston, brought from the other end of the long circulation. The ecology is local, but its supply chain is the whole mountain.

**The window freezes but the water never does.** Each dawn, the violet sky outside dims to pink, and frost-ferns spread across the outer face of the glass. The light in the tank goes soft and milky for an hour. Then the sun climbs, the frost burns off, and the column of light returns sharp and swaying. This is the tank's day. The plants tilt toward it, the shrimp spawn in it, the old fish hangs in it, and the stroke goes on through it without pause.

**The stroke does not stop.** Nothing living here remembers a silence longer than eleven seconds. The shrimp's clicks, the eels' timing, and the lean of the weed are all built around it. If the pump ever faltered, the whole tank would lose its tempo at once. The stillness would be the loudest thing that had ever happened here.

---

## The Last Trace

On one wall, half-hidden behind a curtain of weed, a ladder climbs from the floor to the dome. Its rungs have eroded to pale knobs, and the snails have polished them bright. Beside it, at the height of a person's chest, is a small rectangular plate, riveted on at the corners. Whatever was written on it has worn away completely. The metal is as smooth as the inside of a shell.

Someone once stood here. They climbed up through the mountain they had built to check that the water was reaching the top. They looked through the window at the violet sky. They made a note, perhaps, and went back down.

The water is still reaching the top. The tank is warm. The light is falling through the glass. Every eleven seconds, the heart of the mountain pushes, rings, and pulls, and the green room at the summit of the world sways to it and goes on living.
