# The Foundry Steppe

It begins as a colour before it becomes a place: a long bar of rust laid across the top of the world, flatter than anything that high has a right to be. The plateau sits well above the surrounding lowlands, so high that the sky thins to a hard enamel blue at the zenith. The heat does not thin with it. The heat comes up from the ground.

## The Ground

The tableland is old basalt, iron-heavy, and it has been weathering for so long that it no longer looks broken. There are no cliffs on its surface and no scree, no sharp edges anywhere. The rock has been worn into broad shallow pans, each a few hundred metres across, separated by low rounded ridges like the rims of plates set edge to edge. The pans are floored with a fine oxide powder, ochre at the centres and deepening to blood-brown at the rims. Where wind has scoured the powder away, the bedrock beneath is polished smooth, almost slick, and carries a dull metallic sheen. It rings faintly when struck.

There is no water. In the hottest hour of the day, small bright beads of something appear in the low places of the pans and run slowly downhill. They are not dew. They are tin, sweated out of the rock by the heat, and they harden again into grey threads when the shadows reach them. Over millennia these threads have laced the pan-floors into a filigree, and the whole plateau, seen from the rim, has a faint silver venation, as though it were a leaf, though nothing here has ever been a leaf.

The only thing approaching vegetation is a crust the colour of verdigris that grows on the north faces of the ridges. It is something between a lichen and a corrosion. It may be a reaction or it may be alive, and on the Steppe the difference is not always kept.

## The Herd

Then there are the stokers.

The first one is visible from twenty kilometres away, and at first it looks like a hill that has begun to smoke. Closer, it resolves into an animal the size of a cathedral. Its body is a low barrel of banded iron plate, dark and pitted with long exposure. It walks on sixteen legs, and each leg is a jointed piston. Along its spine runs a row of chimney-like vents, and from them rises a slow, dense, shimmering exhaust, dry as an oven's breath, smelling of sulphur and hot copper. Its head is a broad scoop of a jaw that works the ground. It eats the rock. It shovels up oxide powder and polished basalt, crushes it in a gizzard of grinding plates, and smelts it inside a firebox you can see glowing through the seams of its flanks, a dull orange like a banked forge behind louvres.

Behind it, it leaves a trail of slag: black and green glass, glossy, cooling in long ropes that crack as they cool. Much of the plateau's surface is ancient slag, weathered back to powder, eaten again, and smelted again. The stokers have been processing the tableland for so long that there may be no original rock left on the surface at all. Every grain has passed through a body.

Once you know how to look, there is a second one. It stands far beyond the first, a smudge of heat-haze on the horizon. Then there is a third, and a fifth, and then dozens. The herd is enormous. There are thousands of stokers, perhaps tens of thousands, spread across the whole breadth of the tableland, yet no two ever stand closer than a kilometre or so apart. The land between them is empty: bare pans, silver threads, heat. You can walk for an hour and touch nothing, and the whole time you are surrounded. That is the herd's peculiar geometry. It is overwhelming in sum and vacant in every particular. It is a crowd made almost entirely of distance.

## The Small Life

Among and upon the giants lives everything else, and the everything else is teeming.

**Bearing-beetles** roll across the pans, each a polished steel sphere about the size of a fist. They move in loose, clicking drifts that follow the stokers' slag trails to feed on the cooling glass. When a drift of them crosses bare bedrock, the sound is like hail on a tin roof, and it goes on for minutes.

**Flywheel ticks** cling in their millions to the stokers' flanks. They are disc-shaped and spin constantly, drawing some kind of energy from the heat of the plating. A stoker's side seen at close range shimmers with them, a whole hide of tiny whirring wheels.

**Wire-moths** are the strangest of the small things. Their wings are membranes of drawn copper so thin they are translucent, and they fly only in the late afternoon, when the air has built up enough charge. The plateau is electric. The friction of dry wind over oxide powder charges the atmosphere until your hair lifts and your fingertips spark on the rock. The moths feed on that charge. They gather in clouds above the ridges, and when a cloud grows dense enough it discharges, a blue-white arc leaping from moth to moth to ground with a crack like a rifle shot. Afterward, the moths glide down stunned and glowing and then rise again.

Nothing here was built. There are no roads, walls, or ruins, and no maker's mark on any plate. Yet the whole ecology is industrial. It has furnaces and gearing, rolling stock and electrical discharge, extraction and refinement. It works like a factory that no one designed, and it looks as if it had grown the way a reef grows.

## The Sound

The Steppe is loud, and its loudness is structured. The deepest layer is the stokers' stride. Each piston-leg strikes the ground roughly once a minute, and the impact carries through the bedrock for tens of kilometres, so that the whole plateau lives inside a slow, overlapping drumming. It is never quite regular, because the stokers are never in step. Above that sits the grinding of their gizzards, a sound like millstones heard through a wall. Their vents emit a low steady roar, and every hour or so each one releases a long descending whistle through some internal valve, a note that other stokers across the horizon answer. Then comes the hail-rattle of the beetles, the insect whine of the ticks, and the rifle-cracks of the moth storms.

At midday, when the heat peaks and every firebox burns hottest, all of it swells together into a sound that is closer to a pressure than a noise. You feel it in your sternum. Conversation is impossible. The land hammers and hums.

## What Is Wrong Here

The longer you stay, the harder it becomes to trust the Steppe to stay in one piece.

**The sounds arrive from the wrong places.** A stoker's footfall reaches you before its leg comes down, and often from a spot a few hundred metres ahead of where the animal stands. It is the spot where the animal will be, days from now, at its slow pace. The plateau seems to carry sound forward in time along a stoker's path. If you stand in the silent gap between two animals, you sometimes hear footsteps pass right through you from a stoker that is still on the horizon.

**The count does not hold.** Count the stokers visible from a ridge and you get a number. Count again and you get another. It does not matter how carefully you do it. Some who have studied the Steppe believe there are not thousands of stokers at all, but very few, possibly only one, seen at many moments of its long life at once. The plateau, they think, has folded its own history flat, so that the same beast in its youth, its prime, and its senescence walks the tableland side by side. This would explain why the stokers vary so much in weathering, from bright near-new iron to plates so eroded they look like rock, and why they never approach one another. They may be keeping apart the way a thing keeps apart from itself.

**The shadows lean toward the sun** in the late afternoon, very slightly at first and then unmistakably. Nobody knows why. The wire-moths fly along the shadows, not the light.

**Distance is uneven.** The ridges between pans take much longer to cross going east than going west. The horizon uphill is nearer than the horizon downhill. A stoker you walk toward for an hour can end up no closer, and then, after you stop and sit, it is suddenly beside you, the ground shaking, its firebox glow warm on your face, its ticks whirring a metre away.

## The Cycles

Almost nothing here happens quickly, apart from the moth storms. The stokers move perhaps a few dozen metres a day. The herd as a whole migrates across the tableland in a circuit that may take several thousand years, eating a band of rock and leaving slag behind to weather back into powder for the next pass. The tin threads grow by the width of a hair each season. The verdigris crusts advance up a ridge-face by a finger's breadth in a human lifetime. The heat rises and falls with the day, but even the day seems long, stretched and sluggish at noon.

At night the air cools only a little. The ground gives up its stored heat, and the seams in the stokers' flanks glow in the dark like the windows of distant mills: dull orange points strewn across the black plateau to the horizon, each one slowly breathing. The drumming continues. The whistles call and answer. Overhead the thin sky fills with stars. On the ground below, in the empty kilometres between the great forges, the bearing-beetles roll quietly downhill in the dark, following the silver threads toward the lowest places, where the tin gathers.
