# The Flue

It sits at the bottom of a shallow basin, barely above the level of the surrounding fen. From outside it looks like a single tree, or a knot of trees fused together: a round, rough-barked tower perhaps twelve metres across at the base and four times that in height, narrowing slightly toward the top like a bottle. Its bark is grey-brown, deeply furrowed, and so old that the furrows have worn smooth again, rounded at the edges like river stones. Moss grows in the grooves and ferns in the moss. Ivy climbs the ivy. Nothing on its surface is not alive.

Walk around it and you find the opening on the leeward side, a low arch where two great buttress roots part. It is just tall enough to enter without ducking. Inside, the tree is hollow.

---

## The chamber

The hollow is the whole of it. The living wood forms only a shell, a metre or two thick, and inside that shell there is nothing but air: a single vertical room rising forty metres to a ragged circle of sky. Standing on the floor and looking up is like looking up a well from the bottom, except the well is made of wood and it breathes.

The inner walls are the colour of old leather, with long vertical grain like the fluting of a column. In places the grain swerves around strange inclusions. There are horizontal bands of rust-red heartwood at regular intervals, a hand's width apart, ring upon ring all the way up. Where the light catches them they show a faint crystalline glitter. There are also shallow, square-edged pockets in the wood, and in one place, about head height, a long curved scar whose shape is a quarter of a perfect circle.

These are the only signs that anything was ever made here. Long ago, before the fen had fully drowned the valley, this was the site of a chimney: a brick stack over a furnace, an engine house, a works of some kind whose purpose no one remembers. When the works fell silent a seed lodged in the mortar. The tree grew up the inside of the stack and then around it, root over brick, sheathing it completely. Over an immense span of time the brick softened, crumbled, and was taken up into the wood. The iron bands that once held the chimney together rusted to stains. The tree simply kept the shape. It is a chimney made of a living thing, faithful to a form it inherited and no longer needs.

It still does what a chimney does.

---

## The draught

The first thing you notice inside is the wind. It is not blowing around you but *through* you, steady and upward, pulling at your clothes and hair toward the sky. The fen outside is cool and heavy with moisture, and the air in the basin is always slightly cooler still, pooled at the lowest point. The hollow's interior is fractionally warmer: the living wood respires, the mass of rotting leaf litter on the floor gives off a gentle heat, and the thousands of bodies roosting above add their own. That small difference is enough. Cold air slides in through the root-arch at the base, warms by a degree, and rises. The column never stops.

The sound this makes is the second thing you notice, and after that you cannot notice anything else. The flue *roars*. It is a deep, continuous organ-pipe drone, a fundamental so low it registers more in the sternum than the ear, with a shifting haze of overtones above it as the air eddies over the fluted grain. When a gust crosses the mouth of the stack the pitch leaps, whooping, and then settles. It sounds like the engine house never closed, like a furnace still drawing somewhere beneath the fen. People who have stood in old working mills say it sounds exactly like that: the breathing of a building built to burn.

But nothing burns. There is no smoke, no ash, no heat worth the name. Only the pull of the air and the enormous hollow voice it makes.

---

## The swifts

The third thing, once your ears adjust, is the birds.

The upper two-thirds of the inner wall is swifts. Not covered in swifts; it *is* swifts, a living plaster of dark bodies clinging to the grain with their tiny hooked feet, shoulder to shoulder, in numbers impossible to estimate. Tens of thousands. Chimney swifts have always roosted in chimneys, and this is the oldest chimney they know. The rising air suits them. They drop from the rim at dusk in a long spiralling funnel, a black tornado pouring down into the stack, and in the morning they boil up out of it again and scatter over the fen to feed.

Their chittering is constant, a fine high screeching layered over the drone of the flue. Twice a day, at the great exodus and the great return, it rises to something close to deafening, the whole column vibrating with wing-beats and cries. Between those tides the noise sinks only a little. Something is always shifting, squabbling, resettling. The flue and the birds together make a sound that never falls silent and never changes in character: a roar with a shriek on top of it, day after day, century after century.

Below the swifts, on the lower walls, there are bats: long-eared and pipistrelle, hanging in the deep grooves where the old brick courses have left ledges in the wood. Owls nest in the square-edged pockets. There are beetles in the soft lower wood, woodlice in the damp, spiders stringing webs across every crevice where the updraft brings them a steady harvest of insects lifted from the fen. The floor is a deep, soft mat of guano and fallen feathers and leaf litter, rich and black and faintly warm, where fungi grow in pale shelves and a small ecosystem of worms and springtails works through the endless fall from above.

Yet for all this life the room is empty. Everything clings to the walls. The centre of the hollow, that forty-metre column of rising air, holds nothing at all. No branch crosses it, no web spans it, nothing hangs in it. A feather released at the floor rises straight up and out the top without touching anything. It is a vacancy with a living skin.

---

## The water

The fen outside is not wet in the way a lake is wet. It is damp: saturated peat, sedge, a skin of moisture on everything, a few black pools in hollows that never quite dry and never quite fill. Rain falls here often but lightly, a fine drizzle that beads on the moss more than it soaks.

Inside the flue, water appears differently. The cool air coming in at the base carries moisture, and as it rises it meets the slightly cooler upper walls and condenses. The inner surface of the stack is always faintly beaded with dew. Droplets gather in the vertical grain and run down it in slow threads, joining, thickening, and at certain points along the old iron-stain bands they drip free and fall. The drip is continuous but sparse: a single bright bead every few seconds from somewhere high in the dark, falling the whole length of the column and landing with a soft tick on the floor. You can hear these ticks even through the roar, because they are the only sounds that come at intervals.

Where the drips land most often, the floor has worn into shallow basins, each holding a few centimetres of clear water. This is the only open water inside the tree. The bats drink from it at dusk. Its surface trembles constantly with the draught, but it never overflows and never dries. The drips replace exactly what the air carries off.

---

## The unchanging

Nothing here moves on any timescale you could watch. The tree grows, but so slowly that its growth is measured against the erosion of its outer bark, and the two have been in balance for longer than the fen has had its name. Each year it puts on a ring inside and loses a ring's worth of weathered skin outside, and so it stays the same size. The swifts arrive in spring and leave in autumn, but a new generation fills exactly the places the old one vacated. The draught rises at the same speed. The drips fall from the same bands. The pools stay the same depth.

Even the seasons barely register. The fen is cool in summer and a little colder in winter, damp always. The flue sounds the same in every weather. The only real change is the daily tide of swifts: out at dawn, home at dusk, a pulse so regular it feels less like an event than like the breathing of the place itself.

This is what very old things become. The furnace went cold and the brick went to dust and the iron went to rust, and what remains is the shape of an industry kept alive by a tree that doesn't know it's a chimney, drawing a wind that carries no smoke, housing a nation of birds, roaring for no one. It is small enough to walk around in a minute. It is loud enough that you cannot think inside it. It is empty at its heart, and it has not changed in a thousand years, and it will not change for a thousand more.
