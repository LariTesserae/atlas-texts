# The Breathing Town Under the Summit

## The Approach

The mountain is new. You can tell by its edges. Nothing on it has been rounded yet. The ridgelines are serrated like broken bottle glass, and the scree fields are not yet scree but slabs, still fitted to the places they cracked from. Its black and oxblood rock is less than a few centuries out of the earth. It rises so far above everything that the sky around it is no longer blue. At midday it is the deep bruised violet of an hour after sunset, and the brightest stars stay visible through noon. The air is thin enough that breathing feels like drinking from an empty cup.

It is also hot. The heat does not come from the sun, which at this altitude is small and white and strangely weak. It comes up from below, through the stone. The summit cone is warm to the touch like the flank of a sleeping animal. In places it is too hot to rest a palm on. Vapor seeps from hairline cracks and freezes, then unfreezes, then drifts, depending on which way the wind is breathing. The rock sweats, and the upper slopes are always faintly beaded with condensation that never quite becomes a stream.

Some two hundred meters below the summit, on the leeward side, there is a fissure. It is narrow enough to walk past without seeing. Its lips are fresh obsidian, glossy and uncracked, as if the mountain had been cut with a knife and the wound had only just begun to seal. Warm, wet air flows out of it in slow pulses, one exhalation roughly every forty minutes.

Inside is a town.

## The Chamber

The fissure opens into a gas bubble, a void left when the eruption that built the mountain trapped a lung of volcanic breath in cooling rock. The chamber is roughly the size of a large church, egg-shaped, tilted a few degrees off level. Its walls are smooth, dark, and faintly glazed, the way the inside of a kiln looks after a firing.

The town fills the lower third of this egg.

It is small. The streets are a hand's width across. The tallest tower reaches perhaps to your hip. Doorways are the size of a thumbnail, and windows are pinpricks. Yet it is plainly a town, not a model or an ornament. It has the unmistakable density of real settlement: streets that narrow as they climb, buildings leaning on older buildings, stairs doubling back on themselves, plazas where several routes converge, gutters, arcades, bridges between rooftops, and a quarter where everything is newer and more regular than the rest. There are perhaps eleven thousand structures. No two are identical. None is inhabited, and nothing has ever lived here.

The material looks like pale grey-green stone, finely grained, somewhere between sandstone and bone. It is not stone. It is a mineral precipitate. It crystallizes out of the vapor that fills the chamber, and it crystallizes *in the shape of a town*.

## How It Grows

Every forty minutes or so, the mountain exhales.

The breath begins as a faint pressure in the ears, then a warmth, then a slow thickening of the air until the chamber is full of hot fog. It glows faintly red from below, where the floor is thinnest over the magma. For about six minutes the town disappears.

When the fog thins, the town has changed.

The changes are small. A new storey has been added to a building in the eastern quarter. A bridge that was half-finished now reaches its far rooftop. A stair has grown three more steps. An alley that was open is now roofed. The deposition happens only on surfaces that are *meant* to receive it, and on nothing else. Your hand, if you leave it resting on a rooftop through the exhalation, comes away wet but uncoated. The unbuilt walls of the chamber stay glassy and bare. Only the town grows, and it grows *as a town*, following something like a plan, or something like a grammar.

Close inspection shows the precipitate is layered in bands finer than hair, each one a single breath. Old structures have tens of thousands of bands. The newest are translucent, almost gelatinous, and they firm up over the following hours with a quiet ticking sound.

The town is never finished. As new storeys rise at the top, the oldest foundations are slowly swallowed. The chamber floor is still soft in places, still barely plastic, and the heaviest districts are sinking into it. On the western edge you can see rooftops at floor level, their buildings fully submerged. Nearer the wall, only chimney-tips show. Beyond them, a few faint geometric ridges in the glass mark where streets once ran. The town is a slow wave moving across the chamber floor, rising on its eastern shore and drowning on its western one.

## Water

Water is everywhere here and nowhere in quantity.

Between exhalations, the vapor condenses on every surface. It gathers first on the chamber ceiling, which is coolest, and falls in a slow, fine, irregular rain: a drop here, a drop there, a few per second across the whole chamber. The town is built to receive it. Every roof is pitched. Every roof has gutters. The gutters feed downspouts, which feed channels in the streets, which feed cisterns. The cisterns are small round basins, the largest the size of a teacup, sunk into plazas and courtyards.

The cisterns fill and slowly overflow into lower channels. Those lead downhill toward the drowning western edge, where the water pools briefly on the soft floor and hisses away as steam, to be breathed back into the next exhalation. It is a closed, small, patient circulation. No one drinks from the cisterns. The town holds its water the way a mouth holds breath.

On the surface of the oldest cisterns there is a thin iridescent film, oil-sheened, violet-green. It is the closest thing to life in the chamber. It is not alive. It is a self-copying mineral skin, a lattice that reproduces its own pattern across the water's surface and dissolves back when the water rises. It has no metabolism, no cells, and no drive. It only repeats. Where two films meet, they form a sharp seam, and neither crosses it.

## Light

The chamber is dim, but not dark.

There are three sources of light. The first is the floor. In the thin places it glows a sullen, barely visible red, like an electric element seconds after being switched off. This light comes from underneath, so the town is lit from below. Every building casts its shadow upward onto its own upper storeys and onto the chamber ceiling. Seen from above, the ceiling holds a faint, enormous, inverted map of the town in shadow.

The second source is the windows. The pinprick windows of every building glow very faintly orange. There is no fire inside them. The interiors of the buildings are hollow, and they trap heat until the air inside radiates visibly. The town looks inhabited at night, lamp-lit in thousands of tiny rooms, and it is only temperature.

The third source is the fissure. For about an hour each day, when the sun is at the right angle, a single blade of thin white daylight enters through the crack and crosses the chamber. It moves slowly over the rooftops like the hand of a sundial. Some structures seem to be aligned to it. A narrow avenue in the eastern quarter runs exactly along the line the blade traces at one particular moment of one particular season, and its buildings are a slightly different, whiter shade than their neighbors.

## Sound

It is quiet here, and the thin air makes it quieter.

Sound carries poorly at this altitude, so what little there is arrives soft, close, and slightly muffled. You hear the drip of condensation, irregular, each drop landing on a roof or in a cistern with its own small pitch. You hear the ticking of fresh precipitate setting, a dry, delicate crackle like frost forming, heard only if you hold very still. During exhalations there is a low rush from the fissure, not loud, more a pressure than a sound. And between exhalations, very occasionally, the floor settles: a single deep, soft *tock*, felt more than heard, as the western districts sink another fraction of a millimeter.

There is no wind inside. There are no voices, and no echoes worth mentioning. The glazed walls should echo, but the town scatters sound. Its eleven thousand surfaces break every noise into pieces too small to return.

## The Founding Stone

At the center of the oldest surviving plaza sits an object that is not part of the town.

It is a dark sphere about the size of a plum. It is heavy, far heavier than it should be, heavy enough that it has pressed a shallow bowl into the plaza beneath it. Its surface is too smooth to be natural and too featureless to be manufactured in any way a person would recognize. There are no seams and no markings. Under magnification its surface does not resolve; it stays smooth at every scale you can bring to it.

It is cold. In a chamber hot enough to make you sweat, it is cold enough to fog. Condensation forms on it continuously. It does not drip. The beads grow, detach, and *float* in slow, perfect spheres, suspended in the air a few centimeters above the stone. They drift in slow orbits. When they finally touch a nearby building, they are absorbed into it, and that building grows a little faster than the rest.

This seems to be the origin. Somehow the stone organizes the vapor. It imposes a pattern on the precipitation, a set of instructions carried in heat and humidity and something else, something that bends deposition toward roofs and stairs and gutters and away from the bare chamber walls. The town is the stone's handwriting. Whoever placed it here, if anyone did, left no other trace. The mountain is younger than the town's oldest bands, which suggests the stone was here first, buried in the magma before it rose, and that it began building the moment it found itself in a hollow.

The streets nearest the stone are the most intricate and the most finely made. Moving outward, the architecture grows slightly simpler, slightly more repetitive. Near the chamber walls it degrades into forms that are almost-buildings: walls without roofs, stairs that lead into rock, doors that open onto solid glaze. It is as if the stone's reach has limits, and at the edges the grammar begins to forget itself.

## Rules of the Place

- The town grows only during exhalations, and only on itself.
- Nothing foreign is ever built upon. Objects left in the chamber stay clean.
- Water circulates but does not escape. The chamber loses almost nothing to the fissure, and the drip rate does not change year to year.
- The town moves east and sinks west, at about the width of one building per decade.
- The founding stone does not move, does not warm, and does not wear.
- The floating droplets never collide with each other. Their orbits pass close, and adjust.
- The town has streets, but no street reaches the fissure. No path leads out. Every route eventually turns back toward the stone.

## What It Is Like to Be Here

It is warm, damp, and very still. The air is so thin your heartbeat is loud in your ears. Before you, in red-lit dimness, a town the size of a garden lies with its thousand small windows lit, and nobody is home. Every forty minutes the fog comes up out of the floor like a slow tide. When it recedes, a staircase somewhere has grown three steps longer, the eastern rooftops have crept another hair toward the wall, and the old quarter has settled a little deeper into the dark.

It is building toward something. Or it is simply what the stone does, and the mountain happened to give it a room.
