# The Cut Cistern

The first thing is the light. It falls straight down through a square shaft cut in the rock, a little over a body's width across, and strikes water so clear that it doesn't stop at the surface. It goes all the way to the floor and lights the pale stone there, then bounces up and fills the whole chamber from below. The room glows the way a lantern glows, from the inside. The walls are white limestone, freshly cut, and they throw the light back and forth until there are almost no shadows. At noon it is almost too bright to look at. The water turns the color of the palest possible blue, and the ripples on its surface print moving nets of brilliance across the ceiling.

The chamber is small, perhaps fifteen paces long and eight wide, and the ceiling is a low barrel vault. It sits beneath a hillside, maybe four body-lengths under the grass, and from above you would never know it was there except for the shaft. That opening sits flush with the ground, rimmed with fitted blocks and half hidden by a thornbush that has already started to lean over it.

Almost all of it is underwater. The waterline sits a hand's breadth below the spring of the vault, so the room is a drowned box with a thin lens of air pressed against its ceiling. Everything that matters happens below that line.

---

## The Stone

Nothing here is natural except the rock itself, and even the rock has been completely reshaped. Every surface carries tool marks. On the walls they run in long diagonal grooves, set in bands where one worker stopped and another began, the angle shifting slightly from band to band. Near the corners the marks shorten and crowd together where the chisel had to work in close. The floor has been dressed flatter and smoother, with fine parallel scratches where someone dragged a sanding stone and grit across it over many days.

The cutting is recent. The edges have not softened, and the arrises of the vault ribs are still sharp enough to cut a palm. In places the tool marks still hold a faint powder of stone dust that the water has not yet washed out. Underwater the walls have the dry, chalky brightness of new work. There is no patina and no staining yet, only a faint green haze starting in the corners nearest the light.

Low on the north wall a band of darker stone runs across the chamber, a softer bed the cutters met partway down. You can see where they struggled with it. The chisel marks become gouges, and two places have been patched with fitted plugs of better stone, mortared in with lime that is still whiter than the wall around it. A single row of shallow sockets runs along one side at shoulder height. These are holes for timber scaffolding, now empty and filled with water. Small things like this show the shape of the work that happened here. The workers are gone, but their decisions are everywhere.

## The Water and Its Heat

The water is warm, close to body temperature and a little above. It rises from the rock itself. The cutters were following a warm seep, and at some point they broke into its source. At the east end of the chamber, near the floor, there is a rough opening that was never dressed. It is the only irregular hole in the room, the place where the builders' plan met the spring's intentions. Water pushes out of it steadily, and you can see it happening: the warmer water shimmers against the cooler water above, a moving blur like heat over a summer road, bending the lines of the stone behind it.

The spring carries minerals. On the rim of the rough opening, and along the path the outflow takes across the floor, a thin crust of travertine has begun to grow. It is cream-colored, grainy, and no thicker than a fingernail. Given enough centuries it would soften every chisel mark in the room. For now it is only starting, a pale smear marking the current's preferred route, as if the water were already drawing a map of itself.

A few fine bubbles rise from the opening in intermittent threads and cluster on the underside of the vault, where they collect into a silvery skin that trembles and occasionally lets go all at once.

## The Sound

The room is loud, and that surprises you, because everything visible looks so still.

The water leaves through a channel cut in the west wall at the waterline. It is a squared trough the width of two hands, sloping away into darkness through the rock toward some field or village downslope. Everything the spring pushes in must go out that way, so the channel is always running full, and at its mouth the water drops over a lip carved into a deliberate step. That step makes the noise. The drop pours into a lower basin inside the wall that you cannot see, and the vault takes the sound and multiplies it. It is a deep, continuous roar with a rattling edge, like heavy rain on a roof heard from inside a drum.

Underwater the sound changes but does not diminish. It becomes a pressure more than a noise, a low throb felt in the chest and the jaw. The spring adds its own voice down there, a gritty hiss where water forces its way through the rough opening, and now and then a knocking from somewhere deeper in the rock, a pulse of pressure moving through fissures no one has seen.

Above the waterline, in the thin band of air, there is also dripping. Condensation beads on the vault, runs along the tool grooves, and falls. Each drop strikes the surface with a small bright note that is somehow audible even over the roar, like someone tapping a pot in a crowded room.

## The Sparse Green

Life here is limited to the light.

Directly beneath the shaft, on the floor where the sun lands hardest at midday, there is a patch of green film about the size of a sleeping mat. It is a soft algal fuzz that sways in the current, the only thing in the chamber that moves slowly. It is thickest at the center and thins to nothing at the edges, making a map of where the light falls over the course of the year.

Higher up, where the shaft passes through the soil layer above the rock, a few plants have found footing. A small fern grows out of a mortar joint, its fronds uncurling downward toward the warmth and damp. There are one or two tufts of moss, and a hanging root from the thornbush above that has pushed through the shaft wall and dangles in the air, its tip just short of the water, reaching and not quite reaching.

That is all. There are no fish, no snails, no insects skating the surface, no frogs in the channel. The water has been moving too fast and for too short a time, and it comes from too deep. Nothing living has found its way in yet. The chamber is new enough that it has not yet been discovered.

## The Day

Everything here follows the sun.

At dawn the chamber is dim and gray-blue. The shaft is a pale square overhead, and the water is only a slightly lighter darkness. The roar seems louder in the dimness because there is nothing to look at.

Then the sun climbs and a column of light comes down the shaft, first catching the western lip, then sliding down the wall, then reaching the water and plunging to the floor. For an hour or two around noon the room is incandescent. The floor lights up, the walls light up, the moving reflections cover the vault, and every tool mark casts a tiny crisp shadow that shivers as the surface ripples. The algae patch glows green-gold. Heat haze from the spring becomes visible as a trembling column of distortion. Even the rising bubbles flash.

The light moves across the floor during the day, and the algae have followed it: the patch is slightly elongated east to west, stretched along the path of the sun.

In the afternoon the column leans and climbs the eastern wall, and the chamber cools in color though not in temperature. By dusk it is a dim warm box again, loud in the dark, steaming a little where the air meets the cooler evening coming down the shaft. Mist gathers in the shaft at night and drifts upward in slow curls.

## The Rules

Several things are true about this place.

The water level never changes. The overflow channel was cut precisely, and whatever the spring does, the surface stays pinned to the same line. The builders set it there so the chamber would always be full and always be draining. It is a container that is also a passage.

The spring is not constant. Some days it pulses harder, and on those days the roar rises, the heat haze thickens, and fine grit is carried in from the rough opening and settles as a pale dust on the floor that the current then slowly sweeps west. After heavy rain on the hill, a milkiness comes into the water for a day. The light turns soft and diffuse, the tool marks disappear, and the chamber looks for a while like a room made of cloud.

The stone is changing. It is changing slowly, but it has only just begun, so the changes are the most visible thing about it. The mineral crust is building up and the algae are spreading. Mortar joints are darkening from their first bright white. The fern will grow larger, the root will lengthen until it touches the water, and something will eventually come down the shaft or up the channel and live here.

For now it is a single made room, sharp-edged and brilliant, warm and full and roaring. It is hidden under a hillside with one square eye open to the sky, and the only thing that moves in it is water.
