# The Laying

## I. First Impression

It begins as a floor.

You stand on brick. The bricks are hand-sized, slightly irregular, laid in a herringbone pattern that runs in every direction to the edge of sight. They are warm through the soles of the feet, warm the way a hearthstone is warm an hour after the fire has died. There is a thin skin of water over everything, a millimetre or two, and it does not run anywhere. It stands in the joints between bricks, pooled in the shallow thumb-dips pressed into each one, trembling slightly as if something far off were walking.

The light is the colour of a kiln seen through its spyhole: a low, sullen orange that doesn't come from any direction in particular. There is no sun. The sky, if it is a sky, is a close, heavy ceiling of steam, and the light seems to seep up out of the ground and reflect back down off the underside of the vapour, so that everything is lit from beneath and above at once and nothing casts a clean shadow.

It is very hot. Not a dry heat. The air is thick enough to lean against. Breathing is like drinking.

It is quiet. The quiet is the first thing that seems wrong, because the place looks like it should be loud. It looks like a worksite.

## II. The Floor Is Not Finished

Walk a while and the herringbone changes. First the bricks lose their glaze and become matte, then soft-edged, then soft. A few hundred paces further and they are not fired at all but raw, grey-brown, still slick, still bearing the flat marks of the board they were struck on. Then there are gaps where no brick has been laid, and in the gaps is slurry. It is warm mud with a skin on it, and the skin wrinkles and smooths as you watch.

Beyond that, the floor is only marked out. Taut lines of something like cord, pale and fibrous, run across the slurry at knee height and are pinned at intervals by stakes of fired clay. They describe a grid, and the grid keeps going. Here and there a single brick sits alone in the mud at an intersection of the cords, as if someone had set it down to check the spacing and then walked away.

Nobody has walked away. There are no footprints anywhere. Not yours either: the thin water fills your prints and the clay slumps back, and within a minute there is no record you passed.

This is the first rule you learn. **The ground remembers the hand but not the foot.** Every brick carries a thumbprint in its upper face, pressed deep, the whorls crisp. Every one you check has the same thumbprint. The ridges match exactly. But the size is wrong from brick to brick. On some the print is the size of a child's thumb. On others it spans the whole face of the brick. On a few, far out on the unfired margins, the print is so large that the brick is only a small part of it. You can stand in the loop of a whorl and see the ridge curve away across forty or fifty bricks, a shallow furrow running over the joints as though the whole floor had been pressed at once, and the bricks laid afterward, into the mark.

## III. Weather

The place is large enough to have its own weather, and the weather is steam.

The heat lives in the ground. Everything fired is still cooling, and everything unfired is baking slowly from below, so the whole plain breathes water upward. On the near scale it is a constant faint mist that beads on the skin and runs. On the far scale it becomes fronts.

You see one coming as a wall of pale vapour, lit orange from beneath, sliding across the herringbone at the speed of a walking person. Where it passes, the air goes white and blind and furnace-hot, and the brick underfoot hisses. When it clears, the ground behind it has changed. Raw bricks have gone hard and red. Slurry has set into a crazed, cracked crust. Once you watch a front pass over a stretch of marked-out grid, and when it lifts, the grid has been filled: a thousand bricks laid, jointed, and fired in the time it took the fog to move by, each with its thumbprint, each still smoking.

Other fronts undo things. They come in lower, darker, heavier with water, and they rain. The rain is hot and falls in fat, slow drops, and where it lands fired brick softens back to leather-hardness, then to clay, then to mud. Whole courses of wall slump like wet bread. The water carries the clay away in slow brown sheets, not downhill, because there is no downhill, but toward the nearest unmarked space, where it pools and settles and waits for the cords.

So the plain is always being made and unmade, in patches the size of provinces, under weather that is less like climate than like a pair of hands working a surface: firing here, wetting there, smoothing, pressing. Nothing up close ever seems to move fast. But climb anything tall and look out, and the plain boils. Red spreading into grey, grey dissolving to brown, fronts crossing each other, patches of finished floor the size of countries sinking back into slurry while elsewhere new ground hardens out of the mud.

## IV. Things That Stand Up

The land is low. There are no hills. But the plain is not flat everywhere, because things have been built on it.

**Walls** come first, and most often. They are single-brick thick, rarely more than head height, and they run straight for enormous distances before turning at exact right angles. They enclose nothing in particular. You can follow one for a day and find it forms one side of a room so large that its opposite wall is below the horizon. Other walls are short, a few metres long, standing alone in the open with finished ends. They have mortar on top as if the next course were about to go on.

**Scaffolds** stand against some of the walls. Nothing grows here, so there is no wood, and the scaffolds are made of fired clay: long hollow tubes like drainpipes, lashed together with the same pale cord that marks out the grid. The lashings are careful, tight, and knotted in ways that look practiced. On the platforms lie tools. There are hand-axes of some dark knapped stone, still sharp. There are mallets with clay heads and clay handles. There are flat wooden-looking brick moulds that turn out, when you lift them, to be fired clay pressed with a wood-grain pattern, as if made from memory of wood by someone who had never seen a tree. There are small copper chisels, green at the edges, blunted with use.

Every tool is warm. Not sun-warm, since there is no sun. Hand-warm. The mallet handles are worn smooth in two places where fingers would wrap. If you pick one up, the grip fits you. It fits anyone. The wear rearranges itself, slowly, to match whatever hand holds it.

**Kilns** are the tallest things. They are beehive domes of brick, some the size of a hut, some the size of a cathedral, a few so vast they are visible from a day's walk away as dull, glowing humps under the steam-ceiling. Their fire-mouths face all directions. Inside is red light and a heat that stops you at the threshold. No fuel is visible. Nothing burns. The heat simply is, as if the kilns were not burning something but remembering having burned.

Stacked inside the smaller kilns, you can sometimes glimpse through the heat-shimmer what is being fired. Usually it is bricks. Sometimes it is other things: stakes, cord-spools, tools. Once, in a kiln the size of a barn, you see a stack of doorways, fired frames, each carrying a single enormous thumbprint on its lintel, waiting to be set into walls that don't exist yet.

## V. Sound

It is mostly quiet, and the quiet is textured.

There is the hiss. It is everywhere at a low level: water meeting hot clay, a sound like distant surf that you stop noticing after an hour and then hear again whenever you stand still.

There is the tick of cooling. Fired brick contracts as it loses heat, and the plain is full of tiny cracks and pings, irregular as rain on a roof, always nearby.

And then there are the work-sounds, which are the reason the quiet feels so strange. Occasionally, and never near you, you hear a mallet: three flat knocks, the rhythm of someone tapping a brick level in its mortar. You hear the scrape of a trowel. You hear, once, a rope creak under load. You turn and there is nothing. The scaffold nearest the sound is empty, its tools lying where they were. But the brick on the top course is new. The mortar under it is still wet and squeezed out at the joint in a neat bead, and a trowel-stroke has cut it flush.

You never hear voices. You never hear breathing, or footsteps, or anything alive. Only the sounds of tools, in the right order, from just out of sight.

## VI. Where It Stops Holding Together

The further you go, the less the place obeys itself.

**The order of work slips.** In the middle regions you start to find walls built top-down: upper courses hanging finished in the air, mortared to nothing, with the lower courses laid in behind them afterward. In some places the bricks are fired before they are shaped. You find hard red blocks with the rough cloud-shape of undivided clay, and next to them the same clay, raw, cut neatly into bricks. It is as if the cutting happened after and the firing before, and the clay couldn't agree.

**Scale stops being fixed.** The thumbprints are only the first sign. Further out the bricks themselves vary. There is a course of ordinary bricks. Then a single brick you could lie down on. Then a course of bricks too small to pick up, like grains, laid with perfect joints in a wall no taller than your ankle, stretching off for kilometres. There are kilns you could hold in your hand, glowing like coals, fired full of bricks smaller than seeds. There are scaffolds so large their lashings are cords as thick as a body, tied with the same knots.

**Distance breathes.** Walk toward a far kiln and sometimes it arrives in an hour, sometimes it never gets closer, and sometimes you realise you have been walking along the base of its wall for most of a day. You didn't notice the scale change, because everything around you changed with it. The mist, the water in the joints, and your own thumbprint pressed in the soft clay all keep proportion.

**The grid overwrites things.** Out on the raw margins, the cords keep going in straight lines across whatever is in their way. A cord runs through a wall. It doesn't pass through a gap; it goes straight through solid brick, and the brick along its line is subtly different, freshly laid, as if the wall had been taken apart and rebuilt around the cord. A cord runs through a kiln. Occasionally a cord runs through you. You feel no touch, only a faint pressure, a sense of being measured. When you step off the line, the patch of mud where you stood is marked out with stakes at each corner, ready.

## VII. The Horizon

In every direction the horizon is the same thing, and you only understand it after a long time looking.

It is a wall.

It is not near. It is at the very limit of sight, where the orange light and the steam-ceiling close together. It is so tall that it runs up into the vapour and out of view. It is so long that it curves, very gently, all the way around. Its surface is bricks the size of fields, each one pressed with the same thumbprint. Scaffolds cling to its face, climbing up into the fog. Fronts of steam roll along its base, firing and unfiring the lowest courses.

The wall is unfinished everywhere. There are gaps in it, sections still marked out in cord across empty air, courses that begin and stop. Through the gaps there is nothing to see. There is only more dim orange, more steam, and in one place, very far, what might be the back of another herringbone floor, laid face-down, upside-down, the same pattern. From it the water in its joints hangs upward in slow drops and falls toward the sky.

## VIII. What the Place Is

There are no builders. There is no life at all. Nothing grows in the cracks, nothing crawls on the walls, and no algae greens the standing water; it is too hot, too new, or simply not included. The only organic-seeming thing is the pale cord, and when you pick apart a strand it turns out to be clay as well, drawn fine and fired soft.

But the plain is being made by hand, with primitive tools, according to a plan. That is the one thing that never breaks down. However scale slides and order inverts, the grid is square, the herringbone is true, the walls are plumb, and the joints are even. Someone with a mallet, a stone axe, a mould, and a length of cord is building a world on a floor of warm mud. They are building it brick by brick, the way you would build a house, only they are building it everywhere at once, and faster than weather, and they are never quite there when you look.

The water keeps welling up through the joints. The heat keeps rising from below. The fronts keep crossing, firing and dissolving. The thumbprint, the same one, on every brick at every size, keeps being pressed into the new clay a moment before you arrive.

If you stand very still in one of the unlaid places, in the slurry between the cords, you will feel it eventually. There is a gentle, enormous pressure from above, warm, patient, and even. The mud around your feet rises in whorls.
