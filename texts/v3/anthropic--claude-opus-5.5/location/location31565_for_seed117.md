# The Ram House

It is a hollow and not a valley. You come down to it the way water does, through a cleft in grey limestone no wider than a doorway, over steps worn into shallow bowls. Each bowl holds a finger's depth of water. The air cools with every step and grows heavier, until at the bottom it feels less like air than like the breath of something that has been underground a long time.

The hollow is perhaps forty paces across and roughly round. The walls lean inward as they rise, so the sky shows only as a pale, ragged slot far overhead, a strip of white that never quite becomes daylight down here. What reaches the floor is a grey, sourceless light, like the inside of a shell. After a while your eyes open to it. Then you notice the other light: a single electric bulb in a cage of rusted wire, bolted to a post in the middle of the pool. It glows the colour of weak tea. Every eleven seconds it brightens a little and fades again.

## The Beat

The hollow keeps time.

At its centre, half-drowned, stands a hydraulic ram, an old self-driving pump of the kind once used to lift water uphill with no fuel but the fall of water itself. Its cast-iron body is the size of a crouching animal. Its waste valve lifts, the water rushes, and the valve slams shut with a sound like a door closing in a distant house, felt more in the sternum than heard. *Thunk.* Then the long exhale of the pressure chamber, a gurgle in the delivery pipe, and silence. Eleven seconds. *Thunk.*

No one knows how long it has been running. Nothing here needs to know. The delivery pipe climbs the north wall in brackets rusted to lace and ends in open air ten metres up, sheared off wherever it once led. Every beat, a small cough of water spills from its broken mouth and falls back down the wall into the pool it came from. The pump lifts the water, the water falls, and the circuit has never broken.

Beside the ram, fed by the same channel, turns a small undershot wheel no wider than a cartwheel. Its paddles are furred with mineral. It drives a dynamo in a squat brick housing that has lost its roof, and the dynamo feeds the one bulb. The current wavers with the water, and the water pulses with the ram, so the light breathes on the beat. The filament must have fused or thickened at some point into something tougher than it was made to be. It has outlived every other artifact of the people who built this place.

## Stone That Grows

The limestone weeps. Every surface is wet, though not with running water. Water beads out of the rock itself, as if the walls were sweating slowly, and gathers into drops that hang, thicken, and fall. Each drop leaves behind a trace of calcite. Given long enough, that trace has overgrown almost everything.

The hollow is established in a way few places are. It does not look ruined so much as absorbed. The brick housing of the dynamo is sheathed in creamy travertine, its corners rounded like soap. The wheel's spokes are fattened with it. Chains that once hung from the walls have become stone ropes, each link welded to the next by mineral, some fused at their lower ends into the rimstone below, so they are no longer hanging but standing. There are lumps along the pool's edge that were probably tools, a bucket, perhaps a boot. Now they are only suggestive shapes under a glaze the colour of old ivory.

The pool floor is terraced with gours, the small scalloped dams that dripping, carbonate-heavy water builds for itself, shelf below shelf. They step down toward the ram like a flight of shallow basins. Each one holds water so still and clear it is invisible except where the bulb-light catches its rim.

If you break a piece of the travertine off the pipe brackets (you shouldn't), you'll find it banded. Each band is hair-thin, and in some places the rhythm is plain enough to count: thicker, thinner, thicker, thinner. Some say the stone records the beat. Where the pump has stuttered, in a hard frost or a flood, the banding falters too, like a skipped line in a ledger.

## No Green

Nothing here photosynthesises. There is too little light and too much lime. The slot of sky is too narrow and too high for any seed that falls through it to find a purchase worth taking. Moss would grow on the steps above if it could, and a little does, right at the lip of the cleft. Below the third turning of the stair the green gives out completely.

In its place there are films. Every wet surface carries a thin, living skin: bacterial mats in rust and cream and a bruised slate-violet, feeding on iron leached from the old machinery and on sulphur seeping from the rock. Around the base of the ram, where iron meets water most intimately, the mats hang in soft orange curtains that sway with every pulse. Where the dynamo housing drips into the pool, there are white filaments, fine as combed hair, streaming out in the faint current. They glisten. Touch them and they come apart like wet smoke.

That is the whole of the flora, if it can be called that. It is enough, because everything else eats it.

## The Crowd

For all its stillness, the hollow is crowded. It takes a minute to see this, the way it takes a minute to see stars.

**Snails** come first, because there are so many. They are tiny, rice-sized, with translucent shells gone chalky at the whorl, and they cover the gour rims, the ram's flanks, and the lower walls in a grainy crust that turns out, on closer inspection, to be moving. They graze the mats. They move in pulses. Each time the ram closes and the pressure wave shivers through the stone, every snail on the north wall inches forward a fraction and stops, as though the beat were a signal to step. From across the pool the effect is of the whole wall very slowly breathing.

**Amphipods** swarm the gours: blind, colourless shrimp no longer than a fingernail, so densely packed in the still basins that the water seems to shimmer with them. When the bulb brightens they scatter. When it dims they drift back.

**Eels** live in the deep channel where the ram draws its water. They are long, dark, and very old, some as thick as a wrist. They lie in the intake current facing upstream, nosing among the loosened mats and the shrimp that tumble in with each surge. They have learned the machinery. Watch long enough and you'll see one rise to the lip of the waste valve and wait, mouth open, for the flush. The strangest thing about them is their stillness in the presence of the dynamo's leak. The pool around the brick housing carries a faint current. You would feel it only as a vague tingle if you put your hand in, but the eels orient to it and hang in precise arcs around the housing like iron filings around a magnet. When the current fades, in a dry spell when the wheel slows, they become restless and unmoored, and they coil through the channels as if looking for something they've lost.

**Cave crickets** cling to the ceiling in the dry-ish overhangs, pale and long-legged, their antennae twice their body length, trembling. There are hundreds. They drop to scavenge whatever falls through the slot above: beetles, leaves, a dead bird on rare lucky days.

**Bats** roost higher still, in the cracks near the sky. At dusk the slot fills with their leaving, a dark trickle up and out. At dawn they return in reverse, pouring down into the hollow. Their droppings, over centuries, have built a soft brown cone on the east floor, where a second, fiercer ecosystem of mites, springtails, and pale fungus gnats teems in the warmth of decomposition. It is the only warm place in the hollow. On cold nights the crickets gather at its edges.

**Dippers** are the only creatures that come and go by daylight: small, round, sooty-brown birds. They follow the spill from the broken pipe down the wall, bobbing on the wet ledges, and walk straight into the pool and under it, wings half-open, to hunt shrimp along the gour floors. They are the loudest thing in the hollow, and even they are quiet: a thin, metallic zit, and the click of their feet on stone.

Everything touches everything. Snail on snail, cricket beside cricket, shrimp in water so full of shrimp there is barely room to swim. Nowhere is empty. The dense life is as much a part of the hollow as the stone, a living crust layered over the mineral one.

## Weather Inside a Room

The hollow makes its own weather, on a small scale.

Cold air pools here, heavier than the air above. When the day warms the rock at the lip of the cleft, moisture condenses as it drifts down, and a mist forms and settles in the hollow like milk poured into a bowl. On most mornings it lies knee-deep over the pool. The bulb makes a soft glowing coin within it, pulsing. The pipe's spill falls through the mist and vanishes before it lands.

When it rains above, the hollow floods in minutes. The weeping of the walls turns to streaming, the gours overtop, and the ram's rhythm quickens under the pressure. *Thunk-thunk-thunk*, faster, the bulb flickering nearly continuous. The whole place goes briefly frantic. Snails surge up the walls, eels rise into the flooded upper basins, and the dippers stay away. Then the water drains back through fissures in the floor, the beat slows, and within an hour the hollow has returned to its eleven-second patience.

In deep winter the walls grow ice beards. The spill from the pipe freezes as it falls and builds an organ of icicles down the north face, which the next beat's water cracks and rebuilds. The sound of that cracking, a small bright tinkling every eleven seconds, is the closest the hollow comes to music. The ram has never frozen. The water rising from underground is always a few degrees above the air, and a thread of vapour lifts from the waste valve all winter like breath from a sleeping animal.

## Quiet

It is not silent here. There is the ram's closing, low and felt. There is the drip, everywhere, from hundreds of points at once, each drop a separate tiny note, never in unison and never quite random. There is the dynamo's hum, just at the edge of hearing, which you only notice when it wavers. There is the shush of the pipe's spill against the wall.

But beneath all of it lies a deep stillness, the quiet of a place walled off from wind. You can hear your own heartbeat, and after a while you'll catch yourself breathing on the ram's rhythm. Most visitors do. There is a sense, never quite proven, that the living things of the hollow have all done the same. Put your ear to the wet rock and you can hear the snails' shells ticking faintly against the stone in unison, eleven seconds apart.

## The Rule

If the hollow has a law, it is this: everything here depends on the beat, and the beat depends on nothing but falling water.

The ram needs no fuel and no tending. The wheel needs only the channel. The bulb needs only the wheel. The mats need the iron that the old machinery gives up, a molecule at a time, as it slowly dissolves into the pool. It is a gift that will eventually run out, though not for centuries. The snails need the mats, the shrimp need the snails' leavings, the eels need the shrimp, and the bats' long dark river of droppings feeds the warm cone that the crickets huddle against. The stone grows over all of it and records the time.

The machinery is slowly being eaten, and the eating keeps the place alive. The mineral skin that keeps sealing the iron away from the water also slows that feeding, and so the hollow holds itself in a kind of balance, consuming its inheritance just fast enough to live on it and slowly enough to last.

Some day the ram will seize. Calcite will finally jam the waste valve, or the iron body will thin through, and the beat will stop. It's hard not to wonder what happens then. Would the bulb go out, the eels lose their bearings, the snails stop mid-step on the wall? Or would they, after a long pause, begin moving to some other rhythm the hollow still holds: the drips, the mist, the daily river of bats?

For now there is only the grey light, the breathing bulb, the living walls, and the sound of a door closing somewhere far away, every eleven seconds, as it has for longer than anyone remembers.

*Thunk.*
