# The Exhale

## The Plain

You would not find it by looking. The land for fifty miles in every direction is gneiss worn down to its roots: a shield so old that its mountains are gone and its valleys filled and emptied and filled again. What is left is a plain of low grey whalebacks, knee-high and smooth as river stones though no river has touched them in an age. The rock is banded pink and charcoal, folded into swirls that were once the insides of mountains. Wind has polished the bands. Grit has rounded every edge. Lichen tried once and failed; its ghosts show as pale rosettes on the north faces, dead so long they have become part of the stone's colour.

It is cool here even at midday. The air is thin and dry and tastes of chalk. There is no standing water, only a skin of frost in the lowest seams at dawn, which is gone within the hour. Nothing grows. The ground is a mosaic of gravel lag, pebbles packed so tight by wind that they form a pavement, varnished dark on top and pale beneath.

## The Hollow

Then, in one fold between two whalebacks, the land dips. It is not much: a bowl perhaps forty paces across and the height of two people deep. At its bottom, half-buried in its own rubble, sits the fan house.

It was built of yellow firebrick when there was a mine here. The mine is under your feet, a mile of drifts and stopes in a seam of dry evaporite far below the gneiss. Nothing remains of it on the surface except this: the upcast housing, where a great fan once pulled foul air out of the workings. The headframe is gone, and so are the rails and the town. The brick has weathered to the texture of stale bread. Iron straps have rusted into brown ribbons that hold nothing, and the roof has half fallen in, so a ragged square of sky hangs over the interior.

The mine still breathes. Deep ground holds a steady temperature, and the long shafts connect to other shafts miles away, collapsed and choked but not sealed. When the surface air cools at night the mine exhales. When it warms by day the mine inhales. The breath is constant and faint, dry as paper, smelling of salt and old iron.

Standing at the lip of the hollow, you feel it first on your face, a cool pressure rising. Then you hear it.

## The Noise

The fan house is full.

Swifts nest in every gap in the brickwork, thousands of them. The mortar has eroded out of the joints, leaving slots a finger deep, and every slot holds a cup of feather and spit glued to the brick. The nests run in rows, courses on courses, following the bonds of the masonry, so the walls look as though they were laid in birds. Where the joints are too shallow, the swifts have built on each other's nests, so clusters bulge out like dark fruit.

They scream. That is the only word for it. It is a thin, high, relentless shrieking, dozens of voices at once and hundreds a moment later as a flock pours in through the broken roof. Inside the brick shell the sound doubles and redoubles. It is loud enough to feel in the teeth.

Under the screaming there is a clatter. The fan is still there: a steel wheel twice the height of a man, bolted into its scroll casing over the shaft mouth. Its blades are warped and rusted but not seized. The mine's exhale turns it, slowly, backward. With every revolution one bent blade strikes the casing, clack, and a loose plate somewhere hums in sympathy. The rhythm drifts with the breath of the mine: fast in the cold hours before dawn, slow and stuttering at noon, stopping entirely for a few uncanny minutes when the pressure inside and out comes into balance. In those minutes you can hear the individual wings.

## The Shaft

Look down past the fan, through the grating. The upcast shaft drops into darkness, lined with the same brick for the first hundred feet and then raw rock. It is the other half of the colony.

Bats hang in the shaft by day. They pack themselves so densely on the upper walls that the brick is invisible, a brown pelt that shifts and ripples. Their chittering rises with the mine air, a dry static beneath the swifts. Every surface below them is coated in guano, and because the air is so dry the guano does not rot. It accumulates. Over the decades it has built into grey drifts and terraces on every ledge, piled against the grating in dunes, dusted over the fan's hub.

The guano is the ground of everything else. It is the soil of a place without soil.

## The Small Ones

Crouch at the edge of a guano drift and it moves. Dermestid beetles work the surface in their thousands, small and oval and brown, each patterned with a faint pale band. They eat the dead: fallen chicks, bats that lose their grip in the winter torpor, the husks of insects dropped from full crops. They strip a carcass in a day. What they leave is a fine white lace of bones that the draught sorts into windrows along the floor, delicate as frost.

Cave crickets graze the guano itself, pale and long-legged, springing away from any shadow. Mites swarm the nests in clouds you see only when light slants through them. Pseudoscorpions no bigger than a grain of rice hunt the mites. Spiders have strung the corners of the housing with sheet webs so heavy with dust that they sag like grey hammocks, and every web holds a spider waiting for the crickets.

Rats live in the rubble at the base of the walls, fat on fallen eggs, and wholly unafraid. A pair of kestrels nest in the chimney stub that still stands at the north corner. At dusk an owl comes.

Nothing here eats a plant, because there are none. The whole economy is imported. The swifts feed miles away, high over the plain, on the aerial plankton that drifts in on warm fronts: gnats, aphids, ballooning spiderlings, winged ants carried from somewhere green, far beyond the horizon. The bats hunt the same thin harvest by night. They carry it home in their bodies and drop it here as guano, feathers, eggs and corpses, and the hollow lives on the leavings. It is an oasis made entirely of animals.

## The Light

The light is ordinary daylight, cool and clear, falling through the broken roof in a hard-edged square that crawls across the far wall through the day. But there is one other light.

In the fan's casing is a bulb in a wire cage, a service lamp from when the fan was driven by a motor. The motor is still coupled to the shaft. When the mine exhales hard and the fan spins backward fast enough, the motor becomes a dynamo. The windings are corroded and the brushes nearly gone, but on cold nights, in the deep hours, the filament warms to a dull orange ember.

It is not bright. But on a plain with no other light for fifty miles, it is enough. The moths drawn across the plain are few but steady. They circle the cage. The bats have learned it. On the coldest nights a small crowd of them wheels around the lamp, snatching moths out of the orange glow, and the clack of the fan and the hum of the plate and the click of bat calls blend into a sound like a machine working.

The bulb should have burned out a long time ago. It hasn't. Nobody knows why. It is probably because it is almost never fully lit, a filament kept forever at the edge of burning. But standing in the fan house at three in the morning, watching the ember pulse faster as the mine breathes out, it is hard not to feel that the place is keeping it alive.

## The Day

At dawn the exhale is strongest and the fan clatters hard. The bats come home in a funnel, pouring down through the roof and into the shaft with a sound like tearing cloth. For a quarter of an hour the air is full of both kinds of wings, the swifts going out and the bats coming in, and the screaming and the chittering cross. Then the bats settle and the swifts are gone into the sky, and the hollow is briefly almost quiet: the fan slowing, beetles ticking, a rat scratching.

At midday the mine inhales. The draught reverses and flows down the shaft, pulling dust and feathers with it. The fan stalls, twitches, turns a quarter-turn forward and stops. Warmth, as much as there ever is, collects in the bowl. The rock of the whalebacks ticks as it expands.

In the late afternoon the swifts return in waves. They fly low over the plain in screaming parties, stream around the fan house three times, five times, twenty, and then crash into the walls, each bird hitting its own slot at full speed and vanishing. The noise peaks. The kestrels take a straggler. The square of sunlight climbs the wall and goes gold, then grey.

At dusk the bats rise. The mine begins to breathe out again. The fan begins to turn.

## What It Is

It is a small place. You can walk across it in a minute. Beyond its lip there is only a plain worn so flat by time that it has forgotten it was ever anything else, cold and dry and silent. Inside, there are more living things per breath of air than almost anywhere, layered on every surface, packed into every crack, screaming, clicking, crawling, all fed by a sky they bring home in their bodies.

The oldest part is the rock. The youngest part is the swift that hatched this morning. Between them is a ruin that was built to pull poison out of the ground and now, turned backward by the earth's own breathing, keeps a small light burning for the things that took it over.
