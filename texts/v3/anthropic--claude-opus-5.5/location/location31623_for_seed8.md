# The Pans

The land has run out of ways to fall. Whatever mountains once stood here were worn to their roots so long ago that the roots have been worn too, and what remains is a plain of rock laid bare to the sky. It is not quite flat. It swells and settles in rises no taller than a person, spread across kilometers, so that a traveler crossing it feels the ground tilt without being able to say which way. Water feels it too, and is just as unsure.

It rained three days ago. It will be weeks before the land forgets.

---

## The Floor

The ground is a pavement: old granite and ironstone, weathered past cracking into something closer to skin. Most of it is a crust of laterite, rust-red and plum-dark, pitted like the rind of a fruit and hard enough to ring faintly under a heel. Where the crust has worn through, pale granite shows, rounded and grainy, slick where water runs across it. In places the two have fused into a mottled stone, brick-colored and bone-colored, that looks cooked.

Nothing here is sharp. Every edge was taken long ago. Boulders that were once tors have slumped into low whalebacks, their tops polished by rain, their flanks peeling in thin curved sheets that lie around them like shed bark. Some sheets have rotted to grit. Some are still whole, and if you step on one it rocks and gives a hollow clack that carries.

Between the rock domes lie the pans: broad, shallow basins where fine sediment has gathered over an unknowable span of time. The sediment is so fine it is nearly clay, pale gray to cream, and after rain it becomes a sheet of standing water a hand's depth deep and sometimes half a kilometer across. Under the water the clay is soft as custard and warm as blood.

## The Water

Water here does not flow so much as spread. With no real slope to follow, rain moves across the pavement in thin sheets, a glaze barely deep enough to wet the ankle, sliding from pan to pan through channels worn only a finger deep. You can watch a sheet of water edge across the rock at walking pace and then stall, undecided, filling some small hollow before it continues.

The rock holds water in a hundred forms:

- **Gnammas.** Rock holes, round and smooth-walled, dissolved into the granite over ages. Some are cups, some are basins wide enough to lie down in. Their water is clear and faintly tea-colored, warm at the top and cool at the bottom, with a fine silt of red grit and dead insects in the base.
- **Seep lines.** Along the flanks of the whalebacks, water weeps out of the exfoliation joints for days after the rain, so the rock shines in dark stripes. The seeps feed mats of blue-black algae that dry to crackling paper at the edges and stay slick and living in the wet center.
- **The pans.** They shrink by evaporation, never by draining. Each day the shoreline retreats a few meters and leaves a crust of salt and a scalloped tidemark. By the end of the dry-down each pan is a bright pale floor with a few last pools in its lowest point, crowded with every living thing that needs them.

The air is thick. In the mornings, water rises off the warm pans as a low mist that hangs knee-high and burns off before the sun is two hands above the horizon. By midday the humidity makes distances waver: far rock domes float above bright bands of false water, and the real pans and the mirages become hard to tell apart.

## The Light

The light is strong, direct, and clean after the rain has washed the dust from the air. It reflects off everything. Each pan is a mirror, each wet rock face a flare. Around noon the whole plain turns silver and red, sky above and sky below, with the dark rock between them like a seam.

Shadows are few and short. The only real shade lies under the lee of the larger boulders, in the rare tree crowns, and under the overhangs where exfoliation sheets have lifted from the rock but not fallen. Those overhangs are cool, damp, and occupied.

Evening is the land's best hour. Low light pulls every small ripple of the ground into relief, and the plain that looked featureless at noon shows itself as a vast washboard of swells and hollows, each hollow holding a pool of copper sky.

## What Grows

Little. The soil is thin where it exists at all, and what exists has been leached for so long that it holds almost nothing a plant can use. Vegetation lives where water and grit happen to meet:

- Around the pan margins, rings of **sedge and rush**, bright green now and fattening on the rain, fading to straw as the water retreats. The rings mark old shorelines, so each pan wears concentric bands of green, yellow-green, and dead gold.
- In the deeper gnammas, **rosette plants** grow submerged, small and pale, with leaves like stars. They will flower briefly above the surface just before the pool dries, then die back to a seed that can wait for years.
- On bare rock, **resurrection mosses and ferns** that look like scraps of burnt paper through the dry months. Three days after rain they are a vivid velvet green, crowded into every crack.
- Scattered, never clustered, are **trees**: low, crooked, broad-crowned, with smooth pale bark that peels in red strips. Each one stands in a crack where the rock has split deeply enough to reach water held far below. They are very old. They may be the only things on the plain that grow taller than a person, and the distance between them is often longer than the eye can comfortably judge.
- **Carnivorous plants** live along the seeps: sundews studded with glistening droplets, and bladderworts trailing in the shallow sheet-water. Where the rock gives nothing, they take nitrogen from the insects.

## The Makers

Nothing here was built by hands, but some things were built.

At intervals across the higher, drier rises stand **termite mounds**, rust-colored spires and fins made from the laterite itself, chewed and cemented and raised again. From a distance they read as the ruins of some vanished architecture, since they are the only vertical forms on the land besides the trees. They are not ruins. Many are centuries old and still occupied. They are aligned north to south, a dry wall of them facing the sun edge-on to keep cool, and they outlast almost everything else.

The birds build too: mud nests plastered beneath the rock overhangs, cup against cup, and on the pans at dry-down, shallow scrapes lined with shell and grit.

## The Noise

It is loud here, and the loudness comes almost entirely from animals.

**The frogs** are first and largest. For the few weeks after rain the pans and gnammas become breeding pools, and frogs that spent the dry months sealed in cocoons of shed skin under the clay come up together. At dusk the chorus begins. It is not one sound. It is a dozen species layered: a deep repeated *bonk* like a stick on a hollow log; a fast metallic ticking; a long rising trill that goes on until it seems it must be a machine; a bleating that sounds almost like sheep. The combined sound is a physical pressure. At its peak it drowns out your own footsteps and your own voice, and it goes on until nearly dawn. It carries for kilometers across the flat water, and you can hear the separate choirs of distant pans rising and falling out of phase.

**The birds** own the day. Waders stalk the pan shallows — stilts, avocets, sandpipers, a long-legged heron — and flocks of small finches move between the seeps and the sedge in pulses of chatter. Around each large gnamma, parrots gather in a shrieking crowd to drink, then leave all at once with a roar of wings. Overhead, swallows and swifts hunt the insect swarms in fast passes, crying thinly.

**The insects** fill whatever gaps remain. Cicadas scream from the scattered trees, which in this landscape means the sound comes from isolated points, as if the trees themselves were ringing. Midges rise from the pans in columns that hum audibly. Dragonflies clatter.

Under it all are the land's own sounds: the drip and trickle of the seeps, the hollow knock of a loose rock sheet, wind hissing through the sedge rings, the soft *plip* of a surface broken by something small. On the rare still noons, when the birds have gone quiet in the heat and the frogs are buried, you can hear the pans themselves ticking as the clay begins to crack.

## What Lives Here

Fauna are abundant, but they are abundant in clusters. The plain between the pans is nearly empty, and life gathers at water with an intensity that surprises.

- **Shield shrimp and fairy shrimp** hatch from eggs that lay dormant in the clay, some for decades. Within days of the rain the pans are clouded with them: tadpole shrimp plowing the soft bottom with shields like tiny horseshoe crabs, fairy shrimp swimming upside down, translucent and pinkish. Their lineage is ancient, as old as anything here. They live a few weeks, lay eggs, and die as the water goes.
- **Tadpoles** in dense black schools, eating algae, shrimp, and each other.
- **Water beetles and backswimmers**, which fly in from somewhere unknown after rain.
- **Monitor lizards** patrol the pan margins, swinging their heads low, tasting the air. Smaller lizards bask on the warm rock, dark-backed and flattened, and flick into the crevices when a shadow passes.
- **Wallabies**, small and rock-colored, live in the shade of the boulder overhangs and come out in the evenings to drink. You hear their feet before you see them, a quick soft thump across the stone.
- **Bats** leave the deeper rock overhangs at dusk in streams that twist over the pans, feeding on the midge columns.
- **Snakes** follow the frogs.

The animals keep a rhythm. The early morning belongs to birds at the water. The heat of the day belongs to lizards and insects. Evening brings mammals and bats, and night belongs to frogs.

## How the Place Works

The rules here are patient.

**Nothing drains.** Water leaves only by rising: into the air, into the plants, into the animals that drink it. So the land breathes water rather than carrying it. Each rain comes in, spreads out, and is slowly exhaled, and the salt it carried stays behind. Pans that are oldest have the whitest floors.

**Everything waits.** Eggs in the clay, seeds in the grit, frogs in their cocoons, moss curled dry on the rock. The wet season is a brief, loud interruption in a much longer silence, and most life here is built to survive the silence and spend itself in the interruption. A rain may come in a year, or not for three. The clay remembers.

**Erosion is almost finished.** The rock loses a grain at a time. Each season a few more exfoliation sheets fall, a few gnammas dissolve a hair deeper, a termite mound slumps and another rises. Measured against a lifetime, the land does not change. Measured against its own age, it is nearly done, a surface close to the final level it can reach, where the forces that raise land and the forces that wear it have both gone quiet.

**The land sorts by millimeters.** A rise too low to see decides where water pools, where sedge rings grow, where frogs gather and birds follow. Standing on the plain, you feel it as flat and open; living on it, the animals know it as intricate, full of small edges.

## Across the Distance

From the top of one of the larger whalebacks you can see a long way: the plain running out in every direction in bands of red rock and silver water, with a lone tree here, a line of termite spires there, a pan flashing white where it has already dried. Far off, at the edge of the visible, another cluster of rock domes rises like a low herd of animals, bluish in the haze. Between here and there are perhaps a dozen pans, each with its own ring of green, its own birds, its own chorus waiting for night.

The sky is enormous. Clouds build in the afternoons, tall and white, and some days they bring a short hard storm that drums on the rock, fills the gnammas to overflowing, and starts every sheet of water moving again. Most days they don't. The pans shrink a little. The shoreline moves a few meters closer to the center. The frogs call a little more urgently.

It will dry. It always dries. Then it waits, sealed and silent under the sun, until the next rain wakes everything at once.
