# The Seam

## The crack

Near the top of a mountain whose summit is still arguing with the sky, there is a crack. From the valley you can't see it at all. From the air it looks like a pencil line on a gray page, a hairline fault running across a shoulder of rock at almost nine thousand meters, where nothing should live and nothing should be built.

The crack opened eleven years ago in a single night. The mountain shrugged. The people in the valley felt it as a long, low shiver and went outside to look at nothing. By morning the shoulder had split, and the two faces of the split were raw. Fresh stone that had never touched air lay exposed along three kilometers, bright as cut bread.

Inside that crack there is now a town.

## Walls

The Seam is narrow. At its lip it is perhaps forty meters across. It tapers as it goes down, until three hundred meters below the rim the walls come within an arm's span of each other and then close into a darkness no one has measured. Everything in the town lives between those two walls, and the walls are young.

You notice this first with your hands. Nothing has been softened. Every edge is a blade. The stone is a dense blue-black rock shot with pale feldspar. It breaks in conchoidal curves, like flint, and it holds its edges the way glass does. Gloves wear through in a week. The workers wrap their forearms in leather and tape. Children learn to touch the rock with the backs of their knuckles, never their palms.

There has been no time for weather to make talus or scree slopes, and no time for soil. Where rockfall has come down it lies in angular heaps at the bottoms of ledges, each piece keeping the sharp geometry of the fracture that made it. The smell is gunflint and hot matches, the smell of stone struck against stone. It never fades, because the stone never stops breaking.

## The walls move

The Seam is still opening. On an ordinary week the walls drift apart by two or three centimeters. On a bad night they can jump half a meter. Every so often the fault catches and releases, and a section of the town tears loose and falls into the dark below.

So nothing in the town is fixed. Every bridge between the walls sits on a telescoping joint: nested steel sleeves, greased with graphite because oil freezes, marked along their length with painted bands. Crews walk the bridges in the dim hours and read the bands the way sailors read a tide. Blue means fine. Yellow means reset the pins. Red means evacuate the span and cut it loose.

Houses are not built into the walls. They hang from them, bolted to one face only, so that when the gap widens they are not torn in half. Each dwelling is a steel box the size of a large wardrobe, insulated with felt and ore-sacking. They are stacked like swallows' nests in vertical columns, ladders running between them, gantries overhead. From the upper platforms you can look across the gap and see the opposite wall crusted with the same boxes, windows glowing sodium-orange, maybe four thousand people living in a slot you could throw a stone across.

It is crowded the way a hive is crowded. Every ledge wide enough for a boot has something on it: a tool rack, a cable anchor, a sleeping worker, a coil of hose, a bird.

## The thin air

At this altitude the air is a third of what it should be, and this is the town's great secret: it is nearly silent.

Sound does not carry well in air this thin. The rockfall that would roar in a lowland quarry arrives as a soft, powdery rumble. The ore crushers, huge iron jaws bolted into a chamber cut deep in the eastern wall, make a sound like someone kneading dough in the next room. Workers stand shoulder to shoulder and still lean in to speak, lips nearly to ears. Much of the talk happens in lamp-flashes and hand-signs, a dense, fluent pidgin of the fingers. Two people across a bridge can hold a whole conversation by the tilt of a head-lamp.

Above the rim the wind screams. It pours over the mountain at a hundred and fifty kilometers an hour, and you can see it: snow streamers, ice crystals, torn cloud shearing across the gap. But the crack is below the wind's path, and the screaming reaches the town as a faint, endless hiss, like surf heard from inland. Children born here are startled by loud noises. They have never heard a door slam properly.

## Light

The Seam receives, at best, a few minutes of direct sun each day. Around local noon a blade of light slides down one wall, gilds a strip of the town for the length of a shift change, and withdraws. People stop what they are doing to stand in it. Otherwise the light is the gray of a room with the curtains drawn, bounced down between the walls and diluted at each bounce.

The town supplements this with sodium lamps, strung everywhere on sagging cables, and with head-lamps on every helmet. The overall impression is a deep blue-gray dimness speckled with thousands of small orange sparks. Some are stationary and some are moving, climbing ladders and crossing bridges and swinging at the ends of cables.

## What the town is for

The fracture exposed a seam of ore where the fault ran through an old intrusion: a vein of heavy, silvery-green mineral rich in chromium and something else the company does not name in public. It was never supposed to be reachable. The mountain handed it over overnight.

So the industry is extraction. Galleries are driven horizontally into both walls, following the vein, and the ore is brought out to the gap on narrow-gauge carts pushed by hand. It is dropped onto conveyor belts that climb the eastern face in switchbacks to the crushing chamber, then to the rim, where cable cars carry it down the mountain in iron buckets. These are the town's only link to the world. They descend fourteen kilometers to the valley railhead in a single span of cable, and the round trip takes nine hours.

Power comes from the wind. The rim is lined with turbines built short and ugly: squat three-bladed things bolted into the rock at angles, their housings armored against ice. The wind never stops, so the power never stops. The whole town is electrified, if unevenly. There is a constant faint electrical smell of ozone and hot insulation, and small blue arcs dance at bad connections.

Combustion engines are almost useless here, since there is not enough oxygen to burn diesel properly. The few machines that need more than electricity run on compressed air, charged at the rim by the turbines and piped down in steel lines that frost over and weep white rime. Pneumatic drills, air-hoists, air-brakes on the carts. The town breathes compressed air in small, hard hisses.

## No water

There is no water in the Seam.

Snow blows in constantly, but the air is so dry and so thin that snow does not melt. It sublimates, passing straight from ice to vapor and vanishing. A drift that falls on a ledge at dawn will be gone by afternoon, leaving the stone bone-dry and faintly powdered with dust. Frost forms on metal surfaces every night and is gone by morning. Nothing drips. Nothing pools.

Every drop of water in the town is carried up by cable car, in ceramic crates packed in felt, and rationed by weight. It is used, collected, filtered, used again. The ore is crushed dry. Drill dust is managed with vacuum lines and cloth masks, not spray. People clean themselves with fine abrasive powder and cloths. The one cistern in town sits in a locked chamber in the western wall with a guard at the door. Children believe, not entirely wrongly, that you could buy a house with a full bucket.

The dryness gets into everything. Lips split. Skin cracks at the knuckles. Static builds up on clothing and discharges in small sharp sparks when two people touch. Handshakes crackle.

## The sieve of the sky

Here is the thing no one expected: the Seam is full of life.

It is not plant life. There are no plants to speak of. A few rosettes of orange crustose lichen have established themselves near the rim, the size of fingernails, eleven years of growth. A gray, velvety mold grows on the sacking walls of the mess halls, fed on dropped crumbs and breath-moisture. That is all.

But the animals are abundant. They come because of the wind.

The great wind pouring over the mountain carries things with it. It lifts insects from the lowland valleys, thousands of kilometers of farmland and forest, and hurls them up and over the peaks: aphids, midges, moths, flying ants, beetles, spiders ballooning on threads, pollen, seeds, dust. Most of this "aeolian fallout" would scatter over the snowfields and die. The Seam is a trap. The wind curls down into the gap and slows, and everything it carries drops out of it, onto every ledge and gantry and roof in the crack.

On heavy days the fallout is a steady drizzle of small bodies, many still alive, stunned by cold. They collect in drifts in corners. They cling to lamp glass, drawn by the light. The sodium lamps each wear a shifting halo of moths.

And where the fallout is, the hunters follow.

Jumping spiders are everywhere, small, dark, and fuzzy, with large forward eyes that catch the lamplight. They live in the cracks of the young stone, in the joints of the bridges, in the seams of the houses, emerging to stalk the fallen insects. They are so numerous that a lamp-lit wall seems to shimmer with them. Workers find them in their boots, in their gloves, in their soup. They are considered lucky. Killing one is not done.

Springtails swarm on the insect drifts in their millions, tiny gray-blue flecks, flicking into the air when disturbed like a puff of smoke. Small predatory beetles hunt the springtails. Wingless flies of some unnamed kind walk the walls.

And then there are the birds. Alpine choughs, black with yellow bills, nest in their thousands in the gantries and under the eaves of the hanging houses. They feed on the fallout, on the spiders, on anything the town drops. They are tame to the point of insolence. They sit on railings a hand's width from a worker's face and watch him eat. They steal gloves and bolts and bits of foil. At the noon light they rise all together from both walls, and the air in the gap turns into a soft, whirling black storm, strangely quiet in the thin air, a hush of wings.

At night, small pale bats emerge from somewhere below, from the dark closed end of the crack, and hunt the moths around the lamps. No one knows how they got here. No one knows what else lives down there.

## Turbulence

Put all of this together and the Seam is never still.

The walls creep. Rock pops from the faces with sharp small reports, stress releasing from stone that was deep underground eleven years ago. Rockfalls come down several times a day, and the town has learned to read the signs: dust sifting from overhead, choughs lifting suddenly from a ledge, the spiders all running upward at once.

Crews are always rebuilding. Somewhere in the town at every hour someone is re-pinning a bridge, re-bolting a house to a fresh face, cutting loose a span that has pulled into the red, stringing a new cable. Carts rattle along the galleries. Belts climb. Cable buckets swing out from the rim into the gale. The birds whirl, the fallout drifts down, the spiders stalk, the springtails burst up like smoke. Clouds sometimes pour into the gap from above like milk into a glass, and for an hour the town works inside a lamp-lit fog, and then the wind drags the cloud out again.

It is an anthill, a beehive, a factory, and an avalanche in slow motion, and it all happens in near-silence.

## The mountain rising

One more thing, which the townspeople do not discuss much with outsiders.

The mountain is still rising. This is ordinary geology; the range is young and the plates are still pushing. But in the Seam you can feel it. The company surveyors mark a benchmark at the rim every year, and every year it is higher, by more than it should be, by several centimeters. Sometimes, on very still nights, if you press your ear to the rock, you can hear it: a deep, slow, grinding creak, like a ship's timbers, very far down.

The children call this "the mountain breathing in." They believe that one day it will breathe out, and the Seam will close. The miners laugh at this. But no one builds anything in the Seam meant to last longer than a season, and when the rock creaks at night, the whole town, all four thousand of them in their hanging boxes, goes quiet and listens.
