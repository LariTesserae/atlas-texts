# Ashcarr

## The Basin

The place sits in a shallow bowl of land, barely lower than the country around it. You notice you have descended only because the air changes. It gets cooler and heavier and smells of cut stems and wet bark. The ground under your feet goes soft by degrees, from turf to moss to deep, springy sphagnum that holds your footprint for a full minute before it rises back.

There is no river. Water comes from the air. Most nights a fog pools in the bowl, too shallow to be called a lake of mist but deep enough to drown a man's knees. By mid-morning it has condensed onto every leaf and beam and wire, then drained down into the moss, which drinks it and holds it. By afternoon the upper surfaces are dry again. The whole settlement lives on that daily rhythm of wetting and drying, and it has done so for a very long time.

## What It Is Made Of

From the rim, Ashcarr looks like an orchard someone forgot to stop tending. Look again and the trees are standing in rows too regular, their trunks too square, their crowns flattened into roofs.

The buildings are grown. Hornbeam and field maple have been pleached for generations, their branches bent, bound, and grafted to one another until they fused into continuous living walls. A wall in Ashcarr is a lattice of limbs as thick as a thigh, with the gaps filled by ivy, fern, and a dense felt of moss. Doorframes are two trunks that were trained to lean together and graft at the top, so each lintel is a knot of healed bark shaped like a clasped hand. The roofs are yew, clipped flat and so dense that rain beads and rolls off. Inside, the light comes green and dim through the leaves.

Almost nothing is built from dead material. The few exceptions are iron: axles, pins, gear teeth, ratchet pawls, the copper of the wires. The metal is old and dark. Where it passes through living wood, the wood has grown around it and swallowed it, so every pin is sunk in a collar of scar tissue. Some iron has been engulfed completely, and you can find it only by the bulge in the bark and the faint rust weeping from a crack.

There are perhaps fourteen structures, spread over ground a brisk walker could cross in ten minutes. They stand well apart, with long lawns of moss between them. The emptiness is deliberate: each building needs room for its roots, its light, and its engine arm. The streets are simply the paths left between root zones, wide and green and faintly sunken from use.

## The Engines

This is what Ashcarr is for.

Each building has one or more long arms of wood extending from its flank, like the boom of a crane or a bent bow laid on its side. The arms are alive and still rooted, but they have been cultivated for one property: they bend with humidity. They were grown from reaction-wood, the stressed timber a tree lays down on the tension side of a lean. They were layered, split, and regrafted over decades until each arm became a composite of fibers that swell at different rates. When the fog comes in, the arms curl. When the day dries, they straighten.

The movement is small, a hand's breadth at the tip of an arm twenty paces long. But it is enormous in force, and it happens every day without fail. Each arm tip is pinned to an iron rod, the rod to a ratchet, and the ratchet to a train of gears inside the building. Curling winds the train forward, and straightening winds it forward again through a second pawl. Over a day, each engine turns its main shaft perhaps a few hundred times.

That is enough. The shafts drive slow machines:

- **Presses** that squeeze fiber out of harvested nettle and bracken.
- **Carding drums** that comb the fiber.
- **A rope walk** in the longest building, where a twisting head crawls down a hundred-foot green tunnel over the course of a week, spinning cord behind it.
- **A single paper mill** that beats pulp so slowly it seems not to move, until you come back an hour later and see the vat has changed color.

And in four of the buildings, the shafts turn **dynamos**.

## The Electric Light

At dusk, when the fog begins to gather and the arms begin to curl, the dynamos wake. Copper wire, strung on living posts and sunk into the bark like everything else, carries the current to lamps. The lamps are glass bulbs with thin carbon filaments, hung in the crooks of trees along the paths.

They do not shine brightly. They glow orange and uneven, brightening as the fog thickens and the arms bend harder, dimming toward midnight as the wood reaches full swell and the motion stops. On very still, very humid nights the lamps pulse faintly, following the slow breathing of the arms. This is one of the few places where you can see the weather's rhythm made visible in light.

On dry nights, the rare ones when no fog comes, the lamps stay dark and the town sits blind. The people of Ashcarr take this as an omen, or at least as a nuisance.

## The People

There are people, but few, and you may not see them at first. They wear undyed nettle cloth that goes grey-green with damp, and they move slowly and seldom speak above a murmur. Their work is almost all maintenance:

- **Resetting the pawls** when a ratchet slips.
- **Clearing moss** out of gear teeth, since moss colonizes any surface that stays still for a week, including the machines.
- **Re-pinning the arms.** This is the most serious job, and the reason the town cannot be abandoned.

The wood grows. Every year each arm is a little thicker and a little longer. Its bend changes, its tip wanders, and the iron rod that was perfectly aligned last summer now pulls at an angle and grinds. So every spring the tenders unpin every arm, measure it with knotted cord, and pin it again at a new point. They sometimes graft on a fresh length and sometimes prune back an arm that has grown too strong. An overgrown arm will not break its iron. It will slowly lever the whole building off its root plate.

The buildings shift too. Walls thicken inward. Doorways narrow over decades, and every few generations a door must be abandoned and a new one trained. The town has a map, kept in the paper mill, but it is redrawn every ten years, and the old maps disagree with each other in small ways. Paths drift. A building on the oldest map stands two paces east of where it stands now, and no one moved it.

## Age

Ashcarr is old in the particular way living things are old: nothing in it is ruined, but nothing is new either.

The oldest hornbeam walls have trunks so thick and fused that the individual limbs can no longer be told apart. They have become solid slabs of bark, rippled like poured wax. The iron in them is almost entirely hidden. The moss on the lawns is so deep that anything dropped sinks and is gone. Tenders say that if you dug down you would find old gear wheels, dropped tools, and whole broken engines, all sunk into the peat under the moss and preserved there, black and soft.

The whole settlement has settled into itself. It does not decay, because decay here happens only at the edges, in fallen leaves and pruned twigs, and it is swallowed at once. It does not grow much larger, because the tenders prune it back. It sits in a long, careful equilibrium, adjusting endlessly to stay the same.

## Sound

Ashcarr is mostly quiet. That is the first thing visitors remark on, and after a while it is the thing they notice least.

Under the quiet there is a texture:

- **The ratchets** click, not in rhythm, since each engine moves at its own pace, but as a scattered ticking that rises as fog comes in and fades as the day dries. At the peak of the evening curl, the whole basin ticks softly, like a room full of slow clocks.
- **The wood** makes a low creak from the arms, too deep to hear clearly and more felt than heard, especially if you put a hand against a wall.
- **Water** drips off the yew roofs in the morning, onto moss, which absorbs the sound almost completely.
- **The rope walk** gives off a faint whirr from the twisting head, audible only if you stand at the mouth of the tunnel.
- **The dynamos** make a thin electrical hum at night, just at the edge of hearing, a pitch that seems to change as you turn your head.

There are no engines in the loud sense, no steam and no combustion. Nothing is burned here. The industry is entirely quiet, a factory whose power is weather and whose machinery is trees.

## What Lives Here Besides Plants

Very little.

The moss and the pleached trees leave almost no room for anything else. They are too dense, too tended, too thoroughly occupied. The animals that do live here are small and specialized:

- **Slugs**, large and pale grey, which graze the algae on the iron. The tenders tolerate them because they keep the metal clean.
- **A single species of small brown beetle**, which lives in the gear housings. Its grubs eat the moss that creeps into the teeth, and it has become a partner of sorts. Tenders seed new housings with beetle eggs.
- **Wrens**, occasionally. One or two pairs nest in the thicker walls and sing at dawn, loudly for such small birds and loud for this place. Their song is the brightest sound in Ashcarr.
- **Moths** at night, drawn to the dim lamps, though not many. The light is too faint to gather a crowd.

Nothing large enters the basin. Deer come to the rim and look down, then turn away. Perhaps the smell or the ticking keeps them off. Perhaps it is simply that there is nothing down there a deer could eat that someone would not notice.

## The Day, the Year

**Dawn:** Fog lies thick, and the arms are fully curled. The lamps have gone out as the engines stopped. Wrens sing. Water drips everywhere.

**Morning:** The fog lifts, and condensation drains into the moss. The arms begin to straighten, and the ratchets pick up their ticking again. The presses and drums turn. Tenders walk the paths checking pins.

**Afternoon:** The upper surfaces are dry and the arms nearly straight. The engines slow, and the quietest hour of the day arrives. Light falls through the yew in green, moving patches.

**Dusk:** Air cools, and fog gathers in the low places first, then fills the bowl. The arms begin to curl, and the lamps glow orange and brighten. The ticking returns, faster.

**Night:** The lamps pulse with the breath of the wood. The dynamos hum, and moths drift. Near midnight the arms reach full curl, the engines stop, and the lamps fade to nothing.

Through the year, the balance shifts. In the wet months the fog comes heavier and the arms bend harder, and the work runs faster. There is more rope, more paper, and brighter lamps. In the dry season, a few weeks in late summer, the fog may fail for nights at a time. The engines barely move, the lamps stay dark, and the town waits. In winter the fog freezes onto the branches as rime, the arms lock stiff, and nothing turns at all. The tenders use that season for the heavy work: pruning, grafting, re-pinning. By spring the rime melts, the first fog of the thaw rolls in, and the whole basin begins, slowly, to tick again.

## The Rule of the Place

Everything in Ashcarr moves, and nothing moves quickly. The weather drives the wood, the wood drives the iron, the iron drives the work, and the work pays for the pruning that keeps the wood from outgrowing the iron. It is a machine the size of a village, and it is also a garden, and both halves are always slightly out of true, forever being coaxed back.

If the tenders left, the engines would keep running for a year or two on the fog. Then the arms would outgrow their pins, and the rods would bend and snap. The walls would close their doors and the moss would fill the gears, and Ashcarr would become simply a wood, a strange square-trunked wood in a low basin with iron inside its trees. For a long time afterward, on foggy nights, you might still hear a faint, scattered ticking from deep in the bark, where some buried ratchet was still counting the weather.
