# The Reed Warren Under the Salt Plain

You would hear it before anything else, and you would hear it only there.

On the surface there is almost nothing: a low, cracked plain of pale mineral crust under a thin sky, shallow swells worn so smooth that the horizon seems to sag. There are no rivers and no memory of rivers. But in one place, where the crust dips into a basin no deeper than a man is tall, a ring of grey stubs breaks through the ground. They look like the stumps of burned fence posts, knee-high and weathered to the grain. Put your ear to one and it is humming. Put your hand over its open top and the hum rises half a tone, as if something below has noticed.

Below is a city. It is small and completely dark, and it sings.

---

## The Hollow

The cavity sits under the basin like an air bubble trapped in glass. It is a lens-shaped void about two hundred paces across, and at its tallest it would clear a two-story house. The mineral crust roofs it over and floors it. Everything between roof and floor is plant.

It is not plant the way a forest is plant. There are no leaves, no soil, no green. There is only structure: a single organism, or a single colony so old that the difference no longer matters. It has grown into the void as a lattice of hollow stems. The thickest are as wide as a barrel and the thinnest as narrow as a finger. They branch, fuse, and branch again, arching from floor to ceiling and back. Their walls are astonishingly thin. Knock on one and it rings like a clay pot. Press too hard and your thumb goes through a skin no thicker than eggshell, into a tube of cold, still air.

This is the first strangeness. The place is entirely vegetation, and it is almost entirely empty. The plant fills the hollow the way a skeleton fills a bird, taking up the whole shape and very little of the volume. If you could weigh all of it, the whole city might not outweigh a wagonload of hay. The rest is air: chambers inside chambers, voids inside the walls of voids. The organism grew as a scaffold of absence.

## The Streets

At some point, someone came down here.

Nothing remains of them except what they did. They did a great deal. They took the lattice and made it into a town. With flint and chert, and with tools of hardened stem sharpened on the silica of the plant itself, they cut. They carved doorways through the barrel-trunks and hollowed rooms into the swellings where branches fused. They split the smaller stems lengthwise and laid them flat as floorboards. They wove the finest tendrils into cord and hung footbridges between the upper junctions. Alleys run where they sawed straight corridors through the tangle. There are squares where they cleared the lattice back to the crust, and ramps of stacked, split reed climbing to galleries near the roof.

In the dark you would find it with your hands. There are thresholds worn to a shallow saddle, and window-holes whose rims have gone soft and rounded. There is a long street floored with rows of halved stems, each plank dished in the middle as if walked on for a thousand years. Niches in the walls hold small objects. Flint blades still fit the palm. Spindles are carved from pith. Cord is wound on sticks, and the fiber has turned to something like paper under the touch. One doorway has knots tied into the fiber above it, a sequence of loops and hitches. Perhaps it was a name, or a warning, or an address.

There are no bones, no hearths, no soot. Nothing here was ever burned, because nothing here was ever lit.

## The Growing

The plant did not stop when the builders left, and this is what makes the place feel inhabited.

Wherever they cut, the organism healed along the cut. It sealed each wound with a raised seam of new stem, and the seams became the grammar of the city. A doorway, once carved, is now framed by a thick lip of living growth. A street, once sawn, has grown walls. Over ages the plant has begun to repeat them. At the city's edges, where the builders never reached, there are doorways no hand made. The lattice has grown them itself, in the right proportions, at the right intervals. There are unfinished streets that trail off into tangle. There are rooms with no way in, perfectly shaped and sealed. There is a footbridge of woven tendril grown, not tied, strung between two junctions where no bridge was needed.

The city is still being built. It is being built very slowly, by the thing it was built from, according to a plan that the plant seems to have learned. It keeps copying the plan into the dark.

## What It Lives On

There is no water here. The crust is bone-dry, the air is dry enough to crack skin, and the stems contain nothing wet at all. Cut one open and the pith is chalky and fibrous. It smells faintly of cold straw and struck flint. Whatever keeps the organism alive, it is not moisture.

The best answer the place offers is the sound.

## The Sound

The surface above the hollow is a little warmer than the hollow itself, and the plain outside is colder still at night. Through those stubs on the surface, and through hairline cracks in the crust, air is always moving. It is a faint, steady draft, pulled one way through the day and pushed the other way through the night. The draft enters the lattice and finds ten thousand hollow tubes of every length and bore. Every one of them sounds.

It is loud. It is not a whisper or a moan. It is a full, standing drone, the whole city resonating like a pipe organ with every stop pulled out. The deepest tones come from the barrel-trunks, so low they are felt in the sternum more than heard. Above them the middle stems hold a thick, reedy chord, and the fine tendrils add a high, shimmering hiss like wind through dry grass. The builders' carvings changed the music. Every doorway they cut opened a new vent. Every room they hollowed became a chamber that booms. The streets run through the drone the way corridors run through a cathedral's echo. Walking one, you pass through distinct zones of pitch. A square hums in one key, a narrow alley whines in another, and a sealed room nearby throbs through its walls like a held breath.

Where the sound is strongest, the plant is thickest. The stems nearest the great resonant chambers are fat and dense-walled. In quiet corners the lattice thins to brittle lace and crumbles to dust at a touch. Put your palm flat against a loud wall and you can feel the tissue trembling, and the tremble has a faint warmth that the air does not. The organism appears to feed on vibration. It turns the constant shaking of its own walls into slow growth. It grows toward loudness. It shapes itself into better instruments. Its grown doorways and unasked-for bridges are tuned. Each new structure deepens the drone a little, and the drone feeds the growth.

## The Slow Cycle

Nothing here happens quickly. The draft reverses twice a day, and at each turn there is a brief moment, a few breaths long, when the city goes almost silent. The pipes empty. The chord collapses into a hiss, then into a faint ticking of settling fiber. In that hush you can hear how large the emptiness is. Then the air turns and the drone swells back up from the lowest note, register by register, until the whole hollow is roaring again.

Over years the pitch drifts. As the great stems thicken, their tones drop. Over centuries whole chords migrate, new rooms sealing and opening, and the city's key slowly changes. The knotted cords above one doorway may once have been music: a notation for the chord the builders heard, a chord that no longer exists here.

## The Wearing

It is ancient in a way the surface can only imitate. The draft never stops, and the fine mineral dust it carries has been sanding the lattice since before the builders came. Every edge is rounded and every surface polished to the smoothness of old horn. Stems that stand in the full stream of air are worn so thin in places that light would pass through them, if there were light. The oldest parts of the city are wearing away as slowly as the newest parts grow. Near the center, the first-carved district has been abraded almost to nothing: a field of low, smooth stumps and faint floor-grooves, like the footprint of a town. The drone there is soft, a single low tone. It is where the city is ending.

At the edges, the city is beginning. In between, for as long as the air moves, it plays.

## Being There

You would be cold, a dry, steady cold that sits in the joints. You would be blind, and would stay blind. You would move by touch and by pitch, learning that one tone means an open square ahead and a rising whine means a narrowing street. You would smell straw, stone, and old paper. Your footsteps and your voice would vanish into the drone, and you would feel the walls hum under your hands like something breathing.

You would find no one. No insect crawls the fiber, and no bat hangs from the roof. Nothing but the plant has ever been alive down here, except once, briefly, the people who taught it what a city was.

It has not forgotten. In the dark, in its own time, it is still building one, and it sings as it goes.
