# The Uncovered Shelf

## The ground

It is a shelf of stone high on the shoulder of a mountain range, above almost everything that breathes easily. The ridgelines around it run off in both directions for kilometers, gray and white. Below, far below, a layer of cloud lies flat as a floor. The air is thin enough that the sky, where it shows at all, is a bruised indigo even at midday.

Nothing about the ground looks old. The rock is gneiss, banded dark and pale, and it has not weathered at all. It is polished. Run a hand across it and it feels almost like glass, cold and faintly slick. Long parallel scratches cut across it, all pointing downslope, some fine as hairs and some as deep as a finger. They are the marks of stones that were dragged across it inside the ice. There are curved chatter-marks where boulders skipped and bit. There are smooth humps whose upslope faces are polished and whose downslope faces are plucked and angular, the stone torn away in blocks.

This rock was under ice until very recently. The glacier that sanded it still sits at the head of the shelf, a gray-white wall perhaps forty meters high, and the wall is backing away. Each summer it retreats and leaves another band of naked stone, still wet from the ice's underside, never before touched by light or wind or rain. You could walk from the ice edge outward and pass through time. Stone that is a few weeks old gives way to stone that is a few years old, then to stone uncovered when someone now living was a child. At the far lip of the shelf, where the ground drops toward the cloud, the rock has been open to the sky for perhaps eighty years. Even there it is barely touched. Here erosion is not a sculptor that has finished. It has only just started, and in places it has not started at all.

## Water

Everything is wet, but seldom in a way you could drink from. The glacier face drips constantly. A curtain of beads runs along its base, falling onto the stone in a line of small, steady taps. Under the ice, meltwater runs in unseen channels and comes out at the snout as a milky slurry the color of dishwater or weak tea. It is loaded with rock flour, stone ground finer than talc.

This water spreads across the shelf in sheets only a few millimeters deep. It is not yet organized into streams, because the ground is too new to have channels. It finds the scratches and follows them, so that in low light the whole slope seems ruled with fine silver lines. Where it pools in hollows the ice left behind, it forms small milky tarns. By the afternoon they have skins of slush, and by dawn they are frozen through.

The rock weeps. Water that soaked into the fractures during the brief thaw of the day seeps back out along the joints at night and freezes into glazes and beards of ice. By morning every crack is jacked a hair wider. This is how the erosion starts, one freeze at a time: splitting the stone open in thin plates that will one day become scree.

The atmosphere itself condenses. Cloud rises up the valley walls in the afternoon and swallows the shelf, and for hours everything drips. Rime grows sideways on the windward faces of boulders, feathers of ice pointing into the wind. When the cloud tears open, the rime sublimates into the thin, dry air without ever melting.

## Light

The light is almost always dim. It is not dark, but it is attenuated. Cloud sits over the shelf or under it, or both at once, so that you stand in a gray slot between two ceilings. The sun, when it shows, is a white disk with no warmth that you can feel, and shadows under it are sharp and blue.

The best light comes at the edges of the day. Just before dawn the eastern peaks go from charcoal to rose. For about ten minutes the wet stone of the shelf turns copper and every meltwater line glows. Then the cloud closes and the world returns to pewter.

At night the glow comes from below. Far down, beneath the cloud floor, there are lowlands you cannot see, and in them there are cities, foundries, and power stations. Their light comes up through the cloud as a faint, sick orange stain on the undersides of things, strongest in the southwest, the direction of the largest of them. Nothing on this shelf was made by anyone. But the shelf is lit, faintly, every night, by the industry of the plains.

## The ice as a ledger

Look closely at the glacier face and you see that it is striped. Pale layers alternate with thin dark ones like the pages of a book seen edge-on. Each year's snow buries the last year's dust, and the dust is not only dust. The dark bands carry soot: black carbon from coal and diesel and burning fields hundreds of kilometers away. It rose in the lowland haze and rode the monsoon and the westerlies and fell here with the snow.

The deeper and older layers are paler. The upper layers, the ones laid down in the last century and a half, are darker. The ice is a ledger of the smokestacks.

The soot does more than record. Darkened ice absorbs the weak sun it would otherwise have reflected, and melts faster. On the flat top surface of the glacier, the black grains gather in clumps called cryoconite. Each clump melts itself a little vertical hole, a perfect cylinder a few centimeters wide, with a dark pellet of grit and bacteria at the bottom. Thousands of these holes pock the ice like a honeycomb. Each is a miniature furnace driven by sunlight and coal dust, boring down into the glacier.

So the whole shelf is the product of a machine, but the machine is elsewhere. This is the industrial world's footprint, printed in stone rather than steel. No engine stands here, and no pylon, no road, no bolt. The engine is the warmed air and the darkened snow, and its output is new ground. In summer the meltwater leaves the shelf, drops through the cloud, and down in the valleys it spins the turbines of dams that feed the grid, whose glow lights the cloud at night. The loop closes somewhere out of sight.

## Vegetation

Life has hardly caught up with the retreating ice. The newest stone is sterile. Walk outward and the first colonists appear: a gray-green stain in the lee of a boulder, a crust of lichen no bigger than a coin, map lichen in yellow-green blots edged with black. These grow perhaps a millimeter a year. A lichen the size of a palm here is older than any person alive.

Further out there are mosses. They form tight, dark cushions in cracks that hold a little rock flour and a little water, and they freeze solid every night and thaw every noon. Near the far lip, on the oldest ground, a handful of flowering plants have arrived. There is a cushion plant forming a hard, tight dome the size of a fist, packed with dead leaves inside to hold heat. A saxifrage wedges itself in a joint. There are one or two tufts of sedge. They flower in the brief window after the solstice, tiny white and pink, and are pollinated by flies. Plants cover perhaps one or two parts in a hundred of the surface. The rest is rock.

## Fauna

This is the strange part. The shelf is nearly barren of plants, yet it is thick with animals.

Most of what lives here does not eat what grows here. It eats what arrives. The wind that climbs the valleys each afternoon carries a steady fall of small lives from the lowlands: aphids, gnats, moths, winged ants, seeds, pollen, and the gossamer threads of ballooning spiderlings. They are lifted thousands of meters in the rising air and dropped, chilled and dying, onto the snow and stone. Ecologists call this an aeolian ecosystem: a food web fed by the wind.

On the glacier surface, in the evening, the ice worms come up. They are thin, dark, a centimeter or two long, and there can be thousands in a square meter. They rise from the ice as the sun goes down to graze on pollen and algae and the red snow-algae that tint the old snow pink in patches. By full dark they have sunk back into the ice. Springtails and glacier fleas dust the snow like pepper. Where the snow meets stone, wolf spiders and jumping spiders hunt among the boulders. They are black, to catch what heat there is, and they wait for the wind to deliver.

Birds work the shelf in loose, busy flocks. Snow finches pick windfall insects off the snow edges. Choughs with yellow bills and red legs tumble and shriek on the updrafts. Their calls are the loudest sound in the place, and they are not loud. A pair of lammergeiers patrols the ridge. They are enormous, rust-bellied vultures whose wings barely move, and they drop bones onto the new slabs to crack them. Out on the older ground, where moss gives a little cover, pikas have dug in. They are round, earless-looking, and quick, and they cut sedge stems and pile them to dry. Their thin whistle carries a long way. Snow leopards use the ridge as a highway, a dusk shape seen once a season, and blue sheep graze the far slopes where grass is thicker.

In spring and autumn the sky itself becomes a route. Bar-headed geese pass over in lines, higher than any other migratory bird, crossing the range in a single push. They fly at night and in the pre-dawn, low enough sometimes that you hear the creak of wing feathers and the soft honking before you see them.

Yet for all this abundance the shelf feels empty. The animals are many but small, or scattered, or passing through. The volume of air and stone is enormous, and the lives in it are specks. You can stand for an hour and see only a chough, a dropped moth, a single spider on a boulder. You have to kneel and look at the snow to see that it is crawling.

## Sound

It is mostly quiet. The dominant sound is drip, steady and multiplied along the ice face into a faint, continuous patter, like rain heard from indoors. Beneath it is the trickle of sheet water moving across stone, so thin it barely makes a sound at all.

Now and then the glacier speaks. There is a deep crack like a distant rifle when a crevasse opens, or a long groan as the ice shifts on its bed. In the afternoon, when the day's thaw loosens the frozen joints, there is rockfall: a single stone clattering, then a hiss of grit, then nothing.

The wind comes and goes. When it blows, it moans across the ridge crests. When it stops, the silence has a pressure to it, and you can hear your own pulse in the thin air.

High above, once or twice an hour, comes the one sound here that is made by an engine: the faint, rolling roar of an airliner crossing at altitude, invisible above the cloud. It swells and fades over a full minute and then is gone.

## Rhythms

Everything here runs on cycles, and they are quick.

**The daily cycle:** Nights freeze. Water stills, rock weeps and glazes, and ice worms sink. At dawn there is copper light and the cracking of ice skins. Through the late morning the thaw builds. Drip becomes trickle and the tarns loosen. In the afternoon the upslope wind rises, carrying its freight of insects, and the cloud climbs to swallow the shelf. Birds feed hard, spiders feed, rockfall peaks. At dusk the ice worms rise and the wind drops. At night the orange glow of the lowlands shows on the cloud beneath.

**The seasonal cycle:** In winter the shelf is buried under wind-packed snow and almost everything sleeps or leaves. In the short summer, melt and wind-fall peak together, the cushion plants flower, and the pikas cut hay. In spring and autumn, geese pass overhead.

**The long cycle:** The ice retreats, and the stone and life follow it upward. Each year the band of new ground widens, and the colonists creep after it. Lichen follows the edge of the ice, moss follows the lichen, and the cushion plants follow the moss. The soil that will one day cover this shelf does not exist yet. It is being assembled grain by grain from rock flour, windblown dust, dead insects, and bird droppings. A thousand years from now this could be a meadow. For now it is almost entirely raw.

## What it is like to be here

You feel the cold as ache in the hands and the thin air as a tightness behind the eyes. You feel, strongly, that the place is new, that you are standing on ground nobody has stood on because until very recently there was no ground to stand on.

You feel, too, the presence of machines you cannot see: in the orange glow at night, in the black bands in the ice, in the roar of the airliner, in the certainty that this water is going to turn a turbine far away. There is no ruin here, no rusting hulk or fence post. Nothing has been built. And still this is an industrial landscape, as surely as any foundry yard. It is built out of the industry's consequences instead of its buildings, one soot-dark layer of snow and one freshly uncovered slab of stone at a time.
