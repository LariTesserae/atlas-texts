# The Held Summit

## Approach

From far below, from the dry brown foothills, it looks like weather. A white cap sits on the highest massif of the range, as if a single cloud had caught on the peaks and stayed. Travelers assume it is snow, then ice, then cloud, and each guess fails a little more on the long ascent. The mountains around it are what mountains at that height should be: bare rock and blue glaciers, air so thin it barely holds a sound, a sky darkened almost to indigo at noon. The cap never moves. Seasons pass under it. Storms break against its flanks and slide around it the way water parts around a stone.

Near the top, where lungs are burning and the rock is rimed with frost, the climber reaches a wall that cannot be seen. It is felt first as warmth on the face, as if someone had opened an oven door a hand's width away. Then comes the smell: rot, flowers, wet earth, the sweet ferment of fallen fruit. Then the realization that the frost on the rocks ends in a line. The line is not ragged. It curves across the mountainside with the smoothness of a drawn arc, running up over boulders and down into gullies without breaking. On one side is lichen stiff with ice. On the other, a hand's breadth away, is moss so saturated it drips.

Step across, and the pressure takes you. Your ears pop hard. Air floods your chest, thick and heavy, sea-level air, hot as a greenhouse in August. Your breath fogs the other way now, not from cold but because the atmosphere itself is so full of water it condenses on your skin. Within a minute you are drenched in sweat and vapor, and the sound arrives all at once, because inside the boundary there is enough air to carry it.

## The Enclosure

The Held Summit is a basin and a crown of broken peaks, perhaps twenty kilometers across, enclosed beneath something the few who have written about it call the Lid, or the Mantle, or simply the Hold. It is not a dome of any material you could strike. It is a region in which air stays. The atmosphere inside is dense, hot, and wet, a pocket of lowland jungle climate lifted almost to the stratosphere and kept there. From inside, the Lid is visible only where water collects on it: a vast, faintly pearled ceiling some four or five hundred meters above the highest ground, where condensation beads and gathers and falls. Clouds form against its underside and hang there like a second, lower sky, and through them the sun comes diffused and soft, ordinary daylight, though the sun outside is fierce and violet-edged at this altitude.

It rains constantly, though not in storms. The Lid weeps. Drops the size of grapes release from the ceiling and fall half a kilometer through the warm air, so the rain inside the Hold is warm, heavy, and slow-seeming, each drop audible where it lands on a leaf. Some places sit beneath a steady drip-line, where the ceiling's curvature channels water together, and there the fall is a continuous silver thread from sky to ground, a waterfall with no cliff above it. These threads never move. The trees beneath them have grown into shapes that catch and split the stream, and over the centuries the rock below has been bored into perfect round wells, some of them deeper than a house.

## Terrain and Stone

The ground is old volcanic rock, black and dense and warm to the touch. The warmth is important. It is not sun-warmth. It comes up from inside. Press your palm to any exposed boulder and it will be warmer than your blood, as if the mountain were running a steady, permanent fever. Whether this heat is geological or engineered, or whether that distinction still means anything here, no one has settled. The rock sweats. Mineral water seeps from every fracture, carrying dissolved lime and silica, and wherever it runs it leaves deposits. The result, after a very long time, is a landscape draped in travertine.

The slopes of the basin are stepped in cream and rust-colored terraces, pool after pool spilling over scalloped rims, each rim built millimeter by millimeter from what the water left behind. Some terraces are the size of a hand. The great ones below the eastern crags are the size of fields, shallow blue-green lakes held in stone basins, steaming faintly, fringed with reeds and pale grasses. Erosion here is not violent. There are no floods, no freezes, no cracking frosts. The water is too constant and too gentle. Instead the land has settled into itself. Every edge is rounded. Every hollow has filled with soil. The peaks inside the Hold have the soft profiles of old teeth, worn by nothing harsher than dripping. Here and there the black basalt shows through the pale mineral skin like bone through a thin place in the flesh.

Where the ground rises toward the rim, the basalt breaks into towers and fins, weathered into honeycomb by the weeping water. These towers are the wettest places in the Hold. Their surfaces are never dry. Moss, ferns, and small orchids cover them completely, so from a distance they look like green pillars wrapped in fur, and water sheets down their faces in glistening films that catch the light.

## Vegetation

The growth is dense and layered, but it is not quite the jungle of any lowland. It is lowland life that has lived a very long time under a strange sky, and it has made adjustments.

The canopy trees are broad and relatively short. There is no wind here, none at all, so nothing has to stand against weather. But there is always a ceiling, and in the oldest groves the trees have learned it. They grow up to a certain height and then spread sideways, forming flat-topped crowns that interlock into a continuous roof. Beneath that roof is a second, darker world. In the few places where trees have grown right up into the condensation zone, where the drip-line clouds hang lowest, their topmost leaves are pale and translucent, and they take in water directly from the fog.

The trunks are almost invisible under epiphytes: bromeliads holding cups of warm water that each contain their own ecosystems of larvae and tiny frogs, hanging mosses in long gray-green beards, orchids in mad profusion. Strangler figs have grown over one another in successive generations until some of the oldest are hollow lattices with nothing at their centers, cathedrals of root through which you can walk. Tree ferns crowd the terrace edges. Giant-leaved aroids, some with leaves longer than a person, fill the understory and turn slowly over weeks to follow the brightest patch of the pearly ceiling, which shifts as the condensation shifts.

Around the steaming pools grows a sedge whose flowerheads are heavy and white, and whose pollen hangs in the humid air so thickly at certain seasons that the lower basin turns faintly gold. Along the black towers a creeping succulent-thing spreads, fleshy and ribbed, that stores the warmth of the rock. At night its tissues glow very faintly, a dull ember-orange you can only see with dark-adjusted eyes, mapping the heat of the mountain across its skin.

Nothing here is seasonal in the usual sense. The temperature varies by only a few degrees across the year. Instead the plants follow slower rhythms. Some species flower together once a decade. One tree, the one the old writers call the ceilingwood, flowers every hundred and nine years, and when it does the whole basin smells of cinnamon and wet iron.

## Fauna

The air is full of animals, and nearly all of them are loud.

The frogs are the foundation of the sound. There are dozens of species, in every bromeliad and pool and wet crevice, and they call almost continuously. Some make a pure bell-note, some a ratcheting click, some a deep, throbbing hum that seems to come from the ground. Their chorus rises and falls in slow waves that pass across the basin, so standing on a terrace you can hear a surge of calling begin on the far side and roll toward you over several minutes, like a wind moving through grass. The insects join it: cicada-like singers that drone at the pitch of a saw blade, crickets, a beetle whose wing-cases clap. Under the canopy, it is often too loud to speak at an ordinary volume.

Birds are abundant and very strange, because they have never left. The creatures of the Hold are sealed in. A bird that flies past the boundary finds itself in air too thin to hold its wings, at a cold that kills in minutes. The species that survived are the ones that learned the edge. They fly in wheeling flocks beneath the Lid and turn sharply before the rim, and some of them have become so shaped by enclosure that their flight is almost all gliding. They coast in long, slow spirals on the thermals rising from the warm rock, riding up nearly to the condensation ceiling and descending in lazy loops for hours without a wingbeat. The largest of them have broad, pale wings patterned like the ceiling-clouds, and from below they vanish against it.

There are hornbill-like birds with swollen, hollow casques that boom when they call. There are tiny iridescent nectar-feeders that hover at the orchid-towers. There are ground birds, heavy and flightless, that pick through the leaf litter and roost in the hollow fig-cathedrals. Their calls are harsh and very carrying.

In the canopy live small, long-limbed primates with enormous eyes, and larger slow-moving browsers, something like tree sloths, whose fur holds green algae so completely that they appear to be moss. On the terraces are aquatic things: blind white fish in the deep drip-wells, salamanders the color of the travertine, a long-bodied otterlike hunter that moves from pool to pool at dawn. Bats swarm up at dusk in columns and feed along the underside of the Lid, where insects gather in the condensation, and their clicking is a high, dry static over the evening chorus.

The insects are enormous by the standards of thin-air places. The dense atmosphere and the heat allow them to grow. There are moths with wingspans wider than a hand, dragonflies over the pools like small hawks, and on the black towers, snails with translucent shells that collect the faint glow of the ember-succulent and carry it slowly about at night like lanterns.

## The Edge

The strangest place in the Hold is its boundary, the rim where the Lid comes down to meet the mountain.

Here, the dense warm air ends at a line. On the inside, jungle grows right up to it, and the plants at the very edge are stunted and leaning inward, as if flinching. On the outside there is only bare frozen rock and the vast blue silence of real altitude, the other peaks of the range standing white and remote against a nearly black sky. Sound does not cross. The roaring chorus of the Hold stops at the line as if cut with a knife, and a step outward plunges you into a silence so total you can hear your own blood.

Water does cross. The terraces spill toward the rim, and in several places streams run right off the edge of the basin and out through the boundary into the cold. The moment the water leaves the warm pressure it boils violently in the thin air and freezes almost in the same instant. Over centuries, these outflows have built the Curtains: vast pale cascades of frozen water down the outer cliffs, columns and fringes and organ-pipes of ice hundreds of meters tall, rimed with frost-feathers, glittering in the harsh outside sun. From inside the Hold, looking out through the boundary, you can see the Curtains hanging below the rim like frozen smoke. They grow a few centimeters a year. The oldest reach nearly to the glaciers below, and in their lowest reaches the ice is ancient and blue-black.

The boundary is not perfectly sealed. At a handful of points around the rim, the Hold leaks. Warm air escapes through these gaps in a thin, steady, endless stream, and the escape sings. The Whistles, as they are called, are one of the defining sounds of the place: pure, sustained tones, each at its own pitch, audible from kilometers inside the basin beneath the frog chorus. The deepest of them is almost too low to hear and is felt instead as a pressure in the chest. Where each Whistle stands, a plume of vapor streams outward through the boundary and turns instantly to ice crystals, a glittering horizontal banner that drifts away across the empty sky.

## The Anchors

The Hold is held by something, and the something can be seen.

Around the rim, at irregular intervals, stand the Anchors. There are perhaps forty of them, though some are fallen or buried. Each is a slender column, a few meters thick and sometimes over a hundred meters tall, rising from the rock to vanish into the condensation ceiling. They seem to connect ground to Lid. Their material resembles obsidian that has been polished and then wetted: black, lustrous, faintly translucent at its edges, where a deep violet shows through. But it is not quite solid. Pressed hard, the surface gives very slightly, like the skin of a fruit, and returns slowly. It is always warm, warmer than the rock.

Each Anchor emits a tone, a low steady hum at the edge of hearing that underlies all the other noise of the Hold. Near an Anchor the hum becomes a physical presence. Water beading on its surface forms small standing patterns, rings and lattices that shift very slowly, like a vibration made visible. Animals avoid touching the Anchors but gather near them. The heat attracts insects, the insects attract birds, and the bases of the columns are always busy.

No one knows what the Anchors are. They do not look built in the sense of being assembled. They have no seams, joints, or markings. But they are arranged too regularly around the rim to be natural, and they clearly do something. When one of them fell, according to the only account that survives, the boundary near it sagged inward by a hundred meters over the following fifty years, and the jungle there died back as the thin cold crept in. The dead ground is still visible, a gray scar of leafless trunks in the northwest.

The Anchors grow. They grow extremely slowly, by accretion, adding a thin glossy layer that can be measured only across generations. At the base of the tallest there are older, broken stumps of the same material, suggesting that the columns have been replaced, or have replaced themselves, several times. Some believe the Anchors are machines. Some believe they are organisms. Some believe the distinction was abandoned long ago by whoever made them.

## Traces

There are very few human marks in the Hold, and they are scattered, and most of them are old.

A few stone huts stand on the terraces, low round buildings of fitted basalt, their roofs long since fallen and replaced by the roots of trees that grew in through the walls. One of them still has a hearth. On a rock shelf above the great eastern pools is a flat circular platform, perhaps fifteen meters across, made of the same black glassy material as the Anchors. It is perfectly level and perfectly smooth, and nothing grows on it. Rainwater that falls on it spreads into a thin sheet and runs off the edge without pooling. Birds land there and seem uneasy.

There is a rope bridge over a deep drip-well gorge that has been maintained, crudely, by someone, fairly recently. Its ropes are fiber and still fresh. No one was found near it. Deep in the fig-cathedrals there is a single small instrument, a thing of pale metal and crystal the size of a head, grown into the roots of a strangler fig, lifted a meter off the ground by the tree's slow expansion over what must have been centuries. It still ticks, very faintly, once every few minutes. Beside several of the Whistles, on the inner side of the boundary, small stones have been stacked in careful cairns, and in the cairns are offerings: shells, dried flowers, bits of colored glass, and once, a child's shoe.

Whoever tended the Hold, or lived in it, or merely visited, has left almost no trace of what they believed about it. The marks suggest a patient kind of attention: people who did not change the place much, who stayed a while and went away, who returned now and then over very long spans of time.

## Time

The Hold does not change quickly. Its weather is almost motionless. Its rain falls in the same places year after year. The pools fill their stone basins and add their thin mineral layers a fraction of a millimeter at a time. The Curtains of ice grow down the outer cliffs by centimeters. The Anchors thicken by the width of a fingernail in a lifetime.

But it does change. The boundary breathes, very slightly, over periods of decades, expanding and contracting by a few meters. The jungle creeps outward when the Lid swells and dies back when it shrinks, and along the rim there are bands of different-aged forest that record these slow pulses like tree rings. The ceilingwood flowers every hundred and nine years. The great glide-birds live, by some accounts, for two centuries. The frog chorus moves across the basin in waves. The Whistles hold their notes without end.

To stand inside the Hold is to stand in a place that has been going on, steadily and loudly and in almost the same way, for longer than anyone knows, in defiance of everything the mountain around it should be. Hot and wet and full of life, sealed in a bubble of kept air at the top of the world, humming, weeping, ticking, singing, patient as stone. Outside, the thin blue silence waits at the edge, and the ice grows down the cliffs, one frozen drop at a time.
