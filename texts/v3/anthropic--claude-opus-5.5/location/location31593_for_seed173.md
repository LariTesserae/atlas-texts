# The Low Hall at Number Four Intake

It is a single room, and it is entirely underwater.

You find it the way the eels do, by going down. The shaft begins as a square of paler green above you and narrows into a vertical throat of concrete. The walls have gone soft with age. The corners of the formwork, once sharp enough to cut a hand, are rounded like river stones. The aggregate stands out in relief where the cement between the pebbles has been sucked away grain by grain over what must be centuries. Iron ladder rungs once climbed the east wall. What remains are rust-blossoms, orange and lobed like fungus, each the size of a fist, spaced a regular forearm apart, marking where the rungs used to be.

At the bottom, the shaft opens into the hall.

## The room

It is not large, perhaps the size of a village chapel, rectangular and barrel-roofed. It was built by people who thought water was something to be managed and who poured their confidence in reinforced concrete. Along the long walls, pilasters rise to a ribbed ceiling. Each rib has shed its skin, and the rebar inside shows through as dark parallel lines, like the veins in a leaf seen against light. In places the steel has swollen and burst the concrete outward, and those wounds have healed over with mineral crust, cream and rust-streaked, so that the ceiling looks scarred rather than broken.

The floor is not visible. Where it should be lies a fine grey silt, ankle-deep, very still at the edges and constantly moving in the center. Things poke up through it: the stub of a railing, a valve wheel standing on edge, a row of iron bolt-heads where some long-gone machine was anchored. Everything that was ever made of wood is gone. Everything made of copper has turned the color of verdigris and gone brittle as biscuit. Everything made of bronze has survived.

The bronze is what the room is for.

## The turbine

At the far end, set into a circular pit, is the machine. It is a Francis-type runner, maybe three meters across, its curved blades cast in a single piece of phosphor bronze. It sits inside a spiral casing of riveted steel plate. The casing has mostly corroded to lace, so you can see the runner through it, as if the machine had been drawn in cutaway for a textbook.

It is turning.

Not fast. Somewhere above and behind the wall, the old dam still holds back most of a reservoir, and a fraction of that head still finds its way into the penstock, through a gate that jammed partly open long ago and never closed. The water comes out of a slot in the wall like a pressed breath, hits the runner, and pushes it around. The bearings were water-lubricated lignum vitae, which lasted far longer than anyone would have guessed, and then failed. Now the shaft rides on its own worn bronze, shimmying in its housing.

This is where the sound comes from.

## The sound

Sound travels well underwater, and the hall is a resonating box of stone. What you hear is layered and never stops.

At the bottom is the penstock itself, a low roar you feel more in the chest than the ears, steady as a held organ note. Above it is the runner, which gives off a rhythmic grinding thump once per revolution, *whum — whum — whum*, from the shaft knocking against a flat worn into its bearing. Above that is a high, irregular chatter of metal on metal from loose plates in the spiral casing. They vibrate in sympathy and sometimes, when the flow surges, rise to a shriek that sends every fish in the room flickering sideways at once.

Then there is the animal sound, which is louder than you would expect. The pistol shrimp in the silt snap constantly, a crackle like fat in a pan, spread through the whole floor. The drum-fish that shelter behind the pilasters grunt to each other in the evenings, a boomy croak that bounces off the barrel ceiling and returns doubled. Mussels, when the current slackens, click their shells, millions of tiny latches closing.

It is never quiet in the Low Hall. The creatures that live here were born into the noise and do not know it is noise.

## The light

Light comes from two sources.

The first is the shaft. On clear days it lets down a column of green-grey light that falls onto the silt in a rough square. Motes turn slowly inside it, and the fish pass in and out of it as if through a curtain. It reaches perhaps a third of the room. The corners are dark.

The second source is the lamp.

On the wall above the turbine pit is a single industrial fitting: a heavy bronze cage and a thick glass dome, gasketed for wet service. Inside, a filament still sometimes glows. It does not glow constantly. The turbine drives, through a shaft that goes up into the wall, a small generator that someone once installed for exactly this purpose: to light the room for maintenance crews without running cable from the powerhouse. The generator's windings are enclosed and oil-filled and have mostly held. The glass has kept the water out. The filament is carbon and very old.

So the lamp breathes. When the flow is strong it brightens to a dull amber, making the whole room briefly warm-colored, the silt gold, the fish throwing long shadows up the ribbed ceiling. When the flow falls off it dims to a coil of red wire and then to nothing. It pulses faintly with the *whum* of the runner, a flicker you notice only after watching for a while. The crews who hung it are long dead. It has outlasted the town, the road, and the language on the brass plate beneath it, which is now too corroded to read.

## The cycle

The room has a rhythm, and the rhythm comes from the reservoir.

Far above, the lake rises with spring melt and falls through the dry months. When it is high, the head is greater, the jet through the jammed gate is stronger, and the runner spins faster. The Low Hall enters its loud season. The roar deepens, the casing shrieks more often, and the lamp burns nearly steady. The silt in the center of the floor lifts into a slow cloud that swirls with the outflow and makes the room murky. Fish that feed by sight retreat to the corners. The eels come out.

When the lake falls, the room calms. The runner slows to one sluggish turn every few seconds, each thump distinct, and sometimes, in the lowest weeks of late summer, it hesitates at the top of its stroke and seems about to stop. It never has. The lamp goes dark for days at a time. The silt settles and the water clears so that the shaft-light reaches almost to the pit. This is the breeding season for almost everything in the room.

There is a shorter cycle too. Upstream, some mechanism, perhaps a float valve or a siphon in a spillway, trips once or twice a day and sends a surge through the system. The creatures know it is coming, though you would not. A few minutes before, the drum-fish go silent. Then the roar climbs, the lamp flares amber, the loose plates scream, and the whole room shakes for perhaps a quarter hour before sinking back.

## The life

The Low Hall is crowded with animals, though the space never feels full. There is a great deal of water between them.

**Mussels** cover every surface that faces the current: the lip of the penstock slot, the leading edges of the pilasters, the upstream face of the spiral casing. They grow in black, glossy mats so thick that the concrete beneath has not seen water in generations. They filter the outflow, and the water leaving them is noticeably clearer than the water entering.

**Crayfish** live in the rust-blossoms and in the cavities where rebar has burst out. They are pale, nearly translucent, and larger than seems right. They come out at night and walk the edges of the silt.

**Eels** live in the penstock. Nobody knows how far up it they go. They emerge in the loud season and hang in the jet with their heads upstream, barely moving, taking whatever the current brings. In the quiet season they vanish.

**Drum-fish**, a dozen or so, are heavy-bodied and silver-bronze. They hold territories behind the pilasters and defend them with display and sound.

**A shoal of small minnow-like fish**, several hundred strong, lives in the shaft-light and moves as one body. They avoid the turbine pit, and they panic at the shrieks.

**A single large catfish** lives under the turbine casing, in the dark of the pit, and has perhaps lived there a very long time. It eats minnows that misjudge the current and get pulled down. It is the room's only real predator, and the whole ecology bends around its patience.

Vegetation is scant. A thin brown film of diatoms coats the floor in the shaft-light. A few strands of green filamentous algae grow near the top of the shaft where the light is best, waving in the up-draft of the room's exhale. On the lamp's glass dome, where warmth and light concentrate, there is a small circular lawn of green, the only place in the room where anything photosynthetic grows thickly. The crayfish graze it at night when the lamp is dark.

## What the place is

The Low Hall is not a ruin in the usual sense, because it has not stopped doing its job. It was built to take water from a height, turn a wheel, and make light, and it still does all three. It does them badly, inefficiently, with a grinding knock and a flickering filament, but it does them. The builders are gone. The machine has become a kind of weather in the room, the source of the current, the noise, the seasons, and the light, and the animals have fitted themselves into it the way river animals fit themselves around a waterfall.

Eventually, the shaft will finish wearing through its bearing, or the jammed gate will finally rust shut, or the dam above will fail and the whole valley will drain. Then the runner will stop, the lamp will go dark for good, and the silt will settle into a floor, and the Low Hall will become quiet for the first time since it was poured.

It has not happened yet. Right now the runner is turning. *Whum.* The lamp brightens. The minnows turn together in the green light. *Whum.* Somewhere under the casing, the catfish waits.
