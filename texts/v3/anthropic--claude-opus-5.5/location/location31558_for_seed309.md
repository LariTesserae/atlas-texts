# Stenning Deep

## The Descent

There is no road down. There was once, a cut switchbacking into the hillside with a narrow-gauge line on it, and you can still find the rails if you dig. But the cut has closed over like a healed wound, and the way in now is a shaft, a round brick throat forty feet across. Its walls are furred so thickly with root and moss that the brick shows only where something has scraped it: a claw, a wing, the steady friction of millions of bodies passing.

You hear the place before you see it. At the lip of the shaft there is a sound like surf, rising and falling on a long interval. A little lower it separates into parts. There is a deep, slow *thud* you feel in your sternum more than hear. Over it runs a sheeted hiss, and over that a high glittering racket that never quite stops. By the time the shaft opens out into the cavern, the noise is so large it has texture. It sits on your skin, and the air is thick enough to chew.

## The Chamber

Stenning Deep is not big. From the foot of the shaft you could throw a stone across it, if a stone could get through the air without hitting something alive. It is a single cavern, roughly bowl-shaped, maybe three hundred yards end to end, with a ceiling that climbs to a cathedral height in the middle and pinches down to a crawl at the edges. Once it was a quarry-pit that broke into a natural void. Then it became a pumping station, a brickworks, and a single street of workers' cottages, all sunk beneath the hill, where the clay was good and the water had to be kept out.

The water was not kept out. Something else came in instead.

Every surface in the Deep is plant. I don't mean covered in plant, I mean made of it. The floor is a mat of root, springy and faintly warm, layered so deep that a crowbar driven down finds only more root, then more, then a darkness that smells of cellar and tea. The walls are root. The ceiling is root, hanging in ropes and curtains and braids, some thin as hair, some thick as a man's waist. It is a single organism, or behaves like one. The old workers called it the Fig, though it is no fig anyone would recognize. It is a strangler, and it strangled a town.

Its trunk, if the word applies, is the pumping house. You can still see the building's outline: the tall arched windows, the gable end, the square stack of the chimney climbing into the dark. All of it is sheathed, gripped, and replaced by a lattice of fused root. The original brick has long since crumbled to red powder inside its wooden cage. What stands now is a pumping house grown in wood, faithful to the shape of the thing it ate, down to the keystones over the windows. The keystones are knots.

## The Engine

It still runs.

That is the first strange thing, and the one you cannot stop hearing. Inside the grown pumping house is a Cornish beam engine, iron, forty feet from cylinder to pump rod, the great rocking beam balanced on its pivot like a seesaw for giants. The iron is almost gone. What is left is rust held in place by root, and the root has done more than hold it. The Fig has grown *into* the machine, through the valves, around the piston, up the plug rod, and in doing so it has learned the machine's shape. Over a century and a half it has replaced the engine part by part with living wood. Where there was iron, there is now dense, oily, pale heartwood, polished by its own motion.

The beam rocks once every forty seconds or so. It is not driven by steam. It is driven by the tree.

The Fig drinks from a seep at the bottom of the cavern, a sluggish, mineral-warm trickle that is the only standing water in the Deep. It draws that water up by root pressure, the ordinary botanical trick scaled up past reason. The pressure builds in a swollen chamber of tissue where the old boiler was, a bulb of wood the size of a cart, veined and faintly pulsing. At a threshold, a valve of green tissue gives. The pressure shoves the piston, the beam tips, and the whole cavern goes *thud*. Then the valve seals, the bulb begins to fill again, and the Deep takes a breath.

At the far end of the beam, the pump rod plunges down the old mineshaft and lifts water that nobody needs anymore. The water pours out of a wooden spout into a wooden launder, runs the length of the chamber, and dribbles back into the seep, where the tree drinks it again. It is a closed loop, a machine doing its work for no one, and it has been doing it so long it has become a metabolism.

The rhythm governs everything. The tree's growth comes in forty-second pulses, and you can watch a tendril extend a hair's width on each stroke. The animals move to it. The drips fall to it. When the valve gives, fine spray hisses out of a hundred leaks in the bulb, and for a moment the air near the engine house is a warm fog that settles on everything. Then it dries, slowly, until the next stroke.

## The Lamps

The Deep is dim, but it is not dark, and the light is industrial.

There was a gasworks here, a small one, feeding lamps along the cottage street and in the engine house. The gasholder is still there, a squat cylinder in a cage of root. It is fed now by the rot of the Fig's own fallen matter. A century of leaf-drop, dead root, droppings, and carcasses mounds up against the gasholder's base in a compost heap two storeys high. It ferments, and the gas it gives off finds its way through cracked mains into the old iron lampposts, which the tree has also grown around and copied.

So the street is lit. Twenty-odd lamps, spaced wide apart, burn with a low blue-and-orange flame that stutters when the pressure drops. Each makes a small, soft globe of visibility, and between the globes lies a thick brown darkness. The flames never go out. No one has lit them in a hundred and forty years. They are the dimmest possible version of a city at night, a few isolated coals of civilization in a room full of wet shadow, and every one of them is a theatre of hunting.

## The Living Cloud

The fauna of Stenning Deep is not a population. It is a condition of the air.

Start with the moths. They come to the lamps, as moths do, in densities that make the flames flicker, a turning cone of powdery wings around each post, thousands deep. Their dust coats the lamp glass, and the tree has learned to grow its lamp-housings with fine hairs that catch it. Whether it eats the dust or merely tolerates it, no one knows.

Then the swiftlets. They nest in the chimney, the grown wooden stack of the engine house, whose inner flue is lined floor to crown with half-cup nests of hardened saliva, so many that the flue has narrowed to a crack. They are small, dark, and never still. They navigate by clicking, a dry rattle like a fingernail on a comb, and there are so many of them that the clicking fuses into the glittering high noise you heard from the surface. They hunt the moths at the lamps, stitching through the cones of wings, and their clicks bounce off the root walls and come back doubled.

Bats share the upper dark with them. Several species keep strict hours, and they do not so much fly as pour. The ceiling holds them in clusters that look like fruit, and the clusters crawl. Under the densest roosts, the root floor is black with a guano layer that crunches, warm to the touch and alive with beetles.

The beetles are the floor's second skin. The commonest is a dull bronze scarab about the size of a fingernail, and they turn the guano and leaf-fall and deliver it, eventually, to the gasholder heap. They move in lanes, worn paths through the root mat, and the paths lead so unerringly to the fermentation mound that the workers' old joke was that the gasworks had hired them. Among them are the predators: long, pale centipedes that lie flat in the root-cracks and uncoil into the traffic, and wolf spiders whose eyes throw back lamp light in green pinpricks by the thousand, a second, lower constellation under the lamps.

And the crickets. Cave crickets, spindle-legged and blind, clot in the corners of the cottages. They chirr, and their chirring locks to the engine stroke, so that on each *thud* a wave of cricket-song rolls outward from the engine house and breaks against the far wall.

All of this is loud. It is louder than a working factory floor. The sound is the air itself: engine beat, spray-hiss, the comb-rattle of swiftlets, the wind of bat wings, cricket-surf, the patter of droppings, and the endless low crackle of a billion small feet on dry leaf. There is no silence anywhere in the Deep, except for one place, which comes later.

## The Street

The workers' cottages stand along what was Pumphouse Row. There were twelve of them once. Now there are seven, set far apart along a broad, curving strip of root-mat that used to be cobbled. The gaps between them are wide and dark and heavy with moth-drift. The Deep is crowded with life but empty of things. The seven cottages feel like the last sentences of a letter that trailed off.

They have been eaten like the pumping house, sheathed and hollowed and replaced. Their shapes are precise: doorsteps, window-ledges, a boot-scraper in wood beside a door, a drainpipe grown as a hollow liana. Inside, the tree has done something harder to explain. It has grown the furniture. There are chairs of root in the positions where chairs stood. There are beds, with pillows in pale, soft pith. There is a kitchen range whose iron has been replaced so faithfully that the little decorative scrollwork on the oven door is there in grain. A kettle sits on it, wooden, full of water the tree keeps topped up by condensation. The kettle is warm. It is always warm.

This is where the silence is. Inside the cottages, the noise of the Deep drops away. The walls are thick with root, and the tree seems to keep them dense on purpose, so that stepping through the doorway is like pressing your head into a pillow. Even the crickets are quiet here, and the moths do not come in. The rooms are dim and still and smell of warm wood and something like bread. It is very hard, standing in one, not to feel that you are expected.

The strangest of the seven is at the end of the row, where the ground slopes toward the seep. It is new. There was no cottage there on any plan. The Fig grew it, from nothing, sometime in the last few decades, following the pattern of the others: door, windows, chimney, boot-scraper. It is a younger green and less weathered, the windows filmed with a thin living membrane where glass would be. Inside, the furniture is still forming. A chair stands half-grown, legs and seat but no back yet, and each engine stroke lengthens the tendrils rising where the back will be. On the range, the kettle is only a bud.

Nobody knows who it is for.

## Weather

The Deep has weather, small and local, driven by the engine and the tree.

Every forty seconds, the spray. Every few hours, when the mound shifts and the gasworks surges, the lamps flare brighter all at once and a hot, sweet stink rolls through the chamber. The moths go frantic, and the swiftlets feed in a frenzy that doubles the noise. Twice a day there is rain. Condensation gathers on the ceiling roots until the whole underside of the roof is beaded, and then, in a single slow shudder, perhaps triggered by the accumulated weight or by some decision of the Fig's, it lets go. Warm, silty drops fall across the whole chamber for about a minute. The beetles close their wing-cases. The bats shake themselves in their clusters, a noise like a flock of umbrellas opening. Then it stops, and the root floor steams.

The great weather is the Exodus. At dusk, measured by some signal no one has identified since there is no sun here, the bats leave. They go up the shaft in a spiral, a column of wings a million strong, and the roar of their passage drowns even the engine. For twenty minutes the Deep is half-empty and strangely calm, lamp light suddenly clearer. The moths drift and settle. The swiftlets go to their nests. Then, just before the column's tail has cleared the shaft, the swiftlets start their own ascent, clicking, into the night above.

At dawn, all of it comes back down.

## The Terms

A few things are true of Stenning Deep, in the way that laws are true.

The engine never stops. If it slowed, something would go wrong with the tree, and the tree is what holds the roof up. There are places where old brick-dust rains from between the roots when the beam comes down hard, and the Fig answers within days by thickening the root there. The whole cavern is braced in wood, and the wood is braced by rhythm.

The tree copies what it holds. Given iron, it grows iron-shapes. Given a house, it grows a house. Its imitation is not ornamental. The grown engine works, the grown lamps light, the grown kettle stays warm. Whatever it learned from the town, it learned as function.

The animals keep the tree, and the tree keeps them. Beetles feed the gasholder, the gasholder lights the lamps, the lamps gather the moths, the moths feed the birds and bats, the birds and bats drop guano, the guano feeds the beetles and the tree. The engine pumps water through all of it. It is an economy with no money and no management, running at full production, and its product is itself.

And the tree is still growing a house.

## Leaving

When you climb back up the shaft, the noise thins by layers. First the crickets fall away, then the hiss, then the high glitter of the swiftlets. Last of all is the engine beat, which stays in your chest long after you've stopped hearing it. For days afterwards you catch yourself counting to forty.

Somewhere down there, on each stroke, a wooden chair is growing its back.
