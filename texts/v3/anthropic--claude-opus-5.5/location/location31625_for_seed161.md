# The Crown of Haum

## Approach

You come up out of the cloud.

For days the mountain has been nothing but slope. There is grey scree and then grey scree again. The mist below is wet and cold, and it clings to your clothes. Then the mist thins, and you step through its upper surface as if climbing out of a lake. The cloud sea lies behind you, white and unbroken to every horizon. The air here is thin and completely dry. Your lips crack within the hour. The sky overhead is a deep, bruised blue, almost violet at the zenith, and the sun seems very small.

Ahead is the summit. It is not a peak. Whatever peak this mountain once had is gone, and has been gone for longer than anything else you will ever see. The rock has been worn down by wind, frost and grit through ages without number. What remains is a broad, low dome, smooth as a knuckle. The stone is a rust-and-charcoal colour, polished in places to a dull sheen, pitted in others like old bread. Nothing sharp survives up here. Every edge has been rounded and every cliff laid back into a slope. The whole summit is a shallow shield of rock, tilted slightly toward the east.

In the middle of the dome is a darkness. It is a stain of green so deep it reads as black. It is maybe four hundred paces across, and from it rises a haze of smoke and a sound.

## The Sound

The sound reaches you before anything else does. Across the bare dome it arrives as a single thick murmur, like surf. As you get closer it separates into its parts:

- **Birds.** Tens of thousands of swifts wheel over the green in a loose, screaming column, pouring in and out. Their shrieks are high and continuous and overlap until they become texture.
- **Insects.** Underneath the birds runs a steady resinous buzz, rising and falling in waves. This is the beetles, and there are more of them than of anything else here.
- **Stone.** Rhythmic knocking and grinding: querns, mallets, chisels. Many are going at once, never quite in time.
- **Voices.** People calling across short distances, children shrieking, someone singing the same four notes over and over. A dog barking at the swifts.
- **Breath.** Beneath everything is a low, uneven exhalation, like a large animal asleep. This is the ground itself.

It is loud. It is not deafening, but it never pauses. The people who live here speak in raised voices even when they are being tender, and they find silence frightening. They will tell you so.

## The Bowl

The green is a hollow. It was once the throat of the volcano. It has long since slumped and filled, and it is now a gentle basin, perhaps thirty feet below the level of the dome at its deepest. The rim is a lip so worn you could step over it without noticing. Nobody steps over it carelessly, though, and the reason is the first rule of the place: **the lip is where the dry world ends and the kept world begins.**

The volcano is almost dead. It is not entirely dead. From cracks in the basin floor, very old heat still rises. It does not come as steam, because there is no water left in this rock to make steam. It comes as dry, warm air, faintly mineral, with a trace of sulfur and something sweetish underneath. The vents breathe irregularly. One sighs for a minute and goes quiet for an hour. Another has breathed steadily for as long as anyone's grandmother's grandmother can remember. This warmth pools in the bowl, trapped under the canopy. On the bare dome outside, the night frost splits stone. Inside the bowl, it is always warm, a little like a room where bread has recently been baked.

The warmth is why anything lives here. The frost is why anything drinks.

## The Plants

The canopy of Haum is made of one kind of plant, grown into many shapes over an immense span of time. The people call it *aum*, and they named the town after it, or the other way round. Nobody is sure.

An aum is a rosette. Its leaves are thick as a thumb, leathery, grey-green on top and silvery-furred beneath. Each rosette sits at the end of a woody stem, and the stems branch, fork and fork again. Over centuries a single aum becomes a low, spreading dome of hundreds of rosettes, held up on a gnarled trunk as wide as a house. Their crowns interlock. From above, the bowl is one continuous roof of leaves, and almost no direct light passes through.

This is why Haum is dim. Under the aum it is a permanent late dusk: a greenish, brownish half-light, filtered and re-filtered. Midday comes through as a scatter of small bright coins moving slowly across the ground. Sometimes a single hard shaft stands where a rosette has died and not yet been replaced. People gather in those shafts to do fine work, and they argue over who gets them.

The aum drinks frost. Each night, out on the bare dome and on the upper canopy, the thin air gives up what little moisture it holds as hoarfrost. It collects most of all in the furred undersides of the aum leaves, which are shaped to catch it. At dawn the frost melts in the rising warmth of the vents, and the leaves take it in. That is all the water there is, a few mouthfuls per rosette per night. The aum hoards it. Its stems and trunk are full of a thick, clear, faintly bitter sap. There are no springs in Haum, no wells, and no rain that anyone has seen. **Every drop of water in the town has passed through a plant.**

Under and among the aum grows everything else the aum permits:

- **Cushion-herbs**, dense and springy, carpeting the gaps between roots in hummocks of tiny grey-green leaves. They smell of resin and pepper when crushed.
- **Stone-ivy**, which climbs every wall and pillar and wraps them in waxy, coin-sized leaves. It is part of why the buildings look grown rather than built.
- **Bellflower vines**, dangling from the canopy in ropes of pale, bitter blossoms. The beetles live for them.
- **Lichens** in astonishing variety: orange crusts, black tar-spots, pale green beards, and yellow dust on the north faces of stones. Some of these are among the oldest living things in the world, and they grow slower than anything else. A lichen patch the size of a palm on the old temple wall is said to have been that size in the founding stories. It probably was.

The ground itself is not soil in any ordinary sense. It is a deep, fine, dark litter made of fallen leaf, beetle-frass, bird droppings, ash, dust blown up from the lowlands, and the powder of the rock. It has accumulated over ages in the warm, dry, sheltered bowl. It is soft underfoot and slightly warm. It smells like a spice cupboard. In places it is taller than a person. Nobody knows how deep it goes, because nobody has ever dug to the bottom.

The bowl is lush, but it is a dry lushness. It is thick, waxy, furred, resinous and slow, a growth that has had forever to fill every available space and has done so.

## The Animals

Haum is crowded with life, and most of it did not ask permission to be there.

**The swifts** are the loudest. They are small, dark, sickle-winged birds that nest in the upper walls, in the canopy, and in every crevice of the old stone. They feed on the insects that swarm over the aum and on the drifting insect life blown up from the cloud sea, which they hunt in great sweeping flights out over the white. At dusk they return all at once, in a vortex that roars down into the bowl like water down a drain. It is the loudest moment of the day. Children stand in the lanes and let the swifts miss them by a hand's width. The swifts' droppings are collected, dried and traded as fertilizer and as a component of plaster.

**The beetles** are the most numerous. They are bronze-backed and thumb-sized, with a buzz you feel in your teeth. They crawl over the aum in shimmering sheets, feeding on bellflower nectar and on the aum's own sap where the bark is cracked. Their larvae live in the deep litter and churn it endlessly. The beetles are why the litter never compacts. They are also a staple food, eaten roasted on hot stones by the vents, and crunchy.

**Rock-rabbits**, which are not rabbits, are round, short-eared, sand-coloured animals the size of loaves. They live in the stone walls and the foundations of houses. They whistle to each other constantly, a sharp two-note call that is part of the town's background noise. They are half-tame. They eat cushion-herb and fallen leaves and anything left unguarded. Every household has its own colony, and the rabbits are considered to belong to the house they live under.

**Bats** come out when the swifts go in, and the handover at dusk is a riot in the air. The bats are small and brown, and they cling in clusters to the undersides of the aum rosettes by day. There they look like dried seed pods. They are ignored until they fall on someone.

There are also:

- **Geckos** on every warm wall, grey and splay-footed, clicking.
- **Moths** that come to firelight in such numbers that lamps are kept screened with fine-woven leaf.
- **Spiders** strung between the stone-ivy, whose webs catch the frost at dawn. People collect the frost from them in small cups in the early morning, a delicacy and a ritual.
- **A single species of large lizard**, slow, heavy and orange-flecked, that basks at the vents. It is considered sacred, or at least is never harmed, and it wanders through houses as it pleases. Nobody knows how long they live. There are about thirty. They are all named.
- **Dogs**, a small, wiry, bat-eared breed that the people brought with them. They serve mostly to bark at the swifts and to keep the rock-rabbits honest.

## The Town

Haum is small. You could walk from one side to the other in the time it takes to eat a meal. It is also dense past the point of reason. Every part of the bowl not occupied by an aum trunk is occupied by building, and much of what is occupied by aum trunks is built around, against and into.

The houses are stone. The volcano provided the stone long ago in the form of pumice, scoria and a dense grey lava. All of it has been quarried from the bowl floor and re-used, then re-used again. The pumice is light and easy to shape with harder stone tools. The grey lava is used for thresholds, querns and hearths. Walls are dry-laid, then plastered with a mixture of ash, swift-droppings and aum sap. The plaster sets hard and waxy and takes the colour of the stone beneath it. The oldest walls are almost black with lichen and age.

The houses are built on houses. When a building fails, it is not cleared. It is filled with litter and rubble, and a new house is built on top. The oldest parts of Haum are therefore below the newest. In places you can see the layers where a lane has cut down through them. There are foundations on foundations on foundations, in slightly different styles of stonework, going down into the dark litter. Some families live in rooms with doorways halfway up the wall, because those doorways once opened at ground level. A few people keep the old underground rooms as cool stores, shrines or places for the dead.

The town has no single plan, but it has an order:

- **The lanes** are narrow, often barely shoulder-wide, and they wind between house-walls and aum trunks. Many are roofed by the canopy. Some are roofed by the houses themselves, where upper storeys have been built out across the lane to meet the house opposite. Walking through Haum is a matter of passing through passages, courtyards, tunnels and half-rooms. The line between street and home is thin and often ignored.
- **The vent-courts** are small open spaces around the larger vents, paved with grey lava worn smooth. Here people cook, dry things, warm themselves on cold nights and gather to talk. Each court belongs loosely to the houses around it. The steadiest vent has the largest court, called simply *the Breath*. That is where the town meets, where disputes are settled, and where the orange lizards gather in greatest number.
- **The tapping-houses** are built directly around the largest aum trunks. The trunks are the water, and the water is everything. Aum sap is drawn by cutting small, shallow notches into the bark and fitting them with bone or stone spouts. It drips into clay jars. It must be done slowly. An aum overtapped will sicken and take a generation to recover. Every trunk has a keeper, and the keepers know each tree's temper. They talk to the trees. They say the trees talk back, in the way the sap runs. Tapping-house doors are always open and always watched.
- **The terraces** step down the inside of the bowl's lip. They are ancient, low, stone-faced shelves of litter, where the people grow the few things that are not aum: a starchy tuber, a pungent onion, a bitter grain. These are watered with sap, one cupful at a time, and fertilized with swift-droppings and beetle-frass.
- **The old temple** stands near the centre, or what is left of it. It is the oldest standing structure. Its walls are carved with patterns so worn you can only feel them with your fingers, not see them. Nobody remembers its original purpose. It is used now as a granary, a meeting-house, and a place to go when you want to be alone, although you never are.

The tools are simple. There are obsidian blades, lava hammers, bone awls, and scrapers of hardwood from the aum's own heart. There is some copper, traded up through the cloud from the lowlands in small amounts: pins, fish-hooks that are used for other things, and a few precious chisels passed down through families. The pottery is dense, dark and unglazed, and it is excellent. Cloth is woven from the fibres of dead aum leaves, and from the silky fur on their undersides, which is combed out, spun and dyed with lichen.

## The People

There are a few hundred of them, perhaps more. They live on top of one another, and they are loud with each other because they have to be. They are small, lean and dark-skinned, and many have heavily furrowed palms from a lifetime of stonework and sap-tapping. Their lungs and hearts are adapted to the thin air. They find the lowlands wet, heavy and suffocating. The few who go down to trade come back quickly.

They are careful with water in a way that shapes everything. Cups are small, and a cup is always finished. Washing is done with fine ash and a rub of oiled leaf. Cooking is by roasting, drying and baking on hot vent-stones. Nothing is boiled, because boiling wastes. Tears are thought of as a kind of gift, and wiping them away is considered slightly rude.

They have a few rules they will tell you about:

1. **Nothing is thrown over the lip.** The dome outside is dead rock, frost and wind. Whatever goes there is lost. Everything the town uses stays in the bowl, as refuse or as litter or as the next generation's foundation.
2. **No aum is cut down.** Branches may be pruned. Dead rosettes may be taken. Sap may be tapped with care. But the trees are older than the houses, the temple, and the families. They are the reason there is a town. When an aum dies, which is rare, the whole town mourns, and the trunk is left standing until it rots, which takes centuries.
3. **The Breath must never be covered.** The steadiest vent is kept open to the air, and nothing may be built over it. It is the warmth of the town, and there is a vague, old fear that covering it would anger it or choke it.
4. **The dead go to the canopy.** They are wrapped in aum-fibre cloth and carried up into the crowns of the old trees. There they are laid among the rosettes, where the dry, warm air and the resin preserve them slowly. The beetles and the birds take what they take. After a generation, what remains is gathered and placed in the underground rooms of the old houses. So the ancestors are overhead, then underfoot, and never outside.

## Time

Almost nothing in Haum happens quickly.

The aum grow slowly. A rosette takes years to open fully, and a trunk puts on a finger's width in a lifetime. The litter builds up slowly, a few grains a year, and the houses rise with it. The lichen spreads slowly. The vents change slowly: one dies over a century, another opens over another. The cooking courts shift to follow them across generations. People speak of a house as being "near the old Breath" and mean a vent that has been cold for three hundred years.

The days have their rhythm. Frost comes at night on the canopy. Dawn brings the melting and the drinking, when the leaves turn briefly dark and glossy and the air smells, very faintly, of wet. By mid-morning the leaves are grey again. Then come the long dim day of work and noise, and the swift-storm at dusk. The bats follow, then the moths and the lamps and the fires by the vents. Voices carry across courts late into the warm night. Through all of it runs the hum of the beetles, the breath of the ground, and the knocking of stone on stone.

The mountain is so old that it has nearly stopped being a mountain. Given time, the dome will wear down further, the bowl's lip will vanish, and the last heat will leave the rock. On that timescale Haum is a brief flicker, a warm green patch on the summit of something that was once enormous and is now almost gone.

To the people inside it, though, the bowl is the world: warm, dim, crowded, loud, and very slowly deepening. Around them lies a dead dome of rock. Around that lies a sea of cloud, and past the cloud lies nothing they particularly need.
