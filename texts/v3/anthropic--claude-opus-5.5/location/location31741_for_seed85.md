# The Stillwater at Kessra Col

## Approach

The mountain is young. It has not had time to become rounded. Its ridgelines are knife-edges of dark andesite and black glass, fractured along clean planes the way a struck stone fractures. The faces still shine where they broke. At a little over four thousand meters, just beneath the summit, the rock opens into a caldera no wider than a large town square. The caldera is full of water.

That fact alone would be unremarkable: mountain lakes are common. But the water does not stop at the caldera's rim. It stands above it, a clear, dark lens rising another two hundred meters into the thin air. It has no container that can be seen and no visible edge except the faint shimmer where water meets sky. The shape is not quite a dome. It is closer to a teardrop lying on its side, bulged toward the north. Wind moves across its surface and does not raise a ripple. Snow falls onto it and does not melt or sink. The flakes rest on the skin of the water like dust on a tabletop, and in the morning they are gone. No one has seen where they go.

Inside the water there is a small city.

## The Water

You notice first that it is perfectly clear.

Ordinary seawater and lake water are full of particles: silt, plankton, the ghosts of dead things, dissolved color. This water has none of that. Light entering from above does not scatter or turn blue with depth. It simply thins. At the surface the day is present but muted. Ten meters down it has faded to the grey of late dusk. Forty meters down it is night, and it has not grown dark gradually the way a room darkens. The light has been spent, like a coin passed from hand to hand until nothing is left.

The temperature is six degrees Celsius. It is six degrees everywhere: at the top of the lens, at the caldera floor three hundred meters below, against the black rock of the walls, inside the cracks. There is no thermocline and no warmer layer near the surface. The water has no gradients of any kind. It is equally salty, equally cold, and equally pressurized everywhere. That last fact is the first sign that something is deeply wrong, or deeply deliberate. You can swim to the caldera floor and your ears will not hurt. The weight of water above you has been cancelled or redistributed so that it presses on nothing.

Because there are no gradients, there are no currents. The water does not move at all. When a hand passes through it, it parts and closes again. The disturbance does not spread outward. It stops within a hand's width, as if the water refuses to pass motion along to its neighbors. A swimmer leaves no wake.

Things placed in the water stay where they are put. A stone released at arm's length hangs at arm's length. It does not sink or drift. Buoyancy, like pressure and temperature, has been settled. This is the rule the whole place rests on. Nothing here falls. Nothing rises. Nothing is carried anywhere.

## The Rock

Because nothing moves, nothing erodes.

The caldera walls look exactly as they did when the rock cooled and cracked and the water came in. Every conchoidal fracture in the obsidian is still razor-sharp. Columnar joints stand in exact hexagons, their edges unblunted. Loose scree sits on slopes steeper than scree can naturally hold, because it has no reason to slide. In places a boulder rests half a meter above the ground it was falling toward when the water arrived. You can pass a hand through the gap beneath it.

The mountain outside is weathering the normal way. Frost splits it, wind scours it, and it is slowly becoming older. Inside the lens, the rock is permanently new. It is a moment of geology preserved in cold, clear water.

There is no life on the rock. There is no algae, no film, no crust of bacteria, no lichen at the waterline, because there is no waterline in the usual sense. The stone is as clean as the day it formed, and it shows the colors of fresh volcanic glass: black, bottle-green where it is thin, and a faint oily iridescence along fracture faces. When you shine a light on it, the light reflects back with nothing between you and the surface to soften it. The shine is almost painful.

## The City

It is not large. The whole settlement could be crossed on foot in ten minutes, if you could walk here. Instead you drift, pulling yourself along the guide-lines the builders left: thin filaments of some grey material stretched in straight lines between the buildings. They do not sag, because nothing here sags.

There are perhaps forty structures, widely spaced across the caldera floor and up the lower walls. Most are towers. They are tall and narrow, and each looks as if it was cut from a single piece of the same black glass as the mountain. The cutting was done by a method that left no tool marks. Their surfaces are faceted like the natural fractures of the stone, so at first you cannot tell where the mountain ends and the architecture begins. Some towers rise directly out of the caldera wall, attached at the base and continuous with the living rock. Others stand free on the floor. Several are not attached to anything at all. They hang in the water at various heights, perfectly vertical, some with their bases forty meters above the ground.

The towers have openings but no glass in them, because no glass is needed. The water inside a room is the same as the water outside. There are no doors in the ordinary sense, only apertures. There are no stairs, and at first this seems strange until you remember that the builders would have simply risen and stopped wherever they liked. The interiors are divided by floors that serve no structural purpose. They may have been meant for organization, or for the comfort of having a surface underfoot.

Inside, objects remain.

In one room there is a bowl at the height of a table, but there is no table. The bowl simply rests in the water at the height where someone set it down. Beside it, a small ring of dull metal hangs a few centimeters to the left, as if it had just been taken off. In another tower, hundreds of thin plates of the grey filament-material are arranged in a slow spiral, each one exactly where it was placed. The pattern could be a library or a sculpture or the working parts of a machine. In a third, a single strand of something like hair or fiber stretches half the width of a room, held in a curve it should not be able to keep.

None of it is covered in dust, because there is no dust to settle. Everything is exactly as it was left. There is no way to know how long ago that was. The absence of decay makes the past unreadable. It might have been a year or ten thousand years.

## The Light

The city is dark, but not completely dark.

Several towers contain what the builders seem to have used as lamps. These are small nodes of pale material, roughly the size of a closed fist. Each gives off a cold, steady white glow. Because the water has no particles, their light does not diffuse. It does not form a halo or fill a room. Each lamp illuminates only the surfaces its rays actually strike, and the space between stays black. You see a lit patch of wall, a bright edge of a floor, and the side of a hanging bowl, all separated by darkness, like fragments of a room lying in an empty sea.

Some of the lamps cast beams instead of glowing in all directions. A beam leaves one tower, crosses thirty meters of open water, and strikes the base of another. From the side, the beam is invisible. With nothing in the water to catch the light, you cannot see the ray itself, only its starting point and its end. Two points glow in the darkness, one on each tower, and your mind draws the line between them that your eye cannot.

A few of the beams end in open water. They reach a certain point and stop, and there is no surface there to receive them. It is as if the light has simply been told where to finish. If you move your hand into that space, your fingers light up. Pull your hand back and the light is gone again. No one knows what the beams are for. They have been shining without flicker or dimming for as long as anyone has been coming here to look.

## Sound

The water carries sound poorly, and there is very little sound to carry.

What you hear most is your own body: the soft pulse of blood in your ears, the rasp of breath through whatever apparatus lets you breathe down here. The water does not creak or gurgle. There are no bubbles, because nothing here rises. The rock does not groan, because there is no pressure or heat pushing on it.

There is one sound that is not yours. Every so often, at intervals long enough that you begin to doubt it, a single low note moves through the water. It is felt as much as heard, a faint pressure in the chest. It does not seem to come from anywhere in particular. It swells and fades over about three seconds and leaves no echo. Some visitors believe it comes from the free-floating towers. Others think it is the lens itself, the invisible machinery holding the water in shape, making a small correction. Visitors who have timed it say the interval is not regular. It follows a pattern too long to hold in memory, one that may repeat over years.

## The Creatures

Something lives here. It is not plant and not quite animal, and there is very little of it.

The most common is a long, pale, ribbon-like creature about the length of a forearm and nearly transparent. It moves by rippling along its whole length. Because the water will not carry motion, it cannot glide. It must push against the water continuously, and the moment it stops, it stops completely, suspended wherever it happens to be. You find them hanging motionless in the dark between towers. For most of their lives they do nothing at all, and you cannot tell whether they are resting, sleeping, or dead. Then one will suddenly move, crossing a few meters in a slow, deliberate undulation, and stop again.

No one has seen them eat. There is nothing here to eat: no plankton, no plants, no organic matter in the water. Some visitors believe they feed on the light, drifting into the invisible beams and taking something from them. It is true that they are most often found near the lamps.

There is a second creature, rarer still. It is a small, jointed thing with eight legs, about the size of a thumbnail and the same black as the glass. It walks on the surfaces of the towers and the caldera wall, gripping the stone, since walking is the only way to move here without constant effort. You can watch one crossing the face of a tower for an hour and it will travel less than a meter. They sometimes cluster around the objects left in the rooms, touching them with their front legs and then moving away, as if checking that each thing is still where it belongs.

Visitors have counted perhaps a dozen of the walkers in the entire city and fewer than fifty of the ribbons. Their numbers do not seem to change. No one has found their young, their dead, or any sign that they reproduce. They may simply be as permanent as everything else here.

## What It Is

The simplest explanation is that someone built a city in a lake at the top of a mountain, then stopped the lake.

The builders took the water and removed every gradient from it: temperature, pressure, salinity, motion, buoyancy. They reduced it to perfect equilibrium, and then they held it there. The city exists inside that stillness. Because it is not subject to erosion, decay, or change, it does not age.

What was it for? A refuge, a vault, a monument, an experiment? The objects left in the rooms suggest that people lived here, at least for a time. The towers floating above the floor suggest that the builders did not share our ideas about ground, falling, or up and down. The lamps and their beams suggest a purpose that has continued long after its makers left.

Perhaps it was never meant to be lived in. Perhaps it was built to preserve a single moment, the instant the mountain cooled and cracked, the instant someone set a bowl down in a room and took off a ring and turned away, and to keep that moment exactly as it was for as long as the equilibrium holds.

On the surface, far above, snow keeps falling on water that will not take it in. Around the lens, the mountain is growing old. Inside, the rock is new, the lamps are lit, a pale ribbon hangs motionless in a beam of light it cannot see, and a small black walker crosses a tower face at the speed of a slow thought. Nothing here has changed, and nothing is going to.
