# The Stridulant Tableland

## The Edge

The plateau announces itself long before you reach it, but not by sight. At first it's a pressure in the chest, a hum below hearing. Then, from the dry scrub country that surrounds it, you see a wall.

The wall is not stone. It is wood, or something close enough to wood that the word has to do. Along its whole visible length, from horizon to horizon, a cliff of grey-brown root rises some four hundred meters out of the lowland talus. It is fluted and grooved like the flank of an ancient cedar. Water and wind have worked it for longer than anyone has measured, so the grooves are smooth, deep enough to walk into, and worn to a sheen near the bottom. In places the root-cliff has split and healed, split and healed, until the scars form terraces and overhangs and long slanting ramps. The old rock of the plateau still shows here and there as dark basalt knuckles, gripped and half-swallowed. Most of it is gone. It has been digested, lifted, or replaced. What was once a mountain tableland is now a single living thing sitting where a mountain used to be.

The people who live on it call the climb *going up into the noise*.

## The Surface

At the top, the land opens flat.

It is flat the way a lake is flat, except that it is solid and green-black and it breathes. The surface is made of leaves, or leaf-scales. Each is roughly the size of a door, leathery, thick as a thumb, and laid in overlapping ranks like the scales of a pinecone flattened across a continent. They are waxy on top, pale and furred beneath, and they rise and fall very slightly, all together, on a cycle of a few minutes. Standing still, you feel the ground inhale.

The plain runs for hundreds of kilometers in every direction. Nobody has crossed it in a straight line and returned to say how far. It is not all one texture. In some regions the scales lie tight and glossy, a polished floor that throws back the sky. In others they have lifted and curled at the edges, forming shallow cups. In the oldest regions the scales have thickened over centuries into humped, bark-crusted mounds, like the knuckles of the cliff wall, and the walking there is slow.

What strikes a newcomer most, after the scale and the sound, is the emptiness. The vegetation is total, since there is literally no ground that is not plant. But almost nothing stands up from it. The plain is open. You can see for fifty kilometers, and within that distance there might be three things taller than a person:

- a **stalk-tower**, a single emergent stem as thick as a house and three or four hundred meters tall, ending in a crown of fleshy orange bracts;
- a **burl**, a swelling where the plant has grown around something (a boulder, a fallen tower, a dead animal of unknown size) and kept growing;
- very rarely, a **hut**.

Between these presences there is only the breathing floor, the shimmer of heat, and the cloud-shadows crossing.

## The Heat

It is hotter up here than it has any right to be.

The plateau sits high, and in the lowlands below the nights are cold. But the plant makes its own heat. You can feel it through your feet. The scales are warm to the touch at dawn, before the sun has done any work, and by midday the surface bakes. The air above it wavers so strongly that distant stalk-towers seem to wade.

The warmth is metabolic. Something in the deep tissue, the old root-mass that fills the space where rock used to be, is burning slowly, the way certain flowers warm themselves to spread their scent. The whole tableland is a smoldering body. At night it glows faintly in infrared to anything with the eyes for it, and there are things up here with the eyes for it.

Because of the heat, the plateau makes its own weather. Every day the air above the center rises in a single enormous column. By early afternoon a cloud has formed there: flat-bottomed, towering, anvil-topped, and permanently anchored. The tableland people call it the **Lid**. It never drifts. On a clear day you can see it from anywhere on the plain, a white mountain standing on a green one. Sometimes it rains out of the Lid onto the central region, a brief hammering downpour that lasts less than an hour. Sometimes it doesn't. The outer regions almost never see rain at all.

## The Water

This is a damp place, not a wet one. The water is always there, but it is always small.

At dusk, when the heat begins to fall off, the plant exhales. The breathing cycle slows and deepens, and from pores along the underside of the scales comes a warm, heavy vapor. Where it meets cooler air, it condenses. For an hour after sunset the whole plain steams gently. By full dark every curled scale-cup holds a few fingers of water. The water is clear, faintly sweet, and slightly resinous, and it tastes of what cut pine smells like.

That is the water you drink up here. There are no streams, springs, or wells, only these ten thousand million cups filling each evening and draining back through the scale-pores by midmorning. Travelers learn to walk at dusk and dawn and to drink as they go, dipping as they pass. In the old mound-country the cups are deeper and hold water all day, and animals gather there.

In the Lid's shadow it is wetter. The afternoon rains pool in long channels between scale-ranks, and there are true ponds, briefly, green and loud with larvae. They are gone within two days.

## The Sound

Nobody who has been up here describes the sound first. They describe it last, because they can't get to it.

It comes from under the scales. The leaf-floor is not solid all the way down. Beneath the surface ranks is a layer of hollow, spongy tissue, meters deep and honeycombed with chambers and passages. That layer is full of insects.

There are more of them than anyone can hold in their head. They are stridulators, heavy-bodied and hand-long, related perhaps to cicadas or crickets, though they are neither. Each one sits in its own resonant chamber in the spongy tissue and sings by drawing a ridged leg across a ridged wing. The chambers amplify it. The scales above act as soundboards. Across the whole tableland, the song rises.

It doesn't start at dawn. It starts about an hour after, when the scales have warmed. First comes scattered clicking, then a stuttering, then a rising sheet of noise that moves across the plain like a wave. Within twenty minutes of the first chirp it is everywhere. It is not a song anymore but a condition: a pressure in the skull, a buzz in the teeth, a physical force against the eardrums. You cannot hear yourself shout. You cannot hear someone shout into your ear. The air vibrates visibly over the warmest regions. Dust lifts in small standing ripples. Water in the scale-cups shivers into fine patterns of rings.

It goes on, rising and falling in slow swells, until the evening exhalation. Then it collapses in minutes to a thin, distant whine, and then to nothing. In the silence afterward, people cry sometimes without knowing why. The night is not silent either. It holds other sounds, smaller ones, but compared to the day it is the quiet at the bottom of a well.

Everyone on the tableland past the age of forty is partly deaf. Children learn a hand-language before they learn speech. The drum is the common instrument, not for music but for signal. A drum struck against a mound-knuckle sends a thud through the root-mass that can be felt, not heard, several kilometers away.

## The Strangeness

The singing is not incidental.

Watch the scales while the chorus peaks. They move. They don't respond to the breathing cycle, which is slow and uniform. They respond to the song: a flicker, a tilt, a rippling tremor that travels outward from wherever the chorus is loudest. The pores on the scale-undersides open wider during loud swells. The plant is listening, or the sound is doing something to it: shaking loose gases, driving circulation, pumping fluids up through the spongy layer in a way its own tissue cannot manage alone. The insects sing, and the plant breathes more deeply. The plant warms, and the insects sing.

The tableland people have noticed that the relationship goes further. Where the chorus falters, the plant closes. There are regions that have gone quiet: a fungal blight in the insect chambers, a bad dry year, a place where too many birds fed. In those regions the scales stop rising and falling. They grey. Over some years they harden, crack, and begin to peel. The quiet regions become the only true wounds on the plain, patches of dead, silvery, brittle scale-crust that ring hollow underfoot. Eventually the living plain grows back over them from the edges, but it takes generations. On a tableland where everything else is noise, silence is how you know something is dying.

So the people up here do not wish for quiet. They fear it. When a region goes silent, they go there with drums and beat them into the ground from morning to night, for weeks, trying to keep the plant awake. Whether it works, no one agrees. They do it anyway.

A second strangeness goes deeper, and fewer people speak of it. The song is not uniform. It has structure: swells, falls, long slow patterns that repeat over days and move across the plain in consistent directions. Some of the old listeners, with the right kind of deafness, the kind that leaves the low frequencies and takes the high, claim they can feel the pattern through their bones. They say it is not random. They say it moves toward the Lid in the morning and away from it in the evening, like a tide, like something circulating. They say that sometimes a single swell will cross the entire tableland from one edge to the other without breaking, taking three days to do it, and that the whole living floor tilts slightly toward it as it passes.

They do not say what they think it is. They say that the plant is very old, and very large, and that it has had a long time to learn to use its voice.

## The Fauna

The singers are only the base of it.

Anything that can eat a hand-long insect has come up onto the tableland, and there is no shortage of food.

**Scale-shrikes** are small, fierce, iridescent birds that hunt by listening for silences. The instant a singer stops to molt or move, a shrike is on it, prying it out of its chamber with a hooked beak. Their flocks run into the tens of thousands, and they move across the plain in dark ribbons with the swells of the chorus.

**Walkers** are six-legged grazers the size of cattle, with broad padded feet. They graze the thickened mound-country and eat soft new scale-growth at the edges of healing wounds. They are nearly deaf, like everything else up here that lives long, and they are indifferent to people. Their herds are small and sparse, a few dozen scattered across a region the size of a small country, and you can see them from very far away because nothing else is tall.

**Borers** are the size of a dog and eyeless, with heavy pale heads. They tunnel through the spongy layer eating singers in their chambers. You know one is near when the chorus dies directly under your feet in a trail that moves.

The **Lid-swifts** live their whole lives in the air above the plain. They never land, eat singers that fly up during the breeding swarms, and drink from the Lid. On afternoons when the Lid rains, they come down out of it in a dark spiral you can see from a day's walk away, millions of them.

The **heat-eyes** hunt at night, when the singers are quiet and the scales still glow. Few have seen them clearly. People describe long, low bodies, many limbs, and faces with pits like snakes'. They seem to hunt walkers, and sometimes people who sleep on the open plain instead of inside burls.

Then there is the **swarm**. Once a year, sometimes twice, the singers breed. It happens region by region, spreading across the tableland over several weeks. On the day a region swarms, the song reaches a pitch beyond anything in ordinary days. Then the scales lift, all of them, a hand's width off the floor, and the singers come up out of the spongy layer into the air.

For about an hour the sky is not visible. The air is made of wings. The sound is physical enough to knock birds down. People caught out in it lie flat on the scales with their heads wrapped in cloth and wait. When it's over, the swarm has moved on or mated and fallen, and the plain is carpeted a finger deep in dead and dying singers. For a week afterward everything on the tableland eats well: the shrikes, the walkers, which will eat meat when offered, the heat-eyes, the people.

## The People

They are few, and they are spread thin across a very wide place.

They live in **burls**. A burl makes good shelter. Its wood is dense and dry, it is not part of the singing layer, and inside it is almost quiet, merely very loud. People hollow them with hand tools. Their adzes and scrapers are made of chitin from singer shells and walker bone, edged with black glass knapped from the basalt knuckles at the cliff edge. Obsidian is the most valuable thing on the tableland, because it can only be gotten at the rim, and the rim is far from nearly everywhere.

A burl-home is a single room, sometimes two, carved round and smooth, with a low door facing away from the prevailing wind. The walls are sealed with a boiled resin tapped from the scale-undersides. The resin hardens amber and keeps out the insects. Inside there is a sleeping shelf, a hearth, and a store of dried swarm-meat in resin-sealed baskets. Sometimes there is a drum.

There are perhaps a few thousand people across the whole of the tableland. There are no villages. Families live alone or in pairs of burls, days apart from each other. They move seasonally, following the healthiest singing, the deepest scale-cups, and the walker herds. Their marks on the plain are small and scattered:

- lines of notched chitin stakes pushed into the scale-joints to show routes to water;
- piles of swarm husks, bleached white, heaped beside burls as windbreaks;
- a burl, long abandoned, with its resin walls cracked and its doorway facing nothing;
- very rarely, at the edge of a silent region, a ring of drums left behind: old, split, weathered to grey, sitting in a circle on dead scale where someone once tried to wake the plant and then gave up or died.

They have no metal. They do not farm, since there is nothing up here to plant that the plant would not eat. They do not build anything taller than a burl or anything that cannot be carried. They take swarm-meat, water, resin, obsidian, and sometimes walker hides. They trade by meeting at the rim, where the obsidian is, once every few years, in a gathering where they communicate entirely by hand and drum, and where the lowland people sometimes climb up to meet them, sick and dizzy from the noise within an hour.

## The Day

**Before dawn.** The plain lies dark and warm. The scale-cups are brimming. A faint glow comes up from the ground itself, and the only sounds are the small ones: the drip of condensation, the click of a heat-eye's claws somewhere far off, the slow deep sigh of the breathing cycle. People drink. The walkers drink. The Lid is absent. It dissolves each night and re-forms each day.

**Dawn.** The sun comes up over a floor so flat that the light arrives sideways across the entire tableland at once. Every scale-cup catches it. For a few minutes the plain looks paved with fire. Then the heat begins.

**An hour after dawn.** The singers start. A scattered clicking, a rising wave, and then the full chorus.

**Midmorning.** The scale-cups drain. The shrikes come out in their ribbons. The air shimmers. Far off at the center, a small white knot of cloud appears where the Lid is forming.

**Noon.** The heat is enormous. The Lid has grown into a mountain. The chorus is at its peak, and anyone outside a burl is lying in shade or walking with cloth over their ears.

**Afternoon.** If it rains at the center, the Lid-swifts come down in their spiral. Somewhere, a swell crosses the plain, and the scales tilt toward it.

**Dusk.** The chorus collapses within minutes. The silence falls. People weep, or don't. The scales exhale their vapor, and the whole plain steams gold, then grey. The scale-cups fill. The heat-eyes wake.

**Night.** The plain glows. The breathing slows. And far off across the dark floor, faintly, carried through the root-mass rather than the air, someone is drumming at the edge of a silent place, trying to keep a piece of the world from going quiet.
