# The Hightable

## The Roof

The first thing you notice is the sky, because there is so little of it above you.

The Hightable is a plateau the size of a continent, raised so far above the rest of the world that most of the atmosphere lies beneath it. At noon the zenith is a deep indigo that slides toward black, and the brighter stars never quite leave. The sun is small, white, and hard-edged. It lights everything evenly and without warmth. Shadows here are sharp as cut paper, and a step out of the light feels like a step into another room.

Yet the place is warm, and the warmth comes from below. The crust beneath the Hightable is thin and old, and the planet's interior heat seeps up through it slowly, the way heat moves through the lid of a covered pot. Put your palm flat on the ground at dawn and the rock is warmer than the air, a steady blood-heat. The people who live here call the stone *the hearth* without meaning it as a metaphor. The warmth rises from the ground and escapes into the thin cold sky, and nearly everything alive or built on the Hightable depends on that crossing.

## Terrain

The plateau is not flat, though from a distance it looks it. It has been exposed to wind for longer than anyone can reckon, and it shows every stage of that long weathering.

The dominant forms are the **yardangs**, long streamlined ridges carved by the prevailing wind into shapes like overturned hulls. They run in parallel for hundreds of kilometres, all pointing the same way. Some are the height of a house. Others rise three hundred metres and stretch a day's walk end to end. Between them lie corridors of **desert pavement**: pebbles packed so tightly by millennia of deflation that the ground resembles a mosaic floor. The stones are glazed with a dark varnish that shines like old bronze in low light.

Every exposed rock has become a **ventifact**, faceted and polished by sand into smooth planes that meet at crisp edges. Whole boulder fields look deliberately cut. Travellers have mistaken them for ruins, and occasionally ruins have been mistaken for them.

Rising from all this, spaced at great distances, are the **mesas**. These are the high tables of the Hightable itself, remnants of an even older surface that everything else has been worn down from. Their flanks are banded in rust, bone-white, ochre, and a peculiar grey-violet. Their tops are flat and weather-scoured. Most are empty. Some hold cities.

Nothing here looks raw. There are no fresh scarps and no jagged talus. Everything has had time to settle into the shape the wind prefers. The land is not eroding so much as finished eroding, holding a form it reached long ago.

At the plateau's far margins the ground simply ends. The escarpment drops away in stepped cliffs into a permanent sea of cloud several kilometres below. No one on the Hightable has seen the lowlands. The cloud sea is white by day and bruise-coloured at dusk. On clear nights it glows faintly from beneath, and nobody here has explained why.

## Water, Almost None

There is no surface water. There is no rain, no dew, and no frost, because the ground is too warm and the air too dry. Water on the Hightable exists almost entirely *inside stone*.

The pale bands in the mesas are gypsum and related minerals, whose crystals hold water locked in their structure. That locked water is the only real reservoir on the plateau, and the cities exist because of it.

## The Cities

Settlement here is sparse. A city might be three, five, or ten days' travel from the next. Each one sits atop or within a mesa rich in hydrated mineral. From far off, a city shows itself as a thin plume of steam standing perfectly vertical in the still morning air, visible for a hundred kilometres.

The steam comes from the **kilns**. At the heart of every city is a mineral-roasting works: long galleries of retorts where quarried gypsum is baked and its water driven off as vapour. The vapour is then caught in towering condensers, stacked finned chimneys that radiate heat into the black sky until the vapour beads and runs down into cisterns. A city's wealth is measured in cistern-depth, and its age by how far its quarries have eaten into the mesa. The oldest cities are hollow. Their mesas have been mined into vast pillared halls, with the living city perched on a shelf of rock that grows thinner every century.

The kilns are fired by the hearth. Deep bores go down into the warm crust, and the heat is carried up through ceramic pipes filled with a heavy mineral oil. There is no combustion, since fuel is nearly nonexistent up here. The Hightable runs on *difference*: warm below, cold above, and machinery set in the gap between them.

That is the industrial character of the place. It is a civilisation of **heat engines**:

- **Differential engines** the size of cathedrals, whose pistons move once a minute, drawing power from the gradient between a deep bore and a radiating fin-field on the mesa top.
- **Flywheels** of banded stone, some forty metres across, turning in pits so slowly that the eye cannot see them move. They store the engines' work through the day and release it at night.
- **Updraft towers**, hollow stacks hundreds of metres tall built over heated collector-floors, with slow turbines in their throats. On the pavement plains they stand alone like chimneys left behind by vanished houses.
- **Cable lines** strung between mesas across the yardang country, some spans running tens of kilometres. Enclosed cars creep along them at walking pace, taking days to arrive. Along these cables the cities trade water, glass, metal, and news.

Electricity is common. It hums quietly in copper and in something the cities call *lodestone wire*, and it lights the hollow halls in steady amber. But the machines are all slow and nearly silent. They have no need to hurry. The heat will still be there tomorrow, and it will still be there in a thousand years.

The buildings are stone, dense and low, and they sit close together on the mesa tops like barnacles. Most were cut from the mesa itself, so a city is often continuous with its own foundation. Walls grade into cliffs, and stairways turn into fissures. Roofs are covered in radiating fins, fields of pale ceramic blades angled to the sky. Seen from above, a city looks like a patch of frost-feathers on dark rock.

Glass is the other great product. Fused from the plateau's quartz sands in solar-and-hearth furnaces, it goes into windows, cisterns, and lenses. The largest cities have lens-towers, great clear eyes that focus the thin hard sunlight onto smelting floors. On certain afternoons a bright point sits at the top of such a tower, too intense to look at.

## Life Without Leaves

There are no plants here, not even the ghost of one. Nothing green has ever grown on the Hightable. Yet the plateau is crowded with animals, and working out how took its inhabitants centuries.

The bottom of the food web has two sources.

**The varnish.** The bronze sheen on every pavement stone is a living film, a crust of chemical-eating microbes that draw energy from the manganese and iron in the rock and from the faint warmth rising through it. It is thinner than paint and grows immeasurably slowly, but it covers the entire plateau. Taken together, that is an enormous amount of food.

**The updraft.** Warm ground under thin air makes the Hightable a vast thermal engine of its own. Air rises constantly from the heated pavement, and the escarpment edges draw columns of lowland air up out of the cloud sea. That lowland air carries **aeroplankton**: spores, pollen, drifting mites, tiny winged things, and organic dust from a living world no one here has seen. The Hightable is fed from below, in every sense.

On these two foundations rests an abundance of fauna:

- **Pavers.** Low, broad, disc-shaped grazers the size of dinner plates. They inch across the pavement on muscular undersides, scraping off varnish with mineral-hardened teeth. Their shells are layered with the metals they concentrate from their diet, iron, manganese, and traces of copper, and in low light they gleam like dropped coins. Herds of hundreds move through the yardang corridors so slowly that a herd seems to be one more feature of the ground. Behind them they leave pale trails where the varnish has been eaten away. The trails take decades to darken again, and from the air the plains are scribbled with them.

- **Shell-smelters.** The cities herd pavers. Their shells, gathered after natural death, are among the richest ore on the plateau, and whole industries rest on it. Paver-drives are slow affairs lasting seasons, with herders walking beside the animals for weeks. Metal on the Hightable is, quite literally, grown.

- **Thermal kites.** Wide, membranous fliers that ride the rising air in their thousands. They rarely beat their wings. They hang in the updrafts over heated pavement, sweeping aeroplankton from the air with fine combs along their leading edges. At midday the sky above the plains is dotted with them, stacked in slow spirals kilometres high, each turning in perfect silence.

- **Chimney-swarms.** The updraft towers concentrate aeroplankton, and so they concentrate everything that eats it. Towers are wreathed in clouds of small fast fliers, and their inner walls are lined with roosting colonies. Tower-keepers harvest guano from the collector floors. It is one of the few organic fertilisers on the Hightable, traded down the cables to the cities' sealed fungal cellars.

- **Stilt-hunters.** Tall, thin, many-jointed predators that stand motionless among the ventifacts and look so much like weathered stone that they are nearly invisible. They feed on pavers, piercing the shell seam with a single long mouthpart. They move perhaps once an hour. Travellers learn to count the "rocks" around them and count again later.

- **Steam-drinkers.** Around every city, drawn by the kiln-plumes, gather creatures that live on moisture: moths with feathered mouths, small scaled things that lap condensation from the fins, and slender birds that perch in rows along the condenser chimneys. Cities are oases, and every animal on the plateau seems to know the way to one.

- **The Reckoners.** Least understood of all are the Reckoners, large, slow, many-legged, and armoured in plates like weathered slate. They wander alone across the deep pavement, never in herds and never near cities. They do not seem to eat. Some believe they feed directly on the hearth-warmth, pressing their broad underbodies to the ground. They sometimes stand still for years. They sometimes arrange stones. Their arrangements are circles, spirals, and long straight lines, and they are always oriented to the same bearing as the yardangs. No one has seen a Reckoner die.

## Weather of Its Own

The Hightable is large enough to have its own climate, and that climate moves on its own schedule.

Beyond the daily cycle of thermals, the plateau has one great weather system: **the Drift**, a single immense standing dust storm, hundreds of kilometres across, that circles the plateau once every eleven years. It moves no faster than a person walks. Its approach can be seen for weeks as a tawny wall on the horizon, gradually swallowing the mesas one by one.

When the Drift passes over a city, the city seals itself. Its engines keep turning, and its people live for a month or two in amber-lit interiors while fine dust sifts over everything outside. When the Drift moves on, it leaves behind a new layer of sand and a scoured sky. It also leaves a great bloom of life, because the storm carries enormous amounts of lowland dust and aeroplankton. The seasons following a Drift are years of plenty. Paver herds swell, and kites darken the sky. A city's calendar is counted in Drifts.

## Sound

The Hightable is mostly quiet, because thin air carries sound poorly. Voices are small and flat and die within a few metres. A shout across a canyon arrives, if it arrives at all, as a faint thin note.

What you hear instead is close and quiet. There is the tick of stone expanding in the morning sun, and the soft hiss of sand moving along the pavement in the evening winds. Kite membranes flex with a faint papery flutter. A herd of pavers passing makes a steady grinding hush. Inside the cities there is the low, almost felt hum of the engines, too slow to be a rhythm, and the occasional deep knock of a piston reaching the top of its stroke. On still nights, the condensers make a faint musical dripping into the cisterns.

The loudest sound on the Hightable is the Drift. It arrives as a long, rising sigh, the sound of an entire continent's air turning over.

## The Rules of the Place

Some truths that everyone on the Hightable learns:

- **Nothing is fast.** The engines, the herds, the storm, and the cable cars all move at the pace the warm ground permits. Hurry is a lowland idea, and a foolish one.
- **Warmth rises; life lives in the rising.** Heat from below, the cold sky above, and everything built or born sits in the crossing between.
- **Water is stone.** To drink is to quarry. Every city is slowly eating the mountain it lives on, and every city knows it.
- **Metal is grown.** It comes from the herds, and the herds come from the varnish.
- **The edge is not the end.** Something lives below the clouds. Its dust feeds us. Its light glows at night. We do not go there.
- **Do not move the Reckoners' stones.**

## Dusk

Evening comes quickly. The small white sun drops toward the cloud sea, and the long shadows of the yardangs stretch across the pavement plains like the wake of a ship. The varnish catches the last light and burns briefly copper across a thousand kilometres. The thermals weaken. The kites spiral down and settle on the mesa flanks, folding their membranes around themselves like grey cloaks.

Stars fill the sky, and there are more than anyone below the clouds could imagine. In the cities, the amber lights come up in the stone halls. The flywheels, heavy with the day's stored work, begin their long release. On the dark plain, a stilt-hunter takes a single step.

Beneath all of it, as it always has, the ground stays warm.
