# The Undertube

The ground ends, and there is no cliff. The plateau above is a dry, wind-scoured sheet of black rock and thin grass, and in it lies an opening, a long oval of collapsed roof with its edges still sharp as broken pottery. Cool air rises out of it, and with that air comes a smell and a sound. The smell is ammonia and wet stone. The sound is a hiss that is really millions of small noises too close together to separate.

A slope of fallen rubble leads down through the collapse, blocks of basalt the size of carts wedged against one another, their faces unweathered and glassy. There has not been enough time here for anything to round off. The rock that fell is still in the angles it fell in. Below, the tube opens, and it keeps opening.

---

## The Shape of It

This is a lava tube, or several of them. They are the hollow veins a lava flow left behind when its crust cooled and the molten interior drained away downhill. In geological terms that was recent, a few thousand years ago at most. The flow came down a gentle grade and branched, rejoined, and branched again. Where three channels merged, the roof widened into a chamber so broad that its far walls are only a suggestion, a darker darkness past the reach of any light.

The main tube runs for kilometres. Its cross-section is a low arch, sometimes twenty metres high, sometimes forty. It is floored with ropy pahoehoe that set in the act of flowing, so that walking on it feels like walking on the frozen surface of a slow river. Along the walls run shelves, the "bathtub rings" left each time the lava level dropped and paused. Each shelf is lined with a lip of glassy rock, and on every lip something lives.

The ceiling is the strangest part. As the last molten rock drained, the roof dripped and the drips froze. What hangs overhead is a field of lava stalactites, thin as fingers and blunt-tipped, some no longer than a thumb, some as long as an arm. People who come here call them *teeth*. They are brittle, and a thrown stone will bring a shower of them down, but nothing here throws stones.

There are six skylights along the length of the system, places where the roof gave way. Each is a shaft of grey light falling onto a mound of its own rubble, and each mound is a small island of green. Between the skylights lies a dusk that deepens to full black and then eases toward the next pale shaft. From inside, the whole tube reads as a chain of dim lanterns hung across enormous distances.

---

## The Air and the Water

The temperature never changes. Midsummer on the plateau can be baking and the winter nights can crack rock with frost, but thirty metres down it is always the same cool, around eleven degrees, the chill of a stone cellar. Breath does not fog, but it almost does.

Water is present without being abundant. There is no river down here and no lake. What moisture there is comes through the roof. Rain on the plateau soaks into the fractured basalt and emerges days or weeks later as drips from the ends of the teeth. Some drips fall once an hour, and some fall every few seconds in a rhythm so exact it could be used to count. Beneath the steady ones, the lava floor has been worn into shallow basins no bigger than cupped hands, each holding a clear, cold coin of water.

Where the air from a skylight meets the cooler tube air, condensation beads on the walls. The basalt becomes slick and dark and shines faintly in the grey light. A few metres from the openings, the rock is dry enough to hold dust.

The water is the same as it was last year and the year before that. The pools stay at one level. The drips keep their rhythm. The rock is too young to have grown mineral formations, so even the water leaves no mark of its passage. It arrives, pools, evaporates, and arrives again.

---

## The Living Weight

Standing in any part of the tube for more than a moment, you come to understand that you are never alone and never close to it.

**The bats.** The main chamber, the wide place where the channels merge, holds a colony whose size can only be guessed. The ceiling there is not black. It is brown, and it moves. Clustered on every rough patch of rock, hanging from every tooth, packed shoulder to shoulder in dense mats that ripple when a disturbance passes through them, are bats. They are small free-tailed insect-eaters, and they number in the millions. Their warmth rises, and in the coldest reaches of the chamber you can feel a faint heat coming down from the ceiling, the way you might feel it from a banked fire.

Beneath them lies the guano. It covers the chamber floor, the ropy lava buried under metres of it, a soft brown plain that swallows the foot to the ankle. It is the source of the ammonia that stings the eyes. It is also the foundation of everything else.

**The swifts.** Nearer the skylights, where some light reaches, the walls are crusted with the cup-shaped nests of cave swiftlets, built from their own hardened saliva and bits of moss. The birds navigate the dimness with rapid clicks, a dry rattling like pebbles shaken in a gourd. They pour in and out of the skylights in dark ribbons and never collide.

**The crickets.** Cave crickets carpet the walls in every zone where the dusk turns to dark. They are long-legged, pale, and humpbacked, with antennae several times the length of their bodies. On some stretches of wall no rock is visible between them. Brush a hand near them and a patch of wall lifts and rustles and resettles.

**The guano world.** On the surface of the bat droppings is an ecosystem that works without light. Beetles churn through it, small dark scarabs and pale blind rove beetles. Mites move across it in clouds that look like drifting dust. Moth larvae burrow into it, and the adult moths flutter in the updraft over the chamber and are eaten by the bats before dawn. Pale millipedes. Springtails. Pseudoscorpions no larger than a grain of rice hunt among them. Long-legged centipedes, some as long as a hand, move over the surface with the fluid speed of spilled water, and the sound of a large one crossing the guano is a whisper of many feet.

**The watchers.** On the shelves along the walls, spiders have strung sheet webs between the lava lips, catching whatever the updrafts lift. In the drip-pools live a few transparent amphipods, shrimp-like and eyeless, flicking through their handful of water. At the base of the bat chamber, where the guano thins toward the edges, a pale salamander sometimes rests with its gills exposed to the damp air. It is the only creature here that seems to be doing nothing.

The fauna does not share the space. It saturates it. Every surface that can hold a body holds one. Every hollow has an occupant. The tube is not a cave with animals in it. It is an animal habitat that happens to be made of rock.

---

## The Noise

Nothing here is ever quiet.

The baseline is the colony: a continuous high chittering, the bats' social calls, audible to human ears as a fine grain of sound, like fat sizzling in a pan the size of a field. Beneath it runs the rustle of wings settling and shifting, a sound like wind through dry leaves, though there is no wind and no leaves.

The swiftlets add their rattling clicks near the skylights. The crickets add a faint dry scrape when they move en masse. The drips add a tuning, a scattered and stubborn ticking that echoes in the long tube so that each drop seems to fall twice.

Then there is the emergence. Once each day, at the exact point of dusk on the plateau, the colony leaves. It begins as a thickening of the sound, the chittering rising and quickening. Then the ceiling of the main chamber seems to peel away, and the tube fills with a roar. The noise is not loud in any single voice. It is loud in the way surf is loud: millions of wingbeats combined into a deep, continuous rush that fills the tube from wall to wall. Standing in the path means feeling the air pressed and pulled, the draft of their passage cold against the face. The stream pours up and out through the largest skylight and rises above the plateau in a twisting column visible for miles, like smoke from a fire that isn't burning.

The emergence lasts nearly an hour. Then the tube is almost quiet, which here means the crickets and the drips and the residual chitter of the young and sick bats left behind. Before dawn, they return, dropping out of the sky in steep dives, and the roar comes back in reverse.

Then the swifts, which have slept, come alive and go out to hunt the daylight. The tube hands the sky back and forth between them. This has happened every day for thousands of years. It will happen tomorrow.

---

## The Green

Plants barely exist here, and only on the skylights' terms.

Under each shaft of grey light lies a rubble mound, and on each mound grows a small garden. It is mostly moss, thick and dark green, soaking up the condensation. There are ferns with fronds as delicate as lace, leaning toward the opening above. In the brightest of the skylights, a single stunted fig has rooted in a crevice and climbed halfway up the shaft, its trunk twisted, its few leaves pale.

Beyond reach of the light, a few fungi grow on the guano: white threads, small grey caps, a mould that forms soft furred cushions on old bat carcasses. They are the only things that grow in the dark, and they are not green.

The other vegetation reaches in from above. In places the roof is thin enough that the roots of plateau trees have forced through the cracks. They hang in curtains from the ceiling, pale, fine, and tangled, some reaching almost to the floor. They drip with condensation, and cave insects feed on their tips. From a distance, the root curtains look like the ghost of a forest hanging upside down.

---

## The Few Who Come

There are no buildings here, nothing made and left. There is no mark of permanence except one.

A worn line runs down the rubble of the first skylight and across the ropy floor to the edge of the main chamber: a path. It is not a constructed path, just a trail of basalt rubbed slightly smoother by bare and sandalled feet. On a few low stretches of the ceiling near the entrance, a faint smudge of old soot marks where torches have been held.

People come. Not often, perhaps a few times a year, from the grasslands on the plateau. They come to take guano, which is the richest fertiliser their fields will ever see. They carry woven baskets and wooden scoops, lengths of twisted fibre rope, and lamps made from clay dishes of rendered fat with moss wicks. They work only at the chamber's edge, where the guano thins. They take a little, and the colony replaces it within days. They do not stay. They find the noise hard to bear and the smell harder, and there is a belief, handed down, that speaking loudly in the chamber is a discourtesy to something. They whisper, and they leave before dusk. Everyone knows what happens at dusk.

Their presence registers as nothing to the place. A basket's worth of guano is taken; a basket's worth falls. The path is the only thing they have changed, and it is barely a line.

---

## The Stillness Under the Roar

This is the paradox the place holds. It is one of the loudest, most crowded, most incessantly alive places anyone could stand in. Yet nothing in it changes.

The temperature is fixed. The drips keep time. The rock is too young to have begun eroding and too deep to weather. Seasons pass on the plateau, but here there is only the daily breath of the colony: out at dusk, in before dawn, the same number, the same path, the same roar. The population holds at the level the insects of the plateau can feed. The guano rises at the rate it decomposes. The ferns in the skylights grow exactly as much as the light allows, and no more.

It is an equilibrium so complete that the enormous motion of the place becomes a kind of stillness, the way a waterfall looks frozen when you stand before it long enough. Millions of lives, moving constantly, all describing the same circle.

The lava stopped flowing a few thousand years ago and the tube has held its shape since. The teeth still hang where they froze. The rubble still lies where it fell. One day, very far from now, the roof will fail again, or the colony will move, or the climate above will shift. But nothing here suggests that day, and the place does not seem to anticipate it. It hangs in the cool dark, humming and chittering and dripping, exactly as it was, exactly as it will be.
