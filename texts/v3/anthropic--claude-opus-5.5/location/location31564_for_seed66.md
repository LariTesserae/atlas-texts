# The Tablet of Ossary

## The Shape of It

From the salt flats below it looks like a wall with a cloud resting on top. The wall is an escarpment of pale, sweating limestone that rises almost a mile out of the brine plain and runs north and south past both horizons. The cloud never leaves. It is not quite weather and not quite smoke. It is the breath of the tableland: warm air forced upward, loaded with steam from ten thousand vents, with dust, and with more living bodies than anyone has counted.

On top is Ossary. It is less a city on the plateau than the plateau itself, worked over so long and so completely that no original surface is left. It covers an upland the size of a small nation. Weather crosses it the way weather crosses a mountain range: it gathers over the eastern stacks in the morning, rains on the central wards by noon, and has burned off into haze over the western rim by dusk. Different districts have different climates. People in the lower Guttering carry oilcloth all year. People on the Kiln Heights have not felt rain in a generation, only the constant fine condensation that beads on every surface and runs down.

Everything is wet, but it is not the wet of storms. It is the wet of a greenhouse, of a kitchen, of a lung. The air is warm and never cold, even at this height, because the updraft from the brine flats carries the lowland heat with it. As that warm air meets the stone it gives up its water. Walls weep constantly. Stairways run with thin sheets of water that pool on landings and pour off the edges in threads. The oldest buildings have grooves worn down their faces where the same drip has fallen for eight hundred years, and some of those grooves are deep enough to put an arm into.

## What Grows (Nothing)

No plants grow in Ossary. There is no moss or lichen, not even the grey crust you find on gravestones. For a place this damp that seems impossible until you understand the stone. Ossary is built of, and on, a soft pale rock that the city's own chemistry has turned faintly caustic. Centuries of guano, lime-burning, ash, and runoff have soaked every pore of it. The water that weeps from the walls is slightly bitter and slightly slick. Seeds carried up from the lowlands on the wind land, swell, and die within a day.

What does live on the stone is simpler: bacterial films in rust and violet and a sick copper-green, spreading in slow tidal patterns across the wet faces of buildings. You can read the age of a wall by its bands. Fresh stone is white. Ten years gives a blush of rose. A century gives the deep bruise-purple of the old wards, where the films are thick enough to peel away in sheets like wet paper. They feed on minerals and on the endless fine rain of organic dust that falls from the sky.

That dust is the second clue to how this place works.

## The Column

Below the escarpment, the brine flats stretch a hundred miles to the sea. They are shallow, hot, and lifeless-looking, but they are carpeted in microbial mats, and the mats breed midges. The numbers are astronomical. Every warm afternoon the midges rise off the flats in a haze that turns the eastern horizon a smoky brown. The heat carries them west, and when that moving air hits the escarpment it has only one direction to go.

Ossarans call this the Column. It is a permanent updraft running the length of the eastern rim, a river of air flowing upward, and it is thick with insects. They pour over the lip of the tableland in drifts and eddies. They fall into the streets and gather in the corners of stairwells like grey snow. They are the base of everything. Every creature in Ossary eats midges, or eats something that eats midges, or eats the droppings of something that does.

The city itself was built to serve the Column. The eastern rim is lined with the Throats: enormous funnels of brick and iron that catch the updraft and channel it down into the city through ducts wide enough to drive a cart through. The ducts carry warmth, water, and food into every district. At the base of each Throat, industrial fans the height of houses, driven by steam and very old gearing, pull the air in faster than it would come on its own. You can hear them from anywhere in the city.

## The Swifts

Then there are the birds.

It takes most visitors a full day to understand that the dark haze always moving over Ossary is not smoke. It is swifts, hundreds of millions of them, possibly more. They are small, dark, and sickle-winged, and they never touch the ground. Swifts cannot perch. They have short legs and grasping feet that can cling to vertical surfaces and nothing else. In Ossary they have found the largest vertical surface in the world.

Every wall, chimney, tower, and flue belongs to them. The city has grown around them the way a shell grows around its animal. Ossaran architecture has no flat roofs and almost no horizontal ledges. Buildings rise in stepped towers faced with rough, grooved brick, and the rough texture is deliberate: it gives the birds purchase. The great chimneys of the industrial wards are roosts first and chimneys second. At dusk the swifts come home and pour into them in spiraling funnels that look like smoke running backward, a black tornado reversing itself into a flue. One chimney may take in a million birds in twenty minutes.

The sound is the city's defining fact. By day the screaming of the swifts is a constant thin shriek at the top edge of hearing, so pervasive that Ossarans no longer notice it. Visitors go half-mad in the first week. Beneath it runs the wingbeat roar, a sound like surf or distant rail traffic, the combined air of countless wings. At dawn and dusk, when the flocks lift off or settle, the noise swells until you cannot hear someone shouting beside you. Conversation stops. Everyone waits. Children are taught to finish their sentences before the Turning.

## The Other Ones

The swifts dominate, but they are not alone. Ossary is dense with animal life in every layer.

**Bats** take the night shift. As the swifts pour into the chimneys at dusk, the bats pour out of the undercity: from the drainage tunnels, the old quarries, the vaulted cisterns under the central wards. They are a different sound, a dry chittering and a leathery rushing. They hunt the midges that keep rising in the warm night air. On the eastern rim at midnight, the Column is a vertical river of bats.

**Snails** cover the lower walls, thousands per square yard in the wettest districts. They are small, pale, and translucent-shelled, grazing the bacterial films in slow waves. You hear them before you see them: a faint continuous crackle, like frying, which is millions of radulae scraping stone. They are the city's cleaners and slowly its sculptors. Over centuries their grazing has smoothed and softened the lower courses of every building, rounding every corner and blurring every carved inscription below head height.

**Beetles** handle the guano. Glossy black dung-beetles work the gutters in armies, and their larvae churn the deep guano beds of the old roosts.

**Gulls** have come up from the distant coast. They are loud, huge, and vicious, and they hold the rooftops of the harbor wards, preying on young swifts.

**Rats** are everywhere, of course, fat and slick and unafraid, living on the dead: the thousands of birds and bats that fall every day.

**Spiders** hang their webs across every alley and duct mouth, some colonial, their shared sheets of silk spanning entire courtyards and sagging silver-grey under the weight of water and caught midges.

Nothing in Ossary is ever still. Look closely at any surface and something is moving on it.

## The Industry

Ossary is not a city that happens to have animals. It is a city that lives off them.

**The Guano Works** are its oldest and greatest industry. Beneath the major roosts, centuries of droppings have built up in layers dozens of feet thick. The layers compress into a dark brown stone that is rich beyond measure in nitrogen and phosphorus. Ossarans mine it. They cut it in blocks, cart it on rail to the kilns, and render it into fertilizer, which travels down the escarpment on cable-lifts to be shipped to farmlands a continent away. Ossary grows nothing, yet it feeds half the world's fields. The mining is careful and slow, governed by ancient law. A roost can only be dug from below, and only while the birds are away for the season.

**The Nest Harvests** are the second trade. The swifts build their nests from their own saliva, which dries into a pale translucent cup. Ossarans harvest the abandoned ones after the breeding season. Nest-gatherers climb the chimneys on iron rungs set into the brick, in near darkness, among millions of birds. The nests are boiled into a glue so strong and so resistant to water that Ossaran bookbinding, shipbuilding, and instrument-making are known across the world for it.

**The Fanworks** run the Throats. Their engine houses contain the largest steam machinery in the city: coal-fired, belt-driven, ancient and constantly repaired. Their thudding is the city's heartbeat, a deep slow pulse felt in the stone at about forty beats a minute.

**The Chitin Mills** are newer. They process the endless drifts of dead midges into a pale flour that can be pressed into sheets or rendered into a kind of lacquer. Many Ossaran windows are panes of pressed chitin, amber and faintly cloudy. Under them the light in old houses turns the color of weak tea.

All of it is loud. The fans, the kilns, the rail carts, the cable-lifts, and the steam whistles marking each shift change all sit layered beneath the screaming of the swifts.

## The Light

Ossary is not a dark city. It is high enough that the sky above the haze is a clean, hard blue, and on most days the sun falls strong and white through gaps in the flocks. The light moves, though. When a large flock passes overhead, a shadow sweeps across the district like a cloud crossing a field, except that the shadow flickers. Ossarans have a word for the particular strobing shade of a low flock: *sieving*. On the brightest days the light comes down through the moving birds like light through water, dappled and shifting, crawling across the wet pale walls.

The walls themselves shine. Wet stone throws the light back. From the western rim at sunset, the whole city seems to glow: a vast soft field of pale towers, all sweating and gleaming, with the black spirals of the evening swifts funneling down into it.

## The Rules

Some things are true in Ossary that are true nowhere else.

**Nothing is flat.** Flat surfaces collect guano and water, rot, and grow slick with films. Ossaran streets are ramps and stairs, all angled toward drains. Roofs are peaked. Even tables are slightly tilted, and Ossarans learn to set their cups in the carved rings that every tabletop has.

**No one kills a swift.** This is the oldest law. It is enforced less by courts than by common understanding that the birds are the city. A person who kills a swift deliberately is exiled down the escarpment. Swifts that fall dead are gathered by designated collectors and rendered with ceremony.

**The Turning is sacred.** At dawn and dusk, work stops. The fans are geared down so the birds can move freely through the Throats. People step off the stairs and stand against walls. This is partly courtesy and partly survival: the density of the flocks at the Turning is enough to knock a person from a stairway.

**Time is measured in strata.** Ossary changes slowly, and its people think in its slow terms. A new building takes a generation to become truly part of the city, the time it takes for the films to color it, the snails to soften its corners, and the swifts to take it as a roost. Ossaran histories are dated by guano layers: the Year of the Thin Bed, the Deep Century. The city is settling into the tableland at about an inch per lifetime, sinking into its own foundations as the stone softens. Old wards stand lower than new ones. The oldest quarter, the Cup, sits in a shallow basin of its own making, a district slowly subsiding into the rock beneath it, always wet, always dim, and older than any record.

## The Slightly Wrong Thing

One more thing, which Ossarans do not like to discuss with visitors.

The swifts are getting larger.

Not quickly. Over centuries, the birds of the old roosts have grown measurably bigger than the swifts of other lands. The oldest chimneys hold birds nearly twice the size of their wild cousins, and their screaming has dropped half a tone deeper. Some of the nests in the Cup's ancient flues are the size of washbasins. No one is sure why. The theories include the warmth, the endless food, and the stone itself.

There is also the matter of the patterns. On certain still evenings, when the Column is calm and the flocks move slowly overhead, their shapes seem to repeat: particular spirals, particular folds, forming and dissolving and forming again in the same places over the same districts. The nest-gatherers, who spend their lives among the birds, will tell you quietly that the flocks have their own geography. They say there are roads in the air above Ossary as fixed as any street, and a second city up there, moving, made of birds.

The Ossarans below build their towers a little higher every century. Some say they do it to reach the Column. Others say the birds are asking for more room.
