# The Held Chord

You come to it through a crack. Nothing announces it. Somewhere beneath a flat, stone-coloured country there is a fissure in the bedrock barely wider than a shoulder. It drops at a slant through twenty body-lengths of dry, sharp-edged rock and opens into a hollow about the size of a threshing floor. The ceiling is low enough that a tall person could touch it standing on a stone.

The first thing that reaches you, long before you can see anything, is the sound.

---

## The Sound

It is a chord, and it is loud. It is not painful, but it presses into the skull the way a hand presses into dough. Standing in the hollow, you feel it in the hinge of the jaw and the thin bones behind the ears, and eventually in the soles of the feet through the rock. It has four or five tones, stacked, some low enough to feel more than hear, one high and thin as a wire. They never change. They do not pulse or swell or fade. The chord was sounding when the cavity first split open, and it will be sounding when whatever is above has worn down to sand.

At first you assume the rock makes it. The hollow is shaped like the inside of a bell: a rough dome, with walls that curve inward toward the floor. You might imagine some deep hum in the earth, amplified. But nothing moves in the air here. There is no draft, no breath from the crack, no rising heat you can feel on the skin. The air is warm and utterly still, the same warmth at the ceiling as at the floor, the same at the back wall as at the entrance. There is no gradient to push anything anywhere.

The sound is alive. It comes from the animals.

---

## The Light

Your eyes adjust slowly, because there is not much to adjust to.

The floor of the hollow is split by a seam that runs lengthwise like a healed wound. It glows a dull, even red, the colour of a coal just before it goes grey. It is the only heat source, and it does not flicker. By its light you can make out the walls, and you see that the cavity is young.

Nothing here has been worn down. The rock broke recently, in the way stone counts time, and the break faces still carry their original edges. Planes of pale crystal jut from the walls, clean and faceted and sharp enough to part skin if you brush them carelessly. No dust has settled on them. No water has ever run over them to soften a corner, because no water has ever been here. The minerals are the dry kind: gypsum blades, a cloudy salt that forms in cubes, dark glassy veins that break into curved, knife-edged flakes. Stepping carelessly produces a sound like breaking crockery. Stepping carefully produces no sound at all against the chord.

There is a second light, fainter and greener, scattered across the walls and floor in patches. That light is the plants.

---

## The Growing Things

Given the dryness and the dark, the vegetation should not be lush. It is lush all the same.

The plants do not drink, and they do not use light in any way you would recognise. They take heat from the red seam through long, pale roots. Those roots spread across the floor in branching fans and slip into hairline cracks in the salt. Their veins carry no water. They carry an amber oil, thick as honey. Break a stem and it beads slowly, smelling faintly of pine resin and hot metal. The oil never dries, because nothing here evaporates. A broken stem simply stays broken and beaded, exactly as it was left.

What they eat is the sound.

The broadest plants hold up leaves that are not really leaves. They are membranes: round, thin, taut as drumskins, stretched between radiating ribs of waxy tissue. Some are as small as a thumbnail and some are as wide as a cart wheel. Every one of them is angled toward a source of the chord and trembles in it. The trembling is how they feed. Vibration becomes growth. Each membrane glows faintly green at its centre, brightest where it trembles hardest. The glow is a by-product, the way a hand grows warm from clapping.

So the plants grow toward the sound, not toward light. They lean and reach across the cavity in long curving arcs, their rib-frames bending to catch a particular pitch. Low tones feed the great slow membranes near the floor, which move so gently you can only see it if you watch one edge against a crystal. The high wire-tone feeds a different family: clusters of tiny, stiff, bright discs on thread-thin stalks, which shiver so fast they blur.

There are also mats of something like moss. It is dense, springy, and dark, and it grows along the red seam where the heat is most direct. It absorbs sound rather than using it, and walking on it is the only place in the hollow where the chord grows a little quieter underfoot. There are hanging vines that coil down from the ceiling in corkscrews. Their spiral shape is tuned, and they hum faintly along with the chord, adding nothing to it and never falling out of it.

The overall impression is abundance with room between. Each plant has its own territory of air, its own share of vibration, and it does not crowd the next one. Between them are open floor, bare crystal, and red-lit stone. The hollow is full, but it is not choked.

---

## The Singers

The animals are everywhere, and every one of them is spaced apart from the others.

The most numerous are beetle-like creatures the length of a finger joint, with shells the dull bronze of old coins. They cling to the crystal faces, each on its own facet, each an exact arm's length or more from its nearest neighbour. They do not move from these posts. Each one rubs a ridged leg against the edge of its shell continuously, producing a single pure tone. The crystal it clings to rings with that tone and multiplies it. Beetles on the large crystals make the low notes, and beetles on the small ones make the high. There are hundreds of them. Together they are the chord.

They are fed by what they feed. The membrane-plants tremble and grow, and they shed a fine, pale, pollen-like dust that sifts down onto the crystals. The beetles eat it without ever pausing in their stridulation. What the beetles pass drops to the floor and feeds the moss. The moss's roots return something to the seam, or seem to. The loop is closed and exact. As much grows as is eaten. As much is sung as is heard.

There are other creatures too. Soft, pale, many-legged grazers move slowly across the moss mats, and they are silent. Their bodies are covered in fine hairs that stand upright in the sound like grass in a breeze. Somewhere in the ceiling there are things that hang like dried seed pods, only occasionally visible when one opens a pair of translucent, ribbed wings. They never fly. They only unfold, hold the wings in the chord for a long time, and fold them again. They may be feeding the way the plants do. There is also a single larger animal, which may be the only one of its kind here. It is a flat, grey, heavy thing, like a stone with a mouth, lying half-buried near the seam. It has not moved in the memory of anyone who has come here. It might be dead. It is warm to the touch, and when you put your ear against it, you hear the chord from inside it, perfectly, as though it were swallowing the whole of it.

---

## The Marks

Someone has been here. They are not here now, but the evidence is scattered and undisturbed, because in this place nothing disturbs anything.

A rope of braided fibre hangs down from the entrance crack. It is twisted from the tough outer skin of the vine-stems and stiff with dried oil. It is the way down and the way back up.

Near the entrance there is a ring of flat stones, set carefully on bare rock, with nothing in its centre. Nothing is needed. The seam gives all the heat there is, and no fire has ever been lit here, because a fire would breathe and there is nothing here to breathe with it.

On a ledge lies a small cache of blades, knapped from the dark glassy vein in the walls. They are beautiful, thin, curved things, the flaking scars on them still crisp. Beside them is a vessel formed from hardened plant resin, shaped around a fist and set aside to cure. It is half full of the amber oil, and the oil has not thickened or skinned over.

Most strangely, there are scratches on one of the largest crystal faces, the one whose beetle produces the lowest note. They are rows of short parallel lines, clustered, made with one of the glass blades. They might be a tally. There is also a second attempt: a small shape carved beside the beetle's post that resembles a beetle, and next to it a new ridge scraped into the crystal edge, as though someone tried to cut a second place for the rubbing of a leg. They might have wanted a sixth tone. They might have wanted to change the chord.

It did not work. The ridge is there, sharp and new, and nothing has ever settled on it.

---

## The Rule

What becomes clear, if you stay, is that the hollow is not waiting for anything.

There is no day here and no season. No rain comes to it, no drought, and no weather of any kind. The seam has always been this colour. The beetles will not tire, and the plants will not outgrow their share. The air will not move. Every element of the place is held in the exact force of every other element, and the thing that holds them is the sound. The chord is not the product of the ecology. It is the ecology's form, the shape that everything here grows into and feeds from and returns to.

This is why the visitors left, perhaps. It was not danger. Nothing here is dangerous, except the crystal edges, and those only if you are careless. But a person brings change in with them: breath, footsteps, the warmth of a body slightly different from the warmth of the rock. The hollow absorbs all of it and gives nothing back but itself. You can sit on the moss for a long time, feeling the chord in your teeth, watching a membrane the size of a shield breathe its green light in and out in time with nothing but the tone. Eventually you will notice that you have stopped wanting anything to happen.

Then you climb the stiff rope back up into the light. The silence up there is enormous, and for days afterward you still hear the chord, exactly, in the space behind your ears.
