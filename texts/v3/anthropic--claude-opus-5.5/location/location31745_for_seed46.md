# The Foundry Blister

Near the summit of a young mountain, too young to have a name or even a finished shape, there is a bubble in the rock. From outside it cannot be seen. The slope is a raw slurry of black glass and rust-colored scoria, still settling, still faintly warm to the touch even at altitude, where the air is thin and dry and sharp. A seam in that slope, a crack no wider than a shoulder, opens into a chamber about the size of a small chapel, and the chamber is working.

## The Shell

The walls are basalt, but not basalt that has cooled the way basalt should. Ordinary lava shrinks as it cools and breaks into columns, hexagons and pentagons, the honeycomb geometry of contraction. Here the columns have come out *milled*. They are perfectly round, dark gray and faintly lustrous, and they rise from the floor at even intervals like the uprights of a press. Some are banded with ridges that could only be called threading. Where two columns meet the ceiling, the rock flares into flanges, and the flanges are studded with nodes of darker glass, domed and regular. No hand placed them. Nothing here has hands. The mountain simply cooled this way, the way frost cools into ferns.

The floor is incomplete. Across roughly a third of it, the stone is finished: smooth, dense, polished as if by long use, though it is perhaps a decade old. Elsewhere the floor thins into something that is not yet a material. It has the color of stone and the weight of stone when you press it, but at the edges of vision it resolves into a faint grid, a lattice of fine lines like the ruling on draftsman's paper. Step on it and it holds. Look directly at it and it becomes gravel. Look away and it is a plan again. The floor has not decided what it is going to be, and it is in no hurry.

The heat is constant and enormous, the heat of an oven set low and left on for a century. It does not come from any single vent. It seeps up evenly from the whole floor, and the air above it is so dry that it seems to have been ironed. Nothing condenses. Nothing drips. There is no water here and never has been. There is barely even the idea of it, only a single pale ring on one wall, mineral-white, left where something *might* once have evaporated but probably never did. Like a stain from the future, it belongs to a flood that has not happened.

## The Growth

And yet the chamber is green. Not densely: the plants stand far apart, each with a wide margin of bare hot stone around it, like exhibits. There are perhaps thirty of them in the whole room. Each one is extraordinary, and each one is doing something.

They do not drink. Their roots, where visible, are fine filaments of something coppery that thread into the basalt and draw on the heat itself. Temperature is their water. They flourish at the hottest points of the floor and yellow slightly at the cooler margins, and a few that grow in the narrow draft near the entrance crack are stunted, pinched, pale as paper.

**The flywheel ferns** are the most common. Each is a single frond unrolled into a perfect disc, flat and wide as a cartwheel, veined in deep green and bronze. The disc turns. It turns so slowly that you would have to sit for an afternoon to be sure of it, one rotation perhaps every eleven days, but the rotation is continuous, driven by some pressure in the stem that builds and releases through a ring of fleshy valves at the hub. Where the rim of one fern passes near a basalt column, the column is worn smooth in a precise arc.

**The bellows plants** grow in the corners, squat, pleated, dark jade. They inflate across a period of weeks, their accordion sides spreading until they are nearly spherical, and then over a single long exhale they compress again, pushing a thread of hot dry air up toward the ceiling. The breath smells of cut metal and crushed stems. If there is a circulation of air in the blister, they are the reason for it. They seem to be keeping the room ventilated for occupants who are not there.

**The wire vine** is rarer. It climbs the threaded columns in tight coils, and its stem is only thinly green; beneath the skin it is metallic, drawn fine and even. Where two vines meet they fuse, stem to stem, forming junctions, and at those junctions small pale flowers open that give off a faint cold spark, a blue point of light no larger than a seed, blinking at intervals of about an hour. The vines conduct. If you laid a palm across two of them you would feel a low pulse through the skin, a mild numb buzzing. They are carrying a current from somewhere to somewhere, and following the vines around the room reveals no source and no destination. The circuit closes on itself. It is powering nothing except its own continuing.

And there is one tree. It stands a little off-center, where the finished floor meets the unfinished, and half its roots grip polished stone while the other half vanish into the ruled lattice of the undecided floor. It is perhaps three times a person's height. Its trunk is ridged and gray-green, and at regular intervals along its branches it bears fruit, if fruit is the word: smooth dark ovoids, heavy, faintly warm. When one ripens it does not fall. It is *ejected*, pushed out along the branch through a ring of tissue that closes behind it, and it lands on the stone with a small dull sound. On the floor beneath the tree lie dozens of these, identical in size and weight to within a hair. The tree is producing units. Nothing collects them. Occasionally, over the course of years, one of them sinks into the unfinished floor, and where it has gone the lattice fills in, and the floor becomes solid there.

## The Wrongness

The light is ordinary. That is the first strange thing. The chamber is sealed except for a crack too narrow and too angled to admit the sun, yet the room is lit evenly, as if by a clear midmorning coming through a high window. There are shadows, well-behaved and crisp. It is only after some time that you notice they all point toward the same spot on the far wall, from every object, regardless of where the object stands. The light comes from everywhere and the darkness gathers in one place. That place, a patch of wall about the size of a door, is slightly darker than the rest of the room, and it is the only surface in the blister that feels cool.

The second strange thing is the repetition. Near the entrance there is a particular stone, fist-sized, with a curl of white crystal running through it like a comma. There is another one, identical to the last fleck, on the far side of the chamber beneath a bellows plant. They are the same stone. Moving one moves the other. Not visibly, not while watched, but turn away and turn back, and both have shifted by exactly the same amount in exactly the same direction. There are at least four such pairs in the room, and perhaps many more, too subtle to notice. The chamber is economizing, reusing parts.

The third strange thing is the matter of before and after. The worn arc on the column is deeper than the fern that wears it could have made in its lifetime. The ejected fruit on the floor includes some with dust on them that has not yet settled from the air. Things in the blister happen in order, but the order has been assembled from pieces, the way a machine is assembled: the housing may be finished before the engine is cast, and here the consequences are sometimes set in place before their causes arrive. Nothing about this is violent. It is simply how the room was built. It was built. It is being built. There is no one building it.

## The Sound

It is mostly quiet. The quiet is heavy and dry and has a texture, the way felt has a texture. Underneath it:

The *tick* of basalt still contracting, very far apart. One every few minutes, somewhere in the walls, a small clean sound like a fingernail on a pane of glass.

The long slow sigh of a bellows plant exhaling, perhaps once a day, lasting nearly a minute.

A hum at the edge of hearing, carried by the wire vines. It rises slightly when the spark-flowers open and falls when they close, so that over hours the room seems to be breathing in electricity.

And very rarely, a deep single *knock* from inside the mountain, far below the floor. The sound of something enormous completing one stroke. The threaded columns vibrate with it, and a fine rain of black glass dust sifts down from the flanges and settles on the flywheel ferns. Then the quiet comes back. Whatever made the stroke will not make another for a long time, months or years. The knocks are counted by the chamber: each time one comes, a single new ridge of threading appears at the base of every column, all at once, as if a gauge had advanced by one notch.

## The Rules

There are no animals here. There never have been. The blister is sealed, dry, and too hot, but more than that, it has no place for them. Everything in it has a function, and none of the functions are for anyone. Whatever an animal would do (carry seeds, turn soil, eat and die and fertilize), the room has already assigned to a plant or a stone or a strand of current.

Heat does the work of water. Things grow toward it, draw it up, and move by it.

Unfinished things are not empty. The ruled floor is a promise the room intends to keep, and it fills in only when something is spent into it: a fruit, a fallen frond, a flake of shed bark.

What is duplicated stays in step. What is reused cannot be separated.

Time is slow, not because it is sluggish but because it is careful. Each process here runs at the pace it needs to run at to be done correctly. A fern takes eleven days to turn once because that is its tolerance. The mountain strikes once in years because that is its cycle.

The dark patch on the far wall, where all the shadows point, grows. Very slightly. Since the ferns began to turn, it has widened by the breadth of a finger. Within it, if you look long enough, you can make out threading, flanges, the domed nodes of glass. It is a doorway that has not yet been cut, the next chamber, drafted but unpoured. The heat behind it is gathering. The wire vines have begun, just in the last few coils, to lean toward it.

## The Whole

From outside, there is still nothing to see: a raw slope, black and rust, shimmering in the thin hot air near a summit that has no name. Inside, in a room the size of a chapel, a factory with no owner and no product runs quietly on heat and green, finishing its own floor, spending its fruit to make ground, keeping its wheels turning for no one, assembling itself out of order toward a shape it already knows.

It does not need to be found. It is not waiting. It is only very busy, very slowly, becoming what it was always going to have been.
