# The Engine Lake

## From Above

At first there is only a plain.

It lies high, well above where trees should stop growing, and it stretches past every horizon. It is flat in the way only water is flat. The air is thin and cool and smells of cut stems and cold iron. The ground is green, a close, even green, and it gives slightly underfoot, the way a mattress does.

It is not ground. It is a lid of leaf.

The leaves are broad, waxy and interlocked. They float edge to edge in a single sheet thousands of kilometers across, and beneath them is water, perhaps a kilometer deep. The plain is the roof of a sea that sits on top of a continent, cupped in a basin of old worn stone. It has been here so long that the question of how it got here has stopped meaning anything.

Put your ear to the leaf and you hear it at once: a vast, slow pounding far below. The sheet trembles with it, too faintly to see.

## The Water

Part the leaves and look down, and the water is clear, with a pale green cast like light through a glass bottle. It is cold, a few degrees above freezing, and it never gets colder or warmer.

It is also alive. There is no single thing in it you could point to. The water carries plant cells in solution, a suspended chlorophyll so evenly spread that the lake is one continuous photosynthetic body. The sea does not contain vegetation. It is vegetation in its liquid phase. Sunlight falling through the lid is absorbed gradually and evenly, so the light dims in a smooth gradient instead of stopping at a line. At two hundred meters it is still bright enough to read by. At six hundred it is the color of deep moss. Near the floor it is a green dusk, but never true dark.

Swimming through it feels like moving through the inside of a leaf. It has a faint viscosity and a faint taste, somewhere between cucumber and copper.

## The Pumps

The pounding comes from the columns.

They rise from the floor to the underside of the lid, spaced ten or fifteen kilometers apart, so that from any one of them the next is only a darker vertical smudge in the green distance, or not visible at all. Between them there is mostly open water. The place is full, but in the way a cathedral is full of air.

Each column is a single plant: a hollow trunk of dense, fibrous, nearly black wood, forty or fifty meters across, with walls several meters thick. It is a pump. Inside, stacked sphincters of living tissue contract and release in sequence, like valves in a heart the size of a mountain. Every ninety seconds a column draws water in through ribbed intakes near the floor, forces it up through its own length, and vents it through fluted openings just below the lid. Each stroke is a single deep blow you feel in your sternum before you hear it: *ТHOOM.* Then comes a long hiss of displaced water, then the clack of great woody flaps seating back into place, then silence for a count of about sixty. Then it happens again.

There are hundreds of thousands of columns, and all of them keep the same beat.

They are not synchronized in the sense of firing together. Each one is offset from its neighbors by a small, fixed interval, so a wave of strokes rolls across the lake forever in the same direction at the same speed. Sound travels far in water, and the whole sea is a single sustained industrial rhythm, thudding and hissing and clanking, layered and overlapped into a roar that never builds and never fades. It sounds like a foundry running at full capacity through a night that does not end.

## Current, Weather, Stillness

The pumps move water in a fixed circulation: down along the floor, up through the trunks, out beneath the lid, and slowly down again through the open spaces between. The lake is large enough to have its own weather, and its weather is this. Standing currents run across it like permanent winds. Stable layers of density are stacked as neatly as geological strata. Great rotating gyres sit anchored to features in the floor, and they have turned in the same place, at the same speed, for as long as anything has been here to measure.

Nothing drifts. A particle released at a given point will follow the same path as every particle released there before it, trace the same spiral, and return. The creatures know this the way birds know a coastline. The currents are roads, and the roads do not move.

Take a reading anywhere in the lake, today or in ten thousand years, and it will be the same. The temperature is the same, the light is the same, and the chemistry, down to the trace metals, is the same. The system is at equilibrium, and not a tense or fragile one. It is the settled equilibrium of something that found its balance long ago and has had no reason to leave it. There are no seasons down here and no gradients that shift. The lid screens out the changing sky above. The pumps, which should be a source of disturbance, are the thing that keeps it all unchanged. They are the lake's thermostat, its circulation and its governor.

What the engine makes, most of all, is sameness.

## The Charge

Near the bottom of each column, where the intakes are, the wood gives way to a banded pale tissue in stacked plates, like the cells of an electric eel grown to the scale of a building. These are the column's batteries. Each pump stroke flexes them, and the flexing builds a charge.

The charge goes somewhere. Threads of darker fiber, a few centimeters thick and hundreds of kilometers long, run from column to column across the lake floor, a network of living cable laid out like a power grid. Current moves through it constantly. You can feel it in the water near the lines as a faint prickle in the teeth. Where two cables cross, or where one runs close to exposed rock, arcs jump between them, blue-white against the green dusk, with a sharp snapping crackle cutting through the deeper rhythm of the pumps. The arcs always happen at the same junctions, at the same intervals. Some of the older crossings have been sparking so long that the stone beneath them has fused into glass.

The electricity is how the columns stay in step. It is the timing signal: the offset between one stroke and the next is set by how long a pulse takes to travel down the cable. The whole lake is one machine, and the grid is its nervous system. It is not thinking. It is clocking.

## The Foundries

Here and there, perhaps one in every few hundred columns, a pump carries a growth partway up its trunk that looks almost deliberately manufactured. It is a swollen gallery of chambers with openings along its underside like the mouths of a press.

These are the seed-foundries. They produce pods.

Each pod is the size of a human skull, smooth, dense and pale green. Every pod is identical, and this is the strange part. They are not similar in the way seeds of a species are similar. They are identical in the way machined parts are identical. They match in mass, in the spiral of ridges on the shell, and even in the one small asymmetric notch near the base. A foundry releases one every ninety seconds, on the beat. The pod drops from the press, catches the standing current, and is carried away along a fixed route.

Nobody knows where they go. Or rather, anyone who follows one finds out, and the answer is circular. The pods travel the gyres for years, sink to the floor at particular places, and split open. What comes out is not a new column. It is more of the water's suspended chlorophyll, released in a measured dose that exactly replaces what has been lost. The foundries are not reproducing the lake. They are maintaining it, turning out replacement parts at a steady rate for a machine that is never allowed to wear down.

## The Floor

The basin floor is old stone, dark and fine-grained. It has been smoothed over unimaginable time by currents that always run the same way, so it is not rough, but polished into long, shallow, directional flutes, like the grain of sanded wood. The ridges all point the same way, recording the circulation the way dunes record a wind.

Over the stone lies a felt of fine white root, the lower extension of the columns and the substrate from which the suspended plant life grows. The felt is dense and springy, knee-deep in places. Push a hand into it and it closes around the fingers, cold and slightly warm at once. That is the electricity. Where the currents are strongest, the felt has been combed flat into streaming white banks that ripple in place and never lift away.

## The Animals

For all the space between the columns, the lake teems with animals. They gather where the machine creates niches, the way pigeons gather around a railway station.

**Intake eels** are long and ribbon-thin, gray with bioluminescent flecks along their flanks. They live at the mouths of the pump intakes in thousands, riding the inrush on each stroke, braced against the woody ribs. They feed on whatever the suction brings in and let the backflow carry them out again. Their whole life is the ninety-second cycle.

**Valve-shrimp** live inside the columns, in the gaps between the sphincters, and are never seen from outside. You can hear them, though, in the brief silences after a stroke: a dry, busy clicking that echoes up and down the hollow trunks, like ball bearings in a drum. They seem to clean the valves. Whether they are parasites or maintenance crew is not clear, and the distinction may not matter here.

**Current-rays** are enormous, twenty or thirty meters across, mottled green and pale. They ride the standing currents with wings barely moving, following the gyres in endless slow circuits. Each ray seems to keep to a single route. Watch a spot long enough and the same ray passes at the same interval, measured in days. They are silent, and the only thing in the lake that is.

**Spark-fish**, small and dense and dull blue, school in tight balls near the cable crossings. When an arc fires, the school flinches as one and flashes with a light of its own, answering the discharge, then settles. Each arc is followed by this brief bloom of living light, in time with the grid.

**Lid-grazers** are soft, rounded things like enormous sea slugs, the color of the leaves. They hang upside down against the underside of the lid, cropping the leaf-roots in slow, even strips. The strips regrow exactly as fast as they are eaten, so the grazers move along the same paths forever, like a mower on a field that never changes.

And **foundry-followers**, eel-like and silver, trail the pods as they leave the presses and escort them along their routes. They never eat them. They swim alongside, nudge a pod back into the current if it strays, and turn back when it sinks. No one knows what they get out of it. It may be simply their function, the way a ball bearing's function is to be round.

## Nothing Built

There are no structures here, and nothing was ever made. No hand has set any stone or cut any cable. Everything in the lake grew, all of it continuously alive, all of it plant. Yet it is unmistakably an industry: pumping, generating, conducting, manufacturing, regulating. It runs at the scale of a continent and makes a noise like a thousand factories.

It is not machinery imitating life, and it is not life that happens to resemble machinery. It is what vegetation becomes when it fills a place completely and has nothing left to grow into. It turns toward process instead of expansion, toward keeping instead of making. The lake reached the edges of its basin long ago. Since then, all its energy has gone into holding itself exactly as it is, stroke after stroke, at one steady cold temperature, under one steady green light.

## The Surface Again

Climb back through the lid into the thin high air and the noise vanishes almost entirely. The leaves absorb it. On the plain there is only wind, cold and steady from the same direction it always blows. There is the pale sky, a sun that is only the sun, and a green flatness running out to every horizon.

The ground still trembles a little underfoot, once every ninety seconds, and it will never stop.
