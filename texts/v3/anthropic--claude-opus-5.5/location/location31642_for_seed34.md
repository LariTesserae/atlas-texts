# The Combing Stones

## The Approach

The plateau rises out of the lowland cloud like a table lifted from a flooded floor. Its edge is a single escarpment, grey and wet, running north and south until it bends out of sight. Every morning the cloud below climbs it. Every morning, at the top, the cloud meets the city.

No one who describes the place from a distance calls it a city at first. From the rim it looks like a second geology, a textured grey that covers the upland from horizon to horizon. It has the grain of scree, or of a lichen seen very close. Only when you walk into it does the grain resolve into walls. There are walls behind walls and walls against walls, stacked cells and roofed lanes, courtyards no bigger than a spread cloak. The whole of it is made of stone laid on stone. Nothing is cut, cemented, or carried on a wheel.

It covers something like the area of a small sea. It is old enough that its oldest parts have stopped looking built and begun to look weathered, the way a riverbed looks weathered. It is still being made.

## The Stone and the Laying

The builders never quarried. Every stone in the city was chosen, not shaped: river-rounded cobbles from the old outwash channels, slabs of slate that split naturally along the frost lines, and blocks of pale granite loosened from the tors by ice. Walls go up by fit and weight alone. A good stone is one that sits. Over centuries this produced a kind of vernacular geometry. Courses bulge where the cobbles are round, lie flat where slate was plentiful, and step in and out where granite blocks refused to be anything but themselves.

Roofs are corbelled. Each course of flat stone overhangs the one below until the two sides nearly meet, and a capstone closes the gap. Inside, the cells are beehive-shaped and dim, and they smell of cold mineral and old smoke. Outside, the domes crowd shoulder to shoulder. Seen from one of the few high towers, the roofline is an endless field of grey knuckles, glistening.

The lanes between them are rarely wider than one person. In places they are roofed over too, so that you walk in stone tunnels lit by gaps where a capstone has slipped. The density is total. There is almost no open ground. Where a wall ends, another begins within an arm's reach.

## The Water

The rule of the city, the thing everything else follows from, is that the walls catch cloud.

The morning fog that climbs the escarpment is saturated and cold. When it meets the city's billions of rough stone faces, it condenses. Water beads on every surface, gathers into threads, and runs down. By mid-morning every wall in the city is weeping. The water finds the joints between stones and follows them, the way it has followed them for a very long time. The builders learned to lay walls with a slight inward cant and to set slates at the base as lips. Now the weeping is gathered into shallow channels along the foot of each wall. The channels feed into gutters, the gutters into cisterns sunk beneath the courtyards, and the cisterns overflow, eventually, into the old outwash channels that drain the plateau.

The city is oriented to the wind. Its main walls run crosswise to the prevailing drift of the fog, like the teeth of a comb. Hence the name, if the place has one: the combing. The windward districts are the wettest. There the stones are slick and black, and the channels run all day. On the lee side, far to the east, the air is drier. The walls there are pale with lichen, and the cisterns are deeper.

Water in the city is never loud. It moves in sheets and threads. It drips from capstones into standing pools with a small, private sound. After rain, which comes often in the afternoons, the whole city runs at once. Even then the sound is a hiss, diffuse and everywhere, like breath.

## The Wear

Nothing in the city is sharp anymore. Steps in the oldest lanes are worn into saddles, and the gutters have been cut deeper by their own water than any builder dug them. Walls in the western districts lean at gentle, settled angles. They have not fallen; they have simply found the shape they prefer. Frost works at the joints every winter night, lifting stones a hair's width. Every thaw lets them settle back not quite where they were.

The city, in other words, creeps. It is in balance rather than at rest. Some walls fail each year, usually in the thaw. A course slumps, or a dome's capstone drops into the cell below. The fallen stones are not wasted. They are gathered and relaid, sometimes in the same place and sometimes a street away. The city is built out of its own collapses. There is a theory, hard to prove, that no stone in the oldest quarter still sits where it was first placed.

## The Growing Things

Very little grows, and what grows is small.

Lichens are everywhere. Grey-green crusts cover the lee walls, orange rosettes mark where birds once perched, and black tar-like films spread in the channels where water runs longest. Mosses fill the joints on the windward faces in cushions that hold water like sponges and release it slowly through the dry hours.

In the courtyards, where cisterns keep the ground damp, there are cushion plants: tight domes of tiny leaves, pink-flowered for two weeks in summer, nearly indistinguishable from the stone domes around them. Here and there a stunted juniper grows out of a wall, bent flat by wind, a hundred years old and knee-high. People leave them alone. A juniper's roots hold a wall together as well as they break it apart, and no one has decided which matters more.

There are no fields in the city. Whatever the inhabitants eat comes up from below the escarpment, or from the peat meadows on the plateau's far northern edge, where the city thins and ends.

## The Animals

They are rare, and they are quiet.

A small round-eared mammal, something like a pika, lives in the rubble of fallen domes. It is seen as a flicker of grey-brown and heard, if at all, as a single thin whistle before it vanishes into the stones. It harvests the cushion plants and stores them in dry cells the inhabitants have long since abandoned to it.

A raptor hunts these, riding the updraft along the escarpment rim in the late afternoon and hanging motionless above the rooftops. It is solitary. Its shadow crossing the domes is more often noticed than the bird itself.

In the wettest districts, big pale snails graze the lichen films in the channels, leaving shining tracks that the next morning's condensation erases.

That is nearly the whole census. On the edges there are occasional ravens. In autumn a flock of small grey finches passes through, eating seeds from the junipers, and is gone within the week.

## The People

They exist, though visitors sometimes doubt it for the first day. The city is vast, and its population is thin across it. Whole districts are empty, maintained by habit and by the occasional passing hand. Where people do live, they live the way the stones are laid: close, fitted, quiet.

They build and rebuild by hand, with no tools but the flat of a palm to tap a stone into its seat. They drink from the walls, cupping their hands under a weeping joint in the morning. They speak softly, because in the lanes voices carry strangely, doubling and dying against the stone. They mark nothing. The city has no inscriptions, no carvings, no signs. You find your way by the grain of the walls, by which way the lichen grows, and by the sound of water getting louder toward the cisterns.

## The Day

The dynamics of the place are daily, and they are large.

**Before dawn,** the city is cold and dry-surfaced. The stones have radiated their heat into a clear night sky, and a thin rime whitens the capstones.

**At first light,** the lowland cloud begins to climb. It comes over the escarpment rim as a slow grey wall. It pours into the windward lanes, fills the courtyards, rises between the domes. Within an hour the western city is inside cloud. Visibility drops to a few walls' length, sound flattens to nothing, and the stones begin to weep.

**By mid-morning,** the fog front has rolled miles inland. Behind it the western districts are running with water. Ahead of it the eastern districts still stand in low, clear sun, casting long shadows across the domes.

**By noon,** the cloud has spent much of its water on the stones. It thins and lifts, and the light comes back hard and clean, normal daylight, grey stone and white sky. The city steams faintly.

**In the afternoon,** something happens that only makes sense at this scale. The city is so large, and its surface so rough and so wet, that it breathes its own weather. Warmed stone and evaporating water send air rising in columns over the districts. Cumulus build above the city in lines, following the grain of the walls. By three or four they are dark-bottomed. By five, on most days, they rain back onto the city that made them: short, heavy, quiet showers that set every channel running at once.

**At dusk,** the cloud streets drift east beyond the city's edge and dissolve over the dry plateau. The sky clears. The stones cool. The last water drips from the capstones into the cisterns, and the pika whistles once from a fallen dome.

**At night,** it is nearly silent: only the tick of cooling stone and the slow drip of water finding the joint it always finds.

## The Seasons

In summer the fog is thickest and the city wettest. The cushion plants flower and the cisterns fill.

In autumn the finches pass, and the afternoon storms grow violent enough to bring down walls.

In winter the fog freezes on contact, and the windward districts are glazed in rime ice inches thick. The combing becomes literal: long feathers of ice grow into the wind from every stone edge, sometimes an arm's length long. They are beautiful and heavy, and they break walls. When they melt in the late-winter sun, the whole city drips for days.

In spring the walls that failed are gathered up and laid again.

## What the Place Is

It is a city the size of a weather system, built by hand out of stones that were already there, fed by a cloud that comes to it every morning. It has been built long enough that it has become a landform, and it behaves like one. It erodes, settles, drains, and generates its own rain. Its people keep it the way a streambed is kept by its stream: continuously, quietly, without ever finishing.

Standing in one of its lanes at mid-morning, inside the cloud, you can hear almost nothing. There is only water running in threads down the stones on either side of you, close enough to touch, all the way to the edge of the world.
