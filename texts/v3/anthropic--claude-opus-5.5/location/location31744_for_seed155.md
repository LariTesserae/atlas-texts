# The Relapsing Works of the High Table

## The Plateau

It begins as a horizon that is too straight.

You come up out of the cloud on a switchback that was once a road and is now a groove worn into living root. The last of the mountain falls away beneath, and ahead is the Table: a plateau so old it has forgotten it was ever a range. Whatever peaks stood here were ground down over spans of time that make the word *erosion* feel impatient. What is left is a tableland a day's walk across in any direction. It is almost perfectly level, and it sits high enough that the clouds move below its rim like a slow grey sea.

The air is thin and cool, around the temperature of a cellar. It carries a smell you will spend days trying to name: wet sawdust, ozone, green sap, and faintly, something like hot iron.

Nothing here is stone anymore, or almost nothing. The ground is a root-mat many metres deep, a felted mass of fibre so old and tightly woven that it rings faintly when you stamp. Here and there a knuckle of granite breaks the surface, rounded smooth as a bar of soap. These are the last bones of the mountains, and the roots are slowly taking them too. Pressed close, they are warm, and you can feel something working inside them.

The whole Table is vegetation. Moss covers the ground, and lichen covers the moss. Small grasses, knee-high sedges, and cushions of pale succulent grow from the lichen. The air itself is full of drifting seed, spore, and pollen in slow gold currents that catch the light. There is no bare surface anywhere. If you sit still long enough, a fine green down begins on the cuffs of your trousers.

Standing out of all this, spread widely across the plain, is the city.

## The Works

It is an industrial city. You understand this before you understand anything else, because you can hear it.

The first buildings are perhaps a kilometre off. Chimney-stacks forty metres tall rise from long-roofed halls, with gantries, water towers, and the diagonal lattice of a conveyor housing. Further out, scattered to the edge of sight, stand others: a rail-yard, a turbine house, a row of kilns like upturned bells, and a clocktower. They are placed the way a city is placed when the land is cheap and flat, with a great deal of space between them. Long green emptiness separates each structure from the next, crossed by rail lines and cables.

Every part of it is plant.

The chimneys are hollow trunks, their bark laid in rings like courses of brick, and the bark keeps the colour of brick: a dull, cured red-brown. The halls have walls of tight-grown timber whose grain runs in the pattern of mortared blocks. Their roofs are sheet-thin, overlapping leaves, broad as tables and ridged like corrugated iron. The water towers are gourds the size of houses, standing on legs of living cane. The rails are two parallel roots running kilometre after kilometre across the Table, each exactly the gauge of the other. The sleepers beneath them are cross-grown runners sprouting little leaves at their ends.

Up close, the illusion does not break. It deepens. Rivets are knots. Hinges are joints of flexed green wood that open and shut with a creak. Gauges on the boiler-houses are round seed-heads whose inner florets are arranged in numbers around a stiff central stalk, a needle that quivers and rises. Chains hang from the gantries, woody vine in interlocking links, heavy and cold to the touch and faintly damp.

## How It Came to Be

This is the first rule of the place: **the city was not built of plants. It became plants.**

Somewhere in the long past the Table was a working city of iron and brick and steam. Then, over an age of weathering, the kind that flattens mountains, the living mat crept into it. It did what minerals do to buried wood in the petrifying mud of rivers, only in reverse. Cell by cell, grain by grain, each particle of iron and fired clay was replaced by living tissue. Every bolt was replaced with a knot of the same shape. Every beam was replaced with heartwood of the same span. The mortar lines became bark seams, the rust became lichen, and the paint became pigment in the leaves.

The replacement was perfect, and so was its memory. The plants did not only take the shape of the machines. They took their function. The city never stopped running.

## Sound

The noise of the Works carries across the whole Table. It is loud, but it is not a metal noise. It is the noise of an industrial city played on wood.

Drop-hammers inside the forging halls are great pistons of heartwood. Their fall is a deep percussive *thock* that you feel in your sternum from half a kilometre away. The rhythm comes in groups, four blows and a pause, and every hall keeps its own tempo. Walking between them, you pass through overlapping measures, as if moving through the sections of an enormous orchestra.

Belts of woven fibre run between drive-wheels with a whine that rises and falls. The turbine house makes a vast hollow roar, like wind in a well. Steam leaks from the joints of cane pipework in long wet hisses. The rail-trams scream as they run, gourd-bodied cars with seed-pod wheels on their root rails, because the friction of wood on living wood makes a sound like a stick drawn along a fence. That sound is a thousand times louder than it should be, and sweeter, and more awful.

The worst sound is the shift-whistle. At irregular intervals, which you cannot predict but come to dread, every chimney sounds at once. They are hollow reed-stacks forty metres tall, and they give a chord so low and so vast that the moss on the ground flattens outward in rings around each stack. Seed-drift in the air is blown into sudden clear bubbles of emptiness. You will cover your ears and it will not help.

Between whistles, when the wind is right, there are moments of odd softness. Beneath the Works you can hear the root-mat itself: a continuous low creak, the sound of a floor settling in an old house, made by a floor a hundred kilometres wide.

## Water

The Table is damp but not wet. There are no rivers up here, no standing lakes, and rain is occasional and thin. What moisture there is comes mostly from the Works.

The steam the city breathes out rises from the chimneys in white columns, cools in the high air, and falls back as a fine drizzle. The weather is therefore strongest near the halls. Each building sits inside its own soft personal rain. The moss grows lushest in a circle around it, and the circles fade into drier, yellower moor in the long spaces between. A map of the vegetation would show the city as a scatter of green rings on ochre.

Mornings are foggy. Cold air settles on the root-mat overnight, and at dawn a low mist lifts off the ground to waist height. The building-trunks rise out of it like the pilings of a pier. Every surface sweats. Chains drip. Gauge-faces bead with condensation. Water collects in the cupped leaves of the roofs and runs down leaf-gutters into cisterns. These are hollow burls at the base of each hall, full of clear, faintly sweet water that tastes of pine.

A few places hold standing water: the old quench-tanks beside the forges, still full, dark, and cold. Something grows in them in long ribbons that pulse when the hammers fall.

## Power

The city is electrified.

Transmission lines run between the halls on tall living poles: vines, taut and glossy, strung in parallel sets of three. They carry current. You can hear them hum on quiet mornings. Where they cross the damp moor, the drizzle beads along them and sizzles. At every junction and insulator, which here is a bulb of hard white seed-casing, the air smells sharply of ozone. At dusk you see small blue arcs jumping at the joins, and moths gathering to them.

The current comes from the turbine house, where tall spiral ferns stand in a water-race fed by the cisterns. They turn slowly, and they are both the wheel and the generator. Nobody has ever found where the energy truly originates. The water is too little and the ferns turn too slowly for the power the city draws. The vines carry more than they should. The accounts do not balance.

## What the Works Make

The loading doors of the halls open, sometimes, on hinges of green wood. Out come crates of wicker on the gourd-trams. They carry the product along the root rails to a rail-yard at the edge of the Table, where the rails end at the rim of the cloud and the crates are tipped over.

Open one before it goes, and it is full of seeds.

The seeds are small and hard and perfectly shaped. There are seeds like hex bolts, seeds like cogwheels with tiny teeth, seeds in the shape of springs, of valves, of pressure gauges no bigger than a fingernail. Push one into the moss and water it, and within a week it will have germinated into the thing it resembles. It will be a living valve in green wood, opening and closing on its own. Leave it longer and it will begin, very slowly, to grow the machine it belongs to.

What is below the cloud-sea, where the seeds have been falling for longer than anyone can reckon, is not known.

## Presences

There are few animals on the Table. It is not a lifeless place, but everything here is plant, and it seems to leave little room for anything else.

There is a moth, large and slate-grey with wings patterned like hammered sheet metal. It nests inside the junction boxes of the transmission lines and feeds, as far as anyone can tell, on the arcs.

There is one kind of crow. It walks the rails in pairs and picks at the sprouting sleepers. Its call is exactly the squeal of the trams. It is unclear which learned the sound from the other.

Once or twice, on a long walk, you may see a figure on a gantry, or in a doorway, or walking the long straight rails into the distance. It wears overalls and a cap, and it moves in the patient, habitual way of a worker going to shift. Up close, if you ever get close, it is a shell of bark in a human shape. It is hollow inside and green-lined, like a husk from which something has already emerged. The figures are always at the edge of your vision, always walking away. They are the plant's memory of the workers who must once have been here, grown in the same form, with the same purpose, and emptier than anything else on the Table.

## Rules of the Place

The world is thin here. Reality is breaking down along its seams, and the seam it breaks along most often is the old conversion from iron to wood.

**The Relapse.** Sometimes, without warning, a part of the city remembers what it was. A wall you have your hand on turns briefly to brick, cold and gritty, the mortar crumbling under your palm. It lasts one second or two, then it is warm timber again. A chain in the rain becomes iron for the length of a breath, rust flakes falling into the moss, and then goes back to vine. During a strong relapse a whole hall may flicker. You see, just for a moment, a black iron engine-house under a smoke-dark sky, with men on the gantries, and hear the true iron clang. The sky shifts colour, and then it is green again, and the men are husks. After a relapse, the moss around the building dies back in a clean ring, as if poisoned, and takes weeks to regrow.

**Growth rings.** Cut any beam in the city and its rings are not rings. They are street plans. Each layer shows the arrangement of the Works in some past or future year. Buildings appear in the outer rings that are not on the Table now. Some beams have more rings than the city can possibly have had years. Some show the Table with no city at all, or a city that fills it edge to edge.

**Distance.** The spaces between buildings are wide and empty, and not reliable. The street grid is correct only from above, as the crows see it. On foot, walking a straight rail toward a chimney on the horizon, you may reach it in half an hour, or never. The surest way to reach a place is to walk toward its sound. The Works are laid out not by position but by noise. If you can hear a hall's particular hammer-rhythm, and walk toward it with your eyes half closed, you will arrive. Walk by sight, and you will circle.

**The Clocktower.** Its face is a great sunflower, its numerals a ring of petals, its hands two stiff tendrils. It tracks a sun, but not the one in the sky. On clear days, the shadows of every building on the Table fall at the angle the clocktower gives, not the angle of the light. When the shift-whistle blows, the clock jumps, and every shadow on the plateau swings round at once like the needle of a compass.

## Cycles

The Table is not still. Its processes overlap and run at different paces.

Daily, the fog lifts. Then comes the steam-drizzle, the long working noise of the day, the arcs at dusk, and the cold still night. At night the hammers slow but never stop, and you hear the root-mat creak.

Seasonally, the buildings molt. In the cooler months the brick-bark of the chimneys loosens and falls in long curls. It piles at their bases like shed skin, and the new courses beneath are brighter and redder. The roof-leaves yellow, drop, and regrow. For a few weeks, the whole city looks like a ruin being rebuilt.

Irregularly, an engine fails. A drop-hammer splits or a drive-wheel rots, and the hall falls silent, suddenly and strangely. Moss floods into the quiet. Then, over a month or a year, a new hammer grows from the stump of the old one, and one morning the rhythm starts again. The silence it leaves behind is almost louder than the sound.

And very slowly, over the full span of the Table's age, the ground rises into the city. The root-mat thickens and climbs. It is taking the lower storeys of the halls into itself, as it once took the mountains. Doors that were at ground level are now halfway up a slope of moss. Somewhere under your feet are the buried roofs of earlier Works, grown over and forgotten, and still, faintly, if you lie with your ear to the mat, running.

## At the Rim

Walk far enough and you reach the edge of the Table, at the end of the rails, where the crates are tipped. The noise of the Works is behind you, the cloud-sea spreads below, and the seed-drift pours over the rim in slow gold streams.

The wind here comes up from below. It is warmer, and it carries a sound you did not expect: from somewhere down in the cloud, far away, a hammer-rhythm in groups of four, with a pause. It is played on wood.
