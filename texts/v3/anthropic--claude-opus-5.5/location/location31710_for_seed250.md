# The Lifted Water

## I. The Shape of It

The ocean does not lie flat here. It stands.

Across a continent of new black stone, a single mass of seawater rises into a dome several kilometers high at its crown and nearly a thousand kilometers across. Nothing contains it. There are no walls, basin, or rim. By every ordinary rule the water should spill outward and find its level. It doesn't. It holds its shape the way a drop holds on a waxed leaf, except that the leaf is a plateau four thousand meters above the planet's old sea line, and the drop is large enough to have weather inside it.

Call it the Lens. It is less an ocean on high ground than an ocean *placed* on high ground, and kept there.

The plateau beneath it is young. It is so young that the word "landscape" hardly fits yet, because it hasn't had time to become one. There are no valleys, because nothing has carved them. There is no soil, because nothing has broken the rock down. There are no rounded edges anywhere. The stone is basalt and glass, poured and cracked and poured again, and it still carries the exact forms it cooled into: ropes, plates, blisters, columns with facets as crisp as cut crystal. If you ran a hand along almost any surface here, it would cut you.

## II. Under the Dome

Most of the plateau is submerged, and submerged strangely.

The water is extraordinarily clear. It is not the tropical clarity of lagoons but something colder and emptier, closer to distilled water than to sea. There is almost nothing suspended in it: no plankton, no silt, none of the drifting snow of organic matter that clouds every living ocean. Sunlight comes down through kilometers of it at nearly full strength, blue-white and steady, and lays moving nets of caustic light across the basalt far below. At noon you can stand on the floor of the Lens, if you could stand there, and read by daylight under three thousand meters of sea.

It isn't crushing, either, or not as it should be. The pressure falls off wrongly with depth, as if the water weighs less the deeper you go. Whatever holds the Lens up works *through* the water, bleeding some of its weight into a gradient that current physics has no clean language for. Near the floor, loose objects drift upward a little too easily. Bubbles rise in curved paths, bending toward the center of the dome as though they were falling sideways. A dropped stone sinks, pauses, and sinks again in stages, like something descending a staircase no one can see.

This is the technology of the place, and it is nearly invisible. There is no machine. No pylons, generators, or conduits exist anywhere on the plateau. The engineering, whoever did it, lives in the behavior of matter rather than in objects. The only traces are effects: water that stands up, weight that slips, and a hum at the edge of hearing that seems to come from the rock itself.

There is one other trace, and it is easy to miss. In a few places the basalt is scored by perfectly straight lines. They are hairline grooves, kilometers long, crossing lava flows without deviating. They meet at exact angles. They might be survey marks, or seams where something was aligned, or the grain of a field pressed into stone as it cooled. They are the only straight lines on the continent, and they are the closest thing to a made artifact here.

## III. The Noise

It is loud down there, and the noise is geological.

Water carries sound far better than air, and the Lens is full of events. The plateau is still forming. Fissures open in the floor and exhale magma into the cold water, and every one of those contacts is violent. Steam flashes and collapses so fast that the vapor bubbles implode, producing a crackling roar like a forest fire heard through a wall. New rock shatters as it chills. Pillows of lava swell, split, and swell again, each rupture a sharp report. Somewhere, always, something is breaking.

Beneath that runs the sound of the rock itself. Freshly cooled basalt contracts, and a continent of it contracting at once makes a constant low groaning, broken by deep cracks that you feel in the chest more than you hear. When a column-field settles, hundreds of hexagonal pillars shift against one another with a noise like a colossal chord struck on stone.

And under everything is the Lens's own voice. The water at the boundary of the field doesn't sit still; it shears. The result is a vast, droning moan that changes pitch as the dome flexes. When the Lens is stable, it is a steady organ note. When it isn't, the note bends, and the whole ocean sounds as if it's straining.

## IV. Weather Inside the Water

The Lens is big enough to have climate, and its climate is unstable.

The heat from the plateau's vents warms water at the floor. That water should rise and spread, but the field distorts buoyancy, so the convection doesn't close neatly. It spirals. Enormous rotating columns of warmer water form above the most active fissures and twist upward through the dome. They are underwater cyclones, visible only as shimmering refraction, like heat haze made of sea. When one reaches the upper surface, it lifts the skin of the Lens into a travelling hill of water hundreds of meters high, and the moan of the dome jumps to a shriek.

Currents move across the floor at the speed of rivers in flood. They scour nothing, because there is nothing loose to scour. The rock is too fresh and too hard, and the water too clean to carry abrasive sediment. Erosion, which on older worlds is the slow sculptor of everything, has barely begun here. The currents only strip away the thin films of glass flaking off cooling flows and fling them upward in glittering storms that settle slowly in the strange weight.

## V. The Edge

The Lens is not fixed. It drifts.

Over weeks, the entire dome slides across the plateau, a few kilometers a day, like a bubble migrating on a slow wind. Where it arrives, land drowns. Where it leaves, land rises back into the air, streaming.

The edge is the most violent place on the continent. Here the ocean ends in a wall: a curved, near-vertical face of water standing kilometers high, its surface trembling, lit through from behind so that it glows turquoise at the base and shades to white near the top, where it merges with the sky. There is no splash at its foot, only a sharp transition, water on one side and cold air on the other. The field holds the boundary taut.

It doesn't hold it perfectly. Wherever the edge advances over broken ground, the wall sheds. Sheets peel away from its face and fall as curtains of rain. Columns burst outward and collapse in thunder. Mist boils off constantly, so the boundary is wrapped in cloud, and inside the cloud the air roars with falling water. At the trailing edge, the land coming out of the Lens drains in a thousand simultaneous waterfalls, pouring off every ledge and column-top, hissing into fissures still hot underneath. Steam and spray tower above it all.

Standing on the dry plateau and watching the wall approach would be like watching a mountain range made of glass walk toward you through its own storm.

## VI. Cold

Despite the vents, the place is cool. The plateau is high, the air thin and clear, and the water of the Lens, away from the fissures, is only a few degrees above freezing. The exposed ground is swept by wind that comes down off the dome's flanks, chilled by evaporation. Mist freezes into rime on the sharpest ridges at night. By day, in the direct sun, the black rock warms and the frost vanishes in minutes, sending up thin threads of vapor.

The light itself is ordinary, clear daylight from a normal sun. That's partly what makes the place so unsettling. Nothing is hidden in murk or glow. You can see the impossible water plainly, in honest light, standing where it shouldn't.

## VII. What Lives

Nothing moves of its own will here. There are no animals at all: no fish in the Lens, no insects on the rock, no birds on the wind. The emptiness is total, and the continent feels vacant in the way a newly finished room does, clean and waiting.

There is, sparsely, something green.

In the zones the Lens has recently crossed, where rock is wet and not too hot, pioneer growth takes hold: thin films of algae, slick and dark olive, spreading across the glassy surfaces in irregular stains. On older flows, which may have been exposed for a few years, there are crusts closer to lichen, grey-green and flat, clinging in the shadowed sides of cracks. They are barely there, a faint discoloration that becomes obvious only in the wet hours after the Lens has passed.

Whether they arrived by accident or were seeded as part of whatever process is building this place, nothing says. But their distribution is suspicious. They grow densest along the straight grooves in the rock, following the lines like frost follows a windowpane's seams.

## VIII. What This Place Is

It is a world being made, and the making is not finished.

Something set this ocean on a mountain of new stone and left it to move. The Lens wanders, drowning and uncovering the plateau, while the fissures push up more rock, and the rock cools, cracks, and groans. The water cycles through heat and cold, cyclone and calm, and very slowly, where it touches bare stone, the first films of life take hold. No one tends it. No structure oversees it. The process runs on its own terms, loudly, turbulently, in plain daylight.

In ten thousand years there may be soil here, and shorelines, and eroded valleys. The Lens might finally be let down to find its level, or it might still be wandering. For now the place is raw: an ocean standing up, a continent coming into being beneath it, and between them an enormous emptiness filled only with light, cold water, and noise.
