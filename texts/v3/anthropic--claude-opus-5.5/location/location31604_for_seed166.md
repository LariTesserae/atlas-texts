# The Intake Spring

Below the surface of a quiet field there is a hole in the limestone, and the hole is full of water to its brim. From above it looks like a pond no wider than a village square, rimmed with turf and a few leaning alders. But it isn't a pond. It's the open top of a flooded chamber that goes straight down, and nearly all of it lies beneath the waterline.

## The Shape of It

Once you are under, the walls fall away. The rock bells outward like the inside of a bottle, so that three meters down the chamber is twice as wide as its mouth. The limestone is pale gray going to cream, and it has been water-worn for so long that no edge in it is sharp. Old fracture lines have softened into grooves you could lay your arm in. Shelves step down the north wall like the seats of a small amphitheater, each lip rounded and each tread carpeted with a fine buff silt, soft as flour.

The chamber is not large. You could swim across it in thirty strokes. It holds perhaps the volume of a parish church, turned upside down and sunk into the ground. At about twenty-two meters the floor narrows into a slot running northeast to southwest, and the slot goes down past where light reaches. This is where the water comes from. The aquifer breathes up through that crack, slowly and without pause, as it has since before the field was a field.

## Light

The daylight here is ordinary. It enters through the round mouth of the pool and falls as a single column, slightly tilted with the sun's hour, bright green-white at the top and thinning to blue as it descends. The water is so clear that the column has edges. Inside it, suspended grains of silt glitter faintly. Outside it, the chamber's flanks sit in a cool, even dimness where you can still read the texture of the stone. The light doesn't flicker, because the surface above is rarely rippled. The alders break the wind, and the spring rises too gently to stir it.

Below eighteen meters the blue deepens toward slate. At the mouth of the slot it becomes a darkness with no color at all.

## Cold

The water holds at nine degrees, in every season. Frost on the field in January and haymaking heat in August make no difference down here. The aquifer is too deep and too old to notice weather. The cold is not harsh. It is the steady, clean cold of a cellar or the back of a mountain, and it presses evenly on the skin from every side.

## What Grows

Very little grows, and all of it grows close to the light. On the uppermost shelf, where the sun's column touches stone for a few hours each day, there are stands of stonewort. They are brittle, whorled, gray-green plants, crusted with lime, that look more like tiny candelabra than weeds. A dark moss fringes the rim just under the waterline and trails downward in slow tresses. A thin film of green-brown diatoms stains the sunward faces of the rock, fading out a few meters down. Beyond that the stone is bare, clean and pale, furred only with silt.

There are no fish. There are no snails, shrimp, or larvae. The water comes up from so deep, so cold, and so poor in food that nothing has followed it in, and nothing that falls in from the field above survives long enough to stay. It is sterile in the way good drinking water is sterile. The spring is valued for exactly that.

## The One Made Thing

On the east side, bolted to the wall at eleven meters, is the intake.

It is a single iron pipe, broad enough to swallow a child, with a bell mouth covered by a coarse bronze screen. The iron has gone the color of dark chocolate, softened at its edges by a skin of oxide and lime. It rises along the wall on three brackets, breaks the surface under a small concrete apron hidden in the alders, and runs off underground toward a works a kilometer away. Up there, beyond the reach of any sound you can hear, pumps draw on it day and night. The water leaves as cooling water for turbines, as process water for a mill, and as the town's supply, clean enough to go to the tap untreated.

Beside the pipe hangs a single armored cable and a float-gauge chain, both taut and plumb. That is the whole of the human presence: one pipe, one cable, one chain. Nobody comes down here. Nobody needs to.

## Sound

It is mostly quiet. Your ears fill with the soft, pressured hush of water. Under that, if you put a hand flat on the rock, there is a hum. It is not a sound so much as a steady trembling in the stone, the far-off motors transmitted through the bedrock and the pipe. It never rises or falls. Close to the screen it becomes faintly audible, a low, smooth draw like a very long breath being taken and never finished. Sometimes a bead of gas works loose from the silt near the slot and rises, ticking once against the rock as it goes. Otherwise there is nothing.

## The Rule of the Place

Everything here is balanced, and the balance holds. The spring pushes up from below at exactly the rate the pumps take it out through the side, so the level never moves. The gauge chain has not shifted a link in years. The draw is gentle enough that the silt on the shelves lies undisturbed, yet steady enough that the water is never still. There is always a faint, near-invisible drift from the slot toward the screen. A grain of silt lifted into the light column will travel sideways across it, patiently, for a quarter of an hour before vanishing into the bronze mesh.

Nothing grows larger. The stoneworts are the same stoneworts, in the same places, at the same height. The stone erodes, but at a rate measured in the lifetimes of towns. The iron corrodes, but so slowly under its lime skin that it seems to have reached an agreement with the water. The same cold, the same light, the same hum, the same slow sideways drift persist today, tomorrow, and in whatever season it is up in the field.

Beneath the grass, a small, clear room of water breathes steadily into a machine it will never see, and neither one of them changes.
