# The Delta That Builds Itself

## First Impression: Noise

The first thing is the noise, before you see anything or feel the heat. It doesn't arrive as a sound so much as a pressure, a solid thing leaning against your chest. It has no single source. It comes up through your feet and down from the haze, and after a while you understand that it comes from everywhere at once, from the whole visible world and the thousands of kilometers beyond it.

It is the sound of a city being made, unmade, and made again, continuously, across a delta the size of a subcontinent.

## The Ground

The land is low: a vast alluvial plain where some ancient river system, or several braided together, once spread its sediment across a basin so wide the curvature of the planet hides the far edges. The ground rises perhaps a few dozen meters above the sea over its entire breadth. Water is never far down. Dig a hand's depth anywhere the city isn't standing and the hole fills with warm, brown, faintly metallic seep.

But there is almost nowhere the city isn't standing.

The city is made of the delta itself. Its builders, whoever they were, and whether any of them remain is one of the open questions, learned long ago to instruct silt. Clay, sand, and fine river mud are drawn up, sorted grain by grain, and bonded into forms: walls that curve like the insides of shells, towers ribbed like the stems of mushrooms, spans and ramps and terraces glazed smooth and colored every shade of the river. Ochre, umber, slate, a greenish grey, a pale buff almost like bone. Nothing here is imported. The city is a rearrangement of the ground it stands on.

That rearrangement never stops.

## The Tides of Making

Every structure in the delta has a lifespan, and none of them are long. A tower might stand for a season, a footbridge for a few days. Then a slow front of dissolution moves through a district. The locals call it the *slack*, and older texts call it the unbinding. The glaze goes dull. Edges soften. Walls sag like wet paper and slump back into warm mud, which runs down the gutters and channels in thick rivulets.

Behind the slack, never more than a few hours back, comes the *make*. The mud gathers itself. It climbs. Filaments of sediment rise in columns, faintly humming, held by fields nobody sees but everyone feels as a prickle on the skin and a metallic taste on the tongue. Forms emerge the way frost emerges on glass, all at once and everywhere, branching and filling. A new district stands where the old one fell: related to it, recognizably of the same family, but never identical. Streets shift a few meters. A plaza becomes a stair. A dome becomes a spire.

The two fronts sweep across the delta in patterns too large to see from the ground. Seen from very high up, the city is said to look like the surface of a boiling pot, or like the skin of a cuttlefish: waves of dissolution and emergence rippling outward, colliding, interfering, spiraling around fixed points nobody fully understands.

This is the turbulence of the place. Solid ground here is a temporary agreement.

## Why It Is Loud

Making is loud. The silt does not rise quietly. Every column of sorted grain grinds and hisses as it climbs, and the fields that hold it thrum at frequencies that rattle teeth. When a structure sets, its glaze cures with a sound like ice cracking on a lake, a sharp, ringing percussion. Thousands of these happen every second across the visible horizon, so the air rings with a constant rain of distant gunshots.

The slack is loud in a different register. Collapsing walls make a wet roar. Mud sheets down stairways. Old struts give way with deep groans that go on and on.

Under all of it runs the base note: the pumps, or whatever serves as pumps, drawing water up through the sediment and pushing it back down. It sounds like a heartbeat slowed to one beat every few seconds, and it is so low that you don't hear it at all. You feel it in your ribs, and in the ripples that shiver across every standing puddle in time with it.

Nobody who lives here speaks aloud. There would be no point. The people of the delta, and there are people, in enormous numbers, talk with their hands, in a gestural language as rich and fast as any spoken one. Close up, they press palms and read each other's vibrations. Many wear seals of soft glazed clay molded into their ears. Children born here are said to sleep poorly on the rare occasions they leave. Silence frightens them.

## The Wet Heat

The delta is warm, and it is always wet, but not in the way of a swamp. It is wet the way a kitchen is wet when every pot has been boiling for a thousand years.

The making throws off heat. So do the fields, and so does the constant work of lifting and sorting. Across a continent-sized city, that heat adds up to a climate. The air sits heavy, humid, around body temperature, smelling of hot clay, wet stone, and the ozone tang of the fields. Every cool surface sweats. The shaded undersides of arches drip steadily. Walls weep condensation in slow sheets that streak the glaze and pool at their feet.

In the afternoons, the city makes its own weather. Heat rises off the densest districts in great columns, and the moisture lifted with it builds into towering thunderheads that stand over the delta like a second, taller city of cloud. By late day they break. The rain falls warm, hard, and briefly: mineral-heavy, slightly grey with suspended silt, tasting of iron and chalk. It hammers the glazed rooftops with a sound that, astonishingly, you can hear over everything else. For twenty minutes the whole delta is a drum.

Then the clouds tear open, the low sun comes through, and every wet surface blazes. Steam rises from the terraces. The gutters run brown and fast toward the sea.

The light is ordinary daylight, strong and clear under the haze, a little white-gold with suspended moisture. It is the one thing in the delta that behaves as expected. Shadows fall where they should. Morning comes up pink over the rooftops. There is comfort in that.

## No Green

Nothing grows here. Not in the way plants grow elsewhere.

Seeds do arrive, carried by birds or wind or river. They don't last. The slack takes everything. No root can hold in ground that liquefies every few weeks, and no stem can outrun the making. The delta has been bare of plants so long that its people have no everyday word for leaf.

What does persist is thinner and simpler. On the oldest, slowest walls, the handful of structures the slack seems to avoid, there are biofilms: slick skins of microbial growth in rust-orange, violet, and a deep glossy black. They streak downward in the paths of condensation, following the water. In the warm gutters, bacterial mats form ribbons that wave in the current. Where the fields run strongest, some of these films glow faintly at dusk, a dim bluish shimmer along the wet seams of the city. Whether they feed on the fields or are merely excited by them, nobody can say.

That is all the life that roots. Everything else moves.

## The Animals

And there is a great deal that moves.

The delta is crowded with animals, adapted over unknown ages to a world that dissolves under them. They share one trait: none of them keeps a fixed home. They follow the fronts.

**The mudswifts** are the most visible: small, sickle-winged birds in flocks of millions that pour across the sky in shapes like smoke. They nest in the freshly made structures, cementing cups of wet clay into the soft angles before the glaze sets, raising their young fast, in the few days a building lasts, and abandoning the nests before the slack arrives. They can sense it coming, the locals say, a day ahead. When the swifts leave a district all at once, people leave too.

**The ribbon eels** fill the gutters and channels, dense as noodles in a bowl, so many that the flowing mud seems to writhe. They feed on the bacterial mats and on whatever the slack releases. At the edges of the delta, where the gutters empty into the sea, the eel-runs are so thick that fishers simply dip baskets in.

**The glass crabs** are the strangest. They are fist-sized, with shells that are partly their own and partly accreted: fragments of the city's glaze, picked up and fused onto their backs, so every crab wears a patchwork of colored enamel from buildings that no longer exist. They swarm in the slack zones, clicking over the softening walls, eating something in the dissolving mud, some residue of the binding. Where they swarm thickest, the slack seems to move a little faster. Some people believe the crabs are part of the city's machinery, not animals at all. Others shrug. In the delta, the distinction has never been as clear as it is elsewhere.

**Roaches, midges, and mud-wasps** fill every warm crevice. Tall grey wading birds stalk the gutters, spearing eels. Bats, or something like bats, hang under the dripping arches by day in clusters of thousands, and at dusk they boil out in rivers against the orange sky, adding their shrill cries, just audible, to the roar.

**Dogs** run in packs through the streets, lean and russet and unnervingly quiet. They too speak with their bodies, reading the vibrations of the ground. They are said to be the best early warning of an unexpected slack, a local collapse out of rhythm with the fronts. When the dogs run, you run with them.

## The People

The human population is beyond counting. Density is the delta's other great fact: every made structure fills within hours of setting. People climb the new towers while the glaze is still warm, hang cloth partitions, set up stalls, cook, trade, sleep. Markets open on every terrace. Children run the ramps. Everything that can be carried is carried, because nothing that stays put survives.

They are nomads in a city, living a few days or weeks ahead of the slack and following the make. Their lives are organized around the fronts the way farmers' lives elsewhere are organized around seasons. They read the swifts, the crabs, and the pitch of the humming fields. They know the patterns the way sailors know currents, and they know too that the patterns are not perfectly reliable.

They did not build the city. They are adamant about this. They inherited it, or were born into it, or were perhaps left inside it. Its makers, whatever they were, are gone, or became it, or were never separate from it in the first place. The instructions that tell the silt what to become run somewhere beneath everything: deep in the sediment, in the water table, in the slow heartbeat of the pumps. No one has found their source. Expeditions that dig too deep find the mud getting warmer and warmer and more intricately ordered, until it sings in the hand. Then they come back up, or they don't.

## The Old Places

Not everything dissolves.

Scattered across the delta, far apart, stand a few structures the fronts pass around like water around stones. The people call them the *keels*. They are enormously old, and they look it. Where everything else in the city is crisp and freshly glazed, the keels are worn, their surfaces pitted and softened by centuries, maybe millennia, of warm rain. Their forms have been rounded by weather into something almost geological. They look less like buildings than like the eroded cores of hills. Condensation has carved channels down their flanks. The biofilms on them are thick as moss, layered in bands of color like the walls of a canyon.

The keels are quieter inside. Not silent, but quieter: the roar drops to something like heavy surf. People go there to speak aloud, for weddings, for grief, to hear their own voices. Mudswifts nest there year after year in the same cups. The water that weeps down their inner walls is cool, the only cool water in the delta.

Nobody knows what they are. Anchors, perhaps. The first structures, or the last. The places the city remembers.

Around each keel, the patterns of making and unmaking spiral outward in slow rings, a vast, turbulent, deafening, fertile machine turning around a single worn, patient stone. That is the rule of the place: everything changes, constantly and violently and everywhere, except for the few things that have changed so slowly they have become the ground itself.

The rain comes again in the afternoon, and the city drums, and steams, and rebuilds.
