# The Lantern Under the Pan

Above, there is nothing to mark it. A salt flat runs white to every horizon, cracked into plates the size of a sleeping body, and the only sign that the ground is hollow is a faint difference in the sound of your steps. Walk far enough and the crust thins and whitens further, from bone to milk to something almost glassy. Then there is a place where it isn't crust at all.

## The Roof

The chamber's ceiling is a single lens of selenite, a sheet of crystallized gypsum, clear and soft, grown over a very long time in the dark and then exposed from above as the desert ground wore away. It is perhaps four hands thick at the edges and only one at the center. From the surface it looks like a frozen puddle that never melts. From below it is the sky.

Sunlight falls on the salt pan with nowhere to go, and the selenite drinks it. The crystal's fibers run in long parallel columns, and they scatter and carry the light sideways, so it doesn't enter the chamber as a beam. It enters as a glow that seems to come from the whole ceiling at once, with no source. The chamber below is brilliantly, almost unbearably bright, and it has no shadows. A hand held up casts only a faint gray haze beneath itself. Things look lit from inside.

## The Hollow

The space is small by the measure of caves: a rough egg on its side, perhaps sixty paces long and forty across, and deep enough that a stone dropped from the ceiling's edge takes a breath to land. Small as it is, it feels vacant beyond reason. The floor is far down. The walls lean outward from it and then curve back in toward the lens, so the room is mostly air, a bright, warm, still volume of it.

The walls are the old rock the crystal grew in: banded mudstone in rust and cream and a strange mauve, smoothed to a satin finish. Nothing has scoured it quickly. Water has touched it, very slightly, every night for longer than there have been people. The bands don't run level. They slump and fold like a cloth laid over something that has since been taken away. Along the lower third, the rock gives way to a pale crust of gypsum rosettes, hundreds of them, each the size of a palm, their petals knife-thin and translucent at the tips, so the light passing through them blushes faintly amber.

It is warm, the steady warmth of a closed room in the sun. The desert above swings between scorching and cold. Down here the swing arrives late and softened, filtered through crystal and rock, so the chamber holds nearly one temperature all the time, a little above body heat at midafternoon and a little below it at dawn.

## Water

There is no pool, no stream, no drip-ringing. The water here is a matter of timing.

At night the lens cools faster than the air beneath it. The breath of the chamber rises, which is the moisture of every animal in it and of the damp mudstone, and it meets the cold crystal and condenses. By the last hour before dawn the underside of the ceiling is beaded all over with dew, thousands of droplets, each holding a pinprick of the chamber's lingering glow. Then the sun comes up on the salt, the crystal warms, and the dew lets go.

It doesn't fall as rain. The drops slide along the selenite's grain first, gathering and following the fiber lines toward a few low points in the dome, and fall from there as slow, thin threads. There are six of these places. Beneath each, over ages, the floor has grown a shallow basin rimmed with fine calcite terraces, like a pale stack of fingernails. For about an hour each morning the basins hold water, a finger's depth and warm as tea. By noon they are damp. By evening they are dry, glittering with a skin of fresh crystal.

Everything alive here keeps time by those six basins.

## Growth

Vegetation is sparse, and most of it isn't quite what a surface-dweller would call a plant.

Around each basin there is a halo of green: a velvet of algae and moss so short it reads as a stain, brightest at the wet center and fading outward to a rim of gray lichen. Bright as the chamber is, the light is the problem here, not the solution. There is too much of it and no shade, so the mosses grow in the shade they make for each other. Each cushion is domed and packed tight, and only the outer tips are green. Underneath they are colorless and damp.

In the gypsum rosettes, growing between the petals where a little moisture lingers, there is a single kind of fern. It has no proper name. Its fronds are glassy and nearly white, with a faint green only at the veins, and it unrolls so slowly that one fiddlehead takes most of a year to open. Its leaves turn edge-on to the ceiling in the brightest hours and flatten toward it at dusk, so over the course of a day the lower walls seem to shiver, very slowly, like something breathing in its sleep.

That is nearly all of it. Most of the chamber is bare stone and air.

## The Animals

For so empty a room, there are a great many animals, though you have to let your eyes adjust to the brightness to see them.

**The glass moths** are the most numerous. They are small, pale, and translucent-winged, and they rest on the underside of the lens in their thousands, head-down, clustered so thickly near the dome's center that the crystal looks frosted. Through the shadowless light they are hard to find until one moves. They drink the dew directly from the ceiling before it slides away. At the cooling hour after sunset they drift down through the whole height of the chamber, falling more than flying, open-winged and turning, like ash in a draft that isn't there. Then they rise again.

**The pan swifts** nest in a band of softer mudstone high on the north wall. Each nest is a cup of dried mud and moth wing. They leave through a hairline gap at the edge of the lens, a seam where crystal meets rock, just wide enough for a folded bird, and they return at dusk with insects from the salt flat. They are the only thing in the chamber that connects it to the world above, and they bring in the seeds, spores, and dust that everything else here eventually becomes. In the evening they circle the hollow in wide, soundless ellipses before settling. The glow makes their bellies look lit, like coals.

**The tallow snails** graze the basin rims, pale and slow. Their shells are so thin the green of what they've eaten shows through. They move with the wet. In the morning they gather at the water, by noon they have retreated to the edges, and by evening they are sealed to the stone with a membrane of dried mucus, waiting.

**The chalk skinks** are the strangest. They are lizards no longer than a finger, the white-mauve of the wall bands, and they live in the rosettes. They carry pebbles. Each skink keeps a small hoard, a dozen or so flakes of selenite chosen for flatness. At the warmest hour they lay them out on the floor in careful rows and lie on top of them. The crystal flakes stay cool longer than the stone, and nobody knows whether the skinks do it for that or for some other reason. They rearrange their flakes often, and they will fight over a good one. Over generations the floor near the rosettes has been paved in a loose, shifting, glittering mosaic that no single animal made and none of them could explain.

There are spiders too, and silverfish, and a beetle that lives only in swift nests. In the dried basins at night there are tiny crustaceans that hatch from the morning water, live one hour, and leave eggs in the new crystal skin to wait for tomorrow's water or next year's.

## The Slow Light

Here is the thing about this place that doesn't behave.

The selenite holds light. Anyone who has seen gypsum knows it glows a little under certain conditions, but this lens is extraordinary in its thickness, its age, or something about the minerals of the pan. It keeps the day inside itself and lets it out slowly. When the sun sets on the salt flat, the chamber doesn't go dark. It dims by degrees across long hours, bright white fading to cream, then to a buttery gold, then to a faint rose. It doesn't reach true darkness until nearly two hours before dawn, and then only briefly, a short black interval before the sky above lightens again.

So the chamber runs on its own day, lagging the world above by hours. Its dusk comes at the world's midnight. The swifts come home in daylight to a chamber still bright and must wait for a night that arrives long after the one outside. The glass moths fly by the chamber's clock, not the sky's.

The lag isn't constant. It has been shifting for as long as the lens has existed. As the crystal thins by the slow dissolving of the dew, it holds less light and lets it go faster. The chamber's night is lengthening, though imperceptibly. Given long enough, the lens will be clear and thin as a window, and the hollow will simply share the sky's day. Given longer, it will crack and the salt will fall in. Nothing here will see it. The whole ecology is living inside one long, gentle exhale.

## Sound

It is mostly quiet. The rock absorbs noise, and the dome catches and softens what the rock misses. What you hear is the faint tick of a drop landing in a basin, the rustle of moths shifting on the lens like dry paper slowly folded, the soft click of a skink setting down a flake, and the swifts' wings, which make almost no noise at all.

Underneath all of it, at the chamber's warmest hour, there is a sound that seems to come from the ceiling. It is a very low, very soft note, felt in the breastbone as much as heard. The crystal expands as it warms, and the whole lens sings, once, for about as long as a held breath. Then it stops. It happens again at the cooling hour, a note fractionally higher. The animals don't react to it. They have never known the world without it.

## The Rope

Someone has been here. It was only once, and long ago.

Hanging from the seam at the lens's edge, where the swifts go in and out, there is a rope. It is twisted from some coarse desert grass, three strands, the lay still visible, though every fiber is now sheathed in gypsum. It has become a column of crystal in the shape of a rope, and it will never rot. It reaches about two-thirds of the way to the floor and ends in a knot.

Below it, at the base of the wall, there is a scatter of knapped flakes. They are not flint. They are selenite, which is far too soft for tools. Someone tried, broke a dozen edges, and gave up. Among them lies one flake that was ground into a disc with a hole bored through the middle, the kind of thing you might wear on a cord. On the wall above, at shoulder height, there is a single handprint in red ochre. The fingers are spread, and the thumb is pressed flat.

That is the entire record: someone came down on a grass rope into a room full of sourceless light, tried to make blades from glass that wouldn't hold an edge, made something to wear instead, put a hand on the wall, and either climbed back up or didn't.

The skinks have worked two of the failed flakes into their mosaic. The ochre has faded to the rust of the wall bands, and in the chamber's long gold dusk, it is almost impossible to tell apart from the stone.
