# The Floor

There is no ground here that was not poured, forged, riveted or laid. That is the first thing to understand, and it takes a long time to see, because the Floor is so old it has forgotten it was made.

## The Lie of the Land

From a low rise, and there are only low rises, the land runs out flat and dark in every direction. It is a plain the size of a county, grey-black and faintly glossy with wet. It is broken by long, gentle swells and by stubs of darker matter standing up here and there like worn teeth. You could mistake it for a moor after rain, or a tidal flat with the sea gone out. The horizon is soft and close under a low lid of vapour, and the eye keeps finding nothing to hold.

Walk down onto it and the illusion comes apart underfoot. The surface is plate: iron or something like it, laid in sheets each the size of a field. The seams between them have worn into shallow gullies a stride wide. The rivets that once held the sheets have eroded into smooth hummocks, domed and pitted like old stones in a riverbed. Where one plate has sagged below its neighbour, water has pooled and stayed so long that the edges have rounded into beaches. Their sand is black rust grains and fine grey grit that was once concrete.

The swells are not hills. They are machines, or the housings of machines, weathered down over an unthinkable span until only their broad shoulders remain above the plain. One long ridge, perhaps a mile from end to end and no higher than a barn, is the spine of some vast casing. Its surface has gone soft and granular, and it slopes away in the same angle of repose a sand dune would find. The worn teeth are chimneys, or were. None stands more than a few times a person's height now. They are hollow, their mouths ragged, and they are the tallest things for miles.

Everything has been ground toward level. Edges are gone. Corners are gone. Angles that some designer once drew sharp have become curves, and the curves have become slopes. The Floor is a built world that has been eroding for so long it now behaves like geology. It has watersheds, sediment and slow collapse. It is settling into the final form all made things reach if left long enough: flat, low, wet and quiet.

Except that it is not quiet.

## The Sound

The noise comes up through the soles first, before it arrives in the ears. It is a slow, immense stroke, *DOOM*, felt in the knees and the back teeth. After it comes a long exhalation of steam from everywhere at once, a hiss that fills the whole horizon. Then comes another stroke. Between the strokes runs a dense undercurrent of machine sound: grinding, a chattering like a million small gears, a high whine that comes and goes, and deep, irregular booms from much farther off, as if something enormous were dropping loads onto metal in the dark.

It is deafening. It never stops. It does not come from any one place, because the machinery is *below*. The Floor is a lid, and under the plates, under the weathered housings, the engines are still running. No one knows what they are, or whether they have ever been seen. Down there, where the erosion has not reached, pistons still drive, shafts still turn and furnaces still breathe. The sound comes up through a hundred miles of iron and makes the whole plain a soundboard.

The strokes set the time. There is no sun to count by, so the Floor keeps its hours in beats. The great stroke falls about once every eleven heartbeats. Its sub-rhythms, the chatter and the booms, cycle against it in patterns that repeat only over very long spans. If you stay long enough you begin to hear phrases and returns, the way a coast has tides.

## Water

Everything weeps.

The heat of the engines rises through the plates and meets the cool air above. The breath of the vents, thick with moisture, rises and cools and condenses. So the Floor is always wet, never drenched but always beaded, dripping and glistening. Water slides down the flanks of the machine-ridges in thin sheets. It collects in the seam-gullies and runs in slow braided streams along the lines where the plates meet, following a drainage pattern that was once an engineering grid. It has since wandered and silted into something almost natural.

The rain here is not weather in the usual sense. The vapour lid holds its moisture until the great stroke falls, and the shock shakes it loose. So the rain comes in pulses. A soft, warm patter follows each stroke, then there is a pause, then another patter. The drizzle of the Floor is a heartbeat.

The standing water shows the sound in a strange way. Every puddle and pool trembles on its surface in fine, fixed patterns: ridges and nodes, grids and hexagons and concentric rings. They hold their shape as long as the rhythm holds. Wide shallow flats of water on the low plates are printed all over with these standing ripples, sharp and faintly glinting, like etched glass. Each pool carries the fingerprint of the particular frequency passing beneath it. Walking across the Floor, you pass from one pattern-field to another, and you can read, from the water alone, what kind of machinery lies under your feet.

In the deepest basin sits the Stamp. It is not a machine you can see but a place where a single stroke falls harder than anywhere else. There the plates have been hammered down over ages into a bowl a mile across, and it holds a dark lake. The lake does not ripple. It *jumps*. At every stroke its whole surface leaps and hangs for a fraction of a moment, a sheet of water standing a finger's width in the air, and then falls back with a clap you feel in your lungs. The shore around it is worn perfectly smooth, polished to a dull mirror by the endless slap.

## Dark

It is almost always dark.

The vapour lid closes the sky, and whatever lies above it has never been seen. The light that exists comes from below and within, and there is not much of it.

Some chimney-stubs still breathe faintly, and their mouths glow a deep, sullen red, the colour of an ember just before it goes out. From a rise you can see them scattered across the plain like dying campfires, miles apart, each lighting a small ring of wet iron and not much else.

In places the plates have worn so thin that the furnaces show through. These are cracks, or pinholes, or a single hand-sized window of translucent slag, glowing orange from beneath. The light they give comes up through the water on the plates and makes the standing ripple-patterns burn like filigree.

There are also arcs. Here and there the old electrical systems are still alive, buried and corroded. When water reaches a live conductor, it lights up: a blue-white crack of light that jumps and snaps, loud even against the roar, then dies. These are unpredictable. They come in clusters along some forgotten line of current, a string of blue flashes marching across the dark plain like distant lightning run sideways along the ground. Then the blackness returns.

Between these few sources the dark is deep, wet and total. Walking across the Floor means walking from one faint glow toward the next, reading the ground by feel and by the patterns that water makes on it.

## What Grows

No animals live here. Nothing crawls in the seams, nothing flies through the vapour, and nothing burrows into the soft ridges. The Floor seems not to have been made for them, and they have never come.

But things grow, sparsely.

Mosses take hold in the rivet-hummocks and seam-gullies, where rust has turned to something like soil. They are dark mosses, almost black, with a faint coppery sheen, and they grow in low cushions that hold water. Lichens crust the chimney-stubs in bands. Rings of pale grey-green alternate with rust-orange, following the gradient of heat from the red mouth down to the cool base. On the long, gentle slopes of the machine-ridges, where eroded housing has become a kind of gravel, small ferns grow in scattered clumps. They are stiff and dark and curled inward.

Where the plants grow is shaped by the sound.

The noise is not the same everywhere. The rhythms of a thousand buried machines overlap, and where they meet they sometimes reinforce each other and sometimes cancel. Scattered across the Floor, miles apart, are places where the waves cancel almost completely. They are called *nodes*, or *hushes*. In a hush the roar falls away all at once, like stepping out of a river. The strokes still come, but softly, a distant knocking. The water in the pools lies flat and black, with no patterns at all.

The vegetation gathers in the hushes. In a node the moss grows thick and deep, the ferns grow tall, and there are even small, pale flowering things that exist nowhere else on the Floor. They are waxy and white, with five petals, and they open only in the stillness. A hush may be as small as a room or as large as a field. Each is an island of quiet greenery in the endless dark roar. Walking into one feels like entering a chapel.

But the hushes move. As the buried machines drift in their phases over long ages, the patterns of cancellation shift. A node slides, a few feet a year perhaps, across the plates. The vegetation follows it slowly. Growth creeps forward at the leading edge and dies back at the trailing edge, where the roar returns. So across the Floor there are long, faint trails of dead moss and dried fern stalks, the paths hushes have taken, like the tracks of slow creatures crossing the plain.

## The Shift

Once in a long while, the rhythm changes.

It is impossible to predict. Someone who stayed long enough might learn the signs: a certain phrase in the chatter, a long hesitation before a stroke. Then, all at once, every machine under the Floor changes pace together. The great stroke slows or quickens. The sub-rhythms stagger, reorganise and settle into a new key.

In the moment of change, the Floor convulses. The rain comes all at once, the whole lid of vapour shaken loose in a single warm deluge. Every ripple-pattern on every pool breaks and re-forms in a new geometry. The arcs flare in chains across the whole plain, and for a few seconds the land is lit blue-white to the horizon. It is the only time you can see the Floor all at once: the vast grey flatness, the worn ridges, the stub-chimneys and the gleaming water, all of it made and all of it old. Then the dark comes back.

After a shift, the hushes are in new places. The old vegetation is left stranded in the roar, and it begins, slowly, to die. Somewhere else, on bare wet iron, a node has opened, and in its stillness the first thin film of moss begins to grow.

## What It Is

Nobody knows who built the Floor, or what it was for, or what it is making now. Perhaps it was once a factory the size of a country, and its roofs and walls have eroded away, leaving only the floor and the machines below. Perhaps it was always open to the sky, a single made landscape meant to be exactly this. Perhaps the machines below are still producing whatever they were built to produce, and it is going somewhere, down or out through channels no one has found. Perhaps they are producing nothing, and simply running, because nothing ever told them to stop.

What is certain is that the Floor is ending. It is not ending quickly, or with any violence, but the way all old country ends: worn down, flattened out, settled toward level. The ridges will soften into swells, the swells into plains, and the chimneys will crumble into gravel. The plates will rust through, one by one, until the machinery lies open to the rain.

The engines, though, are still running, deep underneath. The stroke still falls every eleven heartbeats. The water on the plates still trembles in its patterns, and the white flowers still open in the hushes. On a low rise at the edge of the plain, in the dark, in the endless deafening roar, there is always a faint red glow, far off, and then another beyond it.
