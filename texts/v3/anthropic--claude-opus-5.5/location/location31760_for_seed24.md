# The Root Chamber

There is a crack in the floor of a dry country. It is barely wider than a person's shoulders and runs slantwise into a low swell of limestone that hardly rises above the plain around it. Nothing marks it except a fig tree that has no business growing here. It is squat and grey-barked, with leaves like leather, and it grows out of bare rock with nothing visible to drink. The tree is the only clue. Its life is underneath.

Go down through the crack, feet first, scraping, and the daylight ends within a body's length. After that there is only the chamber.

---

**The dark**

The darkness here is not night. Night has gradations, stars, a horizon that is a shade less black than the ground. This dark is complete. Open your eyes and close them and nothing changes. After a while the eye starts making its own faint lights, swimming blotches of violet and green, and you learn to ignore them the way you ignore your own pulse.

For a short span around midday, when the sun stands straight over the crack, a single thread of light reaches down. It is too weak to illuminate anything, only enough to be seen. It touches the floor as a smudge the size of a palm, pale gold going grey, and after perhaps half an hour it withdraws. The rest of the day, and all of the night, the chamber is lightless. It has been this way for a very long time.

So you learn the place by other senses.

---

**The warmth**

The first thing you notice is that it is warm. Not hot, not the white glare of the plain above, but warm the way a body is warm, or a loaf a few hours out of the oven. The rock has soaked up uncountable summers and gives the heat back evenly from every surface. Put a hand flat against a wall and it is the same temperature as your hand, so for a moment you cannot tell where skin ends and stone begins.

The warmth doesn't vary. It is the same at noon and midnight, in the dry season and the one that is only slightly less dry. The rock is too thick for the days above to reach through it. Down here there is only the long average of the climate, a single steady number that the chamber has held for centuries.

The air barely moves. There is no draft, because there is only the one opening. The air has settled into itself like water in a sealed jar. It smells of fine mineral dust, dry and slightly sweet, a smell like chalk and old paper. Beneath that is something fainter and older, a trace of smoke that has worked itself into the stone.

---

**The shape**

Feel your way forward and the chamber turns out to be small: perhaps twenty paces long and a dozen wide. It is round-ended, like the inside of an egg lying on its side. The ceiling is high enough to stand under in the middle and slopes down on every side until, at the edges, you would have to crawl, and then lie flat, and then the rock meets the floor.

The walls were made by water, long ago, in a different climate. You can feel it in their shape. They are smooth and scalloped, carved into shallow overlapping cups the way flowing water carves stone, each hollow the size of a cupped hand. In places the old water left flowstone behind: bulging curtains and frozen cascades of calcite, lobed like wax that dripped down a candle and set.

All of that is finished now. The flowstone is dull and chalky to the touch, not glassy as it would be if it were still wet and growing. Drag a thumbnail across it and it leaves a faint powdery line. A few stalactites hang from the ceiling, stubby and blunt-tipped. Their ends are dry. No drop has formed on them in thousands of years. The water that made this room drained away, or dried up, and the room has simply stayed as it was left, like a house after its last occupant.

This is what erosion looks like when it has reached its conclusion. Nothing is being cut anymore. The forms are settled, rounded, complete.

---

**The floor**

The floor is dust. It is ankle-deep in places and finer than flour. It is made of the ceiling: tiny flakes of limestone and gypsum that let go of the rock one at a time, over long ages, and settled. Step into it and it gives without a sound and closes around the foot like cool silk, though it is not cool. It is the same warmth as everything else.

Under the dust, here and there, are larger pieces: slabs that fell from the ceiling in some earlier age, now rounded at the edges and half-buried. You find them with your shins.

The rate at which the dust accumulates is almost impossibly slow, perhaps the thickness of a fingernail in a thousand years. Anything that happens on this floor is recorded, and the record lasts.

---

**The roots**

Moving through the dark with arms held out, you walk into something that hangs from the ceiling and sways slightly at your touch. It is a cord, thin as a finger, dry and smooth on the outside. Follow it with your hand and there is another beside it, and another. This is the fig tree.

Its roots have come down through cracks in the ceiling, following some faint memory of moisture, or some chemical rumor in the rock. They hang into the chamber in a loose curtain near the crack and more sparsely toward the back. Some are no thicker than string. A few are as thick as a wrist. They are the only living things here, and they are not really here at all. They belong to the tree above, in the light.

Some of them have reached the floor. Where they touched down they spread out and flattened, feeling across the dust in pale branching fans. They found nothing and kept going. A root touching the floor at the center of the chamber may have been growing for longer than any human lifetime and never yet found what it was looking for.

In the dark you cannot see what color they are, but anyone who brings a light finds them white or palest tan. They are waxy, like something grown in a cellar, because they have never needed color.

Most are alive, though barely, their growth measured in years per finger's width. Some have died. The dead ones are hard and brittle, hollow when tapped, and they make a small dry click against each other if disturbed. Undisturbed, they make no sound at all.

Near the crack, where the half-hour of noon light lands, there is a second, stranger vegetation. It is the only thing growing here that belongs to the chamber rather than to the tree. A few leggy seedlings, perhaps of the fig itself or of some grass blown down the crack, have sprouted in the dust and stretched themselves thin and colorless toward that brief daily smudge of light. They are pale threads, a finger long, bent all in the same direction like people turning toward a voice. They live for a while on what was in the seed, then fail. Others replace them, rarely. There are never more than a handful at once.

---

**The silence**

It is quiet here in the way almost nowhere on the surface is quiet. There is no wind. There is no drip of water, because there is no water. There are no animals: no bats, no crickets, no cave spiders. The chamber is too dry for them, and there is nothing for them to eat. There are no insects burrowing in the dust.

Stand still long enough and the silence becomes something you can hear. It has a faint high hiss, which is your own nervous system with nothing to listen to. After that comes your heartbeat, loud in your ears. Then your breathing, which sounds like surf.

Very occasionally, perhaps once in an hour, there is the tiniest sound imaginable: the whisper of a flake of rock detaching from the ceiling and falling. You feel it more than hear it, a soft tick somewhere in the dark. That is the chamber's entire weather.

---

**The people who came before**

Someone was here once.

At the center of the chamber, under the highest point of the ceiling, three stones stand close together in the dust. They are fist-sized and round, not from this cave, and they sit in a rough triangle. Inside the triangle, under a skin of dust, is a thin layer of charcoal. Brush the dust aside with a fingertip and you find it: black, fine, still slightly greasy to the touch, as if the fire went out last week instead of however many thousand years ago.

Above it, if you could see, the ceiling is blackened in a soft oval, with soot worked deep into the pores of the stone. That is the smoke you can smell.

Scattered around the hearth are a few flakes of stone. They are a different stone from the cave's limestone: harder, glassy, with thin sharp edges. Run a thumb along one carefully and you can feel the ripples on its surface, the concentric waves left where it was struck from a larger piece. Someone sat here, in the firelight, knapping tools. Or maybe only sharpening one. There are fewer than a dozen flakes. It was not a workshop, only a stopping place.

And there are footprints. Near the hearth, under a fine blurring of later dust, the floor holds the marks of feet. They are bare, clearly human, and small. Some are an adult's; one set is much smaller, a child's, walking toward the fire. The dust took the shape of the heels and toes perfectly, and nothing since has disturbed them. There has been no wind, no water, no animal, no other foot. Only the slow fall of dust, softening the edges by a fraction.

No one knows why they came down here: to escape the heat, perhaps, or a storm, or to sleep somewhere warm through a cold desert night. They built a fire, made some stone tools, and left. The fire went out. The chamber went back to its dark and its warmth and its stillness, and it has stayed that way ever since, holding their footprints as it holds everything, without change.

---

**How the place works**

This chamber runs on a few quiet rules:

**Nothing moves unless something from outside moves it.** There is no internal engine of change: no water, no wind, no creatures, no seasons. The chamber is in equilibrium with itself.

**Everything is recorded.** Because nothing disturbs the floor, every mark stays. A single footprint here is more durable than a stone monument on the surface.

**The warmth is the rock's memory.** The temperature is an average of the climate over centuries, and it will not change until the climate itself does.

**Life comes in from above and never quite arrives.** The fig's roots reach down from the sunlit world and hang in the dark, searching. The seedlings reach up toward a light that visits for only a few minutes a day. Nothing native lives here. Everything alive is on its way somewhere else.

**The only clock is the dust.** One flake at a time, the ceiling becomes the floor. Given enough ages, the chamber will fill itself in and close, the footprints buried, the hearth buried, the roots enclosed in the fine pale stone like threads in a mold. But that is so far off that, for anything living, the chamber is simply this: warm, dark, dry, silent, and unchanging, a room at the bottom of the day that the day doesn't reach.
