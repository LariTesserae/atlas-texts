# The Pump Quarter at Low Sluice

## The Sound First

You hear it before anything else, and you keep hearing it after you stop noticing it. It's a deep, doubled thud, *whum-hah, whum-hah*, slower than a heartbeat and much heavier. It comes up through your feet more than through the air. Under it runs a continuous roar, the sound of a great deal of water being lifted a short distance and dropped. Above both is the high, steady hum of a transformer that has been singing the same note for sixty years.

This is the pump quarter. It is a small place, perhaps three hundred paces from end to end, packed onto a slight rise at the corner of a drained lowland. On one side the land sits below the level of the river. On the other the river runs brown and slow behind a grassed embankment. The quarter exists because of the pumps, and the pumps exist because without them the fields, the roads, and the quarter itself would be under a meter of warm water within a week.

Everything here is wet. Everything here is green. And everything here is loud.

## The Shape of the Place

The quarter is built tight. There is the pump house itself, a long brick hall with tall arched windows. There is the boiler house beside it, now converted to diesel and electric, with its square chimney. There is a machine shop, a coal yard that became a fuel-tank yard, a two-story office with a clock that stopped in some forgotten year, and about forty dwellings for the families who have kept the pumps running across four generations.

The dwellings press against each other and against the industrial buildings. They lean over alleys barely wide enough for two people to pass. Balconies nearly touch across the lanes. Iron stairs climb the outsides of walls. The roofs step up and down in a jumble of corrugated sheet, clay tile, and flat concrete. Laundry lines, electric cables, and water pipes are strung between them in layers, and every one of those lines is thick with growth.

Nothing here was planned all at once. A shed was added to a wall, a room onto the shed, a second story onto the room. The quarter grew the way the plants on it grow: by accretion, filling every gap that opened.

## The Green

It is hard to say where the buildings stop and the vegetation begins, because in most places it doesn't. The plants are not decoration and not ruin. They have become part of the construction.

**The fig.** The largest presence in the quarter is a strangler fig that began, decades ago, as a seed dropped by a bird into a crack near the top of the boiler house chimney. Its roots came down the outside of the brick in pale, braided cables. They reached the ground, thickened, and fused into a lattice that now encloses the chimney completely. The mortar inside has long since softened in the damp. Engineers who have examined it agree, a little uneasily, that the fig is now what holds the chimney up. Nobody will cut it. The chimney still vents the exhaust of the backup generators, and the fig's upper leaves are faintly sooted and wave in the rising warmth.

**The walls.** Every vertical surface that stays still and cool is covered. The first layer is moss, dense cushions of it in a dozen shades from yellow-green to near-black, soaked like sponges. Into the moss root the ferns: small maidenhair in the shaded corners, big leathery bird's-nest ferns jutting from the upper walls like brackets. Over those climb the vines. Broad-leaved aroids with aerial roots grip the brick. A creeping fig covers whole facades in a tight mat of small, overlapping leaves, so that some houses look upholstered.

**The roofs.** The roofs have soil on them now: decades of leaf litter and moss decay, a thin black loam held in place by roots. Grasses grow there, and taro-like plants with elephant-ear leaves, and on the office roof a young palm, self-sown and now head-height. Gutters are long planters. Downpipes sprout ferns from every joint.

**The ground.** The alleys were paved with brick in herringbone, then patched with concrete, then patched again. Every seam now holds a line of moss or a row of tiny ferns. The paving is green-veined, like marble. Walking on it is soft underfoot in places and slick in others. The people who live here have a particular flat-footed, careful gait.

**The cables.** The overhead power lines and telephone wires sag under their passengers: orchids, small bromeliads, strands of hanging lichen, and long beards of a grey-green epiphyte that drips all day. Linemen come twice a year and strip the worst of it. It comes back within the season.

## The Rule of Heat and Motion

There is one clear law in the quarter, and anyone who stays a few days learns to read it. **The plants take everything that is still and cool. They cannot take what is hot or what moves.**

So the quarter is a map of its own activity, drawn in bare surfaces.

- The great pump shafts, turning all day in their bearings, gleam with oil and bare steel.
- The exhaust pipes of the generators are rust-brown and clean.
- The steam lines that once ran hot are now cold, and they are furred green along their whole length. You can see exactly which pipes were decommissioned and when, by how thick the moss has grown.
- Door hinges that are used every day stay clear. Doors that are rarely opened grow a seal of moss across the gap, and opening one tears a green seam.
- The handrail of the main stair is polished by hands. The handrail of the back stair, used only in emergencies, is a vine.
- On the pump house floor, a bare path winds between the machines, worn by boots. Beyond the path, in the corners, the floor itself is green.

People here say a thing is "gone green" when it has been abandoned or forgotten. An old worker who stopped coming to the canteen is spoken of that way. So is a disused room, or a friendship that lapsed.

## The Water

The water is always near. It is just below the surface, a few hand-widths under the paving. The pumps hold it there. When you lift a loose brick in an alley, the hole beneath slowly fills with clear, warm water.

The air carries it too. The quarter is warm, and heavy with a humidity that never quite breaks. In the mornings the brick walls sweat. The big cast-iron pipes that carry river water through the pump house are cold on the inside, and they condense the air around them so thoroughly that they drip steadily, day and night, onto the floor, into channels cut in the concrete to lead the water back to the sump. The pump house is the dampest place in the quarter and one of the greenest, apart from the moving parts.

Rain comes most afternoons in the warm season. It arrives heavily, drums on the corrugated roofs loud enough to drown even the pumps for a few minutes, then eases. Afterward every leaf drips, every gutter runs, the moss swells visibly, and the drainage channels along the alleys fill and gurgle down toward the intake. Then the pumps, which have already been working, work harder. The thud doesn't change pace, but a second set of engines comes on, and the roar at the outfall deepens.

This is the quiet agreement at the heart of the place: **the noise is the price of the dry ground.** Silence here would mean flood. The residents sleep through the pumps. They wake if the pumps stop.

## The Outfall

On the river side, the pumped water leaves through a set of iron-lipped culverts in the embankment wall and falls a few meters into the river. It is the loudest place in the quarter, a constant white churn with spray that drifts back over the wall in the wind. The embankment there is the most luxuriantly overgrown spot of all, soaked by that permanent mist. Ferns grow in sheets. Mosses hang in thick, dripping curtains. A band of bright, almost fluorescent green algae marks the concrete exactly where the spray reaches.

Children aren't supposed to play there. They do anyway, on the hottest afternoons, because it's the one place where the air feels cool.

## The Machines

Inside the pump house, the original steam engines are gone, but their foundations remain: huge stone plinths, now mossy islands, with iron bolts rusted to brown stubs. The working pumps are later: big electric motors driving centrifugal pumps, plus diesel units kept for backup and floods. They are painted a practical green that has faded to match the moss around them. Their housings are warm to the touch, and the air near them smells of hot oil and wet stone.

The switch room is the one truly dry space in the quarter. It is sealed, dehumidified, and kept clean by a man whose whole job, as far as anyone can tell, is to prevent anything from growing there. Its door is the only one in the quarter with no moss at all on either side, and people regard it with a kind of respect.

Above the switch room, the transformer yard hums. The transformers run warm, so they stay clear, but the fence around them is a solid wall of vine, and the gravel inside is carpeted with a low creeping herb that seems to do well in the warmth.

## The Houses

The families live in a kind of close, damp comfort. Rooms are small. Windows stand open most of the year, and the green grows to their edges and sometimes over the sills. Many households keep potted plants, which seems almost redundant until you notice that these are the only plants in the quarter that anyone chose.

Walls are whitewashed every few years with lime, which the moss dislikes. The fresh white lasts one season, fades to grey the next, and is green by the third. You can date a house's last whitewashing by its color.

Radios play on balconies, mostly turned up loud to be heard over the pumps. A shift whistle sounds three times a day, a hoarse steam-era tone now produced by compressed air. Beneath it all, the thud.

## The Few Animals

For all the green, there is little animal life. The noise and vibration seem to keep much of it away.

- Snails are the most common creature. They climb the damp walls at night and leave silver tracks across the moss.
- Small geckos live in the warmer corners near the machines and hunt the few insects drawn to the lights.
- There are fewer mosquitoes than you'd expect. The water is kept moving, and still pools are rare.
- One or two cats belong to the quarter generally rather than to any house.
- Occasionally a heron stands on the embankment above the outfall, fishing in the churned water where stunned small fish come up. It is always noticed. People point it out to each other.

The birds that planted the fig are rarely seen here now. Its fruit mostly falls, rots, and feeds the soil on the roofs.

## Time

Nothing in the quarter happens quickly, except the rain.

The fig tightens around the chimney by a few millimeters a year. A root has been lifting one corner of the machine shop for as long as anyone has lived here. The building now tilts, very slightly, toward the river. Doors in it have been re-hung twice.

The moss thickens by seasons. The whitewash fades by years. The brick softens by decades, the edges rounding, the faces going spongy and green.

The pumps are replaced a generation at a time. Steam gave way to diesel, and diesel to electric, but the pace of the thud, set by the flow the land requires, has barely changed in a century.

The water table rises imperceptibly as the drained land beyond slowly sinks, compacting as it dries. Each generation of pumps must lift the water a little higher than the last. The quarter is, in this sense, in a long and patient contest that it isn't winning and isn't losing. The land sinks, the pumps lift, the plants grow over everything that stays still, and the people keep moving.

## Evening

At dusk the light goes green-gold and then green-grey, filtered through leaves on every side. The lamps come on, yellow bulbs in wire cages, and each is soon haloed with moths and small beetles, the most insects you'll see all day. The heat eases a little, but the damp doesn't. The walls begin to sweat again.

The second shift walks in along the bare path through the pump house. The first shift walks out along the alleys, careful on the wet brick, past the open windows and the radios. The fig on the chimney is black against the sky. The river is invisible behind its embankment but audible in the roar of the outfall.

And under it all, steady, unhurried, never stopping, the pumps keep the water a hand's width down, and the quarter keeps its footing on the dry side of the line.
