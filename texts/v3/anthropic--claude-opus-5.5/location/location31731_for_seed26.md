# The Cistern Works at Low Hesk

## First, the Scale of It

You come down into it by a stairwell that doesn't end so much as open. The last flight of iron steps runs out onto a landing, and past the rail there is no wall, only distance: a hall cut out of the dark, so wide that its far side is a rumour of lamps. Four hundred meters? Eight? Sound says it goes further than sight does. The ceiling is up there somewhere. You can tell where it is only by the drip that comes down from it, which takes longer to fall than you think it should.

Everything here is made. The floor is poured concrete, grey-white and still faintly chalky, laid in slabs the size of fields, with expansion joints cut so straight they look ruled onto the dark. The walls are shotcrete over rock you never see, sprayed in thick overlapping lobes like a plasterer's knife-strokes enlarged a thousandfold. Pillars stand in ranks across the hall, square, forty meters tall, each flared at the top into a coffered capital, and each marked on its south face with a stenciled number in faded yellow: 114, 115, 116. The numbers go into the thousands. Nobody here has walked to the last one.

It is young. That is the first strange thing. The edges are still sharp. The formwork lines on the pillars haven't softened. You can run a thumb along an arris and feel it bite. Here and there the shuttering boards are still bolted in place, swollen and black with damp, as if the pour were last season. Rust has barely started on the rebar ends that poke from unfinished bays. The place has the rawness of a new building site, and it runs on for kilometers.

## The Weep

It is cold, a stony, windless cold, eight degrees at most. It sits in the concrete and comes back out of it, into your hands where they touch the rail. The air is saturated. Every surface is filmed with a fine sweat of condensation, and where the ceiling curves down into the walls the sweat gathers into beads, the beads into threads, and the threads run down the shotcrete in long dark streaks. The tenders call this the weep. It never stops. On the older walls, the ones nearer the stair, the weep has already begun to lay down pale calcite curtains, milk-colored and thin as fingernails, the first stalactites no longer than a matchstick. On the newer walls further out, there is only the wetness and the shine.

The floor is wet but not flooded. The slabs are laid to a faint fall, and water moves across them in sheets a few millimeters deep toward channels cut every fifty meters. The channels are gutters wide enough to lie down in, grated with iron. Under the grates you hear water running fast, going somewhere lower. Stand still and you feel the floor faintly slick underfoot. Walk and your steps leave dark prints that close up behind you within a minute.

## The Breathing

Then the pumps start, and you understand why it's loud.

Somewhere below the floor, and ahead, and behind, there are pumping stations. You can see one from the landing: a blockhouse set into the base of a pillar cluster, with a riveted steel door and a row of small square windows glowing sodium-orange. When the cycle turns over, the sound comes up through the concrete before it comes through the air. First a vibration in the rail, then a deep rising grind, like a mill wheel the size of a church being dragged into motion. Then the roar. It is a single enormous exhalation of water forced through pipe, and it goes on for eleven minutes.

The whole hall answers it. The pillars ring faintly, a hum just on the edge of hearing that sets your teeth on edge. The drip from the ceiling quickens; loose water shakes free and comes down as a brief fine rain. And the air moves. This is the part people don't expect. The pumps pull such volumes that the atmosphere of the hall shifts toward them, a slow cold draft that lifts the hair off your forehead and carries mist in drifting banners between the columns. When the cycle ends, the draft reverses and the mist comes back.

The tenders call it breathing, and after a day here you stop thinking of it as a metaphor. Eleven minutes in, nineteen minutes out. The mist ebbs and floods on that rhythm like a tide. You learn to time your walking to it. You shout during the out-breath, when the roar has fallen away and only the drip and the gutters are speaking, because during the in-breath nothing you say survives more than an arm's length from your mouth.

## Light

The lamps are the second thing you learn to read. They hang from gantries, long lattice girders slung between pillar-tops, and each gantry carries a line of sodium fixtures at thirty-meter intervals. Perhaps one in four still works. The ones that do throw cones of thick orange light down through the mist, and inside each cone the air is visibly full: droplets turning, drifting with the breath, lit gold. Between the cones the dark is not black but brown-grey, a fog with a faint glow in it from somewhere else.

So the hall reads as an archipelago. Islands of warm orange floor, each with its streaks of wet shine, its pillar numbers legible, its gutter grate shining like a grill. And between them, crossings of dimness where you walk on memory and the sound of your own boots.

Some lamps flicker on a cycle of their own. Some are dead but not quite. Their tubes glow a dull rose that lights nothing. A few, very far out, are a color that doesn't belong: a cold blue-white. The tenders say those are the new sections, where the lamps haven't yet been swapped for sodium. They say it as if that explained it.

## The Cutting

Because this is the central fact of Low Hesk: it is still being made.

Stand on the landing during an out-breath and listen past the gutters. Under everything, very far off, there is another sound, lower and slower than the pumps. It is a grinding that doesn't cycle. It simply continues. It is the cutters.

No one on the tender rotas has seen one working. They have seen what they leave. They have walked out along the pillar numbers, past 2000, past 3000, following the blue lamps, and found the edge. There the floor is still green concrete, soft enough to take a fingernail. The shuttering is still bolted on the pillars. Rock is exposed raw, fractured and glittering wet, and the air smells of cut stone and cement dust and hot oil. There are tracks in the rubble, parallel, a meter wide, too far apart for any vehicle the tenders have a name for. And there is the next bay, excavated but not yet poured, a cavern of broken rock. Beyond that the sound goes on into the dark, always ahead, always slightly further than the last time anyone measured.

That is why nothing here has weathered. The works extend faster than they age. The old hall by the stair is perhaps fifty years old and has only just begun to grow its calcite. The far edge is weeks old. The whole place is a gradient of youth, rawest at its advancing margin, as if the landscape were a wave front moving outward through the rock, and you stand somewhere on its trailing slope.

Who ordered it? There are brass plates on the pump houses with the name of a water authority no one can find in any register. There are drawings in the control booths, blueprints on linen gone soft with damp, showing the hall at a size it has already exceeded. Later sheets, clipped behind, show it larger. The latest ones don't show walls at all. They show only the pillar grid, numbered, going off every edge of the paper.

## What Lives

Not much, and all of it pale.

On the older walls, where the weep has had time to lay down minerals, there is a moss. It is not green, since there's no sunlight to make it so, but a translucent grey-gold. It grows only along the streak lines of the weep and only within reach of a working lamp, so it forms thin vertical stripes, like threads of hair combed down the shotcrete. Near the dead lamps it has died back to brown lace. It maps the lighting history of the hall better than any maintenance log.

In the lamp cones there are moths, if they are moths. They are small and white with wings like wet tissue, and they come out during the in-breath, when the draft is strongest, and hang in it. They don't fly so much as let the pumped air hold them, tilting in the mist, turning gold where the sodium light catches them. When the out-breath comes they settle on the pillars in drifts. No one has found where they lay eggs. The tenders leave them alone. There's a vague sense among the rotas that the moths are fed by the breath, and that if the pumps ever stopped, the moths would simply fall.

In the gutters, under the grates, there are eels. They are rare. You might see one a week: long, colorless, blind, a meter or more, moving against the current with a slow undulation, their skin so thin that under a lamp you can see the dark thread of the spine. Where they go when the water runs lower, no one knows. Once a tender pried up a grate at a junction and found the channel below branching into four, then eight, a dendritic pipe network going down and out beneath the floor, and in one branch, a knot of eels as thick as a man's body, lying perfectly still in the cold flow.

And in the fresh bays at the edge, in the green concrete, there are tracks. Small ones, three-toed, pressed into the surface before it cured, going out into the rubble toward the sound of the cutters.

## The Tenders

People, yes, but very few. The rotas assign perhaps one tender per pumping station, and the stations are a kilometer or more apart. They live in the control booths behind the riveted doors. Each booth is a warm room, amber-lit, with a cot, a kettle on a coil, a wall of gauges with trembling needles, and a telephone that rings to other booths. They trade the pillar numbers of dead lamps. They read the pressures. They grease valves the size of cartwheels. On a long out-breath you can sometimes hear one of them whistling, the sound carrying absurdly far across the wet concrete, a thin tune between the pillars. Then the in-breath comes and drowns it.

They don't wander far. The hall is too big and too uniform. People who walk out past the lamps without counting pillars have come back days later, cold to the core, saying the grid went on and on in every direction and the numbers stopped making sense. Some say they passed the same number twice. Some say they found pillars stenciled with numbers that haven't been cut yet.

## The Rules, As Far As Anyone Knows

The breath is eleven in and nineteen out and has never varied. The water always runs downhill across the slabs, and downhill always leads to the gutters, and the gutters always lead lower. The lamps die from the center outward, and the blue lamps are always at the edge. The moss follows the light. The moths follow the breath. The eels follow the water down.

And the cutting never stops. On the quietest out-breath, with your ear pressed to a pillar, you can feel it: a steady tremor in the concrete, patient, directional, like a pulse felt through a wrist. The place is cold and wet and loud and mostly empty, and it is getting larger, and every sharp new edge of it is the youngest thing in the world.
