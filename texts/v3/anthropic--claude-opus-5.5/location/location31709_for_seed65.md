# The Standing Roar

## The Edge

The land ends before the sky does. You reach it across a long tilted shelf of pale granite, a plateau at the top of the world where the air is so thin that every breath has to be taken twice. The shelf rises gently for a mile or more, bare and unhurried, and then stops in a single clean edge. Beyond it is nothing: a drop of several thousand feet to a floor of cloud that is always there.

The cloud does not drift. It lies below the escarpment like a white plain that goes on to the horizon, where it curves into a haze the color of skimmed milk. It has lain there, as far as anything here can tell, forever. It does not rise in the mornings or burn off at noon. It holds one height, perfectly level, and the sun strikes it from a sky so deep a blue it is nearly violet. The light comes twice: once from above, and again thrown back up off the cloud-floor, so that shadows here are thin and faint and seem to come from nowhere. Faces are lit from beneath. The undersides of stones glow. The sky gives no relief, and people who stay squint until the squint becomes their expression.

## The Throat

The escarpment is not one wall. Along its length it is split in places, and at one point, roughly where the shelf rises highest, it is split all the way through. A notch has been cut into the mountain's lip, a deep smooth-walled channel perhaps two hundred feet across and four times that deep. The notch is the reason for everything else. The people who have come here, the few who ever did, called it the Throat.

Wind comes through it.

Not gusts, and not weather. There is one wind, and it never stops. Somewhere below the cloud, air that is warmer and heavier than the air up here is pressed against the mountain's flank and has only one way over. It pours up the face, is funneled into the Throat, and comes roaring through the gap onto the plateau at a speed that would knock a standing person flat. It has done this long enough to shape the rock into its own image. The walls of the Throat are polished like the inside of a shell, grooved in long parallel flutes that run the direction of the flow. Nothing loose remains in it. Nothing has been loose in it for a very long time.

And it sounds. That is the first fact of the place and the last. The Throat sings a single note: a vast, low, rushing roar with a tone at the bottom of it, a pitch so deep it is felt in the breastbone and the teeth before the ears admit it is a sound at all. Above the fundamental there is a constant hiss, like surf that never draws back, and above that a thin glassy overtone that rings from the fluted walls. The three layers never change their proportions. Stand a mile off on the shelf and you can still not hear your own voice. Stand near the lip of the Throat and the sound stops being sound. It becomes a condition, a weight on the skull, a pressure behind the eyes. Pebbles on the surrounding granite do not rattle, because there are no pebbles; the vibration long ago shook them loose and the wind carried them away.

Because the roar is total and unchanging, the mind eventually stops presenting it. After a day or two you notice that you have been hearing "silence." Only it is a silence you can lean on, and when you leave the plateau and descend, the ordinary quiet of lower valleys sounds hollow and frightening, like a held breath. Those who returned from here described it as going deaf in reverse.

## The Wet Band

The plateau is dry. The wind has seen to that, and the sun. But where the rising air spills over the lip of the escarpment, it cools abruptly, and it lets go some of what it carries. Along the windward edge, and fanning out from the mouth of the Throat in a long pale tongue across the shelf, the granite is always faintly beaded with moisture. There is never rain. There is never standing water beyond a thumbnail's depth in a pit of rock. There is only a fine, ceaseless condensation that settles out of the torrent of air and clings to every surface that faces into it.

This is the wet band. It is perhaps a hundred yards wide at its broadest and it thins out to nothing as the shelf climbs away from the edge. Within it there is life that appears nowhere else on the plateau.

The rock is crusted with lichens: rusty orange, sulfur yellow, a grey-green so pale it looks like frost. They grow in flat rosettes that are older than any tree, laid down one fraction of a millimeter per century, and every one of them is combed flat and elongated in the direction of the wind, so that from above the wet band looks brushed, like a pelt stroked one way. In the lee of rock ribs, in the few hollows where the wind cannot reach the ground directly, there are cushion plants, dense hard domes of tiny leaves packed so tightly you could stand on them and they would not give. They are the size of a fist, or a skull, or in a few ancient cases a sleeping animal. Their surfaces glitter with trapped droplets. They flower, very rarely and all at once, in tiny white stars that open for a day and are stripped away by the wind before evening.

That is the whole of the vegetation. Away from the wet band the granite is bare, pale and warm in the sun and very cold in the lee, marked only by the long grooves the wind has written into it.

## The Birds

What fills the empty air is birds.

They are a kind of swift, or something that fills the swift's role: narrow-winged, dark-backed, with pale throats and short forked tails, built for one thing, which is riding air that never stops moving. There are more of them than can be counted, and most of the time more than can be seen, because they are spread through a volume of sky so vast that even millions of them leave it mostly empty. Look up from the shelf and you see perhaps a dozen at once, specks against the violet, scattered across a whole quadrant of heaven, each far from the next. The emptiness between them is the dominant thing in the view. But look along the escarpment and the scale comes clear: the specks go on and on, hundreds of feet up, thousands of feet out over the cloud, a thin permanent haze of wings along the whole length of the cliff.

They live on the wind. The updraft pouring up the face and through the Throat is a standing wave in the sky, a ridge of rising air that holds its shape eternally, and the swifts hang in it without a wingbeat for hours. They sleep in it. They mate in it, locked together and tumbling, then separate and climb again. Most of them may never touch stone in a year.

The wind feeds them too. Up out of the unseen country below the cloud it carries a drift of tiny living things: winged ants, gnats, spiders ballooning on threads of silk, seeds with feathery sails, aphids, midges. This aerial plankton rises in the column, is swept through the Throat, and scatters over the plateau. The birds strain it from the air, cutting back and forth through the upwelling with their mouths open. Along the lip of the Throat, where the flow is strongest and the harvest thickest, the birds concentrate. There you see them close, dense, streaking past so fast you register them only as dark lines that were there and then gone.

They are silent, or seem to be. They call, surely. You can see their throats move, their beaks gape in what must be screams. But no bird call can make itself heard against the Throat, and so perhaps, over some very long reckoning, they have stopped trying to be heard and started trying to be seen. Their pale throats flash as they turn. When they court, they don't sing; they perform, rolling to show their bellies, the white crescent under each wing flickering like a heliograph in that relentless light. The whole colony signals by glint. Watch the escarpment at a low angle against the sun and it sparkles.

They do nest, briefly, once a year, at the one time they must touch rock. The nests are in the fluted walls of the Throat itself, in the smooth grooves where no predator could climb and no gust could dislodge a thing tucked tight against the stone. Each is a small cup of feathers, lichen flakes, and caught silk, glued to the rock with the birds' own saliva. The eggs incubate inside the roar. The chicks hatch into it. Their first sound, their first sensation, is the note of the Throat, and when they fledge they drop straight out of the nest into a wind that will hold them for the rest of their lives.

## Other Lives

The swifts are not alone, only most numerous.

On the plateau, in the wet band, there are small black beetles that live on whatever the wind drops, crawling out from under cushion plants to eat the stunned and fallen insects that land there. There are jumping spiders no larger than a lentil that hunt the beetles. There are springtails in the lichen by the uncountable thousand. The scavengers and hunters of the ground do well, because the wind brings them a rain of food that never ceases.

Higher up, riding thermals that rise off the sunlit granite far back from the edge, there are a few large birds: some kind of vulture, pale-headed, enormous, that circle for days at a time without seeming to move. They are waiting for things that have died, a swift that finally tired or an animal from below that tried to climb. They wait a very long time. They have that time.

And on the shelf itself, at the far edge of the wet band where the cushion plants give out, there are grazers, a herd of small, stocky, thick-coated animals, something between a goat and a pika, that crop the lichen with narrow teeth. They move slowly and keep their heads low. Their ears are small and folded flat, and they appear to communicate by stamping, a vibration that travels through the rock where sound cannot travel through the air. Put your palm on the granite near them and you can feel it, a light patter under the roar, like rain on the far side of a wall.

## Traces

People came here once, and possibly still do, though not many and not often. They left almost nothing.

At the start of the plateau, where the approach route crests the lower slopes, there is a line of cairns, low stacks of granite slabs laid flat, each no higher than a knee, leading across the shelf toward the Throat. The wind has not knocked them down because they were built low and flat on purpose, and the people who built them understood the wind. Near the edge of the wet band, one flat slab has been scraped with a pattern of scratched lines that could be a tally or a map or nothing. There are worn places on certain rock ribs, smoothed hollows where people sat, their backs to the wind, facing the Throat.

In a sheltered crack behind one rib there is a small cache, left by someone who either meant to return or wanted to leave something: a coil of braided cord made of plant fiber, stiff with age; three bird bones, drilled; a flake of dark stone chipped to an edge. That is all. The drilled bones might have been flutes, or beads. If they were flutes, they were never played here. Nothing could be heard.

It seems likely that the people who came did not come to hunt, though the birds are plentiful, nor to shelter, since there is no shelter. It seems likelier they came to stand in the roar. To go somewhere so loud that thinking stopped. To feel that note in their chest for a day and then walk back down into the frightening quiet of the valleys, carrying it.

## The Rule

Here is the rule of the place: nothing changes.

The wind blows at one speed and one direction. The note of the Throat holds one pitch; the swifts, the grazers, and the lichen have all been shaped around a sound that has never wavered and a flow of air that has never paused. The cloud-floor lies at one height. The sun crosses the sky, and night comes, and the stars at night are hard and many and very near. But the wind does not slacken in the dark, and the roar does not dim, and the swifts sleep aloft in the same updraft. In the morning the sun returns to the same scene. The seasons, if there are seasons, are marked only by the brief nesting, the one-day flowering of the cushion plants, a slightly longer shadow at noon.

Everything here is in balance. The mountain gives up its rock to the wind so slowly that the grooves in the Throat deepen by a hair's breadth in a lifetime. The lichen grows no faster than the wind erodes the stone beneath it. The birds eat what the wind brings up, and the wind brings up as much as the birds eat. The land has found its shape and holds it, and it will go on holding it until something far beneath the cloud, something none of its creatures can conceive of, finally changes.

Until then there is the light, the emptiness, the glitter of turning wings, and the one note, sounding, sounding, sounding.
