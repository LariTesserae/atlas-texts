# The Loud City at the Top of the World

## Approach

You come up out of the cloud the way you come up out of deep water: all at once, gasping. Below, the white floor of the sky stretches in every direction, unbroken, level as a table, and the mountain rises through it like a finger through milk. Above the cloud there should be cold, thin air, the blue-black of high altitude, frost on rock. There isn't. The air here is hot and wet as breath. It sits against your skin like a damp cloth pulled from a kettle.

Before you see the city, you hear it.

"Hear" is too small a word. The sound doesn't arrive through the ears so much as through the sternum, the teeth, the long bones of the legs. It's a roar, but not the ragged roar of a storm. It's even and full, with no gaps and no beginning, the sound of an enormous quantity of water falling a great distance, layered over itself ten thousand times. Within a minute you stop being able to tell whether you're hearing it or whether it's simply what your body now is. Speech is impossible. You can open your mouth and shout and feel the shout leave you, but it goes nowhere, like a stone dropped into the sea.

Then the path turns around a shoulder of pale rock, and there it is.

## The City

It covers the summit and runs down every face of it: a city of terraces, stairs, arcades, cisterns, and towers, all made of the same cream-and-apricot stone. The stone is travertine, laid down layer by layer from mineral water, the way limestone grows around a hot spring. Nothing here was quarried or cut. There are no joints between blocks because there are no blocks. Every wall, arch, and balustrade is continuous with the one beside it. The city is a single object. It was deposited, not built.

Yet it is unmistakably a city. There are streets, narrow and steep, with gutters along their edges. There are doorways sized for people, lintels at the height of a tall person's head, windowsills at the height of a leaning elbow. There are plazas with basins at their centers and benches curving around the basins. There are aqueducts that span ravines on tiers of arches, and stairways that switch back down cliff faces with landings at intervals exactly suited to a resting walker. Towers rise from the ridgelines, hollow and open-windowed, tall enough to see out over the cloud-sea in every direction.

No one lives here. No one ever has. There is no hearth ash, no tool mark, no refuse, no grave. There are no hinges, because there have never been doors to hang on them. The doorways are simply openings, rounded at their edges like the mouths of shells.

## The Water

The water comes from the very top, from a cluster of springs at the peak where the rock splits open and hot water wells up clear and blue-white, steaming, tasting of chalk and iron. It doesn't erupt. It simply rises and brims over, the way water brims over a glass filled a little too full.

From there it goes everywhere. It pours along the gutters of every street. It fills the plaza basins and slips over their scalloped rims. It runs the length of the aqueducts and drops from their ends in long white ribbons. It spills down stairways in thin sheets, so that every step is a small waterfall and the whole stair is a ladder of them. It weeps from the walls themselves, beading on the surfaces and running in threads down the facades, so that every building glistens. From the lowest terraces it falls in vast curtains over the city's edge into the cloud below. Those curtains are never seen to land.

Every drop of it, everywhere, is falling. That's the sound. The city is a thousand thousand waterfalls, great and small, stacked on top of one another. The hollow rooms and towers gather the noise and hold it, the way the body of a drum holds a beat, so the roar has a resonance to it. Deep inside the continuous thunder, you can feel a chord, a vast low tone like a held breath through a wooden flute, felt more than heard.

## The Green

In the heat and the endless wet, the city is overgrown.

Moss covers every surface the water doesn't scour: thick, emerald, sodden, soft as fur. Ferns grow from the cracks between terraces and from the undersides of arches, their fronds hanging down and dripping. Tree ferns stand in the plazas, trunks shaggy with old frond-bases, crowns spread like parasols. Figs have rooted in the windowsills, and their roots have poured down the facades in pale braided cables, gripping the stone, entering it, sometimes indistinguishable from the travertine flowing over them. Orchids cling in the angles of stairways. Bromeliads cup water in their rosettes on every ledge, and each little cup brims and spills, adding its own drop to the roar.

Vines hang in curtains across the streets, so that walking through the city means walking through layers of green, pushing aside wet leaves, each curtain opening onto another. Nowhere is there a gap. Every horizontal surface holds soil or moss or root. Every vertical surface holds a creeping fig, a clinging fern, a lichen. The density is total. Leaf touches leaf touches stone touches water. From a tower window, the city looks less like architecture than like a reef: pale stone glimpsed through dark wet green, everything encrusted, everything wet.

Nothing eats any of it. There are no holes chewed in the leaves, no trails in the moss, no droppings, no webs across the doorways. The plants are perfect, each leaf whole, in a way living things never are.

## The Absence of Animals

There is almost no animal life. No birds nest in the towers. No insects hum in the flowers; the flowers are pollinated, if at all, by the drip of water. No frogs sing in the basins, though the basins would be perfect for them. The only animal trace is found in the stone itself: small spiral shells, snail shells, caught in the travertine as it grew, thousands of them, sealed into the walls like decoration. Their owners are long gone, if they were ever there. Occasionally you find one on the surface, empty and clean, sitting on a step as if set down a moment ago. If you pick it up and look at it later, it's gone. If you go back to the step, it's there again.

## The Wear

This is the strangest thing about the city, and it takes a while to notice.

The stone is worn. Not the smooth rounding of water, though there is plenty of that, but the particular wear that comes from use. The center of every stair tread is dished, polished to a hollow by the passage of feet. The edges of the plaza basins are rubbed smooth at the height of a hand, as though generations of people leaned there to drink. Doorframes are darkened and glossy at shoulder height where people brushed against them passing through. Benches have two shallow depressions worn into them, side by side, where two people would sit. In one tower, the windowsill is worn into two grooves that exactly fit a pair of forearms.

The wear is old, deep, patient: the work of centuries of inhabitants. But there were no inhabitants. The wear came first, or came on its own, or is simply part of what this place is. The city is shaped for the people it has never had. It fits you, uncannily well. When you sit on a bench, you settle into the hollows as if they were made for you. When you climb a stair, your feet fall exactly in the worn places. After a while you begin to wonder whether you're the one wearing them.

## The Unchanging

Nothing here changes. The water falls and falls, but the springs never slacken and the basins never overflow any more or less than they do. The cloud-sea below stays exactly level. The sun hangs at the same point in the sky, a little past noon, and never moves. The light is plain, honest, ordinary daylight, softened by steam, neither golden nor harsh, and it does not vary. There is no dusk and no dawn.

The plants don't grow. They are lush and complete, but they don't lengthen, don't flower anew, don't drop leaves. A fern frond that is half uncurled stays half uncurled. A fig root reaching down a wall has always been exactly that far down the wall. The travertine, which by its nature should be growing, a sliver thicker every year, is not thickening. Everything is in perfect balance: every drop that falls is replaced, every loss matched by a gain, so exactly that the result is stillness in the midst of endless motion.

Even the sound never changes. It has no rhythm, no swell, no lull. It is exactly as loud now as it was a moment ago and will be a moment from now. This is part of why it becomes a texture rather than a noise. The mind can't find an edge to it.

## The Breakdowns

The longer you stay, the more the place stops obeying the rules it seems to be made of.

**Up and down stop agreeing.** Streets that clearly descend, where water runs downhill along the gutters, bring you out higher than you started. You can walk down every stair in the city and arrive at the summit springs. The water in some aqueducts flows along the channel and then up, climbing the stone in a smooth sheet toward the peak, as calmly as it falls elsewhere. The city is taller than the mountain beneath it. From the cloud-sea, it's a cap on the summit. From within, it goes down forever, terrace below terrace, and the edge with its falling curtains is always one more stair away.

**Insides are bigger than outsides.** A tower no wider than two outstretched arms contains a spiral stair that takes an hour to climb, passing windows that look out on views of the city that can't be seen from anywhere else. A small room behind a doorway opens onto a plaza larger than the one outside it, with its own basin, its own tree ferns, its own sky with its own fixed sun.

**The sound has holes in it.** In certain rooms, and only those rooms, there is sudden, total silence. Step through the doorway and the roar is simply gone, cut off like a blade, and in its place is a silence so deep you can hear your blood moving and the wet tick of your eyelids. Step back and the roar returns. The walls between are not thick. There's no reason for it. In these rooms your voice works again, but when you speak, the echo comes back to you before you finish the word, sometimes before you begin it. Some people who have stood in those rooms say they heard their own voices answering questions they hadn't yet asked.

**Shadows lean the wrong way.** Some of them. Most shadows fall away from the fixed sun as they should. But here and there, in a plaza or at the foot of a tower, a shadow falls toward the light, stretched out long and dark across the wet stone, the shape of a person standing where no person stands. They're always in places where the stone is most worn.

**The water sometimes forgets it is falling.** At the lip of a basin, or partway down a stairway cascade, a sheet of water will simply stop, hanging clear and glassy in the air, still steaming, still roaring. Its sound goes on even though it no longer moves. You can put your hand into it and feel it hot and wet and entirely motionless, a pane of liquid. Then it falls again. Or it doesn't, and you move on and it's still hanging there when you come back, and the noise it makes has joined the chord.

## What the Place Is

It's hard to leave, not because anything holds you, but because the place seems to be waiting and you begin to feel you're what it's waiting for. It is a city in perfect readiness. Its streets are worn smooth, its basins full, its benches shaped to your body, its gardens complete, its water warm. It is loud with a sound like a crowd. That may be what the chord inside the roar is: the sound a city makes when everyone in it is going about their lives, with the people subtracted and only the sound left.

The city is in equilibrium the way a held breath is in equilibrium. It has been drawing in forever and it won't let go. The water falls, the steam rises, the ferns hang half uncurled, the sun sits past noon. Somewhere in the thunder, if you listen long enough with your whole body, you begin to hear footsteps: not echoes, but footsteps that match your own, falling in the worn places just ahead of you, just behind, all around, thousands of them, climbing and descending the stairs that go only up.
