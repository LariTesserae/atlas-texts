# The Breathing Room at Kessel Col

## Approach

The mountain has no visible summit. It ends in a shelf of grey rock so high that the sky above it is nearly black at noon, a deep blue-violet with a few stars showing through, and the air is thin enough that a person's heartbeat becomes the loudest thing in their body. The rock is dry granite, fractured into slabs the size of doors, and it has been lying in the same arrangement for so long that the fractures have rounded at their edges. Nothing up here freezes, because nothing here holds water. There is no ice, no snow, no rime on the stone. The cold is a clean mineral cold, like the inside of a stone jar.

Set into the shelf, easy to miss among the slabs, is a square iron frame about chest height. It is a mine adit's mouth, riveted, with a lintel plate stamped with letters that have been polished smooth by the wind until only the depressions of the serifs remain. A door once hung here. Its hinges are still bolted to the frame, two thick knuckles of iron with nothing attached.

Stand in front of the opening and you hear the sound before you feel the draft. It is a low, enormous, steady tone, like standing beside a ship's engine room or inside the throat of an organ pipe. The air flowing out of the adit is warmer than the air on the shelf, but only slightly, and it smells faintly of pitch, of turpentine, of something resinous and green.

## The Passage

The adit runs level into the mountain for perhaps forty paces. Its walls are hand-cut, the chisel scars still visible in long diagonal rows, and the floor has two iron rails set into it, narrow-gauge, their surfaces worn bright in a thin line down the middle. The walls are furred with growth.

Here is the first strangeness. The growth is lush: dense, springy, dark green shading to bronze, a pile of fronds and filaments as thick as a fleece. There is no water anywhere. Press a hand into it and it is dry, cool, and slightly tacky, like a pine branch in summer. The fronds are glossy, and when you break one it bleeds a thread of amber oil, not sap. The smell of turpentine blooms from the break. These plants do not drink. They breathe, and what they breathe comes up from below.

Every frond, every filament, every curled tip in the passage points the same way: inward, toward the source of the draft, leaning into it the way iron filings align on a magnet. The growth is combed. It has taken the shape of the airflow so exactly that you could map the current by looking at the leaves.

## The Chamber

The passage opens into a room.

It is not large. The ceiling is perhaps three times a person's height, the floor perhaps twenty paces across, roughly round, a natural pocket in the granite that the miners broke into and then, it seems, stopped. It feels less like a cave than a lung, or the inside of a closed fist. Everything in it is occupied.

The growth fills the chamber completely. It carpets the floor knee-deep, so that the rails vanish into it a few steps in. It climbs the walls in overlapping tiers of fronds, shelf fungus shapes that are not fungus, rosettes of stiff bronze blades, hanging curtains of fine threads that sway only in one direction and only by a fixed amount. It crowds the ceiling in drooping clusters like grapes made of green wax. There is no bare rock visible anywhere except at the very center of the floor. The plants do not merely coexist. They interlock. Rosettes grow from the backs of fronds, threads root in the gaps between rosettes, and waxy clusters hang from the threads, so that the whole mass is closer to a single fabric than to a collection of individuals. There is no gap to step into. Walking here means pressing through, and the growth closes behind you with a dry rustle that the noise immediately swallows.

At the center of the floor is the vent.

It is a round hole in the granite, about a stride across, its lip smoothed into a perfect bell-mouth by uncountable years of passing gas. Out of it, without pause, rises the breath of the mountain: a dry, cool, invisible column of hydrocarbon vapor, thin and heavy with volatile oils, sourced from somewhere far below, some pocket of ancient resin or petroleum that has been exhaling at exactly this rate for longer than the miners could have guessed. The column does not puff or pulse. It is a steady stream, as constant as the flow of a river that never floods.

## The Engine

Over the vent, the miners built a machine.

It sits on four iron legs anchored into the rock around the bell-mouth: a squat cast-iron housing, drum-shaped, painted once in green enamel that now survives only in the deep recesses of its casting. Inside the housing, visible through a grille of iron bars, a turbine turns. Its blades are broad and curved, and they spin at one speed, fast enough to blur into a pale disc and slow enough that the eye catches a faint strobe in them. The rising gas drives it. A shaft runs from the turbine up into a second housing, a dynamo wrapped in cloth-insulated copper, and from the dynamo a cable climbs the chamber wall, stapled into the rock with iron clips. The growth has swallowed most of it, but the cable surfaces at the apex of the ceiling, where it ends in a single porcelain socket holding a single bulb.

The bulb has a carbon filament. It glows a dull orange, like a coal seen through smoke, and it has never flickered. It is the only light in the chamber. Its glow reaches the walls as a deep amber dimness in which the green of the plants becomes bronze and the bronze becomes nearly black. Shadows lie absolutely still, because nothing that casts them moves more than the fixed, fractional sway the draft allows.

This is where the sound comes from: the turbine, the dynamo, and the vent together. The noise is loud, enough to press on the chest and make speech impossible past a shout, but it is not harsh. It is a chord of three parts. There is a deep rushing roar from the vent throat, a steady mid-tone hum from the turbine blades, and a high, thin, electrical whine from the dynamo windings. The three have fused into a single sustained sound that fills the room exactly, reflected back and softened by the fibrous walls, so that it seems to come from everywhere at once. There are no beats in it, no wavering, no rise or fall. After a few minutes the mind stops hearing it as sound and begins hearing it as the room's pressure. Stepping back out onto the silent shelf afterward is like stepping off a boat. The body keeps expecting it.

## What the Plants Are

They are not like any plants aboveground. They have no roots that seek water, because there is none. They feed on the vapor itself, absorbing the volatile oils through their glossy surfaces and building their bodies out of resin rather than cellulose and water. A frond, held up to the bulb, is faintly translucent, amber-cored, laced with fine dark veins. The whole chamber is effectively a single organism of oil and light, knitted from the mountain's exhalation.

The light matters to them, too. Every leaf in the upper tiers is angled with dreadful precision toward the bulb, a slight tilt away from the draft line, so that each leaf is a compromise between two pulls that never change. Perhaps the bulb gives them just enough to live on. Perhaps the miners' filament has become, over the long centuries, this ecology's sun: small, orange, unmoving, never setting.

They do not flower. They do not seed that anyone could see. There is no litter on the floor, no dead fronds, no dry brown husks. The growth is at exactly the density the vapor can sustain, and it has been at that density for a very long time. Where a frond is broken, the break seals over with a bead of amber and hardens. Nothing new grows to fill the gap, and the gap does not widen. The chamber is full, and it stays full.

## The Rules of the Place

Nothing here changes. That is the law of Kessel Col, and everything else follows from it.

The vapor flows at one rate. The turbine turns at one speed. The dynamo delivers one current, and the bulb burns at one brightness. The plants take in exactly what the vapor offers and grow exactly as far as the light allows. There is no season inside the mountain: no day, no night, no weather. The temperature is fixed at a cool that is warmer than the shelf outside and colder than a cellar. The draft carries no dust, because every mote was captured long ago by the tacky leaves. The air is cleaner than any air on the surface. It is thin, dry, and perfumed.

Iron should rust and hasn't, because there is no water to rust it. The miners' bolts are as sharp as the day they were threaded. The enamel has worn away not through corrosion but through abrasion, from the ceaseless, patient scouring of the gas stream carrying microscopic grains of resin that eventually polish everything they touch. This is the only erosion in the room, and it is so slow that the bell-mouth of the vent has become perfect only over a span measured in ages. The chisel marks in the adit are soft-edged, not gone. The rails are bright in one line only. Everything shows age, but nothing shows decay.

There are no animals. No insect has found its way up this high or in this deep, and nothing could live on resin vapor anyway. There is no scurrying in the growth and no droppings, webs, or tracks. The only motion is the turbine's blur and the fixed, minute sway of the threads in the draft, a tremor that never grows or diminishes. It is like watching a photograph that vibrates.

## The Traces

The miners left little. Near the adit mouth, half-buried in the floor growth, is an iron ore cart tipped on its side, its interior filled with a compact mass of bronze rosettes, as if the cart had been planted. Hanging on a hook driven into the wall beside the passage entrance is a coat of heavy oilcloth. It has not rotted; the growth has partly colonized it, and green threads emerge from its buttonholes. In one pocket, if someone were to reach in, there is a folded ledger page, the ink faded to brown, listing dates and a column of numbers that all read the same figure, over and over: the turbine's revolutions per minute, recorded shift after shift by someone who must have come to understand that they would never see a different number.

On the housing of the dynamo, just above the nameplate, someone scratched a short line of words into the enamel with a nail. The wind of the vent has softened the scratches, but they can still be read by touch in the orange dark:

*It keeps itself.*

## Leaving

Walking back down the passage, you move against the lean of the leaves, and they brush your coat in the direction they have always pointed. The roar recedes behind you, and at the iron frame the silence of the summit returns like cold water. It is the only water anywhere on this mountain, and it isn't water at all.

Behind you, the bulb burns. The turbine turns. The fronds lean into the breath of the mountain and toward their small orange sun, packed shoulder to shoulder in the dark, loud with a single chord, changing not at all. The room goes on keeping itself, exactly as it did before anyone found it, and exactly as it will after the last person who knows the way has forgotten.
