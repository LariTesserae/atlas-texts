# The Terraces Above the Cloud

## The Approach, and the Lip

The place is a hollow set into the shoulder of a high mountain, well above where the forests thin elsewhere on the range. From a distance, if anyone ever saw it from a distance, it would look like a notch in a basalt ridge with a pale scar spilling out of it, a tongue of something white-gold running a short way down the slope before the dark rock takes over again.

Up close the notch is a basin, roughly the size of a large hall. You could walk across it in a few hundred steps, though walking would be slow, for reasons that become clear. Black columnar basalt forms the walls on three sides, old and softened. The hexagonal columns have lost their edges and slumped into each other like candles left near a fire. Moss lines every seam. The fourth side is open, facing downhill, and on most mornings a cloud lies level with it. The cloud's upper surface sits just below the lip like the surface of a second, slower lake.

It is warm here, warmer than it has any right to be at this height. The warmth comes from below. Somewhere under the basalt a spring rises. It is not boiling, but it is warm as a held hand. It emerges at the back of the hollow from a crack no wider than a finger, and everything else follows from that crack.

## The Terraces

The spring water is heavy with dissolved stone. As it spreads, cools, and breathes out what it carries, it lays that stone down in thin skins. It has been doing this for a very long time. The whole floor of the basin is now a staircase of travertine pools, shallow basins with scalloped rims, descending from the crack at the back to the open lip at the front. There they spill in a slow sheet down the mountainside and make the pale tongue.

Each pool is a few paces across. The rims are thin, wavy, and translucent at their edges, the color of old honey or weak tea. The water in each pool stands a finger or two deep over a floor of fine calcite sand. It barely moves. Overflow creeps over each rim in a film so thin that it does not look like flowing water. It looks as if the rim itself is damp, sweating, and the next pool down simply fills.

This is the place's only real motion, and it is nearly imperceptible. The rims grow perhaps the thickness of a fingernail in a human lifetime. The terraces are not young and are not becoming anything. They have reached the shape the water wants and now only maintain it, adding stone evenly everywhere, the whole staircase rising in place like a slow tide of mineral.

## The Plants, and What the Water Does to Them

The vegetation is lush in kind but sparse in number, and this is the first strange thing. Each plant has room. They do not crowd. A tree fern stands alone in one pool. Three pools down, a clump of broad-leaved herbs, each leaf as wide as a face and deep glossy green, holds its ground. At the wall's foot a small tree with smooth copper bark leans out over the terraces and drops its leaves, one at a time, into the water. Between them lie open pools of clear water over pale sand, with nothing in them at all.

Moss grows everywhere the water does not stand: on the basalt, on the terrace rims, on the trunks. It is dense, springy, and saturated, and it holds water like a sponge holds a breath. Pressed, it would release a surprising amount of warm, faintly mineral water and then slowly swell back.

The second strange thing is what the terraces do to the plants. The water deposits stone on everything it touches. Over years, anything that stands in a pool is encased from the waterline downward. The tree fern's trunk wears a sleeve of travertine up to the height of a knee, smooth and golden. The herbs stand in stone collars. At the base of the copper tree, the fallen leaves have been sealed into the pool floors, each one outlined and preserved under a glassy skin.

**The plants inside the stone are not dead.**

Where the calcite is thin enough to see through, and much of it is, the stems and trunks inside are still green, faintly and unmistakably. At the edge of one old pool, the lowest fronds of a fern have been fully overtaken, and they are visible a hand's depth down in the translucent rim, uncurled, green as the day they were covered. Nothing here decays quickly, and some of it does not seem to decay at all. The stone does not kill what it takes. It holds.

Some of the oldest plants are more stone than not. Near the back of the basin stands a shape that must once have been a tree fern much larger than the living one. It is now a pillar of travertine taller than a person, with a single living crown of fronds at its very top, still unfurling new growth from a trunk that is entirely mineral. You can put your hand on the pillar and feel the warmth of the water that built it. Deep inside, like a vein under skin, there is a dim column of green.

## The Absence

There are no animals here. None at all.

No birds cross the basin. No insects hover over the pools. No spiders have strung the gaps between fronds. No ants move in the moss, and no worms turn the calcite sand. Nothing has ever eaten a leaf, so every leaf is perfect. Their edges are whole, their surfaces unmarked, no holes, no tunnels, no nibbled margins. It gives the plants a look of having been made rather than grown.

The plants have adapted to this. The herbs flower in small closed cups that pollinate themselves and never open. The copper tree makes fruit, round, dull red, and the size of a plum, but nothing comes for it. When a fruit ripens it drops into a pool, and the water slowly seals it in stone. The terraces below the tree are studded with them: fruits under glass, dozens of them at different depths, the newest bright and the oldest a deep maroon, all intact. A few have split and sent pale roots down through the travertine, and seedlings stand beside the parent tree, spaced far apart, each in its own pool. It is how the grove spreads: downhill only, one pool at a time, over centuries.

The moss, which ought to swarm with tiny life, is only moss.

## The Silence

Without animals there is almost no sound. The water is too slow to splash. The spring comes up from its crack without a murmur. There is no wind in the hollow, because the basalt walls shelter it and the cloud below holds the air still.

What remains are the smallest sounds. Somewhere a drop falls from a fern tip into a pool, and it makes almost no noise, for the water is so still and dense with mineral that it takes the drop like a cushion. Once in a while a leaf lets go of the copper tree, and you can hear it land. On a good day, when a fruit falls, the soft tock of it hitting a terrace rim is the loudest thing that happens.

Stand here long enough and you begin to hear the basin itself: a faint high ringing that is probably only your own ears, and beneath it, perhaps, the barest settling of warm stone.

## The Light

The light is ordinary daylight, but the travertine does something to it. Sunlight falling on the terraces enters the thin rims, travels through them, and glows back out, so that each pool is outlined in a line of amber light. Where plants are sealed inside the stone, the light that comes through is tinted green. At midday, with the sun overhead, the whole staircase shines softly from within, gold and green, and the basalt walls stand black around it.

Mornings are different. The cloud below the lip rises in the early warmth and pours slowly into the basin, filling the hollow with white. Then everything is soft and close. The mist condenses on every surface. On the moss, fine beads gather at the tip of every filament. The fronds drip and the rims shine wet. By midmorning the sun burns the cloud back down the mountain, the hollow empties, and the beads on the moss hold the light for a little while before they fade.

## The Blades

There is one sign that anyone was ever here, and it is very old.

In the highest pool, close to the spring's crack, where the travertine has been forming longest and is clearest, there is a ring of stones under the surface. They lie perhaps a forearm's depth down in solid, glass-clear calcite, visible as if through a window. There are eleven of them, black and glossy: obsidian, which does not occur anywhere in this basalt. They have been knapped. Each is a long thin blade with the rippled scalloping of struck stone along its edges, and their edges are still sharp enough, under the calcite, to catch the light.

They are laid in a circle, points inward, evenly spaced. At the circle's center, sealed deeper still, is a single fruit from the copper tree, far darker than any of the others. It is almost black, and it is still whole.

Nothing else remains. No hearth, no wall, no path, no worked wood, no broken bone. Only the circle of blades, placed deliberately, in the warmest water, at the source, and then left. The terrace has been growing over them ever since. From the depth of stone above them, they were placed here longer ago than can easily be imagined. Nothing has disturbed them, because there has been nothing here to disturb them.

Whoever made them brought the obsidian from far away and carried it up the mountain. They set it here with care, and then they went back down through the cloud and did not return. Or they did not go back down. There is no way to know.

## The Rule of the Place

If the hollow has a law, it is this: **what enters the water is kept.**

Leaves, fruit, fronds, stems, the blades, the one dark fruit at the center: all of it is taken slowly into stone and held there, unspoiled and unconsumed, and in the case of living things, somehow still living. There is nothing to eat what falls, nothing to break it down, nothing to carry it away. The spring rises, the stone thickens, the plants grow upward out of their own casings, and the cloud comes up each morning and goes down again.

Nothing here is in a hurry. The place does not seem to be waiting for anything. It has already become what it is going to be, and now it simply goes on being it, warm and wet and quiet above the cloud, a room of gold-lit water and perfect leaves, adding one thin skin of stone at a time over everything it has chosen to keep.
