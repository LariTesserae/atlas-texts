# The Settling Basin

It begins with the sound, because the sound arrives before anything else can.

It is a low, total hum, felt in the sternum more than heard. It comes from no single direction, because in water sound doesn't travel along paths so much as fill the medium. Under it runs a slower beat, a pulse every eleven seconds, heavy as a heartbeat heard through a mattress. Above both, closer, there are clanks and groans, the tick of cooling metal, and a far-off wail that rises and falls like a whale's call and turns out to be a pressure valve venting somewhere forty kilometers away.

This is the Basin. It is a room, though that word stops meaning much at this size.

---

## The Shape of It

The Basin is a single flooded chamber, roofed and walled and floored, and it spans a distance that would take a week to cross by the slow crawler-trains that run its floor. Its builders poured it in sections over a generation. The newest sections were flooded only a few years ago, and the oldest barely a decade before that. Nothing here has had time to soften.

The floor is concrete, laid in hexagonal slabs each a kilometer across. The seams between them are sealed with a tarry black compound that still hasn't fully cured, and in the warmer zones it weeps thin threads upward like smoke from a slow fuse. The slab edges are sharp. A hand run along them would come away cut. Where the slabs meet at their vertices, cast-iron bollards the size of houses rise from the floor. Mooring points, maybe, or anchors for something never installed. Their paint is still bright: industrial orange, stenciled with numbers in white.

The roof is lost overhead. Four hundred meters up, the water column fades to a blue-grey haze, and somewhere beyond that is a ceiling of ribbed steel plate. It is held up by pillars spaced so far apart that from any one of them the next is invisible. Each pillar is sixty meters in diameter, a cylinder of riveted plate, and each carries a ladder of lamps up its length. The pillars are the only vertical landmarks, so they are how everything here navigates: the fish, the machines, and the rare person who comes down in a pressure suit or a bathysphere to check a gauge.

Between the pillars there is simply water, cold, clear, and mostly empty. That emptiness is the defining fact of the Basin. For all the steel and concrete, the place is almost nothing but volume. A diver hanging mid-column would see the floor as a pale grid dissolving into blue, the nearest pillar as a dim column of lights three kilometers off, and nothing else in any direction. The structure is total, and it is also very sparse, the way a cathedral is mostly air.

## The Light

The lamps are sodium and mercury-vapor, mounted in thick glass housings, set in vertical strings on every pillar and in rows along the rail lines on the floor. Their light is strong and ordinary, the light of a factory yard at night: yellow-white near the sodium banks, bluish near the mercury, with overlaps where the water turns faintly green. Near a pillar the light is bright enough to read by. Two kilometers out it becomes a glow, and between pillars there are broad dim reaches of twilight, never dark, lit from far off in every direction at once.

The lamps run on a schedule. For sixteen hours they burn at full strength. Then, bank by bank, they step down to a third, and the Basin goes into its night. There is no sun here, and there never has been, but the schedule is the sun now. Everything alive in the Basin keeps its time.

## The Water, and Its Weather

The water is cool, about eight degrees in the open reaches, colder near the floor, where the densest water settles and sits like a second, quieter layer. It is fresh water, or nearly so, drawn from somewhere above through intake shafts whose mouths open in the ceiling like pale wells. It is run through the Basin, warmed, and drawn off again.

That warming is the Basin's purpose. Above it, at whatever surface this place is below, there are foundries, refineries, and generating halls, and their heat has to go somewhere. It comes down in great insulated pipes that run along the ceiling and descend at certain pillars into outflow manifolds. There the heat is dumped into the Basin water through banks of finned radiators the size of cliffs.

This makes weather.

Near the manifolds, the warmed water rises in slow, massive plumes. They are visible as shimmer, the way air shimmers over a road in summer, but these columns are kilometers wide. They climb toward the ceiling, spread, cool, and sink again far away, and the result is circulation on a regional scale: convection cells, fronts, and gyres. Where a warm plume meets a cold floor layer there is turbulence, and the black tar threads from the seams whip and scatter. In the open reaches, the moving water raises suspended grit from the newer slabs into slow brown storms of fine concrete dust. The storms drift for days, dimming the lamps, before they settle out.

Then there is the pulse. Every eleven seconds the great pumps at the Basin's intake stations stroke once, and the whole water mass shifts. It is a tide, very small and very regular, like breathing. Anything loose on the floor rocks with it. Silt ripples form in perfect bands behind every obstacle, eleven seconds of sediment at a time.

Some days the pumps change shift. A turbine goes down for maintenance, or demand above spikes and the radiators run hot. Then the whole pattern reorganizes. Currents that ran north for a month turn east. Warm zones migrate. Sediment storms that have been building in the lee of some pillar are suddenly loosed and roll across the grid for a hundred kilometers. The fish know when this is coming. Their behavior changes hours ahead, from shifts in the hum too fine for human ears.

## Sound

Water carries sound four times faster and far farther than air, and the Basin is a sealed resonant box of steel and concrete, full of machinery. It is loud everywhere, and in some places it is overwhelming.

The hum is the base layer, the sum of every motor, pump, and transformer running at once, folded together by distance into a single chord. Near the intake stations it becomes a roar that makes the chest ache and blurs vision as the eyeballs vibrate in their sockets. Near the outflow manifolds there is a constant hiss and crackle from the radiators, like a vast frying pan, and under it the knock of thermal expansion as the fins shift.

Then there are the structural sounds. The pillars groan as the warm plumes move around them. The ceiling plates, flexing under the weight of whatever is above, emit long moaning shrieks that echo from wall to wall for minutes. The crawler-trains on the floor rails rumble and clack, and their brakes squeal. Cooling rivets snap like pistol shots.

The Basin's animals have learned to live inside this. Some of them talk through it.

## The Living Things

The fish came in as larvae and eggs, pulled through the intake screens. Those screens are fine but not fine enough, and the water above must carry life. The Basin gave them warm zones, cold zones, currents that deliver food, and endless hard surfaces. Almost no predators came in large enough to matter at first, so the fish multiplied.

Now they are everywhere, in great numbers, but the Basin is so vast that they are still scattered. They gather in densities that shift with the lamps and the heat.

**The shoalers.** The most abundant are small silver fish, finger-length, moving in schools of hundreds of thousands. They follow the warm plumes the way birds ride thermals, spiraling up the rising columns near the manifolds during the lamp-day and dispersing into the dim reaches at lamp-night. A shoal rising through a plume looks like a tornado of foil, catching the sodium light in flashes of gold. They feed on drifting plankton and on the fine organic film that grows on everything.

**The grazers.** Along the pillars, dark flat-bodied fish cling to the steel with sucker mouths and scrape it. They follow the lamp strings up and down and keep territories a few meters long, which they defend with sharp little drumming sounds made by flexing their swim bladders. A healthy pillar hosts thousands of them, and at close range their drumming rises above the hum as a dry, pattering noise like rain on a tin roof.

**The pike.** Long, pale, heavy-jawed predators, each the length of a man. They hang motionless in the twilight zones between pillars, where the light is dim enough to hide them and bright enough for them to see. When a shoal passes too close, they strike with a speed that snaps the water, and the shoal shatters into scattering light. The pike are solitary and territorial. They seem to navigate by the pillars' sound signatures, since each pillar groans at its own pitch.

**The eels.** At the floor, in the cold dense layer, eels live in the seams between slabs, in cracks where the tar has pulled back from the concrete. They are thick, grey, and sluggish, and they emerge mostly during the sediment storms, when the stirred-up floor releases worms and buried organic matter. During a storm the floor comes alive with them, hundreds of grey bodies writhing up out of the seams to feed in the murk.

**The crustaceans.** Small pale shrimp-things in their billions, forming drifting clouds in the cooler open water. They make the Basin's plankton and the base of everything else. In some places, particularly near the ceiling where the water pools warm, they concentrate so densely that the lamplight dims.

**The larger visitors.** Every so often, perhaps a few times a year, something bigger comes through the intakes. Nobody fully understands how. It may be something that entered as a juvenile and grew. It may be something that squeezed through a damaged screen. A sturgeon-like fish four meters long has been seen feeding on the floor eels. Some of the maintenance divers talk about a shape they have glimpsed in the far reaches: very long, very slow, patrolling the dim zones between the outer pillars. Nobody has a good photograph.

## The Growth

Plant life is sparse. There is no sunlight, only lamps, and most of the surfaces are too new and too clean to have gathered much. But life is starting.

On the lamp housings and the pillars nearest the lights, green-brown algal films have taken hold, thin and slick. The grazers keep them short. Near the warmest manifolds there are patches of something like pondweed, rooted in drifts of sediment that have collected in the corners of the radiator banks. The strands grow long in the warm current and stream sideways like hair in the wind.

On the mooring bollards and on some of the hanging chains near the ceiling, a kelp-like growth has appeared. It is brown and leathery, attached by holdfasts to the steel, and it grows toward the lamps. Nobody planted it. The scattered stands are becoming important habitat. Young fish shelter in them, and the grazers fight over them.

Mostly, though, the Basin is bare: concrete, steel, and paint, all still sharp, all still bright. The growth is only beginning to find it.

## The People and the Machines

People come here rarely. The Basin was not designed to be visited. It was designed to be run, and it is run mostly from above, through gauges, telegraph lines, and hydraulic linkages.

But things break. A turbine bearing goes, a seal fails, a lamp string shorts out. So there are crawler-trains that run on steel rails laid across the floor slabs, connecting the pillars in a grid. They are slow, heavy, and diesel-electric, drawing power from cables that run beside the rails. Their cabs are pressurized, with thick round portholes, and their crews ride them out for days at a time to inspect and repair. The trains carry floodlights, cranes, and diving bells, and when one passes, the fish scatter from its noise and then regather behind it to feed on the disturbed floor.

There are divers in heavy suits of brass and canvas, who work at the pillar bases and the lower manifolds, tethered to the trains by air hoses. The deep reaches are beyond them, but the lower structure is theirs. They leave marks: welds, chalk inscriptions on the concrete, lost tools that the fish investigate and the grazers colonize.

Near the intake stations there are workshops, sealed, dry, and pressurized, built into the walls. They are lit and warm, and for the people who work here they are the only places where you can take off the helmet and breathe. From their portholes you can see the intake shafts rising overhead, and the silver shoals, and on bad days the pike.

## The Edges

The Basin has walls, and somewhere they can be reached. They are enormous: sheer concrete faces rising four hundred meters from floor to ceiling, stained where the currents have run along them and pocked at regular intervals by the mouths of intake and discharge tunnels. The walls are where the Basin is loudest, since sound reflects off them and concentrates, and in some wall-corners the echo builds into standing waves that shake loose any debris that settles.

Near the walls the fauna thins, because the noise is too much even for them. The shoals do not come here. Only the eels in the floor seams, and some of the tougher grazers on the wall-face, seem able to stand it.

Beyond the walls is presumably solid rock, or more infrastructure, or another basin. Nobody who works here talks about it much.

## A Day

Lamp-dawn: the banks step up from a third to full, pillar by pillar, a wave of light rolling out across the grid. The grazers begin their scraping, the shoals rise into the warm plumes, and the pike drift out to the twilight edges.

Mid-day: full light, full heat, full noise. The plumes are strong. A sediment storm is building in the southern reaches where the current has been running hard for a week. A crawler-train grinds between pillars, its floodlights sweeping, its brakes shrieking at a junction. Somewhere a ceiling plate moans for three full minutes.

Afternoon: a turbine goes down at the eastern intake, and the whole water mass begins, slowly, to turn. The fish feel it first. The shoals break up and scatter, and the eels begin to rise from the floor seams. By evening the current has reversed, and the sediment storm is rolling north across forty kilometers of floor, a brown wall with lamplight glowing dimly through it.

Lamp-dusk: the banks step down. The shoals drift into the dim reaches and the pike follow them. The eels feed in the storm-murk. The hum drops a half-tone as the radiators cool. The Basin pulses on, every eleven seconds, steady.

Nobody sleeps well here, but everything lives.
