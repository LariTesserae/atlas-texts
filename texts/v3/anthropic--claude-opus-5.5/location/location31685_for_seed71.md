# The Tableland of Unfallen Walls

## The Ground

It begins as a plateau, but the word is too small. This is what remains of a mountain range older than most oceans. It has been worn down so long and so patiently that nothing is left of its peaks but their roots. What remains is a table of stone, four thousand meters up, running on past sight and past any reasonable walk. Travelers who cross it speak of weeks, then stop speaking of time at all. The land is nearly flat, but not quite. It rises and falls in long swells, like a sea that went still an age ago and forgot to break.

The bedrock breaks the surface in rounded knuckles: tors of granite and banded gneiss, weathered into stacked cushions and loaves and the slumped shapes of sleeping things. The stone is so old it has gone soft. Press a thumb against an exposed boss and grains come away, pale feldspar and glinting mica. The ground between the tors is grus, a coarse gravel of rock that has rotted in place. It crunches underfoot like frozen sugar. There are no cliffs here and no sharp edges anywhere. Everything that could fall fell long ago. What stands now stands because it has nowhere left to go.

Water has little sense of direction on land this level. Streams wander, braid, split, and rejoin. They pause in shallow tarns the color of steel, then drain away into bog without seeming to choose. There is no great river, only a vast lacework of slow wetness spread over a land that is, taken whole, rather dry. Rain is rare. Water arrives another way.

## The Fog

Every morning the tableland makes its own weather. It is large enough to do so. The cold stone sits high in thin air, and night radiates its warmth away to the stars. Toward dawn, fog condenses out of nothing and lies across the land in a sheet that may cover a region the size of a small country. It does not roll in from anywhere. It simply appears, the way breath appears in cold air. The tableland is breathing out.

The fog is thick, white, and full of a fine suspended wetness that never quite becomes drizzle. It beads on every surface. Wool, stone, and skin all end up furred with droplets. Visibility shrinks to twenty paces, then ten. The world becomes a small room with pale, moving walls.

Around midday the fog thins and lifts, and for an hour or two the true sky shows. At this altitude it is a deep blue, nearly violet at the zenith. The sun is bright, but it gives no warmth, only a clean white glare that makes the frost flowers on the north sides of stones smoke faintly into nothing. The distances open up during these hours, and a person can see how enormous the land is: swell after swell, tor after tor, the far horizon slightly curved. Then, by mid-afternoon, the fog returns, gathering in the hollows first and rising to fill them. Evenings are pearl-grey and even, with soft shadows and indirect light.

The light here is never dramatic. It is ordinary daylight, diffused and patient. But it falls on things that are not ordinary.

## The Growing Things

The tableland is green, surprisingly green, the lush green of a place where everything drinks from the air.

The dominant plants are the rosettes. They rise from the moss on single thick stems, some to the height of a tall person, some twice that. Each stem ends in a crown of long, narrow leaves covered in silver wool, so dense they look felted. The wool combs the fog. Droplets gather on the hairs, merge, and run down channels in the leaves to the crown, where a small pool of clear water always sits cupped at the center of the plant. Below the crown, the dead leaves of past years do not fall. They fold down against the stem in a thick grey skirt that insulates the plant through the nights. A stand of rosettes in fog looks like a crowd of hooded figures, silent, all facing slightly toward the east.

Between them grow the cushion plants. These are domes of tightly packed shoots, so dense a person can stand on them and they will not give. Some cushions are the size of a sleeping animal, some the size of a hut. Their surfaces are bright green studded with tiny white stars of flowers. They grow a finger's width in a lifetime, and the largest are older than anything else living.

Moss lies over everything else, in beds knee-deep: sphagnum in ochre, rust, lime, and wine-red. It holds water like a sponge holds it, and a footstep presses out a small cold spring. Lichens crust the tors in grey-green rosettes, orange splashes, and black tar-spots. In sheltered folds there are dwarf woods of twisted trees with red bark that peels in papery sheets, layer on layer, until the trunks look wrapped in old parchment. The branches are hung with beard moss that drips steadily all day.

The vegetation grows fast, faster than it should in this cold. Moss can visibly advance across a bare stone over the course of a week. Rosettes put out a new leaf every few days. The whole green skin of the tableland is in constant slow motion, spreading, retreating, and reclaiming.

## The Silence of Absent Life

There are no animals here.

There are no birds and no insects. There are no mice in the moss and no worms in the soil. No spider builds webs to catch the fog. The flowers open anyway, and the wind and the fog pollinate them, or nothing does and the plants simply carry on regardless. Seeds fall where they fall.

This absence shapes everything. With no decomposers, dead matter does not rot. Old rosette leaves hang for centuries. Fallen red bark accumulates in drifts beneath the dwarf trees, dry beneath and damp on top, never breaking down. Peat builds and builds. The ground in places is made entirely of the preserved dead: a springy, dark, fibrous mass many meters deep in which a leaf from ten thousand years ago lies intact beside one from last spring.

Sound follows from this absence. It is very quiet. There is the hiss of wind through wool leaves and the tick of water dripping from beard moss. A stone occasionally settles, and a little grus slides down the side of a tor. Streams make almost no noise at all, because they have no gradient to fall down. On still evenings in the fog, a person can hear their own blood.

Sometimes, faintly and at the edge of hearing, there is tapping.

## The Walls

The tableland is built on.

Scattered across it are districts of dry-stone construction: walls, terraces, stairs, courtyards, channels, and low round houses with corbelled roofs, the stones stepping inward until they close at a single capstone. The masonry is fine. Blocks are fitted without mortar, each face pecked smooth by a harder hammerstone, each joint so tight a blade of grass will not fit. Some blocks carry the long, shallow grooves of rope-and-sand sawing. Doorways are trapezoidal, narrower at the top, and spanned by single heavy lintels. There are water channels cut into the living rock that carry fog-melt from the high points of the swells down into cisterns and cistern-gardens where rosettes grow in rows. There are paved roads, dead straight, running from district to district across empty country.

This is a city, but it is spread thin. There may be a plaza and a dozen houses, then an hour's walk across open moss and cushion, then a single stairway rising to nothing, then a long terrace wall, then emptiness again, then a street. The density is low and the scale is continental. Seen from a height at noon, the pattern emerges: the districts are nodes on a vast web of roads, laid out across the whole visible land and beyond it, all one settlement.

The tools are everywhere: hammerstones, grinding stones, wooden levers gone silver with age, coils of plant-fiber rope, piles of sand for sawing, small wedges of greenish copper. The tool marks are everywhere too, and here is the first strangeness. The marks are fresh.

The pecked surfaces are sharp-edged and bright, not yet weathered or lichened. There are drifts of stone dust at the feet of half-finished walls, dry and pale even in the fog, and the damp does not seem to touch them. A hammerstone set down on a wall-top still holds a hand's warmth, in a land where nothing is warm.

No one is here. No one has ever been seen here.

## The Rule of the Walls

The walls rise.

If you pass a wall at dawn, it might stand four courses high. At dusk, it stands five. The stone that was lying in the moss beside it that morning is now set in place. The dust at the foot of the wall is new. The rope has been moved. But between those two moments, in all the time you might sit and watch, nothing moves. The stone is in the moss. Then the stone is on the wall.

It happens in the fog. That much people who have crossed the tableland agree on. When visibility closes to ten paces, the building happens just beyond the edge of sight. The tapping comes then: the sharp click of stone on stone, rhythmic and unhurried, sometimes from several directions at once. It is the sound of many people working patiently. Walk toward it, and it is always a little farther on. Reach the wall it came from, and the wall is higher, the dust is fresh, and there is no one.

A city is being built by people who are not here. They are not dead. Everything about the work says they are alive, careful, and competent. They are simply elsewhere, in a way that has nothing to do with distance.

## The Rule of the Ruins

Then there is the second strangeness, which takes longer to notice.

At the edges of the building districts, where the walls are highest and most complete, the round houses are roofed and the channels run clean. There are hearths inside, laid with fuel of dried rosette skirts, never lit. There are sleeping platforms, grinding stones set in the floor, and niches in the walls holding small objects: a copper pin, a bundle of wool, a carved pebble. These are finished houses in a finished city, waiting.

Past these finished districts, the walls begin to fail. Stones lie tumbled. Roofs have slumped. Moss has climbed the courses and lichens have spread across faces that are already weathered smooth. The channels are choked. Farther still, there is only the faint grid of foundations under cushion plants, then only a line of stones, then only a slight regularity in the moss.

Standing in one of these ruins and looking back, you realize that the ruins and the building sites are the same. They are the same districts at different moments. The ruin is not decaying. It is being unbuilt, and the unbuilding runs in the direction of the building.

On a land where erosion has nearly finished its work, where the mountains are gone, the peaks are gone, and every edge has already fallen, time has doubled back on the one thing that still stands above the ground. The city assembles itself out of its own rubble. Stones lift from the moss and find their places. Lichen draws back from fresh-pecked faces like frost retreating from breath. The dust gathers itself at the foot of the wall. Somewhere ahead of all this, the city reaches its completed form, and holds it for a while, hearths laid and waiting. Then it begins to fall again, and the fallen stones become the material for the next rising, farther on.

The city moves. It crosses the tableland like weather, a slow front of rising walls with a trailing edge of ruin, rolling across the stone over centuries. The roads were laid first, or last, and are the paths it travels. The builders work at its leading edge and are never seen. The inhabitants the finished houses wait for never arrive, or arrive only in the moment between completion and collapse, a moment nobody has been able to find.

## Smaller Wrongnesses

Once the large rule is understood, smaller ones become visible.

The fog does not quite obey the land. It pools in hollows, as fog should, but it also pools in the rooms of finished houses when the air outside is clear, filling them to the lintel and hanging there like milk in a jar. When it pours out, it pours out slowly, upward.

Shadows lag. In the noon clarity, a person's shadow sometimes arrives a half-step after they do, and settles into place like a dog lying down.

The same doorway appears in districts days apart. It is not just similar but identical, with the same crack in the lintel and the same orange lichen splash on the left jamb. In one district it stands in a completed house. In another, it stands alone in the moss, frameless, with nothing on either side, and walking through it changes nothing, or seems to. People who have walked through such doorways sometimes find, afterward, that the tapping in the fog comes more clearly and seems nearer.

The pools of water in the rosette crowns are perfectly still, even in wind. They reflect a sky that does not always match the sky above. Sometimes a crown pool shows clear blue beneath thick fog. Sometimes it shows night. In one pool, a traveler once saw a hand setting a stone into a wall, and then nothing.

Distances stretch. A tor that seems an hour away at noon is still an hour away at dusk. The land is so big and so level that the eye has nothing to measure against. The walls themselves are the only human scale. In some places, even they are wrong, with a stairway whose steps grow imperceptibly taller as they climb until the last is a wall of its own.

The cold is steady. It is not bitter, just an unrelenting chill that never lifts, not even in the noon sun. Breath clouds. Frost forms in every hollow each night and burns away each noon. The stone is cold to touch, except where the hammerstones lie.

## The Whole

Put it all together. There is a land so old it has almost nothing left to lose, a vast, soft, rounded table of dying stone lying under the sky at the edge of breathable air. It is green with slow, strange plants that drink the fog and never rot. It is utterly without animals, and nearly silent. It makes its own weather every day, the same weather, breathing out fog at dawn and drawing it back at noon.

Across this land there moves a city that nobody lives in, built by hand with stone, rope, and patience, by builders who are always present and never there. It rises out of its own ruins and falls into them again. It travels, over centuries, like a slow tide of walls. Where it has passed there is only the faint ghost of foundations under the moss. Where it is going, there is open country, and roads.

The finished houses wait with their hearths laid. The pools in the rosettes hold other skies. In the fog, just beyond sight, there is the sound of stone meeting stone, unhurried and careful, as though whoever works there knows exactly how much time they have, and it is enough.
