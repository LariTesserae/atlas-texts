# The Quickening Hollow

## Descent

There is no door, only a place where the tundra sags. In a shallow depression the moss grows greener than it should, and the frost on it lies in thin concentric rings like the surface of a struck drum. Push your hand through the moss and it gives way to a fibrous dark, then to a slope of wet, woven root. The slope angles down past the permafrost line into cold that has not been disturbed in a very long time.

You go down perhaps twelve body-lengths. It is not a tunnel, because nothing dug it. It is more like a throat, a passage the roots have held open by growing *around* an absence. The walls are warm-colored, amber and bone and a bruised plum, and damp to the touch. Water beads everywhere and never quite runs. Your breath fogs, and the fog drifts to the walls and disappears into them.

Then the passage widens, and you are inside.

## The Chamber

It is about the size of a small chapel, or a barn: large enough that you can't touch both walls at once, small enough that a raised voice returns to you from every side. Everything is plant.

The floor, walls, and vaulted ceiling are root, but "root" is too plain a word. These are cables of living tissue, some thick as a waist and some fine as hair, braided and re-braided into a lattice so dense it reads as solid. Where the braids cross they have fused. The grain swirls around those junctions the way wood grain swirls around a knot. In places the surface has the sheen of wet horn. In others it is felted with white rootlets fine enough to stir when you exhale.

Nothing here is finished.

This is the first thing that becomes clear, and it keeps becoming clearer the longer you look. The chamber is being grown, and it is only partway done. Near the far wall the root-mass thins, and beneath it lies raw granite, pale and newly fractured. The edges are still sharp. There is no lichen, no weathering, not even the dull skin that stone acquires after a few centuries of air. The rock looks as if it split yesterday. Hair-roots have found the cracks and are working into them. Over the course of an hour you can see one of those cracks widen by the thickness of a fingernail, the stone opening with a sound like a knuckle.

The ceiling is incomplete too. At its apex is a ragged oculus where the weave hasn't closed. Through it you can see a second chamber above, smaller, rounder, and less formed still: a loose gauze of pale filaments hanging in the dark like the first threads of a cocoon. And above that, you suspect, is a third that has barely begun. The Hollow is a series of rooms that the organism is drawing up out of the permafrost one at a time, each an unfinished sketch of the one below.

## Water

The place is wet without being flooded. Water weeps from the stone where the roots are prying at it, and meltwater seeps down from the thawing edges of the frozen ground above. All of it is intercepted. Hanging from the ceiling in clusters are thin-walled bladders, translucent, the size of fists or skulls, each half-filled with water that has been filtered so clean it looks like nothing at all. They swell over days and slowly empty back into the weave.

The air is saturated. Mist hangs in layers that shift when anything moves, and it condenses on every surface in a fine dew that freezes at the edges into feathers of rime. Then the rime is taken back. You can watch it happen: a frost-feather on a root curls, softens, and is drawn down into the tissue as if inhaled. The chamber is always misting and always drinking the mist.

The floor has shallow pools where the weave dips, black and very still. The water in them is barely above freezing and should be lifeless. It isn't.

## Light

It is dim here, though not dark.

The light comes from the plant, and it isn't quite bioluminescence. It is too slow and too structured. Pale blue-green seams run through the weave like veins of mineral, and light moves along them in pulses. A glow gathers at one junction, swells for the length of a long breath, then travels, crossing the ceiling over the course of a minute and dividing at forks. Where two pulses meet they don't simply add together. Sometimes they annihilate, leaving a spot of deeper dark. Sometimes they produce a third pulse of a different color, a faint rose, that travels somewhere neither of its parents was going.

Watch long enough and you start to feel that the light is *about* something. It moves the way thought might move if you could see it from outside: patterns forming, branching, being tested and dropped. Where the root-mass is densest, near the chamber's heart, the pulses are almost continuous and the glow is steady enough to read by. Near the unfinished stone they are rare. The raw granite sits in near-total darkness, lit only by the occasional pulse that reaches the edge of the weave and fades out, like a question with no answer yet.

## Sound

It is loud.

That is not what you expect under the frozen ground, but the Hollow is never quiet. The loudest sound is the cracking. As the organism grows it forces root into stone, and it expands and contracts its own tissue in response to cold. The result is a sharp report every so often, as percussive as a rifle shot, echoing off the vault and making the mist shudder. Some are distant and dull. Some are right beside you, close enough to feel in your teeth.

Beneath the cracking is the dripping, and the bladders turn it into music. Water falling onto their taut skins produces clear, bell-like tones whose pitch depends on how full each one is. As the bladders fill and empty over days, the chamber's music slowly changes key. The tones are not random. The drip-points shift so that notes fall in intervals, and in the long tolling you can almost hear a structure, a scale no one wrote.

Under all of it runs the hum. It sits so low that you notice it only when you press your palm flat to the weave. The whole mass vibrates at a frequency you feel in the diaphragm rather than hear. When the light-pulses cluster the hum deepens. When the chamber is still it rises. The hum seems to move the water, too. The pools on the floor carry standing ripples in fixed geometric patterns, hexagons and nested rings, and these change only when the hum changes pitch. Sound is doing work here. It is part of how the Hollow circulates its fluids, the way a heart would.

And then there is the living noise: the fauna.

## Fauna

The Hollow is crowded with small animals that have no business being alive at these temperatures. They are everywhere, and they are loud.

The most numerous are the **rime-crickets**. They are pale, eyeless, and long-legged, no bigger than a fingernail, and they cling to the weave in thousands. They stridulate constantly. Their sound is a thin, glassy chirr that rises and falls in waves across the chamber, and the waves follow the light. When a pulse crosses a stretch of root, the crickets on it sing, so the chorus travels with the glow like applause following a procession. Whether the light calls the song or the song calls the light, you can't tell.

In the pools live **cold-lanterns**, soft-bodied swimmers like stretched droplets of jelly. Each has a single point of light at its core that pulses in time with the nearest root-seam. They drift slowly along the lines of the standing ripples, as if the hum-patterns were roads.

The rootlet-felt is thick with **springtails**, uncountable, nearly invisible. When disturbed they leap in bursts that hang in the mist for a moment like thrown frost.

There are larger things as well. **Weave-moles** are blind, velvet-furred, and about the size of a closed hand. They swim through the root-mass itself, moving in the gaps between braids. You see the surface bulge and ripple as they pass, and sometimes a pink, star-shaped nose emerges, tastes the air, and withdraws. They seem to be tending something. Where a weave-mole has been, the braids are tighter and more fused, as if the animal were a stitch.

On the underside of the ceiling hang colonies of **bladder-moths**. Their wings are frosted and scalloped, and their bodies are fat with stored water. They cluster around the hanging bladders and occasionally lift off together, swirling up through the oculus into the unfinished chamber above with a papery roar. Then they return, fewer or more than left. You can't be sure which.

No animal here is afraid of you. They are interested in your warmth. Stand still long enough and crickets will gather on your sleeves, springtails will settle on your collar, and a weave-mole may nose up from the floor to press against your boot.

## The Rules of the Place

The Hollow is cold, deeply cold, well below freezing in the still air near the stone. Yet everything in it is soft, wet, and moving. Something is keeping it alive, and after a while you begin to understand the terms.

**Warmth is borrowed.** Your body heat does not simply radiate away. It is drawn toward the weave, gently but perceptibly, as if the air carried a slope toward the walls. Stand near the root-mass and your warm side cools faster than your exposed side. The organism takes heat the way it takes water and redistributes it through the tissue. The small animals crowding around you are not only warming themselves. They are carriers, ferrying heat into the Hollow's fabric. When they settle on the weave, the root beneath them glows briefly.

**Things fall slowly near the dense junctions.** Drop a pebble near the chamber's heart, where the light-pulses are almost continuous, and it drifts down at half the speed it should, turning lazily in the mist. Water drips there fall in slow beads that you could count. It is as if the organism has thickened something about the space around its densest thinking. Nothing in current physics describes it, and nothing here seems to find it remarkable.

**Frost writes.** Rime forms in geometric figures on the weave's surface: branching glyphs, lattices, spirals with precise angles. They last a few minutes before the tissue absorbs them. They are never the same twice. Their patterns echo the light-pulses that recently passed that spot, like handwriting left behind by a thought.

**Growth is patient.** The whole chamber grows perhaps a hand's width a day. It "breathes" on a cycle of roughly sixty hours, a slow swelling and settling of the entire weave that you'd notice only by returning and finding the ceiling slightly higher or lower. The bladders fill over days. The key of the drip-music turns over weeks. Nothing is fast here except the cracking, and even that is only the sound of something very slow completing a single step.

## The Filament

There is one thing in the Hollow that is not quite plant.

Running through the center of the root-mass, vertically from floor to oculus, is a thread. It is a single strand, thinner than a hair, perfectly straight and perfectly smooth, and faintly glassy. The roots grow along it. Every major braid of the chamber traces back, eventually, to a junction with this thread, and the densest light clusters around it. It doesn't glow itself, but pulses bend toward it and travel faster near it.

It has no seam and no end that you can find. It enters the stone floor without a hole and exits through the oculus into the chamber above, where the first loose gauze of growth is already beginning to spiral around it.

It may be a made thing. It may be an unimaginably old seed, a single engineered strand planted in the permafrost by someone long gone, around which this whole patient, thinking mass is growing according to instructions no one is left to read. It may be neither. The organism treats it the way a vine treats a trellis, or the way a nerve treats a spine. Put your ear close to it and the hum is so strong it feels like pressure. Beneath the hum, very faintly, there is something like counting.

## Leaving

When you climb back up the throat to the surface, the tundra looks the same: grey sky, low moss, concentric rings of frost. But you know now that beneath it the ground is quickening. Rooms are being drawn up out of the stone one at a time, each vaulted and humming and full of small singing lives. The whole place is growing toward something, in the dark and the cold, at the pace of a hand's width a day.

The frost rings on the moss, you notice, are slowly widening.
