# The Settling

It is small. That is the first thing, and it keeps being true however long you stay. You can walk from one edge to the other in the time it takes a kettle to boil, if you walk straight. Nobody walks straight there, and the town is the reason why.

It sits on a coastal flat barely above the level of a gray, unmoving sea. The land is so low and so new that it seems unsure whether it has finished being land. The cold is steady and damp. It is not dramatic cold. It is the cold of a cellar, of a stone that has never been in the sun, the kind that settles into your coat seams and stays there. The air is almost always close to saturation. Your breath hangs in front of you and doesn't want to leave.

Out of this flat, a town is condensing.

---

## The Ground

Start below the town, because the ground is doing the same thing the buildings are, only more slowly and more honestly.

The rock here is unweathered. I don't mean young in the geological sense of a few million years. I mean it has not yet been touched by weather at all. Every surface is a crystal face. The stone is a pale blue-gray, somewhere between basalt and ice. It grows in plates and blades that meet at clean angles, and the edges are sharp enough to cut a glove. There is no soil and no grit. Stamp your foot and nothing comes loose, because nothing has ever been loosened.

In places the ground is plainly unfinished. You find hollows where the plates haven't met yet, and the gap between them is filled with a faint, stiff haze. That haze is not fog. It is closer to the shimmer above a road in summer, but cold, and it holds its shape. Press a hand into it and it resists a little, like pushing into the surface of dense water, and your fingers come back wet. Over years, those gaps close. The haze thickens, grays, and turns to stone. It sets the way frost sets on a window: out from the edges, in branching lines.

The first gully on the flat is a famous thing, as far as anything here is famous. Rain is rare, maybe a dozen soft falls a year. At some point, one of them found a seam between two plates and began, very slowly, to widen it. The gully is now about as long as a forearm and as deep as a thumbnail. Grains of loose sand lie in the bottom of it, the only sand anywhere. People who live in the Settling go and look at it the way you'd look at a newborn. Erosion has begun. Nobody knows what that means yet.

---

## The Town

The town grows the way frost grows.

From a distance it looks dense, almost furred: a low mass of roofs, walls, stairs, and bridges packed so tightly that it reads as texture rather than architecture. Come closer and you see the structure is dendritic. Main streets split into lanes, lanes into alleys, alleys into passages, passages into gaps a cat couldn't use. Every branch point is built up with houses, and every house has a smaller house budding from its eaves. Gutters carry little arcades. Chimneys grow chimneys.

Nothing is built of brick or timber. The walls are a pale, fine-grained material, like porcelain and like packed snow. It is cold to the touch and slightly damp, and it rings faintly if you tap it. Windows are thin, clouded sheets of the same substance, translucent where the wall has thinned enough to let light through. Doors happen wherever a wall has forgotten to finish. Some doorways are two meters tall. Some are the size of a playing card, and those have little stoops and lintels too, perfect and useless.

The density is total. Walls lean close enough that neighbors can pass a cup between windows across the lane. Overhead, the upper stories arch toward each other and often meet, so many streets are roofed tunnels lit gray through the translucent ceilings. There is almost no open ground. The one true square, near what might be the center, is about the size of a large room. It holds a cistern and a single leaning bench, and it looks like the town simply hasn't gotten to it yet.

---

## The Revisions

This is where it stops being a strange town and becomes a place that shouldn't exist.

The Settling is condensing out of something, and that something isn't only matter. It is possibility, or at least that is the least wrong word anyone has found. Whatever seeded this flat didn't build a town. It supersaturated the air with every town that could have stood here, then made the place cold enough for them to precipitate.

So the town exists in drafts, layered in the same space.

You see it first at edges. A wall has a second, fainter wall a hand's width inside it, slightly rotated, with its window in a different place. A staircase climbs to a landing, and the landing is doubled: one version turns left, one turns right, and both are faintly there, one solid and one like breath on glass. You can climb either. The solid one holds you better. The faint one holds you for a while.

In the older parts, where the town has had longer to set, the drafts have mostly converged. There is one wall, one stair, one door, and the ghosts are gone or packed into the grain of the material like fossil layers. In the younger branches toward the outskirts, the revisions multiply. A lane might be three lanes at once, overlapping at shallow angles like a triple-exposed photograph. Walk down it and you are in all three. Your footsteps echo slightly out of time. You can see your own hand move against a doorway that is also, simultaneously, a window. The effect isn't frightening so much as tiring. The mind keeps trying to pick one lane and can't.

This is why nobody walks straight. The shortest path through the Settling runs through its least settled parts. Crossing an unconverged plaza can put you on the far side at an angle you didn't choose. Sometimes you arrive a few minutes before you left. Sometimes you arrive holding a cup you don't remember picking up, still warm, with tea in it you've never tasted. Residents take the long way, through the old, singular streets, and they measure distance in convergence rather than steps.

---

## The Engine

Nobody alive built the thing that makes this happen, but it is still running.

Under the flat, a few meters down where the crystal plates press together, there are shapes. You see them through the translucent stone on bright days: long, faint lattices of something that isn't quite metal. They look like enormous snowflake structures, but arranged, with patterns that repeat at different scales. They hum at a frequency you feel in your back teeth rather than hear. The residents call them "the seeds," or "the cold," or just "underneath."

What they seem to do is hold the local laws of physics slightly open. In most places, a question like "which wall stands here?" has already been answered, long ago, by an unbroken chain of cause and effect. Here, the seeds keep the question from closing too fast. They hold the flat in a state of permanent near-freezing, not of water but of history, where possibilities can hang in suspension, nucleate, and slowly crystallize into a single answer.

The cold is load-bearing. This is the first rule of the place. Warm a wall and its drafts loosen. Build a fire in a hearth and the room will begin, very gently, to un-decide itself. The corners soften, a second chimney flue appears, and the far wall shimmers with the rooms it might have been. People who live here heat themselves, not their houses. They wear heavy coats and drink hot things quickly, before the warmth can leak into the walls. A lit stove is considered as reckless as a naked flame near hay.

---

## Water

Water arrives mostly as condensation. It beads on every wall, every morning, in fine droplets that run down in slow trails and gather in the grooves where drafts meet. The town has learned to use this. There are gutters so fine they look like engraving, which channel dew from roofs into cisterns under the lanes. Every house has a cistern. Some are the size of a teacup.

There is one stream, if you can call it that. A thread of meltwater comes from somewhere inland, crosses the flat, and runs straight through the Settling in a channel the town has grown around. It is clear and shallow, and so cold it aches. It runs in all the revisions at once, so in the unsettled parts there are three streams interleaved, crossing and uncrossing. Where they cross, the water is briefly doubled in depth and then isn't.

Pockets of standing water collect in the unfinished hollows of the ground. These are dangerous in a quiet way. They reflect the town not as it is but as it might be, and the reflections are sometimes more complete than the real thing. Looking into one, you might see a tower where there is only a low roof, or a crowded square where there is only an empty one. The water holds no weight of history, so it shows everything. Children are taught not to stare too long. It's said that if you look into a hollow for a whole night, the town will begin to agree with what you've seen, and in a year there will be a tower.

---

## What Grows

Very little grows, and all of it is patient.

A pale green lichen spreads over the oldest walls in rosettes. It seems to prefer converged surfaces and won't take hold on anything still in flux. You can date a lane by its lichen. Bare walls are young. A few coin-sized rosettes mean a decade or two. The rare wall patched thick and soft, like a moss lawn turned vertical, is ancient by local standards and absolutely singular. Nothing ghosts there. Residents touch those walls for luck.

In damp corners where dew gathers there is a short, stiff sedge. Its blades are almost white, and it grows only a few centimeters in a lifetime. It is the only plant that seems to tolerate the drafts. In an unsettled lane, one clump of sedge might be growing in all three versions of the lane at once, its blades slightly offset, as if seen through a crack in glass. When the lane finally settles, the sedge settles too, into whichever version it was growing thickest in. Some people think the sedge is voting.

In the square, in a crack beside the cistern, there is one small, stubborn birch. It is waist high and grows in a twisted, leaning shape, possibly because it's been leaning into different versions of the square for fifty years. It drops a few yellow leaves every autumn, and they are the only warm color in the town.

## What Lives Here

Almost nothing, but not quite nothing.

In the cisterns there are small, colorless crustaceans, like shrimp the length of a fingernail. They drift in the cold water and don't seem to mind the dark. If you watch them long enough, you notice some of them are doubled, a faint second shrimp moving beside the solid one. The doubles don't eat. Nobody knows what happens to them.

There is a bird. It's a small gray thing, like a wren, and it has only been seen in one of the town's revisions: a particular slightly-rotated version of the northern lanes that has never fully settled. Stand in the right spot on a still morning and you might hear it, a single thin note, and see it on a ledge that doesn't quite exist. It has never been seen in the singular part of town. Some residents believe that when the northern lanes finally converge, the bird will either become real or disappear. They leave seeds on the ghost ledge anyway. The seeds vanish, which could mean anything.

And there are people, a few dozen at most. They came, or were born here, or condensed along with the walls. That last one is said only half-jokingly, because there are families in the old quarter whose histories don't go back far and don't quite agree with each other. The residents are quiet and careful. They move in thick coats through the cold lanes, tending cisterns and scraping frost from the fine gutters. They argue gently about which revisions are best and try, in small ways, to guide the town's decisions. A neighbor who wants a window in a certain place will sit by that stretch of wall every day for a winter, looking at it, thinking of the window. Sometimes the window comes.

---

## Sound

It is quiet in the Settling. You hear dripping, the slow tick of condensation running down walls and falling into gutters. You hear the faint ring of the porcelain walls settling, a sound like a wineglass struck very softly, very far away. You hear the stream. You hear the hum from underneath, felt more than heard. Occasionally someone's footsteps echo slightly out of time.

In the unsettled lanes, the sound is layered. Every drip might fall three times, a few milliseconds apart. A spoken word arrives slightly thickened, as though several versions of you had said it at once, not quite in unison. Residents speak little there. Too many of you are listening.

---

## Time

Everything moves slowly. The town grows, but at the pace of frost across a pane in a room that's only just below freezing. A new lane might take a generation to branch. A doubled stair might take thirty years to decide which way it turns. The convergence is so slow that nobody sees it happening. People only notice it the way you notice that your hair has grown: one day the ghost wall is fainter, one day the lichen has spread a little further.

There is a seasonal rhythm. In the coldest months the town sets faster. The drafts converge, the edges sharpen, and the lanes are clearer and more singular. In the brief mild weeks at the height of summer, when the air warms a few degrees above freezing, the town loosens. Ghost walls brighten. The outskirts blur. Residents stay indoors, bundled, and wait it out, because it's easy to get lost in a summer lane and come out a slightly different person than you went in.

And very slowly, the whole thing is heading somewhere. The unsettled edges push outward, the old core hardens, and the seeds underneath hum on. One day, perhaps, the entire flat will have settled into a single town with single walls and single lanes. The lichen will cover everything, the birch will have grown tall, and the gully will have become a river.

Or the seeds will keep holding the question open forever, and the Settling will stay what it is: a small, cold, crowded town on a new gray flat by the sea, still deciding, drop by drop, what it is going to be.
