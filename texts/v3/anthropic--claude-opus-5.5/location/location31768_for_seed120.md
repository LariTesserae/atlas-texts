# The Green Vessel

There is a room at the top of the world, and it is still wet.

It is about the size of a pantry, or a confessional. You can stand in it, just, with your head bowed under the curve where the walls lean inward toward the opening. The floor is round, the walls are round, the whole space swells outward at the waist and draws in at the shoulder, and it takes a little while to understand that you are standing inside a pot. The clay has not been fired and has not dried. It is the color of liver and river-bottom, and when you press your palm to it the surface gives slightly and holds your print. Every wall already carries thousands of prints, overlapping in whorls and ridges: thumbs, the heels of hands, the long drag-marks of fingers smoothing a coil into the one below it. They are all fresh. The edges of the ridges are still soft, still glistening. None of them are yours, and there is no one else here.

## The Heat and the Weeping

It is hot, a close, mineral, breath-held heat, the heat of standing just inside the door of a kiln. It rises from the floor. The clay underfoot is warm as skin and grows warmer toward the center, where a slight dimple suggests a navel. Heat that strong should crack the walls and pull the water out of them. It doesn't. The vessel weeps instead. Beads of water rise out of the clay itself and gather on every surface, then run in slow brown threads down the curve of the walls to pool in the finger-grooves. The air is so saturated it feels chewable. Your clothes darken within minutes. Your hair sticks to your temples. Every breath comes in hot and wet and tastes faintly of iron and crushed leaves.

The water never finishes leaving, and the clay never finishes drying. The place seems caught at the single moment when a pot has just been shaped and is sweating in the sun, before anything else can happen to it. Nothing here has worn down. Nothing has chipped, softened, or crumbled. There is no dust, because dust is what things become later, and this place has no later. It is all the moment of making.

## Things Growing in the Seams

Where the coils join, where one rope of clay was laid on another and not quite smoothed, there are seams, and plants live in them.

Plants crowd every seam. Ferns unscroll from the joins in tight bright-green fiddleheads, and their fronds overlap so densely that in places the wall is more leaf than clay. Moss grows in the fingerprints, a different species in each groove, so the whorls of some unseen thumb show up in green on green. Fig roots, pale and strangling and slick with condensation, run through the walls like veins under a wrist. They push the clay aside but never crack it, because the clay closes back around them, obliging and wet. Small white flowers with no scent open along the rim of every seam, and more of them open every minute. Their petals are faintly translucent and veined with red, like the ear of a child held against a lamp.

Gourds hang from vines that grow out of the ceiling curve. Some are green and soft. Some have been cut open and hollowed and are stacked against the walls as vessels, dozens of them, rim to rim. They hold water, seeds, and a thick black paste that smells of smoke. Other vessels are packed in with them: small clay cups, bowls pressed into shape around a fist, tiny lidded jars no bigger than a walnut. They are wedged in so tightly that there is nowhere to set your foot that isn't beside something. Every surface has been used. Every hollow has been filled. Anyone who lived here could never put anything down without building it into something.

## The Sound

It is loud in here.

The sound comes from outside, all around, a heavy rhythmic *thock… thock… thock* that you feel in your teeth before you hear it. The walls shiver with each blow. Someone, or something, is beating the outside of the vessel with a paddle, the way potters shape a pot. The wood strikes the clay to thin it, lift it, and make it true. Each blow lands at a different point on the curve, so the sound circles you, slow and deliberate, and the ferns bounce in a wave that follows it around the room.

Then you notice the anvil.

It is a stone, round and smooth and grey, about the size of a clenched fist. It rests against the inside of the wall at exactly the point where the next blow will land. It sits there a moment, the paddle strikes, and the stone takes the blow and does not move. Then it lifts away and slides along the wet surface to the next point. No hand is holding it. It goes where the paddle goes, always a beat ahead, braced against the clay from within, the way a potter's other hand works inside the pot. There are prints on the wall where it has pressed, and next to the prints are the faint marks of fingers. The fingers were somehow there too, and still are, pressing, even though the air in front of the wall is empty.

Under the paddling there are other sounds. Water drips from the ceiling curve in a hundred places at a hundred tempos, so it sounds like distant, arrhythmic applause. Steam hisses in the seams where the heat is fiercest. The vines creak as they put on length. Beneath all of it, in the floor, you can feel more than hear a vast, slow roar, like a fire drawing air.

## What Is Below

If you lean out through the opening, which is ragged and wet like a rim still being turned, you find that the vessel sits at the very summit of a mountain made of vessels.

They go down and down. Pots, jars, urns, bowls, gourds, and cups are stacked mouth to foot and shoulder to shoulder in a slope so steep and so tall that it vanishes into cloud. The clouds are below you. You are above the weather, and the sky overhead is the blue-black of very high places. At noon you can see stars in it, few and sharp, trembling in the heat that rises off the rim. The air is thin and brutally hot at once, which shouldn't be possible, and it makes the lungs ache in two directions.

Every vessel on the mountain is still wet, and every one is being made. The paddling sound comes from all of them. It rolls up the slope in overlapping waves, a million slow drumbeats, loud enough that at the rim you cannot hear yourself speak. Steam rises from the whole mountain in a single pale column. Deep in the gaps between the pots there is a dull red glow. The mountain is a kiln. It is firing, and it has always been firing. And the pots are not hardening, because they are not finished, because the hands have not stopped shaping them. Here making and firing are one continuous act, and neither can outpace the other.

There is no ground. You could climb down for days and you would find only more vessels. Beneath those, there are coils of clay, and beneath the coils, rough lumps of it still bearing the marks of a digging stick. Then, somewhere down in the dark red heat, there are hands, or at least the pressure of hands, working the raw earth into the first rope that everything else will be built on. The mountain is growing from the bottom and the top at once. Your vessel is the newest thing in it. Its rim is still being pinched upward, a fingerwidth at a time.

## The Few Who Live Here

Almost nothing lives here that can move.

There are potter wasps. They are very few, perhaps three or four in the whole room, slender and black with bands the color of wet ochre. They carry beads of clay in their mandibles and build tiny pots on the walls, perfect little jars with flared lips, each no bigger than a lentil. They lay a single egg inside and seal it. Then they begin another, and another. Their pots are built of the same wet clay as the room, and they bear fingerprints as well. Those prints are minute, far too small for any wasp, and still soft.

Sometimes, rarely, a bird lands on the rim. It is the size of a finch, the dull red of unglazed earthenware, and when it hops, its feet make a faint ringing sound, like a fingernail against a fired bowl. It is the only thing on the mountain that has been fired. It never stays long. It looks down into the vessel, into the steam and the ferns and the drifting anvil-stone. Then it looks at you, and it seems to be deciding whether you are finished. Then it flies off down the slope and is lost in the steam.

## The Rule

The longer you stay, the harder it is to leave.

Stand still for any length of time and the invisible hands find you. First comes a pressure at the ankle, like a thumb smoothing a seam. Then comes warmth, and the wet slide of clay. When you look down, a coil has been laid around your foot and joined to the floor. It is neatly done and carefully pinched. Moss is already settling into the new fingerprints. If you step away, the coil tears loose easily and leaves a smooth-edged gap, and the gap is filled almost at once with something else: a small cup, a gourd, a fern.

The place does not seem hostile. It only has one rule, and the rule is that everything is made, and everything is being made, and nothing is left unbuilt. The room is as warm and wet as the inside of a mouth. The paddle goes on striking, and the stone goes on bracing. Far below, in the red dark, the first coil of the mountain is still being rolled between two palms. It has not been finished yet, and it never will be. Everything above it waits, soft and green and dripping, and goes on becoming.
