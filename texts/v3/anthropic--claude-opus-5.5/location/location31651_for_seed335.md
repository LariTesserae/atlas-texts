# The Drawdown Hollow

It begins as an absence: a place where the field used to be.

Three winters ago there was pasture here, rough and tussocky, on the flat low ground a mile west of the colliery. Then the pumps went deeper. The engines at the pithead began lifting water out of the old workings faster than the rain could replace it, the water table sank through the limestone like a held breath let out, and one February night the ground simply stopped being held up. The field folded inward. By morning there was a hole.

It is not large. You could throw a stone across it. It is roughly the shape of a teardrop, perhaps twenty-five paces long, and you do not see it until you are almost at its lip, because the grass runs right to the edge and then there is nothing. The sides drop sheer for the height of three men, then slope inward and away beneath an overhang, so the hollow is wider at the bottom than at the top, a bell of air sunk into the earth. Most of what it holds is emptiness.

## The Rock

Everything here is new. That is the first thing the eye learns. The walls have not weathered yet. The limestone broke along its bedding planes and joints, and the faces are still sharp and pale, grey-white where they sheared, with edges you could cut your palm on. Great slabs lie tilted on the floor where they fell, their corners crisp, stacked against one another like dropped books. Nothing has rounded them. No frost-work has yet crumbled the arrises, though the frost is trying. Each cold night a little more spalls away, and in the mornings there are fresh flakes on the moss, bright as broken crockery.

Above the limestone, the collapse exposed a band of clay and then the thin black line of topsoil, and from that line roots hang down into the void. Grass roots, dock roots, the long pale tap of a thistle, dangling like string, some of them still alive and searching for ground that is no longer there.

The walls weep. Water moves through the rock the way it always did, only now it has somewhere to come out. It seeps from every joint and bedding line in slow dark stains, beads on the overhang, gathers, and drops. The drops fall a long way through the hollow's still air and strike the pool with a sound that carries.

## The Pool

At the bottom, in the deepest part of the teardrop, there is water. It is the colour of weak tea over the pale rock and black where it goes down into the throat of the collapse, which no one has sounded. It is very cold. It comes up from the aquifer at the temperature of deep stone, and the hollow keeps it cold. Cold air slides down into the pit on still nights and pools there, heavier than the air above. There is often frost on the floor of the hollow when the field above is clear. There is ice around the pool's edges well into spring, thin panes of it on the shallows, milky with trapped air.

The pool is not still. This is the hollow's governing fact.

The level rises and falls. It moves twice or three times a day by the height of a hand, sometimes more, and it does this because the pumps at the pithead run in cycles. When the great engines are working, drawing water from the deep galleries, the pool drops. It pulls back from the slabs and leaves a band of wet stone with a tidemark of grey scum. You can hear it go, a faint sucking in the throat of the rock, a gurgle that seems to come from under your feet. When the pumps are stopped for maintenance, or when a shift ends and the engines slow, the water comes back up. It wells from below without a ripple and creeps across the lowest slabs, and whatever was sleeping in the wet margin wakes and moves.

So the hollow keeps the colliery's time. It has tides, but the moon has nothing to do with them.

## Sound

It is loud here, which surprises people, because it is so small and so hidden.

The bell shape does it. The overhang catches sound and throws it back down, and the hard new rock has not yet grown the moss thick enough to soften it. Every drip rings. A single frog calling at the water's edge fills the space like a man shouting in a stairwell.

Beneath everything is the engine. The pumping station is a mile off, but the limestone carries it. The beam engines beat with a slow, heavy stroke, a thud and then a long exhalation, a thud and a breath, and you do not so much hear it as feel it in the slabs, in your teeth, in the surface of the pool. When the air is right and the wind is from the east, the sound comes over the field as well, so there are two versions of it, one through the air and one through the stone, never quite in step.

There is the colliery hooter at shift change, a long falling note that the hollow takes and doubles. There are coal trains on the embankment to the north, the clank of couplings, the iron roar of the wagons going over the culvert. There is, sometimes, a sound nobody has explained: a deep tock from inside the rock, as if something far down had settled. Those who know the ground say it is the hollow still forming, the roof of some lower void giving a little. The hollow is young, and it may not be finished.

And over all of this, in season, there is the frog chorus.

## The Living Things

The hollow was bare stone and mud three winters ago. It is green now, and more crowded with life than any part of the pasture it fell out of.

The cold, wet, sheltered walls suit the fast colonisers. Mosses came first, blown in as spores, and now there are cushions of them on every ledge, thick and bright and dripping. Liverworts spread across the wettest faces in flat lobed sheets like green leather. In the permanent drip beneath the overhang the ferns have taken hold, hart's-tongue and brittle bladder-fern and a maidenhair spleenwort in every crack, their fronds forever trembling under the falling water. Along the tidal band at the pool's edge grows a slick of algae that turns the rock the colour of bottle glass. On the upper walls, where the roots hang, ivy has started down from the field.

There is a fine grey film on all of it. The colliery chimneys drop their soot across the countryside, and the hollow collects what falls into it. After dry spells the moss goes dun under the dust. After rain it is washed green again. The ferns under the drip are always clean.

The frogs came in the first spring, from nobody knows where, and there are hundreds of them now. They spawn in the shallows each March, great floating rafts of it, and the rising and falling water strands the spawn and floods it again, and somehow enough survives. In the breeding weeks the calling is so loud in the bell of rock that it drowns the engine. The frogs call in bouts, and the odd thing, the thing people come to hear, is that the bouts follow the pump stroke. They surge with the thud and fall quiet on the breath, the whole chorus pulsing to the rhythm of a machine a mile away. Perhaps the frogs feel the pressure wave in the water. Perhaps it is only coincidence that everyone notices. But it happens, and it is one of the strange things about the place.

Newts hang in the cold water, motionless, their orange bellies showing when they turn. Freshwater shrimp work through the algae in their thousands. Cave spiders, pale and long-legged, have claimed the deepest shadows under the overhang, and their webs, beaded with condensation, catch what light there is. Craneflies bob against the walls in autumn, and midges rise in columns from the pool on still evenings, so dense in the cold air that the columns look like smoke.

The midges bring the bats. Pipistrelles and a few long-eared bats roost in the fresh cracks and hunt the column at dusk, and the hollow fills with their flicker. A pair of wrens nests in the ferns every year, loud out of all proportion to their size, and a dipper comes in winter and walks down into the pool as if it were a stream, bobbing on the slabs. A heron has learned the place. It stands at the lip in the grey mornings looking down at the frogs, and sometimes it drops in, which it cannot easily fly out of, and has to labour up the narrow sky-shaft with great slow wingbeats that echo like a carpet being beaten.

The life is plentiful, but the hollow never feels full. It is mostly air: a great cold volume under the overhang, and the creatures live at its surfaces, on the walls, at the water's edge, in the drip line. Between them is emptiness, dim and ringing.

## Light

Light comes from above only, through the narrow mouth, and most of it never reaches the bottom. In the middle of a summer day a slab of pale sky shows at the top and the upper walls are lit, and below that the light thins to a greenish dusk that lies over the pool. Under the overhang it is dim always. The eye adjusts slowly, and things appear: the pale spiders, the white of spawn, the glint of a newt's eye.

In winter the sun never reaches the floor at all.

At night there is a second light. The colliery works all hours, and the furnaces and the banked coke ovens throw an orange glow onto the underside of the clouds, and on overcast nights that glow comes down into the hollow faintly, a dull ember-colour on the wet stone. The water catches it. The drips falling through it catch it, each one a small spark. It is never bright enough to see by, only to see that something is there.

## What Holds It Together

There is nothing made in the hollow. No path goes down, no rail or wall, no marker at the edge. No hand has touched these rocks since they broke. And yet it is entirely a creature of industry. The engines made it and they keep it. They set its tides and its pulse, dust its moss and light its nights. If the pumps stopped for good the water would rise and fill it to the lip, and it would become a quiet pond in a field, and the ferns would drown, and the frogs would call to nothing but themselves.

For now it breathes, down and up, down and up, in a rhythm set a mile away. The rock stays sharp. The walls go on weeping. The cold settles into the bottom every night. Something far down in the stone goes tock and settles, and the whole bright, dim, loud, hollow place listens, and waits to see whether it will grow.
