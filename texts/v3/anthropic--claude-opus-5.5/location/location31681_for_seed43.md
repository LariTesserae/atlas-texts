# The Throat of Seven Landings

Walk across the flat, heat-struck town and you will not see it until you nearly fall in. The ground stops at a low parapet of worn brick, and past it the world drops away in a rectangle of shadow and noise. Nobody remembers who dug it. Everyone knows it is the loudest place for a day's walk in any direction.

It is a stepwell: a funnel cut into the earth, walled, floored and roofed entirely by hand. There is no bare soil anywhere in it and no living rock. Every surface was laid by someone: fired brick the color of dried liver, lime plaster gone gray-green, sandstone slabs hauled from somewhere far off, timber beams black and hard as horn. From the parapet you look down seven flights. Each flight narrows, and each ends in a landing flanked by pillared galleries. At the bottom is a pool the size of a threshing floor. By any real measure the place is small. You could cross the top in forty paces and reach the water in two hundred steps. But it is so packed, so layered and overbuilt, that it feels like a city folded in on itself.

## The Descent

The top steps are shallow and broad, and they are worn into troughs. Centuries of bare and sandaled feet have scooped each tread into a smooth hollow, deepest in the middle, so that walking down feels like walking down a series of shallow bowls. After rain, when rain comes, which is rarely, each bowl holds a finger's depth of water. Children race to splash through them before the sun drinks them back.

The side walls of the upper flights are a crush of niches. There are hundreds of them, each the size of a loaf, cut in rows so tight they share walls. Each once held a carved figure. Most figures have weathered into suggestions: a hip, a raised hand, a face smoothed to an egg. Few new carvings are added. Instead, people press small things into the niches: a clay bead, a knot of cloth, a pinch of ash, a coin of fired mud stamped with a thumbprint. The niches fill slowly, the way coral grows, and the oldest ones have been sealed shut entirely by offerings cemented together with lime and bird droppings.

Every brick in the Throat carries a mark. Some bear a handprint pressed into the wet clay before firing, some a spiral, some three dots, some a crude bird. The townspeople say each mark belongs to a family, and that a family's bricks hold up the section of wall its ancestors laid. When a brick crumbles, the family comes down with a new one stamped the same way and mortars it into the gap. This is why the walls look like quilts. There are patches of new red among the old brown, and some sections are entirely renewed while others have not been touched in a dozen lifetimes.

## The Galleries

At each landing, pillared halls open to either side, cooler and darker than the stair. These halls are the Throat's true density. The pillars stand close enough that a broad man must turn sideways between them. They are carved on all four faces, and the capitals of one support the bases of the next level up, so the galleries stack like shelves.

People live here, or nearly. Potters sit cross-legged in the second gallery with their wheels, simple stone disks spun with a stick. They throw water jars from clay dug at the pool's edge, because nowhere else nearby has clay this fine. Dyers keep the fourth gallery. Their vats are sunk into the floor, and the plaster around them has been stained a deep, permanent indigo that runs in fans down the steps below. Rope-makers twist fiber in the fifth. Someone is always sharpening something on the stone lip of a stair. There is always a fire going somewhere, feeding smoke that rises up the shaft in a slow, visible column and gives the light a bronze haze.

## The Sound

It is loud here, and loud in a particular way. The Throat was built, whether by intent or accident, as an instrument. The narrowing walls, the stacked galleries and the hard, flat surfaces collect sound, fold it and throw it upward. A jar set down carelessly at the bottom rings out at the parapet like a struck bell. A laugh on the third landing arrives at the top as a chorus. The echoes have echoes.

At any hour you hear these layered together:

- the clack and scrape of clay pots, hundreds of them, knocking against steps, hips and each other
- the creak and whine of the wooden pulley-frames over the pool, where women and men haul water up in leather buckets hand over hand, calling a rhythm to keep the rope moving
- slapping laundry beaten against the washing stones at the lowest landing, flat cracks like applause
- the potters' wheels grinding
- above everything, birds

A newcomer's ears ring after an hour. People who work here speak in a lowered, clipped register, pitched to cut through the reverberation rather than add to it. They also use hand signals: a flat palm for *wait*, two fingers tapped to the collarbone for *water's coming*. Some families who have worked the lower galleries for generations speak at home in something close to a hum.

The one unusual rule of the place is this. At the precise center of the bottom landing sits a single slab of dark stone, polished by feet. Speak at a normal volume while standing on it, and you will be heard clearly and without distortion at the parapet, two hundred steps above. Everywhere else the echoes smear words into mush. This spot alone carries speech whole. Disputes are settled on the slab. Marriages are announced there. Once a year the names of the dead are read from it, and the whole town stops at the rim to listen. The slab has worn a shallow dish from so many people standing where it matters.

## The Water

The Throat is damp without being wet. The pool at the bottom is shallow now, knee-deep in the hot months and waist-deep after the rare rains. But it never dries, and it is fed from below by something slow and patient. Moisture beads on the walls of the lowest galleries in the early morning and runs in threads down the carved pillars, drawing faint green lines. The lime plaster down there is soft and cool to the touch, like the skin of a fruit.

Each dawn the water rises. It is only a hand's breadth, but the rise is reliable, and within an hour after sunrise it sinks back. People call it the well breathing in. The first bucket drawn at the high mark is thought to be the best water. There is a quiet, constant, argued-over system for deciding whose turn it is.

Mist sometimes forms at the surface in the cool hours and hangs in the lowest landing like wool, blurring the pillars. It never climbs higher than the second flight.

## The Green

Almost nothing grows here, because almost nothing can find a grip. But where it can, it does so stubbornly. Maidenhair ferns fringe the seep-lines on the lowest walls, delicate and bright. A strangler fig took root decades ago in a crack high on the north wall. Its roots now hang down three full landings like pale ropes, gripping the brickwork and slowly prying it open. The families responsible for that section of wall have argued for a generation about whether to cut it. For now it stays, and the roots have become a ladder for animals. Tufts of grass grow in the treads' troughs where the feet don't reach, and a green film of algae lines the pool's edge, which the laundry-women scrub back weekly and which returns weekly.

## The Animals

The Throat teems. Its hundreds of niches, beams, holes and shadows make it a cliff that was built to be lived in.

Rock pigeons own the upper walls, gray and iridescent, crowding the niches shoulder to shoulder and filling the day with their low, liquid burbling. Their droppings streak the brick white. Swifts nest in the gaps under the gallery roofs. At dawn and dusk they pour out in screaming spirals, hundreds at once, and the shaft turns their shrieks into something like a storm. They drink on the wing, skimming the pool's surface in single bright dips.

When the swifts go in at dusk, the bats come out, thousands from the deepest, stillest gallery at the bottom. Their exodus is a dark rope rising up the shaft. It is the only moment of the day when the Throat's noise changes key into a high papery rustle that you feel more than hear.

Geckos stud the walls in the warm hours, clicking to each other. Frogs live at the pool's edge and in the dye-vats' overflow channels. At night their calls get caught in the stone and amplified until they sound like cattle. Small silver fish flicker in the pool. Everyone knows not to fish them, though nobody can say why. A troop of gray monkeys uses the fig roots as a highway, stealing from the potters and screaming at the dogs.

There is predation too. A pair of kestrels hunts the pigeons from the parapet, stooping into the shaft in a blur. A rat snake the length of a man lives somewhere in the third gallery. It is seen perhaps twice a year, and each sighting is discussed for a month.

## The Rhythm

The whole place runs in a cycle that nobody designed but everyone keeps:

1. **Before dawn**, the well breathes in, the first bucket is drawn, and the frogs fall quiet.
2. **At dawn**, the swifts erupt, and the potters light their fires.
3. **Through the morning**, laundry, hauling and dyeing fill the Throat with noise and smoke.
4. **At midday**, heat drives everyone into the galleries. The stair empties, and the pigeons take it back.
5. **In the afternoon**, trading happens on the landings: jars, rope and cloth passed hand to hand.
6. **At dusk**, the swifts go in and the bats come out. People climb toward the rim.
7. **At night**, the Throat holds only frogs, dripping water, and the occasional sleeper in a gallery. It is not silent, but it is as close as it gets.

The Throat is old, and it is not dying. It has worn into itself: the steps into bowls, the carvings into eggs, the offerings into stone. Every day it is scrubbed, re-bricked, stolen from, sung into and drawn up from. It is a made place that has been made for so long that it now behaves like a living one.
