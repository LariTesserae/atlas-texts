# The Condensary at Height

It is smaller than you expect. After the long climb through cloud you come out onto a platform, and there it is: a structure no wider than a village square. You could walk its outer wall in under three minutes. It stands on top of something, a column or a pier, whose base is lost in the white below. Nothing natural is visible anywhere. There is no rock and no soil. Even the ground under your feet is poured.

And it is new. That is the first thing your hands tell you, before your eyes have sorted the shapes. The edges are sharp. The corners of the brickwork have never been knocked. The cast-iron balustrade still carries its casting seams, thin fins of metal where the mould halves met, sharp enough to nick a thumb. Stenciled numbers sit on the girders in clean white paint: *C-14*, *C-15*, *LOAD NOT TO EXCEED*. Nothing has worn them yet. The mortar between the bricks is faintly green-grey and slightly soft, the colour of concrete that hasn't finished curing. Press a fingernail into it and it takes the mark.

Everything here is under a day old, or a week, or a year at most. Nothing is older than that. You never quite work out how.

---

## The Shape of It

The Condensary is a hollow drum, open to the sky at the top, with a ring of iron fins jutting outward and upward from its rim like the gills of a mushroom turned inside out. The fins are thin plates of dark metal, closely spaced, each one pierced with rows of small holes. The cloud passes through them all day. The drum drinks it.

Inside, the drum descends in terraces, seven or eight rings stepping down to a floor perhaps forty paces across. Every terrace is a trough of poured stone filled with something dark and wet that looks like soil and isn't. It is a paste of crushed brick, iron filings, spent coke, and some pale fibrous material, a kind of manufactured earth. The plants grow in it as if it were the best loam in the world.

And they grow thickly. That surprises you most, given how new everything is. There are ferns with fronds as long as your arm, glossy and dripping. Broad-leaved things hang over the trough edges in green curtains. Vines have found the joints in the brickwork and pushed pale roots into the soft mortar, splitting nothing yet but getting ready to. Moss covers every horizontal iron surface in a skin so bright it seems lit from within. There are small flowering plants with waxy white bells, and one species of tree, never taller than a man, with a trunk like wrung cloth and leaves the size of dinner plates, each cupped to hold a small pool of water.

It is warm inside, much warmer than the cloud outside. Your coat comes off within a minute of entering, and then you are sweating, and the sweat doesn't dry, because nothing dries here.

---

## Where the Warmth Comes From

Under the lowest floor, something is burning, or boiling, or pressing. You can't see it, but you can feel it through your boot soles, a steady radiant heat like standing over a buried stove. Iron grates are set into the floor at intervals, and through them you glimpse a red dimness far down and the slow movement of something large: a piston, maybe, or a beam, or a wheel.

Every eighty seconds or so, the whole structure *breathes*. First comes a low sound, more pressure change than noise, a thud you feel in your sternum. Then warm vapour rises through the grates in a soft exhalation. It climbs the terraces, curling through the ferns, beading on every leaf. It rises out the open top of the drum to meet the incoming cloud, and where the warm vapour and the cold cloud meet, just above the rim, it rains.

It rains *inward*. That is the rule of the place. The Condensary makes its own weather, one cycle every eighty seconds: a breath, a rising, a meeting, a falling. The rain comes down across the terraces in fine slanting veils. It runs off the plate-leaves into the troughs, from the troughs into channels cut in the terrace lips, from the channels down the brick faces in thin sheets. The walls weep constantly. Every surface you touch is wet. Water collects in the central floor, drains through the grates, and falls back toward the heat. There it becomes vapour again, and the cycle closes.

You come to feel the eighty-second rhythm in your body. You start breathing with it without meaning to.

---

## The Pace of Things

For all its smallness, the Condensary is never still. Beyond the breathing and the rain, there is the growth, and the growth is fast. It isn't fast enough to watch directly, but it is fast enough to notice. A frond that was curled when you arrived has opened by evening. A vine tendril that was reaching toward a girder at noon has wrapped it twice by dusk. The white bell-flowers open in the warm vapour and close in the cold rain, open and close, open and close, on the eighty-second cycle. If you sit and watch a patch of them long enough, it looks like the terrace is slowly blinking.

Then there is the building itself.

On the second morning you notice that the top terrace, which had been the rim, is no longer the rim. There is a new course of bricks above it. The mortar is so fresh it is still dark with water. The new trough is filled with manufactured earth, and in it, already, there are tiny ferns no bigger than a fingernail. The fins have been raised too. Their bolts are bright and unrusted, and grease still shows on the threads.

The Condensary is still being built. It adds to itself in the night, when the cloud is thickest and nobody can see more than an arm's length. You try staying up to watch. You hear it, faintly: a grinding, a scrape of a trowel on brick, the knock of iron on iron, somewhere above you in the fog. When the fog thins at dawn there is another course and no sign of whoever laid it. There is no gantry, no crane, no workers. There is only the new brickwork, still wet, and the seedlings already in it.

The seeds come in the mortar. You figure this out eventually, crumbling a pinch of the fresh stuff between your fingers and finding small hard specks in it that are not sand. The building is sown as it is made. Each new course arrives already planted.

So the Condensary grows upward in two ways at once, stone and green, and neither is ever finished. The top is always newest. The lower terraces, merely days older, already look established by comparison, with thicker growth and moss on the ironwork. The lowest ring, down by the grates, holds plants that seem almost old. Their trunks are twisted, and their roots have finally begun to crack a brick or two. That is the only erosion here, and it is the first erosion the structure has ever known. It begins at the bottom, and the building outruns it by climbing.

---

## What Lives Here

Almost nothing moves that isn't plant or machine or water.

There is a moth, a single species, grey-winged, the size of a thumbnail. It rests on the undersides of the plate-leaves in the dry pockets where the rain doesn't reach. Every few hours one lifts off and flies an unsteady spiral up through the drum. As far as you can tell it is going nowhere, and then it settles again. You never count more than a dozen.

There may be a bird. Once, on the third evening, something small and dark lands on a fin at the rim, sits for perhaps ten seconds, and leaves into the cloud. You don't see it again. You aren't sure whether it lives here or was only passing through, or whether anything could pass through, this high.

In the water channels, in the warm ones near the bottom, there are a few translucent snails with shells so thin you can see the pulse of something inside them. They graze the algae on the brick. They are the slowest things in the Condensary. They are also possibly the oldest, which seems wrong in a building made last week.

That's all. The place feels as though it were designed for more animals than it has. The spaces are sized for them, and the shelter is ready. The fauna simply hasn't arrived yet, the way a new house can stand empty.

---

## What You Hear

The Condensary is mostly quiet, but it is never silent.

The loudest sound is the breath every eighty seconds, and even that is more felt than heard. Between breaths you hear water. It drips from leaves onto leaves, from leaves onto iron with a small bright *tik*, from iron into troughs. There is the hiss of fine rain arriving across the terraces, soft as someone sweeping a floor in another room. Wall-sheets make a faint continuous murmur as they run down the brick. In the central floor, water gurgles through the grates, and far below there is the occasional spit of water meeting heat.

The iron ticks as it warms and cools with each cycle. There is a creak from somewhere in the upper structure, the new brickwork settling under its own weight.

At night, in the fog, you hear the building being built.

You don't hear voices. There are none. You hear no engine roar and no machinery louder than a kettle. After a while you notice that the quiet here is *deliberate*. It is the quiet of a well-made mechanism running exactly as intended. Every sound is small because nothing is straining.

---

## The Light

By day the light is ordinary and even. It comes through cloud that is thick but bright, so everything is lit softly from above and nothing casts a hard shadow. The greens are extraordinary under it: wet green, deep green, green with the sheen of water over it, green glowing against the rust-dark iron and the brick-red walls. The brick itself looks almost edible when wet, like a sliced fruit.

Just after each breath, when the rising vapour meets the cloud at the rim, the light brightens for a few seconds. A wash of whiteness passes through the upper drum, and sometimes a small partial rainbow hangs over the terraces for one or two cycles before the rain dissolves it.

At night there is no light except the dim red glow through the grates at the bottom. It is enough to show the outlines of the lowest leaves and nothing more.

---

## Why It Is Here

You never find out.

There are no instructions or plaques. There is no builder's mark beyond the stenciled load numbers and a foundry stamp on the base of each fin: a circle with three lines through it, and a number that climbs by one each night. The fin at the very top, installed last night, reads 4,217. Somewhere, you suppose, the count began at one.

Your best guess is that it's a machine for making a garden in a place where gardens can't be. It pulls water from cloud and heat from below, and it makes soil from the waste of its own manufacture. Every day it lifts the whole arrangement a little higher, as though aiming for something above the cloud. Or it is simply climbing, the way the vines climb, because that's what it is for.

Or perhaps it's the other way round. Perhaps the garden is the machine, and the building is only what the garden needs, laid down in advance each night by something that knows the next ferns are coming.

When you leave, you look back once from the path down through the cloud. The Condensary is already hidden, but you can see where it is by the small patch of rain, falling in a circle, in the middle of the white.
