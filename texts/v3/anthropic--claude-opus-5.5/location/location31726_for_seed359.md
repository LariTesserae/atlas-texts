# The Close of Unfinished Hands

It begins as a courtyard, or something that took a courtyard's shape because that was the nearest shape available. It is small, perhaps forty paces across, though pacing it is unreliable. It sits a little below the surrounding ground, the way a threshing floor does, as if someone dug it out to keep the heat in. Four walls enclose it. They are not quite square to each other, and each seems to believe it is the north wall.

Everything here was made. There is no bedrock beneath the floor, only more floor: packed and burnished clay, then a layer of broken potsherds laid edge-up like fish scales, then clay again, and further down, as far as anyone has dug with a bone scoop, more sherds. No one has found earth. The ground is a made thing all the way through, and it is still warm, like a bowl just lifted from the ashes.

## The Walls

The walls are coil-built. Their long ropes of clay were rolled between palms and laid spiral on spiral, and in most places no one smoothed the coils flat. You can run a finger along one ridge and follow it around the whole enclosure, rising a hand's width with each circuit, until it vanishes into the upper wall where the light thins. On every surface, at every height, even the height of the parapet where no one could have reached, there are fingerprints. Thumb-drags. The crescent dents of fingernails. They are fresh. The edges of the whorls are still crisp, never softened by weather, because there has never been weather here.

In places the walls are fired hard and ring faintly when tapped, a dull, sweet *tok* like a pot's rim. In other places they are merely sun-dried, pale and chalky, and flake at the touch. In a few patches they are still leather-soft, and a thumb pressed in leaves a print among the thousands of others. The new print does not look any different from the rest. This is the first thing that unsettles visitors: you cannot tell which marks are yours.

The edges are sharp everywhere. Nothing has had time to wear. Broken sherds in the floor still have glassy, knife-bright fractures. Knapped flint pegs, driven into the walls as hooks or perhaps just as punctuation, keep conchoidal ripples so clean they look wet, though nothing here is wet.

## The Dryness

There is no water in the Close. The dryness is complete in a way that is hard to describe until you have felt it. The air takes moisture from the mouth. Lips crack within an hour. Yet the place is not barren, and this is its central contradiction. It is crowded, overgrown, lush.

The garden here grows on heat and dust instead of water.

## The Vegetation

The plants are clay, or they are something that has agreed to be clay. They rise from the sherd-floor in close, tangled beds, knee-high and shoulder-high, and in the courtyard's center a canopy reaches twice a person's height. Their stems are thrown. You can see the spiral throwing-lines on them, the faint ridges a potter's fingers leave when drawing a cylinder upward on a wheel. At every joint there is a slight twist where the stem was pinched and turned, and from these joints leaves splay out: thin slabs, pressed flat and scored with veins by a fine bone tool. Some leaves are the red-brown of iron-rich earthenware. Some are the grey-black of smoke-fired pots. Some are pale buff, almost white, and these turn slowly toward the heat.

They grow. That is the hard part to accept. They grow slowly, but you can watch it if you sit still through a long afternoon. Dust drifts in the warm air, a fine rose-colored powder that seems to come from nowhere and everywhere, and it settles on the upper rims of the stems and the edges of leaves. Where it settles, it fuses. A leaf gains a fingernail's width of new margin in a day. A stem adds a coil. The growth is dry and granular, like a sandcastle built grain by grain. The heat bakes each new layer just enough to hold.

Some plants flower. Their flowers are small pinched cups, four or five petals pressed between thumb and forefinger. Inside each is a pool, not of nectar, but of a thick ochre powder so fine it behaves almost like a liquid. It sloshes and shimmers when the stem sways, and it pours in slow, silky threads. This is the closest thing to water in the Close, and the fauna live by it.

There are also vines of braided fiber, three-strand plaits of something like flax or nettle-bast. They climb the walls in dense curtains, anchored at each coil-ridge by a tiny loop. Nobody plaited them, or rather, they keep being plaited. At the growing tip of each vine the three strands keep crossing over one another in the steady rhythm of hands working rope. There are no hands. The strands simply keep crossing, left over middle, right over middle, a few crossings a day. Where the vines are oldest they have been knotted into shapes: net-panels, simple loops, and once, on the eastern wall, a figure like a person with arms raised, which some believe is a signature.

In the densest part of the garden the plants lean on one another. Slab-leaves overlap like roof tiles. Stems fuse where they touch, joined by a smear of slip, the thin clay-and-dust paste potters use to weld two pieces together. The beds therefore become a continuous low structure, a single many-stemmed object, and walking through it means choosing which gaps to squeeze into. Your shoulders pick up rose dust. After an hour you are faintly pink.

## The Fauna

The Close is full of animals, and they are made too.

The most common are small beetle-shapes, the size of a thumb joint, modeled from damp dark clay with legs of twisted grass-fiber. They are unfired. Their backs still carry the impression of a palm. They move in brief, halting bursts, as if each step has to be thought about, and they cluster at the powder-pools in the flower-cups, dipping their heads to draw up the ochre dust. As they drink, they grow a little glossier and a little harder. A beetle that has fed well for a season takes on the faint sheen of burnished pottery. A beetle that stays in the sun too long dries and cracks and stops moving. Its broken body is incorporated into the floor, one more sherd pressed in edge-up among the rest. This is how the floor got made. It is, in part, a graveyard.

There are birds the size of fists, pinched from pale clay, with wings of thin slab curved over a finger to dry. They cannot fly in any real sense. They glide from stem to stem across short gaps, falling a little each time, and must climb back up with their stubby feet. Their eyes are two holes punched with a reed. When they open their beaks no sound comes out. Instead, a small puff of rose dust escapes, drifts, and settles on the nearest leaf, where it becomes growth. The birds are sowing the garden. They seem to know it.

There are serpent-coils: single long ropes of clay, not yet laid into a pot, that move across the floor by curling and uncurling. Where they rest for a long time they begin to spiral up on themselves and become vessels. Half-formed pots stand everywhere in the Close, abandoned mid-transformation. Some are three coils high. Some are nearly finished jars, needing only a rim. A serpent that completes itself stops and becomes a jar, and the jar is often found later to contain something: a handful of seeds made of fired clay beads, a folded leaf, a single perfect fingerprint pressed into the inner base, and occasionally, impossibly, a smaller version of the Close.

There are mice made of knotted fiber, and they run. They are the only quick things here. They live inside the walls, in the gaps between coils, and they carry dust in their knotted bodies from one part of the Close to another. There are moths of paper-thin sherd that cling to the cooler walls at dusk.

The density is remarkable. Stand still anywhere in the Close and you will find within arm's reach a dozen beetles, two or three birds, a coil-serpent midway up its own spiral, several mice at the edge of vision, and the constant slow drip of dust from above. Everything is close to touching. Everything is being added to.

## The Sound

The Close is almost silent, and the silence is dry and warm, like the inside of a gourd.

What sound there is comes in small, particular pieces. Fired clay ticks as it cools, *tik… tik…*, the faint expansion and contraction of pottery in the long heat cycles. Dust hisses when a flower-cup tips and pours. Fiber creaks as a vine completes a crossing. There is a dull, low *tok* when a beetle knocks against a fired wall. Now and then comes the sharper sound of something drying too fast and cracking: a leaf splitting along its vein, a bird's wing parting from its body. When that happens, all motion in the Close pauses for a breath. Then it resumes.

There is no wind, only drifts of warm air rising from the floor. No voices, though people who have spent long in the Close report something like a hum just below hearing. It resembles the sound a potter makes under their breath while working, a tuneless contented drone. When they turn toward it, it is gone.

## The Heat

Heat is the Close's medium in the way water is a river's. It comes from below. The floor is warmest at the center and cools toward the walls, and it pulses slowly, rising over several days to a point where the sherd-floor is too hot to stand on barefoot and the air shimmers above the plant-beds. Then it falls away over several more days, until the Close is merely warm, like a hand held near a hearth.

During the hot phase, growth quickens. Dust fuses faster. The unfired animals move more urgently, seeking shade in the lee of plants, and many harden. Some die. During the cool phase, things soften a little. The walls grow leather-soft in patches. New fingerprints appear. The half-finished pots grow a coil or two. The Close seems to be thinking.

Some believe the whole Close is itself in a kiln, a vast unseen firing chamber, and that the pulse of heat is someone tending a fire on the far side of the sky. If so, the tending is patient. Each cycle lasts about twelve days, and the Close has been through an uncounted number. Yet everything still looks fresh, sharp-edged, recently made. It is as if the Close were always in its first firing.

## The Light

The light is ordinary daylight, which becomes strange once you notice there is no sky. Above the parapet the view simply stops: a warm, even, buff-colored brightness, the color of the inside of a bowl held up to the sun. Shadows fall normally under the plants, but when you study them they fall toward the center of the Close from every direction, as though the light came from all the walls at once, or as though the center were the lowest point in a curved basin.

Over the course of a day the light moves around the enclosure, warmer on the walls in the morning, paler at noon, dimmer and redder toward the end. This is not quite like a sun, however. It is more like a lamp being carried slowly around the outside of a thin-walled pot. On some evenings a large, dark, rounded shape can be seen, very faintly, pressed against the light from outside. It might be a palm. It might be a face bent close.

## The Rules

Everything in the Close behaves as if it is being made, and the making never finishes. Several rules seem to hold.

**Nothing is ever complete.** Every pot lacks something: a rim, a handle, a second firing. Every vine is still braiding. Every leaf is still gaining margin. Things that do become complete stop moving and are absorbed into the structure: the floor, the walls, the canopy. Completion is how a thing leaves the living part of the Close.

**Fingerprints are authorship.** A thumb pressed into soft clay here does something. The mark joins the rest, and what it touched begins, faintly, to lean toward the person who made it. Beetles follow. Vines reach. Visitors who press too many marks find that the Close begins to arrange itself around them. Paths open where they walk, and shadows fall toward them instead of the center. It is as if the place is testing whether they are the potter it has been waiting for. Those who stay long enough report that their own hands begin to look unfamiliar: slightly too smooth, the whorls of their fingertips gone faint. The theory is that the Close has been lifting their fingerprints for its walls.

**The inside and outside are not certain.** The finished jars sometimes hold a Close. You can look down through the mouth of one and see a tiny courtyard: four walls, a garden of clay, beetles no larger than grains, the pulse of heat. Some who have looked long enough claim to have seen, deep down in that miniature, a small figure peering up out of a jar. It is not clear which way the nesting runs. It is quite possible that the Close you stand in is itself inside a jar in a larger Close, one among many unfinished vessels on a shelf in a workshop no one has seen.

**The walls do not agree about direction.** Walk toward any wall and it is north. Turn around, and the wall behind you is now also north. Maps drawn here come out as spirals rather than squares. One route along the base of the walls, following a single coil-ridge with the fingertips, does return you to where you started, but you arrive one coil higher each time. Nobody has followed it far enough to learn where it ends.

**Water is unwelcome.** Visitors who brought water and spilled it describe what followed as something like an injury. The clay around the spill slumped and ran, and the beetles fled it. Where it pooled, fingerprints dissolved into smooth blankness. For days afterward that part of the Close refused to grow. Plants leaned away from the spot, and the dust would not settle there. Eventually the heat dried it, and a pale scar remained in the floor, like the rim of a cup ring on wood. The Close heals, but it does not forget.

## Time

Time moves slowly here and in layers. The heat-cycle pulses over days. Leaves grow over weeks. A beetle takes a season to harden from damp to glossy. A serpent-coil takes perhaps a year to become a jar. The walls grow a coil every few years, and the Close as a whole seems, very gradually, to be deepening. The floor is building up, the walls rising, the enclosed space growing a little taller, like a pot being thrown up from its base.

Yet nothing ages. There is no patina, no wear, no dust in the corners. The rose dust is always fresh and always arriving. Every edge is sharp. Every fingerprint is crisp. The place is always new, always being made, and never finished. A person who spends a long afternoon sitting in the shade of the clay canopy, watching the birds puff their silent dust and the beetles drink from flower-cups, may lose track of whether they have been there an hour or a year. Their skin will be faintly pink with dust. Their lips will be cracked. Someone else's fingerprints will be pressed into the soft clay beside where they sat.

The Close holds its breath with them, warm, patient, and almost silent, waiting for the hands.
