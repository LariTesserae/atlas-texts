# The Warm Throat

## Approach

The mountain has no name that anyone uses anymore, but the opening near its summit has one: the Warm Throat. It sits about four hundred meters below the broken crest, on the lee flank where wind rarely reaches. The land around it is bare scoria, rust-red and charcoal, weathered into rounded knuckles and shallow basins by a very long time of heat and grit. Nothing grows here. The air shimmers even at night, because the whole upper mountain is warm from below. A stone set down on the path feels as though it has been sitting in afternoon sun, and that doesn't change at midnight.

The Throat is not large. It is a cleft in an old lava wall, tall enough to walk into without stooping and narrow enough to touch both sides at once. It runs back perhaps thirty paces and then opens into a chamber about the size of a small chapel: an irregular dome, twelve or fifteen meters across, its ceiling lost in the dark. That is the whole place. It is one room with one door, and it is full.

## The Heat

The first thing is the heat, which comes out of the cleft like breath from a sleeping animal. It isn't the wet heat of a jungle or the blast of a furnace. It is dry and thick and mineral, and it smells of struck flint and sulfur and something else, faintly sweet and faintly rank, like a stable where the straw has been left too long in summer. The deeper you go, the more the heat stops being air and becomes pressure. Your skin tightens. Your eyes dry out almost at once, so you blink more often.

The heat comes from below. The floor of the chamber is crossed with old fissures, most of them sealed long ago by their own slag and glass, a few still open. From these rises a steady exhalation of hot, dry gas. It is not quite volcanic steam, because there is almost no water in it. It is more like the breath of rock itself: the mountain releasing heat it has held for ten thousand years and will hold for ten thousand more.

## The Dark

There is almost no light. The daylight from the cleft dies within a few paces and does not reach the chamber. What remains, once your eyes adjust (and it takes a long time), is a dull, bloodless red at the bottom of the deepest fissure, near the chamber's back wall. It is not flame. It is rock hot enough to glow faintly, the color of an iron bar pulled from a forge and left to cool for an hour. It lights nothing. It only tells you where the floor ends.

So for a while you think the chamber is empty. You see the dim red seam and nothing else, and you hear almost nothing: a faint tick of stone contracting and expanding, a soft hiss from the vents. Then you put your hand on the wall.

## The Walls Are Alive

The wall is soft and warm, and it moves.

Every surface of the chamber is covered in animals: the walls, the ceiling, the rounded boulders on the floor, the lips of the vents. They are packed so tightly that no rock shows between them. They are layered, some clinging to the backs of others, three and four and six deep in the warmer places. The people who once came here called them *ash-moths*, though they are not moths. Each is about the length of a thumb joint. They have flattened, overlapping bodies, dust-gray and velvety, and six short hooked legs. They have wings they almost never use, folded like fans of soot-colored paper along their backs. Their eyes are vestigial, small dull beads that catch no light because there is no light to catch.

There are more of them than can be counted. A single square meter of wall might hold four or five thousand. The chamber holds tens of millions. They fill the room the way water fills a cup. Walking in means stepping onto them, and they part slowly around your feet like thick fur. Breathing means breathing the fine dust of their shed scales, which drifts constantly through the hot air and settles on your tongue with a taste like chalk and pepper.

## The Quiet

For all those bodies, the chamber is nearly silent.

The ash-moths make no calls. Their wings do not buzz because they do not fly. Their legs are padded, and when they shift, which is constantly, the sound is so soft and so evenly spread across the whole room that it stops being a sound and becomes a kind of pressure in the ears, like the hush of blood in a shell held to your head. It is the sound of silk drawn across silk, multiplied past hearing. Beneath it are the tick of the rock and the whisper of the vents. That is all.

Visitors who stayed for a while said the silence was the most frightening part. Some said it was the most peaceful.

## The Tide

The chamber is never still, though its motion is slow. It follows a rhythm set by the mountain.

About every five hours, the heat from the vents rises. No one knows why. Perhaps something shifts in the deep plumbing of the old volcano, some chamber filling and emptying. Over an hour or so the exhalation grows stronger and hotter, and the red seam at the back brightens from a glow to something close to a smolder. Then it eases again. The mountain breathes, very slowly.

The ash-moths follow the breath. They live in a narrow band of temperature, a few degrees wide, below which they grow sluggish and above which they die. As the heat rises, the whole living carpet migrates upward, away from the vents, toward the cooler ceiling. As the heat eases, it flows back down. You can feel this happening against your palm: a slow, steady creep, like a tide coming in over sand, except the tide is made of millions of small warm bodies. Over the course of the mountain's breath, the mass rises and falls three or four meters up the walls. Where it has retreated, bare stone shows for a little while, dark and polished smooth by countless generations of feet, until the tide returns.

In the hottest part of each cycle, the ones too slow to climb die. They curl, stiffen, and drop from the walls onto the vents. The chamber floor, beneath the living layer, is a deep, soft drift of their bodies, many meters thick in places, baked dry and compacted over centuries into something like felt, and deeper down into something like stone. Where it has been cut by old fissures, you can see the layers, thousands of thin gray bands, one for every season of dying. The chamber is slowly filling itself in with its own dead.

## What They Eat

There are no plants on the mountain, and nothing blows in on the wind. For a long time this puzzled the people who came here.

The answer is on the rock itself. Where the hot gas comes out of the vents, it leaves a crust: pale yellow sulfur, white salts, and a thin, greasy, gray-green film that is alive. It is a mat of something simpler than moss and simpler than fungus, a film of tiny cells that feed on the chemistry of the mountain's breath the way leaves feed on light. It is the only growing thing in the Throat, and it grows fast in the heat. The ash-moths graze on it. When the tide brings them down toward the vents, they scrape the crust with short, rasping mouths, and the film grows back behind them.

In the drifts of dead moths, pale threads grow too, fine white filaments that feed on the bodies and bind the layers together. Where they break the surface, they stand up like thin hairs, a few millimeters tall. They are the closest thing to vegetation within a day's walk.

## Water

There is almost no water, but there is a little.

At the very top of the dome, where the ceiling narrows to a crooked chimney, the rock is slightly cooler. The faint moisture in the vent-gas condenses there into droplets. It happens slowly: a single drop forms, swells, and falls perhaps once every hour or two. It falls through the dark onto the backs of the ash-moths packed on the chamber floor directly below.

That spot is the most crowded in the chamber. The moths there are stacked so thickly they form a low mound, a living heap, all of them straining toward the point where the next drop will land. When it falls, there is a brief ripple through the mound, a shiver that spreads out a meter or two and fades. That drop is the only water many of them will ever touch. It is enough, just barely, for the whole room.

## The Hunters

The ash-moths are not alone.

Moving through the living carpet, under and among the layers, are the hunters. They are long, pale, segmented things, as long as a forearm and as thin as a finger, with dozens of short legs and blunt, eyeless heads. People called them *threaders*, because of how they move: they slip through the mass the way a needle goes through wool, parting the bodies ahead of them and closing them behind, leaving no gap. There are perhaps a few hundred in the chamber at any time. They feed on the ash-moths, taking one every few minutes, holding it briefly in a ring of fine mouthparts, and then moving on.

Here the chamber shows its one strangeness of light.

When a threader moves close, the ash-moths around it glow. It is a faint, cold, blue-white light, like moonlight on frost, coming from a thin line along the edge of each moth's folded wings. It spreads outward from the threader in a slow ring, moth to moth, dying away after a meter or two. It is too dim to see by, but in the utter dark of the chamber it is visible: small pale ripples blooming and fading across the walls and ceiling, dozens at a time, each marking a hunter somewhere in the mass. Watching them is like watching rain fall on a black pond at night.

No one knows whether the light warns the other moths, confuses the threaders, or does something else. The threaders are blind. They should not be able to see it. And yet where the light blooms, they slow.

## The Marks

People came here, long ago. They left very little.

At the mouth of the cleft is a low line of flat stones, set upright in the scoria and worn round by weather. They may have been a threshold, a boundary, a warning. Just inside, in a shallow niche cut into the wall, sits a clay bowl. The heat has fired it harder than any kiln could, and it has partly fused to the rock beneath it. It is empty except for the dust of shed scales. Farther in, on the chamber floor, a few more flat stones have been set in a rough ring near the place where the water drop falls, their tops blackened and slick.

And there are the hands.

On the chamber's eastern wall, at about the height of a standing adult, are handprints. There are perhaps twenty or thirty, made with some dark pigment long ago, pressed flat onto the stone, fingers spread. They are faded almost to nothing. You would never see them in the dark.

But the ash-moths will not stand on them.

When the tide rises up the eastern wall, the living carpet flows over the stone, thick and unbroken, except in the shape of each hand. There the moths part, leaving the handprint bare. Twenty or thirty pale hands, open in the dark, are outlined perfectly by the warm gray mass around them. When the tide goes down, the hands vanish into the general bareness of the wall. When it comes back, they appear again, every five hours, for as long as anyone remembers.

No one knows why. Perhaps something in the old pigment is bitter to them. Perhaps something else. The threaders do not cross the hands either. On the rare occasions when the blue-white warning light blooms near the eastern wall, it stops at the edge of each print, as if the light too would rather not touch it.

## Being There

To stand in the Warm Throat is to stand in a room that is entirely alive and almost entirely silent, in heat that never breaks and dark that never lifts. Millions of small bodies press softly against your ankles and slowly climb the walls around you. Somewhere above, a single drop is forming. Somewhere in the mass, a pale hunter is threading its way through the dark, and a faint cold light is blooming ahead of it and fading behind. The mountain is breathing. The floor is slowly rising with the dead. The hands on the eastern wall wait for the tide.

It has been like this for a very long time. It is in balance, as much as anything is. It asks nothing of you. When you leave, stepping back out through the cleft into the ordinary night, the air feels shockingly cold, the stars seem impossibly loud, and the ash-moth dust on your skin smells faintly sweet for days.
