# The Tableland of Slow Engines

## The Height

It begins as a feeling in the lungs before it is a place. The air is thin and warm, like air that has been breathed already and given back. There is no wind. Nothing up here has moved quickly for a very long time.

The land is a plateau, though it was not always one. It was a mountain range once, a spine of peaks higher than anything now standing. That was so long ago that the peaks have worn down to their roots. What remains is a table of stone many days' walk across, raised far above whatever lies below. The edges fall away into a darkness that no one has measured. The top is almost perfectly level, not flat the way a floor is flat, but the way an old coin is: every relief rubbed down to a soft suggestion. Here and there a low swell rises, the stump of a summit, rounded like a knuckle under skin. You could walk over one and only know it by the ache in your calves.

The rock is dark basalt shot through with veins of something paler, quartz or feldspar gone milky with age. Where the veins cross they make shapes that look almost deliberate. Everything is covered by the dust.

## The Dust

The dust is the first rule of the Tableland, and the most important one.

It is fine as milled flour, the grey of cold ash, and it lies over everything to an even depth. Roughly a hand's width covers the open stone. Deeper drifts sit in hollows, and the thinnest skin coats vertical surfaces, clinging by some static patience. It is the ground-down substance of the mountains, of the city, and of everything that has ever stood here. There is no water to wash it anywhere and no wind to lift it. So it stays where it settles.

This means that marks persist. A footprint pressed into the dust tonight will be there, crisp-edged, in ten thousand years. The Tableland keeps records without meaning to. Walk far enough and you find the tracks of whoever came before: a single line of prints crossing a square, two sets side by side that stop, turn, and go back the way they came. There is a place near the eastern works where someone once sat down and drew a spiral with a finger. Nobody knows when. It is as sharp as if it were drawn this morning, and in a sense, on the Tableland, it was. The difference between this morning and the deep past has worn away along with the mountains.

The dust swallows sound. It is why the Tableland is so quiet.

## The Dark

There is no sky, or rather the sky is there and holds nothing. No stars, no moon, no glow of cloud, no horizon line where land meets air. Look up and the black is total, a softer and deeper dark than a closed room. Your eyes keep trying to find a texture in it and fail. Some who have lived here say the sky is not empty but finished: whatever lights were in it burned down and wore away, the way the peaks did.

The only light comes from below and from the city, and there is very little of either.

The ground is warm. It is not hot, but it is warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, and the warmth comes up through your soles. In certain places, where the pale veins run deep, the stone glows. The glow is a dull red, barely at the threshold of seeing, visible only after your eyes have lived in the dark for an hour. It is the color of a coal an instant before it goes out, except it never goes out. The glowing seams trace long crooked lines across the plateau like the writing of something enormous, and they give the land its only horizon: a faint scatter of ember-threads fading into distance in every direction.

The heat has been leaving the mountain's buried root since before the mountains wore down. It leaves so slowly that the loss cannot be measured in any life. For every practical purpose the warmth is permanent. The Tableland is warm the way it is dark: as a settled fact.

## The City

The city was built to drink that heat.

It spreads across the plateau loosely, a scattering rather than a crush. There are districts and works, clusters of halls and chimneys, separated by long stretches of open dust-covered stone. From any one cluster you might just make out the next as a pale, patient smudge of light far off. Rail lines connect them, straight as rulings on a page and running for leagues without a curve. The builders knew the land would never shift beneath them.

It was an industrial city. You can read that in its bones. There are brick halls with roofs hundreds of paces long, built over machinery. There are chimneys, dozens of them, standing in rows. There are gantries and bridges of riveted iron, pipework thick as a person that runs along the ground, dives into the rock, and surfaces again elsewhere. There are pylons with arms like scarecrows, strung with cable.

But the city has aged the way the mountains aged, and that is the strange thing. No water weathered it and no wind scoured it, yet it is worn. The bricks are rounded at every edge, smooth as river stones, their mortar sunk into shallow grooves. The iron has not rusted, for there was nothing to rust it, but it has thinned. Girders that once were heavy are now slender as ribs, and rivets have become soft bumps. Window glass has gone completely opaque with a fine frosted etching, as if countless years of simply existing had sandblasted it from the inside. Stairs are dished in the middle as though walked by millions of feet, though few feet ever came here.

Stay in the Tableland long enough and you begin to believe that time itself is abrasive here. Nothing wears things down; they are simply worn by being. The dust is what falls off the world when enough stillness has passed across it. The city has spent so long in stillness that it has sanded itself down toward the shape of the land. Its tallest chimneys are shorter than they once were, measured by the scars where their upper courses used to be. Given long enough, the city will become another low swell in the stone, a knuckle under skin. It is perhaps a third of the way there.

## The Engines

The machines are still running.

Inside the long halls, beneath the frosted windows, are the heat engines. They are vast, and so is everything about them: flywheels taller than houses, pistons like felled columns, connecting rods, governors, valve-gear, all of it black iron worn to a soft satin sheen. They were built to take the difference between the hot rock below and the cooler air above and turn it into motion. For a while, surely, they roared.

The difference is almost gone now. Rock and air have nearly settled to the same warmth. So the engines have not stopped, but they have slowed to the edge of stillness, holding just short of it.

Stand before a flywheel and it looks motionless. Mark its rim with a scratch in the dust, and come back some years later, and the scratch will have moved perhaps a finger's width. Each piston is in the middle of a single stroke that will take a century to finish. The governors' weighted arms are raised in an outward swing, like a dancer caught in a photograph. All of it is in motion, perfectly balanced, and the balance does not change. The engines are the Tableland's clearest expression of its law: this is a world that has almost reached the end of its gradients and then stopped approaching, holding in the last narrow margin forever.

You can hear them if you press your ear against the iron. The sound is not really sound. It is a long low tension in the metal, a note deep enough that it registers in the teeth and breastbone rather than the ear. Every engine in every hall holds the same note. Out on the open stone, far from any works, you sometimes feel it through the ground, faint as a pulse in a wrist. People who lived here said the city was humming itself to sleep. They also said it would never quite get there.

## The Lights

The engines turn dynamos, and the dynamos, turning at the same impossible slowness, still make current. It travels the scarecrow pylons along cables thinned to near-threads and reaches the lamps.

There are not many lamps. Most failed or were never lit. The ones that remain hang on iron posts along the rail lines, over the doorways of halls, and at the crossings of empty streets. Their filaments glow a deep dim orange-red, much like the rock seams. The light is so faint that a lamp illuminates almost nothing but itself: the loop of the filament and a vague halo on its own glass. It will not show you the ground at your feet. It shows you only where the lamp is, and from that, where the city is.

They never brighten and they never fail. They do not flicker. The current is so small and so constant that the filaments neither burn away nor cool. Each is a tiny coal held in place. Walk the rail lines at night, which is always, and you move from one pinprick to the next, each far enough from the last that between them you walk in total darkness. You follow the rail by the feel of it under your boot and the next ember hanging ahead.

Seen from a height, from one of the low stump-summits, the whole city is a sparse constellation laid on the ground: little clusters of orange points, long dotted lines of them running between, and the red threads of the rock seams wandering beneath. It is the only sky the Tableland has. The builders may have meant it that way.

## The Living

Very little lives here, but not nothing.

The first are the **kilnmoths**. They are small and pale, and their wings are so thin and stiff they are nearly mineral, like flakes of mica. They cluster on the lamps, pressed flat against the glass, sometimes a dozen on one bulb and sometimes none for a mile. They feed on warmth; nobody knows what else. They almost never fly. When one does, it moves the way everything here moves: a single slow unfolding of its wings, a drift of a few paces through the dark, a settling. Their wings leave tiny powder-prints in the dust wherever they land, and those prints, too, stay forever. Around the oldest lamps the ground is stippled with them, the record of every moth-landing since the lamps were lit.

The second are rarer. The people who lived here called them **tenders**. They are long-limbed and low-bodied, about the size of a large dog. Their shells are the color and texture of fired clay, warm to the touch, faintly ridged like the bricks. They walk the pipe runs and the rails, very slowly, pressing their bodies against the warm iron to draw heat. Sometimes one enters a hall and lies along a piston or against the hub of a flywheel, and stays there for a year.

Whether they truly tend anything is unknown. But the engines have not seized, and the bearings have not dried. There are faint oily smears on certain joints, fresh on a world where nothing is fresh. And the tenders' breath is the only moisture on the Tableland. When one exhales against cold glass, which is rare, a tiny fog blooms and then fades. That fog is nearly all the water there is. It is the nearest thing to weather.

Their footprints cross the dust in long, patient, perfectly straight lines between the works, lines that someday, not soon, will be crossed by the same animal coming back.

## What Else Is True Here

- **There are no seasons, no days, and no weather.** The warmth is the same everywhere and always. The dark is the same. The air does not stir. A person living here keeps time only by the engines, and the engines keep time in centuries.

- **Nothing decays; things only wear.** There is no rot, no rust, no mold. There is only this slow sanding-away into dust, the same for iron and brick and bone.

- **Everything leaves a record, and nothing erases it.** Because the dust holds every mark, the Tableland is a single vast document of every movement ever made upon it. In the old squares the layered prints of the builders are still visible under a later skin of dust, overwritten but not lost. Those who study it say that with enough patience you could reconstruct everything that ever happened on the plateau, down to where each person paused.

- **Sound does not travel.** Speak aloud in an open street and your voice dies a few paces away in the dust. Two people on the Tableland must stand close to hear each other. The builders' halls are full of speaking tubes, iron pipes running from room to room, because it was the only way to be heard.

- **The city is unfinished, and also finished.** Rails run out into the open plateau and stop at nothing. Foundations were laid for halls never raised. It feels as if the builders stopped mid-gesture, like the engines, and that the gesture is still going on.

## Walking It

To cross the Tableland is to walk for a very long time in warm dark, following faint orange lights, over ground that holds your footprints forever. The silence is so deep that you hear the blood in your ears. Now and then you pass a hall and feel its engine's note in your chest, or come on a kilnmoth on a lamp and watch it not move for an hour. Once in a great while you see a tender lying against a pipe in the dim red glow of a rock seam, its clay shell rising and falling once, slowly, with a single breath.

It is not a dead place. It is a place that has almost stopped and has made its peace with almost. The mountains are nearly gone, the heat nearly spent, the engines nearly still, the lights nearly out. All of it holds right there, at the last warm edge, and it will keep holding longer than anything else has lasted.

Your footprints will stay. Everyone's do. It is the Tableland's only way of remembering, and it forgets nothing.
