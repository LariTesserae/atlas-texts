# The Works at Low Tallow

## The Basin

Low Tallow sits in a hollow of grey clay a little below the level of the surrounding marsh, as if someone once pressed a thumb into the land and built in the print. An embankment rings it, a long curved dyke of rammed earth faced with blue engineering brick, about four men high and walkable along its top. From the embankment you can see the whole of it at once. It is small. You could cross it in the time it takes to smoke a pipe, if the air let you smoke, which it does not. It is too wet and too full.

The floor of the basin is clay that has been trodden, rained on, and dried for so long that it has the texture of old leather: smooth, faintly shining, cracked into plates the size of dinner tables. The cracks never widen. Between the plates runs a fine black silt that holds water the way felt does, so the ground is always damp to the touch but never muddy. Nothing grows in it. Nothing has ever grown in it. No moss holds on the north faces of the walls, no grass comes up between the setts. The clay is stone-dead, and you feel it through your boots, a coldness that is not quite temperature.

The buildings stand far apart, with open yard between them, and the spacing looks deliberate, the way furniture looks in a room arranged by someone who expected many people and got few.

## The Structures

There are nine of them, and you can count them from the embankment.

**The Engine House** stands at the centre: tall, narrow, round-arched windows of greenish glass, walls of the same blue brick gone soft at every edge. The corners are no longer corners but curves, rubbed round by damp and by something else, which you will learn. Inside, a beam engine nods. Its iron beam is as long as a barge, pivoted on a column fluted like a temple's, and it rises and falls once every eleven seconds. It has never stopped. The flywheel beside it is taller than the building's doors and turns with a sound like surf dragged backward over shingle.

**The Chimney** rises from behind the engine house, octagonal, tapering, banded with courses of yellow brick every twenty feet. It is the tallest thing for miles. Its smoke comes out the top in a column that leans always slightly east, always the same amount, and flattens into a grey lid over the basin that never thickens and never clears. That lid is why the light at Low Tallow is what it is.

**The Mill** is a long four-storey block with rows of identical windows and an iron hoist-beam jutting from its gable. Shafts and belts run from the engine house to the mill through a covered bridge of timber and corrugated iron, and you can hear the belts through the walls, a continuous leathery slap.

**The Gasometer** is a great iron drum in a lattice cage, half-risen, painted long ago in a red oxide that has weathered to the colour of dried liver. It does not rise or fall.

**The Cistern** is a square open tank of riveted plates, raised on brick piers near the embankment's inner foot.

**The Canal Arm** is a straight stub of black water about two hundred yards long. It enters through a sealed culvert in the embankment and ends at a wharf beside the mill, where no barge is moored.

**The Terrace** is a row of eight workers' cottages with slate roofs, chimneys, doors, and small square windows. Their doors are shut and their glass is clouded. No smoke comes from them.

**The Weighhouse** is a squat hut beside the mill gate with a great iron platform scale set into the ground before it.

**The Counting Wall** is a freestanding length of brick near the terrace. It bears no roof and belongs to no building. It is painted with columns of white numerals.

That is all. Between these nine things lies open clay yard, grey and leathery and bare.

## The Light

The light is ordinary afternoon light on a cloudy day in autumn, about half past three. It is enough to read by and not enough to cast a shadow with a clean edge. Colours are true but muted: the blue brick looks blue-grey, the gasometer liver-red, the canal water black with a pewter skin. It is the light of a working day that has settled into its second half and will not reach its end.

It is always half past three. Not because a clock says so; there is a clock on the engine house gable, and its hands point to twenty to nine, and they do not move. It is half past three because the smoke-lid lets through exactly that much sky, no more and no less, and the sky beyond the lid, if there is a sky beyond the lid, has not been consulted in a long time.

## The Water

Water at Low Tallow is not rain, or almost never. It is condensation.

The chimney's smoke is wet. The engine's steam escapes in thin plumes from glands and valves and drain-cocks, and the air of the basin is cool, about the temperature of a cellar or a church in October. So everything iron sweats. Every pipe, every rail, every rivet head wears a bead of water. Every window runs on its inside. The underside of the covered belt-bridge drips in a line, like a gutter, onto a stripe of clay beneath that has been worn into a shallow channel by the drip.

All the drips of the basin find their way, by channels worn in the clay over what must be centuries, to the cistern. There is one channel from each building, and they converge like the spokes of a wheel on the tank by the embankment. A final iron pipe carries the water up into the tank by some pressure you cannot see the source of. The cistern is always exactly three-quarters full. It never overflows. The water level sits two rivets below a line of rust on the tank's inner wall, and it has sat there long enough to make that line.

On the cistern's surface floats a skin, pale grey, finely wrinkled, like the skin on hot milk that has gone cold. It is a yeast, or something close to yeast. It is the only thing at Low Tallow that is anything like a plant, and it is barely that. Under a lens it is a mat of cells, colourless, packed. It smells faintly of bread and pennies.

The canal arm is fed by nothing, since its culvert is sealed, and drained by nothing. Its level never shifts.

## The Animals

Now the part that is hard to describe, because it is most of what is here.

From the embankment you do not see the animals at first as animals. You see the buildings as rough-textured, shaggy, faintly moving, as though they were upholstered. Then your eye resolves it.

**The Chimney** is covered in rooks. Every course of brick, every band, every ledge of the octagon's eight faces holds a rook, shoulder to shoulder, facing inward so that their tails hang out over the drop. There are thousands. At the top, where the smoke comes out, they sit in the smoke itself on the iron lip of the cap, and their feathers there are not black but sooted grey, like the smoke-lid. Rooks leave the chimney in a single file that runs as straight as a rope to the mill's hoist-beam, and a single file returns beside it. The two strands never cross or tangle. Each bird in the outgoing line carries in its beak a small crumb of something pale.

**The Mill** is full of rats. You can see them through every window on every floor, a close grey pelt that shifts. In the upper rooms they run in wheels, rows and rows of iron wheels geared to line-shafting. These are not the engine's shafts but separate ones. The rats turn them, and the shafts drive the millstones on the ground floor. The engine, it turns out, drives the belts that drive the sieves and hoists. The engine and the rats share the work. Neither could do it alone.

**The Engine House** belongs to jackdaws. They are smaller and quicker than the rooks, grey-naped, pale-eyed. They crowd the coal bunkers, and each one takes a single lump of coal in its beak and flies with it, one to a window, one to a door, to the firebox mouth. They drop it in and fly out by another opening. The stoke-hole glows with a steady, untroubled orange, and the jackdaws pass before it in a continuous flicker. Their passage wore the corners of the engine house round. A million million wingtips have brushed the brick on the turn.

**The Gasometer** holds bats. In the dark between the drum and its outer wall, and in the lattice cage, they hang in sheets. They do not sleep. They cannot, because they are listening. The gasometer is a resonating drum, and every sound in the basin arrives there. The bats hang in it and click, and you can hear the clicks as a fine high fizzing, like fat in a pan. They are, as far as anyone can tell, keeping time.

**The Canal** is roofed with beetles: whirligigs and water boatmen, mostly, black and oily-bright, so dense on the surface that the water looks like hammered metal. They circle in tight whorls that never collide, thousands of whorls, each a little spinning coin, and the coins are set edge to edge across the whole length of the arm.

**The Weighhouse** platform is crossed, continuously, by mice. They come from the mill in a stream an inch deep, cross the iron plate of the scale, and go into the weighhouse hut, and presumably out the other side, though there is no other side you can see. The scale's needle, visible through the hut's window, stands exactly at one hundredweight and never quivers.

**The Terrace** cottages are full of moths. Their windows are not clouded but furred. Inside, the moths are so dense on the walls and in the air that the rooms look packed with grey wool. There is a lamp in every cottage, a gas mantle, burning, its pale green light filtered through the moths. It is the lamps that keep them. The lamps have never gone out.

**The Cistern** has swifts. They skim the yeast-skin in long curves, each taking a sip or a bite, and rise up over the embankment and come back. They are the only creatures that leave the basin at all. They go up and away over the dyke and down out of sight on the marsh side, and then they return. You never see a swift leave without another coming in.

## The Lanes

Here is the thing about the yards. They are empty.

The open clay between the nine structures, which is most of the basin by area, holds no animals whatever. It is not avoided exactly. It is simply not used. Every creature at Low Tallow travels by fixed lines: the rook-ropes from chimney to mill, the jackdaw-flicker from bunker to firebox, the mouse-stream from mill to weighhouse. There is a line of rats, shoulder to shoulder, along a gutter that runs from the mill to the cistern's foot. A line of beetles is carried by a thin film of water along the drip-channels. Each line is exactly the width of its traffic, and the traffic fills it exactly.

Standing in the yard, then, is a strange experience. Around you, in every direction, at a distance of a stone's throw, the air and walls and water swarm with more life than you have ever seen in one place, a density of breathing that should be unbearable. But the ground where you stand is bare, quiet under your feet, and cold, and nothing comes near you. The animals are overwhelming, and they are also elsewhere.

## The Sound

It is loud. There is no way around it.

The engine is the floor of the sound: the beam's descent every eleven seconds, a deep iron *thoom* you feel in your sternum, and the flywheel's long dragging roar under it. The belts slap. The millstones grind with a sound like distant carts. Over all of it are the rooks: a continuous shouting, a chorus of *kaah* and *kraa* and grating chuckles, so many voices that no single call can be picked out. It is less a birdsong than a texture, like rain on a roof. The jackdaws make a sharper, higher chatter, *tchack tchack*, and there are so many of them that it becomes a hiss. The rat-wheels squeak in thousands and the squeaks have fallen into step, so they pulse. The bats fizz. The mice make almost no sound individually, but their crossing of the iron scale is a sustained whisper, like sand poured slowly onto a drum.

What you notice after an hour is that the sound does not vary. It is loud, and it stays exactly as loud. There are no peaks. No rook rises above the others. No engine stroke falls harder than the last. The whole basin holds one chord, dense and dissonant and huge, and it holds it the way a note holds in a cathedral after the organist has lifted their hands, except it does not decay. Standing in it for long enough, you begin to hear it as a kind of silence. You find yourself able to think in it, or unable to think anything but one thought, slowly. The thought, for most who have come here, is some version of *this is finished*.

## The Loop

Here is the economy of Low Tallow, as far as it can be worked out.

The swifts feed on the cistern's yeast and range out over the marsh and bring back insects. These they drop, stunned or dead, onto the canal, and the beetles eat them. The beetles, when they die, sink, and the canal bottom is a soft black paste of beetle-chitin; the rats dredge it from the wharf edge and carry it to the mill. At the mill, the rats run the wheels that turn the stones that grind the paste, along with yeast-skin skimmed from the cistern by the rats' gutter-line, into a fine pale meal. The meal is what the rooks carry: a crumb per bird from the mill to the chimney, where they eat it, and whatever they do not eat they drop into the smoke's updraft, where it rises with the soot and falls again as a pale dust over the terrace roofs. The moths eat the dust. The bats eat the moths, a precise number each night, except there is no night, so perhaps each hour, or each beat. The jackdaws eat the meal as well. They are fed at the mill's lower door by a spill-chute that delivers exactly as much as they need, in exchange for the coal they carry. The coal comes from the bunkers, which are refilled by the mice, who carry each a single grain of coal-dust from the weighhouse. What is in the weighhouse no one knows. The mice go in with nothing and come out with coal.

The engine's firebox burns the coal. The engine's steam condenses on the iron and runs to the cistern. The cistern feeds the yeast.

Nothing is left over. Nothing is short.

## The Counting Wall

On the freestanding wall near the terrace are painted, in white lead gone slightly yellow, nine columns of figures, one for each structure. Under **CHIMNEY** it says 41,206. Under **MILL**, 88,915. Under **CANAL**, a figure too long to read without stepping closer: 1,340,772. And so on.

These are the counts. People who have made the effort, and it takes a very long time, say the counts are correct. Not approximately. Exactly. A rook dies on the chimney; you see it drop, its body sliding down the brick and going still at the foot, where within a minute a line of rats has come and taken it to the mill. And before the body reaches the ground, another rook is on the ledge where it sat, a young one, feathers still a little ragged, and nobody saw where it came from. The count holds. The paint on the wall has never needed changing. It is weathered, flaking at the edges of the numerals, but the numbers themselves are the numbers.

## What It Is Like to Be There

Cold, first. A damp, still cold, not biting, just close. Your clothes grow heavy with it after an hour. Your hands bead like the iron.

Then it is the smell: coal smoke, wet brick, the ammonia of rookeries, the musk of rats, and under it the bread-and-penny smell of the yeast. It all blends into something like the inside of an old iron stove that has been used for baking.

And the feeling of not being needed. You walk in the yards and nothing looks at you. The rooks do not scatter when you pass under their rope. The mice do not change course when you stand beside their stream. If you drop something, a coin, a glove, a button, it lies on the clay, because nothing will cross the clay. If you drop it into one of the lanes, however, it is picked up at once and carried along, and you will not see it again. Visitors have tested this. Someone dropped a pocketknife into the rat-gutter, and nobody ever found it. The counts on the wall did not change. Whatever came in was absorbed without surplus. The system has room for exactly what it has.

The weathering is the only evidence of time, and it is very deep. The stone steps up to the engine house door are worn into three smooth basins, but by claws, not boots. There are grooves on the mill's window sills where a hundred thousand rat-bellies have slid over them. The yellow bands on the chimney have been polished by rook-feet to a gloss like old ivory. The rivets on the cistern, above the water line, are rounded to low domes by the brushing of swift-wings. Everything is smoothed, softened, fitted to the traffic. Low Tallow has not decayed. It has been worn in, the way a tool is worn in, until it fits the hand that uses it perfectly and will not fit any other.

You may notice the terrace doors. The houses were built for people, eight families' worth, with coal-holes and boot-scrapers and brass letterboxes gone green. The workers who stood in these yards, who fed the fireboxes before the jackdaws did, who ran the stones before the rats: they left. Or they did not leave and became something that can be counted. Or they were never here, and the terrace was built the way a church is built with a door for a king who never comes. The counting wall has no column for them.

## From the Embankment, Leaving

Climbing back up to the embankment's top, you turn and look down one last time.

The basin lies in its thumbprint of clay. The beam nods: eleven seconds, eleven seconds. The smoke leans east exactly as far as it did when you arrived. The rook-ropes stretch between chimney and mill like the rigging of a ship that has been at anchor forever. The canal's hammered skin turns in its thousand little whorls. The cottage windows glow faint green through the moths. The swifts come over the dyke beside you and down to the cistern. They make almost no sound, the only quiet things in the place.

The whole of it roars, and does not change. It is the loudest place you have ever been, and the stillest.

Behind you, beyond the embankment, the marsh is flat and silent and moving: reeds shift in the wind, light changes as clouds come over. It looks, after Low Tallow, almost unbearably loose. You stand between them a while on the dyke's worn brick crown, which is also rounded, you notice, at its edges. Something walks this wall too. You haven't seen it. The count for it is not on the wall.
