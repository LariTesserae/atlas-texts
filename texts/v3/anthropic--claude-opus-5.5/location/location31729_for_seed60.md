# The Works at Hollin Bottom

## The Approach

You come down to it rather than upon it. The land around is low, flat pasture gone soft with rain, and then it tilts gently into a basin no wider than a village green. You could throw a stone across it if anything in it would let a stone through.

It won't. From the lip of the basin, the Bottom looks like a single green thing pressed into the earth. It is dense and domed, with no gaps and no visible ground. It doesn't read as forest or thicket. It is one continuous surface of leaf, bark, tendril and moss, heaped and swollen, like a green loaf that has risen past the edges of its tin. The embankment around it is old engineered earth, faced in places with blackened brick, and the vegetation spills over it like dough.

The first thing you notice is the noise. The second is that the noise is regular.

## The Sound

It is loud. It isn't deafening, but it is loud the way a mill is loud when you stand in the yard: a presence you raise your voice against. Several layers run through it.

Underneath everything is a hum, a low, even tone somewhere near the bottom of hearing. You feel it in your back teeth before you notice you are hearing it. Above that is a constant wet hiss, the sound of a great deal of water moving through narrow spaces under pressure. Above that, irregular and everywhere, is dripping: thousands of drops falling onto metal, onto broad leaves, into standing pools, each with its own pitch. The overall effect is like rain on a tin roof, except it is not raining.

Then, roughly every forty seconds, comes a knock. It is deep, wooden, and enormous, a single stroke like a mallet the size of a cart striking a beam. It comes from the center of the Bottom. After each knock the whole green mass shivers very slightly, a ripple you can see travelling outward across the canopy, and the hiss surges for a few seconds before settling.

You will count the interval without meaning to. Forty seconds, forty-one, thirty-nine. It is not a machine's rhythm. It is closer to breathing.

## Going In

There is no path, but there are ways. The growth at the edge is so tightly packed that it has become structural. Stems as thick as your wrist have grown into one another and fused into lattices, walls and floors, and you walk on vegetation, not through it. Underfoot is a springy mat of living root and moss half a meter thick, saturated like a sponge. Each step presses out water that wells around your boots, warm as bathwater.

It is warm in here, warmer than the pasture above by several degrees. The air is thick and close, the humidity of a glasshouse. Every surface sweats. Leaves hang heavy with condensation, and drops gather at their tips, swell, and fall. The rocks, where there are rocks, weep. You can't tell whether the water comes up from the ground, down from the canopy, or out of the air itself. It is all three, in a constant exchange.

The light is ordinary daylight, green-shifted, filtered through a canopy that isn't very high. Most of the Bottom's volume sits between your knees and a few meters over your head. The space isn't cramped so much as full. Nothing here has room around it. Every stem touches other stems, every leaf overlaps another leaf, and every surface is occupied by something growing on something else growing on something else. Moss grows on vine, which grows on bark, which may itself be the swollen outer layer of a root rising from below. Following any one strand back to where it starts is impossible. After a while you understand why: there is no separate strand to follow.

The Bottom is one plant, or at least it behaves like one. Cut a stem at the edge and sap wells from it under pressure, and the pressure pulses faintly in time with the knock.

## The Machinery

Then you find the first iron.

It is a pipe, a plain cast-iron pipe perhaps thirty centimeters across, running level through the growth at waist height. It is almost invisible at first, lagged in moss, wrapped in a spiral of fine root. But it is warm, and when you put your hand on it you feel the hiss inside: water moving, or sap, or something between the two. Where a joint has cracked, the plant has grown a collar of dense pale wood around it, tight as a bandage, sealed with a resin that smells like pine and hot metal.

Now that you know what to look for, the machinery is everywhere, though never much of it at once. A flywheel stands on its edge, three-quarters swallowed, its spokes threaded with living branches. A row of rivets marches up what you took for a tree trunk until you realize the trunk has grown around a steel stanchion. A pressure gauge sits flush in a wall of bark, its glass clouded but intact, its needle trembling at a point on the dial that no longer has a number. A length of narrow-gauge rail curves off under the moss and does not come back up. A brick chimney, no taller than a two-storey house, rises near the northern edge, so heavily grown over that it looks like a green pillar. Steam drifts from its top in a slow, continuous exhalation.

These are the remains of an industrial works, perhaps a pumping station, a small mill, or a dye works. Something was built here that needed a lot of water and a lot of force. It was never large. The whole thing could have been run by a dozen men. The buildings are mostly gone, or have been absorbed so completely that the only difference between wall and growth is the shape.

But the machines have not stopped.

## The Engine House

At the center of the Bottom, where the knock comes from, the growth opens into something like a room. This is the closest the place comes to open space, and it is still crowded, as full as a working engine house is full of engine.

The room is a chamber of living wood, roughly circular, with walls ribbed like the inside of a ribcage. The ribs are branches, braced and thickened over what must be many decades into beams as solid as oak. In the center stands the engine.

It was a beam engine, the old kind, with a great rocking arm pivoted at the top of a column. One end connects to a piston and the other to a pump rod plunging down into a shaft. The iron parts are still here: the cylinder, the column, and the main pivot, rust-black and slick with condensation. But much of the engine is now wood, and not carved wood. It is grown.

The beam itself is a single living limb, perhaps a meter thick, still barked in places and sprouting leaves along its upper edge. Its ends have swollen and hardened into shapes that exactly match the iron fittings they replaced. The connecting rods are straight saplings, ringed with growth scars where they flex. The gear train that once drove some auxiliary pump is now half iron and half wood. Here and there, where a cast cog lost teeth, the plant has grown new teeth in pale, dense timber. They are the right size, the right pitch and the right profile, meshing perfectly with the iron teeth beside them.

Every forty seconds or so, the plant contracts.

You can see it happen. The walls of the chamber tighten, a squeeze that runs around the ribs from one side to the other. The pressure in the sap-vessels peaks. Somewhere below, water or sap is driven up against the piston, and the great green beam rocks slowly upward. At the top of its travel it stops, trembling. Then it falls, and the pump rod slams home into the shaft with that tremendous wooden knock, and the whole Bottom shivers.

Then the walls relax, the beam settles, and the count begins again.

The plant is running the engine. It isn't being run by it or merely living inside it. It has learned the engine's shape and taken over its work, and it maintains it, replacing parts as they fail with parts of its own making. What it gets from this, if anything, is not obvious. Perhaps the pumping keeps water circulating through the Bottom's deep roots. Perhaps the rhythm itself is useful to it. Perhaps it absorbed the engine so gradually that it never learned to tell the engine's rhythms from its own.

## What the Works Produce

The Bottom still manufactures things.

Along the chamber's lower walls, and in pockets elsewhere, there are nodules. They are hard, rust-red lumps the size of a fist, bulging from the bark like galls. Break one open and it is layered inside like a pearl, alternating bands of woody tissue and something dense and metallic. The plant draws iron from the old machinery, from the waterlogged ground, and from the red-brown water that seeps through everything, and it deposits it in these growths. Over years the nodules become heavier and more metal than wood. Eventually they drop free and settle into the moss, where they lie in heaps like crude ingots.

Near the chimney, where it is warmest, the plant grows long seed-casings that dry into exact hexagonal forms, faceted like nuts for a bolt. They split in the heat and scatter seeds that never seem to take root. No new growth appears anywhere in the Bottom. There is no room.

From the chimney itself comes steam, and in the steam, if you stand downwind, a smell of coal smoke, though nothing is burning.

And at the eastern edge of the basin, one heavy cable runs out through the embankment. It is lead-sheathed, thick as a man's arm, and overgrown for most of its length. Where it passes through the earthwork it is bare, and when you put your hand on it, it is warm and faintly buzzing. The hum you felt in your teeth is strongest here.

The cable goes out under the pasture and away. Nobody seems to know where it leads. It is still carrying something.

## The Living Things

Animals are rare here, and the few there are are particular.

There is a beetle, a small bronze-green thing with a hard flat carapace, that lives in the cracks between iron and wood. It seems to eat the resin the plant uses to seal its joints. You will see perhaps one in an hour, trundling along a pipe or disappearing under a flange. They are almost silent, and their shells are faintly magnetic. They cling to iron more firmly than they should.

Once, perhaps, you might see a heron. It stands at the edge of one of the old settling tanks, a rectangular pool of dark water walled in brick and rimmed with moss, where the water is warm and still. It doesn't fish. It stands with its head tilted, listening to the knock. When you move, it lifts away over the canopy without hurry.

There are no birds singing, no insects buzzing, and no small mammals in the undergrowth, because there is no undergrowth. There is only growth, total and continuous, with nowhere for anything else to live. The plant fills every niche itself.

## The Long Cycle

Most of what happens in the Bottom happens slowly. The beam rocks forty times an hour, but that is the fastest thing here. The nodules take years to ripen. A new wooden cog takes perhaps a decade to grow into place. The chamber's ribs thicken by a hair's breadth each season. The whole mass rises imperceptibly against the embankment, pressing outward, and over lifetimes the earthwork will give.

The oldest people in the nearest village say there is a longer rhythm still. Roughly once every generation, perhaps every thirty or forty years, the knock stops.

For a few days the Bottom goes quiet. The hiss drops away, the dripping continues alone, and the great green beam hangs motionless at the bottom of its stroke. The canopy stills. The steam from the chimney thins to nothing. And the cable at the eastern edge goes cold.

Then, without warning, the knock resumes, sometimes slightly slower, sometimes slightly faster. The plant has adjusted something deep in its workings. Afterward the gauges set into the bark show new readings, and some iron part somewhere in the Bottom will have been replaced entirely with wood.

Nobody knows what happens during the silence. Nobody has been brave enough, or foolish enough, to go down into the Bottom while it is quiet and find out.

## Leaving

Walking back up out of the basin, the warmth falls away behind you and the air thins and cools. The sound is the last thing to go. The dripping fades first, then the hiss, until only the knock is left, carrying across the flat wet fields for a surprising distance, soft and regular as a heartbeat heard through a wall.

From the pasture, the Bottom is just a green hump in the land with steam rising gently from one side. You could mistake it for a copse. You would only know better by the rhythm, and by the cable running away under your feet, warm and humming, toward something you cannot see.
