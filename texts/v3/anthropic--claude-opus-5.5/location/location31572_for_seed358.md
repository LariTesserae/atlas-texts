# The Sump at Hallow Deep

A shaft with an iron cage runs four hundred feet down through shale and coal and a band of grey limestone. At the bottom a tunnel, timbered and running with water, leads to a gate of riveted plate. Past the gate the tunnel stops being a tunnel, and you are standing at the edge of something too large for your lamp to find the sides of.

The first thing you notice is the noise.

## The Noise

It doesn't arrive as a sound so much as a pressure, a thickness in the air that pushes against the chest and the backs of the teeth. It has layers, and after a while you learn to pick them apart.

The deepest layer belongs to the pumps. There are six of them, set on concrete plinths along the chamber's northern wall. Each is a squat electric machine the size of a railway carriage, fed by cables that come down the shaft as thick as a man's arm. Their impellers turn day and night and have done so for thirty-one years. Their sound is a low grinding hum that you feel in your knees more than hear. Outflow pipes rise from them in bundles, bolted to the rock with iron brackets, and vanish up a raise bored through the ceiling toward the surface. Every pipe thrums, and every bracket rattles faintly in its socket.

Above the pumps is water: falling water, running water, water seething over lips of stone. The chamber is fed from a hundred places at once. Warm springs come up through the floor. Seeps weep from the walls. Three true rivers enter from dark galleries to the south and east, and one of them drops forty feet from a notch in the wall into a plunge pool it has been carving for longer than there have been people. That cascade alone would deafen you if you stood beside it. In the chamber it is only one voice.

Above the water is the colony.

## The Colony

The ceiling of Hallow Deep is between sixty and two hundred feet overhead, depending on where you stand. Across nearly all of it, as far as any lamp has ever reached, there are bats. They are not scattered. They are packed so tightly that the ceiling looks furred. A lamp swung upward shows a brown, heaving surface that ripples as the animals shift against one another, and if you look long enough you begin to see eyes.

The miners estimate twelve million. The naturalist who came down in the second year of pumping estimated more, and then stopped estimating. There are at least four species. The small brown ones hang in the densest mats. Larger ones with pale bellies roost in the fissures, and something with long ears keeps to the far eastern galleries. The colony is never silent. Even at rest it produces a constant chittering, a sound like dry rice poured endlessly onto a drum. Twice a day, at dusk and before dawn, though nothing in the chamber marks either, the colony leaves and returns through a chimney in the western ceiling that opens, somewhere far above, onto a hillside of gorse. The leaving takes nearly an hour. During that hour the roar of wings drowns even the pumps, and the air moves in a slow gale toward the chimney, pulling the lamp flames sideways.

Among the bats nest swiftlets, small dark birds that navigate the lightless air by clicking. Their clicks are sharp and metallic, and they come from everywhere. The swiftlets build their nests of dried spittle in the last few feet of cracks the bats cannot use, and their sound threads through the chittering like the ticking of a thousand watches.

Crickets live in every horizontal inch of rock. Long-legged, pale, and nearly blind, they sing in the warm damp with a high continuous trill that you stop hearing within a day and cannot stop hearing again once someone mentions it.

There is no place in Hallow Deep where you can hear a man speak at a normal volume. The pump crews talk with their hands, in a sign language that has grown up down here and is spoken nowhere else. They wear felt plugs in their ears, and most of them are a little deaf anyway.

## The Warmth

It is warm down here, warmer than the surface in any season. The springs rise from somewhere deep, carrying heat from the slow furnace of the earth, and the whole chamber sits at the temperature of a summer evening that has forgotten how to cool. The air is saturated. Water condenses on every surface: on your skin, your lamp glass, the pump housings, the steel of the pipes. It beads, runs, and drips. The rock sweats. Mist rises in pale sheets off the warm pools and drifts in the currents the colony makes, and in the lamplight it moves like slow smoke.

Everything is wet and nothing is ever dry. Paper turns to pulp in a week. Wool mildews. The crews wear oiled canvas and rubber boots, and they carry spare matches in sealed tins, and still the matches fail.

## The Emerging Land

This is what the pumps are for, and it is the strangest thing about the place.

Thirty-one years ago the miners of the Hallow seam broke through the floor of a working and found water. It was not a leak but an ocean, warm and endless, filling a void no one had suspected. The pumps were installed to save the mine, and they have been lowering that water ever since, at a rate of about a finger's width a week.

So Hallow Deep is being born. Every week a new strip of floor emerges, and none of it has ever been exposed to air.

This new land has not been shaped by anything but still water. There is no erosion here and no weathering, only rock as it formed in the dark over ages: smooth, soft, undisturbed, coated in a fine grey silt as delicate as flour. Walk on freshly revealed ground and your boot sinks into sediment that has never been stepped on. It holds the print perfectly, and the print fills slowly with warm water.

The formations are raw and unfinished. The stalagmites down here grew underwater. They are not the tapering spikes of an ordinary cave but rounded, lumpy towers crusted with calcite that crystallized out of still pools. Some are as tall as a house. Some are fused into walls and ridges. Their surfaces are soft enough in places to be scratched with a fingernail, because they have never dried. Where the old waterline sat for a season, each tower wears a flared collar, a shelf of calcite that grew at the surface of the pool. Walking into the low parts of the chamber, you pass through forests of these collared pillars, each ringed with dozens of shelves like the growth rings of a tree, a calendar of the pumping written in stone.

Newly exposed rafts of calcite lie across the floor like broken ice. There are pools rimmed with delicate dams of stone, terraces of flowstone still wet and slick, and crystal beds that glitter briefly when the lamp touches them. Soda straws, hollow stone tubes as thin as a pencil and sometimes six feet long, hang in dense thickets from the lower ceilings, packed so close that you cannot pass without breaking them. They are still growing, each with a single bead of water at its tip.

The miners call the newest ground "the green," though it is grey. Walking the green is a job, done by a pair of men with long poles and lanterns who mark the safe paths with cairns of rock and scraps of red rag. The silt can hide deep holes. The rafts can break. Twice men have gone through into pockets of water still warm from the depths and not come up.

## What Grows

Almost nothing grows in Hallow Deep, but not quite nothing.

Around each of the lamps, the few sodium lamps strung along the main walkways that burn with a sickly orange glow, there is a halo of green. Algae and mosses have come down on miners' boots or on the air and found the light. Each lamp has its own little garden: a film of green on the damp rock, a fuzz of moss in the cracks, and here and there a fern. The ferns are small and pale, their fronds reaching toward the lamp as though it were a sun. Beyond the reach of the light the green fades within a few yards to bare wet stone.

There are fungi on the guano, white threads and small grey caps. On the timbers of the old walkways there are shelves of pale bracket fungus that grow half an inch a year.

## What Lives

Everything lives on the guano.

Beneath the colony, the floor of the chamber is a landscape of its own: dunes and ridges of bat dung, some of them thirty feet deep, laid down over thousands of years. It is warm, moist, and rich, and it is alive. It seethes. Cockroaches cover it in a moving layer so dense the surface seems to flow. Beetles, mites, springtails, and worms work through it. Where it meets the water it slumps into a dark fertile mud that stinks of ammonia so strongly your eyes stream.

The pools are full of fish. They are small, pale, and eyeless, and they school in the warm water in their thousands. At the lamp-lit edges, sometimes, you can watch them feeding on the drifting fragments of guano and insect. There are pale crayfish, too, and translucent shrimp, and in the deepest pools, the ones not yet drained, something the miners call the white eels. They are long, sinuous, and blind, as thick as a wrist, and they occasionally rise to the surface to take a fallen bat.

Spiders stretch webs between the soda straws, and the webs sag under condensation. Centipedes as long as your hand hunt among the rafts. Small frogs have come down the chimney somehow and live in the warm seeps, pale and speckled, their calls lost in the din.

The density of life is overwhelming. There is no square yard of Hallow Deep, above or below, that is not occupied. The ceiling is full of bats. The cracks are full of birds and crickets. The floor is full of insects and the water is full of fish. When the pump crews walk the old paths, cockroaches crunch under their boots at every step, and they no longer notice.

## The Built Things

Human work is sparse here, scattered against the enormity of the place.

There are the pumps, of course, on their plinths, and the pipes, and the cables that feed them. There is a timber walkway on stilts that runs from the gate out along the northern wall and then strikes south across the older, higher ground, rising and falling with the floor, its planks slick with condensation and speckled with guano. It was built in sections, each one extended as the water fell, so it grows older as you walk back toward the gate. Near the gate its boards are black and soft with rot. At the far end they are raw and pale.

There is a pumphouse office, a shed of corrugated iron bolted to the rock near the gate. It holds a desk, a telephone that rings a bell on the surface, a chart of water levels, and a stove that is never lit because it is always warm enough.

Here and there, on the older ground, you find the leavings of thirty years: a broken barrow, a coil of rusted cable, a wooden sign reading DANGER — SOFT GROUND, half sunk in silt. There is a crate of explosives nobody wants to move. There is a small shrine made of a biscuit tin with a candle stub and a photograph of a man, placed where one of the drowned men went through.

At the far southern edge of the lamps, the old gauging station stands on a pillar of rock: a tall iron rod marked in feet and inches, from which the water has retreated so far that it now stands high and dry, its bottom foot still greened with old algae.

## The Rule of the Place

Hallow Deep has one law, and every man who works there knows it. The pumps do not stop.

If they stop, the water begins to rise. It does not rise quickly, because the chamber is vast, but it rises steadily, about the height of a man in a month, and it would drown the green, then the walkways, then the pumps, then the mine above. So there are always two pumps in reserve, and a crew on every shift. Diesel generators stand on the surface in case the line from the power station fails. In thirty-one years the pumps have stopped only twice, for a total of eleven hours. Both times are remembered.

The pumps are losing, slowly. The chamber is so large, and so richly fed, that each year it takes more power to hold the water back. The engineers talk of adding a seventh pump, then an eighth. The miners say the water is patient, and it is. It has been here for longer than the coal above it. It will be here long after the last man leaves.

## Walking Out

When your shift ends you walk back along the walkway toward the gate, past the pumps, beneath the heaving mat of bats, through the mist. The noise follows you into the tunnel and up the cage. It is still ringing in your ears when you step out onto the surface, into cold rain and grey daylight and a silence so enormous that for a moment you think you have gone deaf.

Then you hear a single blackbird singing somewhere in the gorse, and it sounds impossibly clear.

Four hundred feet below your boots, the pumps keep turning, the land keeps emerging, and the colony keeps its long roar in the warm dark, a finger's width at a time.
