# The Lowworks

## First Impression

Nothing here is dry. You notice that before anything else.

There is a basin, a long, shallow rift perhaps six or seven miles across, cut into black rock so new it still has the gloss of cooling on it. The basin is full of warm water, and above the water there is more water. Rain falls so densely that no layer of open air separates sea from sky. The surface of the sea is less a boundary than a change in texture: below, the water is heavy and olive-bronze; above, it is white, streaming, and falling. Standing on the basin floor, you would look up through forty feet of murk, then through a churning seam of foam, then into the downpour, and never find the place where water stops.

The light is dim and comes from everywhere at once, a brownish-green glow like light through a bottle of old beer. Some of it is daylight, strained through the rain and sea. Some of it is not. Some comes from the machines.

## The Rock

The basin is young. You can see it in the edges. The basalt has not been worn smooth. It breaks in facets and blades, obsidian-sharp, with conchoidal fractures that still ring when the current throws gravel against them. Long columnar joints stand in clusters like organ pipes, each column clean-sided, hexagonal, unworn. Between them are cracks so narrow and fresh that their walls still match: if you pressed them together, they would close without a seam.

The rock is still being made. The floor of the rift is laced with fissures that exhale heat and a faint orange glow, and in them lava meets the sea. It does not explode. It muscles out slowly in pillows and lobes, skinning itself instantly in black glass, then cracking and pushing out another lobe. The water above these vents shimmers and roils. The basin is warm, bath-warm near the floor, because it sits over its own foundry.

Every so often, several times an hour, something shifts. The whole floor jolts. A new crack opens across a meadow of moss, a column snaps off at its base and topples slowly through the water, and a plume of silt goes up like smoke from a shot. Then the rock settles and the plants begin to close over the wound.

## The Growing Things

The plants are the second thing you notice, and after a while they are the only thing you can see.

They grow everywhere there is purchase, and most places where there isn't. The basin floor is a quilt of moss so thick that a dropped hand sinks into it to the wrist: velvet-green, bronze, a sulfurous yellow near the vents. From the moss rise ferns, uncoiling and recoiling in the current, their fronds slack one moment and snapped taut the next as a surge goes through. Above the ferns stand trees.

They are not kelp. They are trees in the old, land-living sense: pale trunks, bark like wet paper, branching crowns. They root in the fresh cracks and pry them wider. Their leaves have gone translucent and broad, thin as membranes, and they pulse faintly as water moves through them. The crowns interlace forty feet up, just under the surface foam, forming a canopy that the rain drums on from above. Beneath it the light goes from dim to dimmer, a green cathedral dusk.

Vines hang between the trunks and climb everything: rock columns, fallen columns, the machines. Epiphytes crowd every branch. Mats of something like duckweed, but rooted, cling upside-down to the undersides of ledges. The density is overwhelming. There is no open water anywhere below the canopy that is not threaded with stems, streamers, tendrils, and drifting fragments of leaf. Swimming through it would feel like pushing through a curtain that closes behind you, then another, then another.

The growth is fast, fast enough to watch. A new fracture in the floor is furred with green within an hour. A fallen column is wrapped in vine by nightfall. The plants are not reclaiming anything. There is nothing old here to reclaim. They are simply keeping pace.

## The Machines

They stand at wide intervals across the basin, perhaps a dozen in all, each alone in its own clearing of torn vegetation.

The largest is a pumping station: a brick-and-iron engine house the height of a church, its windows long since blown in, its roof a lattice of girders hung with weed. Inside, a beam engine still runs. The great iron beam rocks on its pivot, slow as breathing, and at each end a piston the width of a room plunges and rises. The engine has no operator and needs none. Its furnace burns, somehow, underwater, a sullen red glow behind the grate, and the water around the boiler is a shimmering column of convection that rises straight up through the canopy and tears a hole in it.

There are others. A single chimney stands in the middle of a moss field, venting something like steam that does not rise. It streams sideways, a long grey ribbon drawn out by the current for half a mile before dissolving. A gantry crane leans at a steep angle, half its legs buckled, its hook still swinging on a chain that runs up into the foam. A rail line comes down one wall of the rift on a trestle, runs across the floor, and disappears into the deepest fissure. Rust-red wagons stand on it at intervals, loaded with basalt blocks and wrapped in vines. Cables run between the machines, sagging through the water on iron pylons. They are live. Wherever a cable has frayed, the water around it flickers blue-white. Tiny arcs crawl over the insulation, and the plants nearest the break have grown in strange, branching, lightning-shaped forms.

The pumps are pumping. Great outflow pipes, riveted iron wide enough to walk through, rise from each engine house and run straight up through the canopy and the surface into the rain. Water goes up them. You can feel the suction at the intakes, a steady pull that bends the ferns toward the grilles. The water goes up and out, and it comes back down as rain.

That is the first thing about this place that stops being merely strange.

## The Sound

It is deafening.

Sound travels well through water, better and faster than through air, and there is nowhere in the basin where the sound stops. The beam engines thud at a pace just below a heartbeat, and each stroke arrives as a pressure wave that you feel in the sternum and the teeth before you hear it. The pumps shriek. Cavitation at their intakes tears the water into bubbles that collapse with a sound like gravel poured on sheet iron, endlessly. The live cables hum in a chord that shifts as the current sways them. The rain on the surface is a roar with no gaps in it at all, a white noise so total it becomes a kind of floor that every other sound stands on.

Under all of it is the rock. The new basalt cracks as it cools, sharp reports like rifle shots. Columns groan. The lava pillows pop and hiss. When the floor jolts, the sound is not a sound so much as an event: a single enormous knock that the whole basin rings with, trees and machines and water, for several seconds afterward.

The sound is loud enough to have physical effects. On flat stones the silt arranges itself into patterns, concentric rings and lattices and starbursts that re-form each time the dominant engine changes pitch. The ferns sway not only with the current but with the beat. Bubbles appear from nowhere in the troughs of the loudest pressure waves, hang for an instant, and vanish. Near the largest engine house the water is so loud it is cloudy, and the plants there grow in rhythm, leaves opening on the downstroke and closing on the up.

## What Is Absent

There are no fish.

There are no crabs in the cracks, no snails on the leaves, no worms in the moss, no shrimp, no jellies, no plankton drifting in the water column. Nothing grazes the plants. Nothing burrows. The water, for all its murk, is lifeless in a way that goes beyond emptiness. It is clean of the small moving specks that make ordinary seawater feel inhabited. The leaves are unbitten. The moss is undisturbed except where the current tears it. Nothing has ever built a nest here, left a track, or died here. There are no shells in the silt. There are no bones.

Under the roar, this absence has a quality almost like silence.

## The Rules

Spend long enough here and the strangeness organizes itself. The place obeys laws. They are simply not the usual ones.

**The flood is still arriving.** At intervals, sometimes minutes apart and sometimes hours, a wall of water comes down the rift from the north: a bore, a rolling front of foam and silt and torn vegetation that flattens the ferns, strips the canopy, and slams against the engine houses. It is the flood that filled the basin. It never finished. It arrives again and again, and each time the water level does not rise. The basin is always exactly full and always, at that moment, being filled.

**Down is negotiable.** In most of the basin, things sink. But there are layers, bands in the water marked by a shimmer like oil on a road, where the rule changes. In one band, loosened gravel drifts slowly upward. In another, sideways. Around the sideways-venting chimney the entire water column is tilted, and the trees there grow at an angle, reaching toward a "up" that lies somewhere to the east. Currents in the basin do not follow any sensible gradient. They form knots and closed loops, rivers of water that run in a circle a quarter-mile wide, and they re-tie themselves when the floor jolts.

**The machines are a closed circuit with the sky.** The pumps draw the sea up and out, and the sea falls back as rain. Nothing is ever drained. The rain is thick because the pumps are strong, and the pumps run because the rain keeps coming. It is impossible to say which began first. The engines may have been built to drain the basin and never managed it. Or the engines may be what makes the rain, and the rain what fills the basin, and the basin what the engines are for.

**Time stutters near the engines.** In the lee of the largest pumping station, a flower opens, closes, opens again, and closes, the same flower, the same opening, a loop of a few seconds. A column falls and is standing, and falls. The swinging hook on the crane chain passes the same point three times on one swing. Farther from the engines the stutter fades, but not entirely. Nothing in the basin quite has a past. The rock is always freshly cracked. The plants are always newly grown. The flood is always arriving.

## What the Place Becomes

It takes a long time to see it, because the basin is so full of motion that it seems to be only chaos: plumes, collapses, surges, arcs of light, the constant tearing and regrowth of green. But the chaos has a direction.

Everything here is being *made new*. The rock is extruded fresh from the fissures and cracked fresh by the jolts. The plants cover each wound within the hour. The flood re-arrives to keep the basin in its first moment of filling. The engines, thudding and screaming, circulate the whole sea through the sky and back, so that no water in the basin has been here long. Even time, near the machines, refuses to accumulate. It loops back rather than pile up.

The Lowworks is a factory, and what it produces is youth. It manufactures the first day of a place, over and over, at industrial scale, with pistons and furnaces and cables. That is why the edges are sharp and nothing is worn. That is why the plants, which need only light and water and a crack to root in, flourish without limit.

And that may be why nothing lives here that moves. An animal needs a yesterday. It needs a place it came from and a path it took, a memory of where the food was. The basin has no yesterday. It has only an enormous, roaring, perpetually renewed *now*, green and black and bronze, thundering at the pace of an engine that never stops and never finishes, under a rain that is also the sea, in the dim warm light at the bottom of a flood that is still, and will always be, coming in.
