# The Kettle Above the Gorge

It is a room, really. A bowl of rock about as wide as a village threshing floor, cupped into the side of a mountain so high that the land around it has fallen away into rumor. Three walls of it are cliff, dark and streaked, leaning inward like cupped hands. The fourth side isn't there. Where it should be, the floor of the bowl runs out to a lip of worn stone, and past the lip there is air: a vast, pale, moving emptiness, so deep that the valleys below appear only as creases in blue haze, and on most days not at all.

The people who know it call it the Kettle, because it steams and because it sings.

---

## The Shape of the Place

The Kettle faces east, on the flank of a peak whose summit is hidden above it in snow and cloud. The bowl was carved long ago, probably by a small glacier that once sat here and ground its seat into the mountain. It melted so long ago that the hollow has had time to soften. The walls are no longer raw. Their edges have rounded. Their faces are layered with lichen in rust and sulfur and grey-green, and every ledge wide enough to hold a fist of soil holds one, and that soil holds a plant.

The floor is gently dished, lowest near the middle and rising toward the lip, so the bowl holds a little of everything that falls into it. Stones the size of sleeping animals lie half-sunk in turf. A single pool, black and cold and shallow, sits at the center. From above you could step across it in three strides. From inside the Kettle, with the cliffs rising all around and the open sky filling the fourth side, it feels like the eye of the place.

The Kettle is small, and it sits in the middle of something enormous and empty. That is the first thing you feel there, and it stays with you.

---

## The Water That Climbs

A stream enters from above. It comes out of a notch in the back wall, fed by snowmelt from the summit fields, and falls in a thin braid down forty meters of black rock into the bowl. It crosses the floor in a hand-cut channel, sheets across the smooth stone of the lip, and drops off the mountain.

It doesn't get far.

The eastern face below the Kettle is sheer for an enormous distance, and in the mornings, when the sun heats the valleys, air rises up that face in a steady, powerful draft. The stream goes over the edge and within a few body-lengths the wind catches it, shreds it, and throws it back up. The water climbs. It comes back over the lip as a fine, cold, drifting smoke that rolls across the floor of the Kettle, beads on every surface, and climbs the back walls in slow veils.

So the Kettle is wet the way few places are. Everything drips. The rock weeps. Moss grows in thick cushions that squelch like sponges and release a small cold breath when pressed. Water runs down the walls in sheets too thin to see, and you notice it only by the shine and by the thousand tiny rivulets threading through the lichen. The air is almost always saturated, cool enough to fog the breath even in summer, and full of the mineral smell of wet stone and crushed fern.

The same water falls, rises, condenses, and falls again, perhaps many times before it finally escapes. People say the Kettle drinks its own river.

---

## Green

For a place this high, it is absurdly lush.

The walls are hanging gardens. Ferns grow in ranks along every crack, some with fronds as long as an arm, drooping and heavy with droplets. Cushion plants form tight green domes on the ledges, studded in season with tiny white and violet flowers. Saxifrages sprawl in wet mats. In the lee of the largest boulders there are thickets of a low, woody shrub with small leathery leaves and bitter blue berries, twisted into shapes that record every wind they've survived.

The floor is turf, springy and dense and mixed with sedge and alpine grasses. The ground is sodden, so it gives underfoot. Around the pool grows a ring of tall, pale-stemmed rushes, and the pool itself holds a waving green weed.

One plant is odd enough that visitors remember it. Along the lip, where the returning mist is thickest, grows a mossy, trailing vine that sends pale threadlike roots out into the open air. The roots don't reach for anything. They hang in the updraft like beards, streaming upward instead of down, and they drink directly from the mist. On strong mornings the whole lip of the Kettle is fringed with them, rising and fluttering like pale hair lifted by an invisible hand.

---

## Noise

The Kettle is loud. The first-time visitor always expects the hush of high places and is always surprised.

The curved walls gather sound and throw it back, so everything spoken in the bowl seems to come from everywhere at once. The falling stream in the back is a constant hiss and roar, doubled and softened by the echo. The wind on the lip makes a low, breathy hum that rises and falls with the gusts, and when the updraft is strongest the whole bowl resonates like the mouth of a jar someone is blowing across. It is a deep note, felt in the chest more than heard. On some days it holds steady for an hour.

Above all of that are the birds.

---

## The Swifts and Everything Else

The cliffs of the Kettle are pocked with cracks and holes, and in the warm months these hold the nests of swifts, thousands of them. They are dark, narrow-winged, and fast beyond reason, and they spend nearly their whole lives in the air. They pour out of the walls at dawn in shrieking streams, wheel through the bowl in tight screaming circles, and dive out over the lip into the void to hunt insects carried up on the rising air. All day they come and go at speeds that make the eye skip. Their calls bounce endlessly off the rock, a thin, high, ecstatic screeching that turns into a wall of sound at dawn and dusk.

Where there are swifts there are falcons. A pair nests on a high ledge on the north wall, and several times a day one of them falls out of the sky past the lip in a stoop so fast it makes a sound like tearing cloth. The swifts scatter in a burst of screaming. Sometimes there is a small puff of feathers drifting down into the emptiness, and the flock re-forms as if nothing happened.

Ravens come too, big and deliberate, riding the updraft without a wingbeat. They land on the boulders and walk about like they own the place, turning over moss and making remarks. They rob the swifts when they can and argue with the falcons often. Their deep croaks are the bass line of the Kettle's choir.

Down among the plants the life is smaller and just as busy. Fat, dark bumblebees work the cushion flowers in the brief warm hours. Snails with thin amber shells climb the wet walls by the hundreds, leaving silver trails. A slender olive-green salamander lives in the moss around the pool. It is cold-sluggish in the morning, and by afternoon it is active enough to snap at gnats. Small furred creatures, round-eared relatives of rabbits, live in the boulder piles, cutting grass and drying it in little stacks on the rocks. Their sharp whistles cut through the din whenever a shadow crosses them.

Wild goats come through as well. They are sure-footed and shaggy, and they climb up from ledges below the lip that seem impossible to walk. They graze the turf in the evenings, lick the mineral seeps on the walls, and leave again the way they came, stepping out onto thin air, or so it looks.

The Kettle is a small room, but the whole mountain passes through it.

---

## The Day

The place runs on a daily breath.

**Dawn** is cold and still. The updraft hasn't started. The stream falls straight off the lip and vanishes, and for a little while the Kettle is almost dry-sounding: just the hiss of the fall and the first whistles from the rocks. Then the swifts erupt out of the walls, and the day begins with a scream.

**Mid-morning**, the sun reaches the valleys, the air below begins to warm, and the updraft comes. You can hear it arrive: first a sigh at the lip, then the rising hum, then the returning mist rolling over the edge in cold, white billows that fill the bowl ankle-deep, knee-deep, and higher. The vine-roots lift. The walls begin to stream. Some mornings clouds form right in the Kettle's mouth, boiling up from below, and for an hour the view is nothing but white, and the bowl seems to float in milk.

**Afternoon** is the loudest time. The draft is strong, the note in the bowl is deep and steady, the insects ride up in clouds, the swifts are everywhere, and the falcons hunt. Brief, sharp showers are common. Rain falls from above and rising mist meets it from below, and for a few minutes the Kettle seems to be raining in both directions at once.

**Evening** is the turn. As the valleys cool, the wind falters, stops, and reverses. Cold air begins to slide down off the summit snowfields, through the Kettle, and out over the lip. The mist drains away down the mountain like water from a basin. The hum dies. The stream once again falls straight into the void. The goats come, the ravens settle, and the swifts pour back into the walls in a final screaming spiral.

**Night** is cold and clear and huge. The stars come right down to the lip. There's the fall, the drip, the occasional whistle, the small sounds of stones settling as frost grips them. Sometimes far below, in the invisible valleys, there is a light, and it is so small and so far away that it is hard to believe it belongs to the same world.

---

## The Marks of People

People have been coming here for a long time, but never many, and never to stay.

At the back of the bowl, beneath an overhang out of the wind, there is a low shelter of dry-laid stone. It is waist-high, roofed with flat slabs and turf, just big enough for three people lying close. The stones are fitted without mortar and furred with moss. The entrance faces the stream. Inside, the floor is packed earth, blackened in one corner by a very old hearth.

The stream's channel across the floor is cut by hand. It is a shallow groove in the bedrock, smoothed by centuries of water, that guides the flow away from the shelter and out toward the lip. Someone chipped it with stone tools, and the marks are still faintly visible along its edges.

Along the north wall, where the swifts nest thickest, a line of wooden pegs has been driven into cracks in the rock, forming a rough ladder up to the lowest nesting ledges. Some pegs are dark with age and slick with moss. Others are paler and newer, replaced within living memory. People come in late summer, after the young have fledged, to gather the old nests. They are said to be good for kindling, or medicine, or something else no one explains to outsiders.

On the largest boulder near the pool, someone has pecked a shallow cup into the top, and it is always full of water. Beside it sits a small bowl carved from wood and weathered silver. Visitors drink from the cup and leave the bowl where they found it.

On the lip there is a single cairn, knee-high, of stones carried up from somewhere else. They are pale and rounded like river stones, clearly not from the Kettle. Tied around its top stone is a braided cord of grass and some kind of hair, frayed and faded and stiff with lichen, and in the updraft it lifts and streams upward like the vine-roots.

That is all. The rest of the place belongs to itself.

---

## The Feeling of It

Standing in the Kettle, you are in a room that is always talking, filled with birds and water and wind, crowded with green, wet in every surface. Then you turn toward the lip and see nothing: no ground, no near thing, only depth, haze, and cloud forming out of nothing, and far off another mountain like a smudge of chalk.

It is a tiny place, rich and loud and busy, perched at the edge of an enormous quiet. The water climbs, the roots grow upward, the rain falls both ways, and the bowl hums like a jar held to the mouth of the sky.
