# The Resin Mill of the High Char

## Approach

You do not see it arriving. There is nothing to see by. You feel it first, a warmth rising through the soles of your feet as the ground tilts upward, and then a smell: hot sap, scorched sugar, the sweetish rot of green wood forced to burn before it is ready. The air thins as you climb. It dries until breathing feels like drawing through paper. Somewhere above, the sky is closed.

The plateau sits a long way up, a broad raised table the size of a county. There is no rock under it. You learn this slowly, because every surface you touch is plant: bark, fiber, gum, husk, root. And every plant has been *made*. Nothing here grew the way things grow. It was shaped, jointed, trained, bred and grafted into function. It is an industrial works grown entirely out of vegetable tissue, and it has been running for so long that it has worn itself smooth.

## Terrain

What would be hills elsewhere are the rounded backs of enormous gears. They are wheels of laminated heartwood a quarter-mile across, laid flat and half-sunk into the mass, their teeth long since ground down into gentle ridges. They still turn, very slowly. If you stand on one for an hour you will find you have moved, and the ridge you faced now lies at your shoulder.

Between the gears run the troughs. These are channels of braided root, polished to a dull gloss by uncountable ages of friction, and they carry the plateau's circulation: slow streams of amber resin, thick as cold honey even in this heat. They are the only liquid here, and it is not water. It is sap cooked down to its hydrocarbons, dense with terpenes, and it glows faintly where it runs hottest, a sullen orange like the last coals of a fire seen through the back of a hand.

Everywhere the ground is packed. There is no soil and no gap. Stems grow through stems. Boiler-gourds swell in the crotches of piston-trunks. Belt-vines loop over axles, and the axles are themselves the thickened limbs of older trees whose crowns were pruned into flywheels ten thousand years ago and never allowed to leaf again. Walk anywhere and you are walking on machinery. Lie down and something beneath you is rising and falling, compressing and releasing, very slowly, like the chest of a sleeper.

## Materials

The makers, whoever they were, worked in a handful of materials, and the mill still uses only these.

**Ironbark** is the structural wood, grey-black and dense enough to ring when struck. It is grown in plates, overlapping like scales, and it has been heated and cooled so many times that its grain has vitrified in places into something closer to glass than timber.

**Coalpith** is the fuel. It is a spongy, black-veined marrow that fills the hollow cores of the great trunks and is fed, a finger's width a day, into the furnaces.

**Gumcord** is the tendon. These are long ropes of cured resin and fiber that stretch between armatures and pull with the strength of a winch.

**Husk** is the skin. It is dry and papery and layered in folds hundreds deep. It covers nearly everything, flaking and regrowing, and it muffles the whole works like snow.

**Seedglass** is the rarest. It forms as small hard beads, clear to smoky brown, in pockets deep inside the furnace-gourds. Holding one to the ember-light shows tiny gear-shapes suspended inside it, perfect and complete.

## Heat and Darkness

The furnaces are gourds. Each is a rounded chamber the size of a barn, its walls a foot of ironbark-lined rind, grown where three or four trunks meet. Coalpith is pushed in through their stems, and inside, in the dark, it smolders without flame at a temperature that would crack stone. The plateau has thousands of them, perhaps more. Their heat moves up through every fiber, and the whole table breathes it out, so the air even at its coolest is fever-hot and bone-dry, and the husk crackles faintly as it shrinks.

Their exhaust made the sky. What rises from the furnace-stems is not quite smoke. It is a fine, oily pollen of soot and spore, and it hangs a few hundred feet up in a single unbroken ceiling that has not parted in living memory, if there were any memory to consult. It is dense enough to swallow sunlight entirely. If a sun exists above it, no trace of it has reached the mill for ages. The ceiling is part of the machine. It holds the heat in, and the heat keeps the resin moving.

So the light here is only what the works make by accident: the orange of the hottest resin-troughs, pinprick reds where a furnace-rind has thinned and glows through, and occasionally a long crawling seam of ember where some ancient wheel's axle has worn hot in its socket. These make islands of dim color in a black that is otherwise total. The eye adjusts, a little, and then stops adjusting. Most of the plateau you will never see. You will know it by touch, by heat on the face, by the pressure of moving air.

## Sound

It is quieter than a factory has any right to be.

The husk swallows almost everything. Gears turn with a sound like breath through the teeth. Pistons (trunks whose inner rings slide over one another, lubricated with resin) rise with a long soft *hhhhhh* and fall with a sound you feel in your sternum more than hear. Belt-vines creak. Furnace-gourds tick as they expand and contract. Resin makes no sound at all.

Under all of it runs a very low tone, too low to be sure of, as if the entire plateau were a single wooden instrument being bowed at a tempo of one stroke per day.

The loud sounds are rare, and they are the sounds of failure.

## Turbulence

Old as it is, the mill is not calm. It is failing and rebuilding itself constantly, everywhere at once. The heat is too great for any tissue to last. Ironbark checks and splits. Gumcord sags, then snaps with a sound like a rifle shot that the husk smothers almost before it reaches you. Furnaces burst. When one does, there is a dull *whump*, a sudden bloom of red light that shows you, for one instant, a landscape of towering jointed forms stretching away on every side, and then the dark closes again and the spilled coalpith smolders.

Nothing is cleared away, because the mill grows over everything. Within days, hours in the hottest districts, new shoots push out of the wreckage: pale, blind, fibrous, already shaped. They curl into sprockets, flatten into cams and thicken into cylinders, then graft themselves onto whatever still turns nearby. A broken axle sprouts its own replacement from its stump. A collapsed wheel becomes a bed of seedlings that assemble into a smaller wheel within the ruin of the larger. A burst furnace becomes the root-mass for three new ones.

This is why the plateau feels so crowded. Everything grows into the space that anything else vacates, immediately and aggressively, so the works are always at the limit of their packing. There is no clearing and no path. Anything you manage to walk through is being walked through in the gap before it closes. Turn around and the way you came is already threaded with new tendrils, groping for a shaft to couple to.

In the busiest districts, near the center where the furnaces are thickest, the collapse and regrowth run so fast that the ground seethes. Ember-light flickers under a surface of pale shoots in constant motion, lengthening, gripping and snapping, rising and falling back. It resembles a fire and a forest at once, a pot at the moment before boiling. And it is almost silent.

## Erosion

Look closely and the age of the place shows everywhere.

The oldest structures have lost almost all their definition. The first wheels have ground down so far that they are only soft swellings in the mass, their teeth worn to nothing, turning on axles that have become wide flattened discs. The great piston-trunks of the outer districts have run their strokes so many times that their shafts are polished to mirrors and their casings sanded almost through. The resin-troughs have cut themselves deep, twenty or thirty feet into the braided root, like canyons carved by honey.

In some regions the mechanism has worn so smooth that you can no longer tell what it did. There are fields of gentle rounded forms, fitted tightly together and turning very slowly against each other, like river pebbles made of wood, that may once have been a gearbox or a loom or a press. They still move. They are still hot. Whatever function they had has eroded into pure motion.

The mill regrows constantly, but it regrows from itself, and the templates are wearing out. Each generation of shoots is a little less crisp than the last. The teeth are blunter and the joints rounder. Given enough time the whole plateau will relax into one smooth, warm, seamless surface, still turning, with nothing left to turn against.

## Ecology

Nothing here has a mouth, a leg or an eye. There are no insects in the husk and no birds under the ceiling. Nothing eats the sap. The mill's life is entirely vegetable, and it has worked out every function an animal would serve in some other way.

Pollination happens by piston. The flowering bodies, small tight cones like fists of ironbark, are grown on the faces of the moving parts, and every stroke presses one cone against another. Seeds travel by belt. They lodge in the gumcord, ride the long loops to distant districts and drop off wherever a vine rounds a pulley. Decomposition happens by fire. Dead wood is fed back into the furnaces, and the ash drifts up to join the ceiling, where it thickens the dark.

There is no water, so there is no rot, no mold and no mushroom. The mill's chemistry runs on oils and resins. Its cells are built around terpene instead of water, and its sap is fuel. In the very oldest parts there is some evidence that it has stopped needing air as well: sealed chambers deep in the ironbark, full of warm resin, in which slow growth continues in total isolation.

## Strangeness

Some things in the mill have no explanation.

**Seedglass.** Those beads with perfect gears inside them form in every furnace. They drift down through the resin and collect at the bottom of the deepest troughs in heaps like amber gravel. Nothing uses them. The gears inside are complete and exact. Sometimes, if you hold one long enough in your palm and it warms to the heat of your hand, you can see them very slowly turn.

**The export.** At the plateau's edges, where the tableland meets a drop you can feel but not see (a sudden cooling of the air, a draft rising from far below), the resin-troughs run to the lip and over. Belt-vines run to the edge as well and reach out into the void. They carry things out and let them go: small bundled forms, sealed husk around a knot of ironbark, each one the size of a fist. If you break one open, there is a perfect miniature of a furnace inside, or a wheel, or a piston, complete and faintly warm. They drop into the dark below. No one knows what, if anything, is at the bottom. It may be very far down. There is no sound of landing.

**The tone.** That constant low note changes pitch now and then, very slightly, over a period of years. The whole mill responds. Entire districts shift their tempo. Furnaces bank or blaze, and the pace of growth and collapse rises or falls. It sounds less like a signal than like a conversation with something too slow and too distant to hear.

**The makers.** There is no sign of whoever designed this. There are no walls, no rooms, no handholds, no stairs and nothing sized for a hand. Some who have walked here come to suspect there were never makers at all. The mill may have made itself, an industry that arose the way a coral reef or a termite mound does. It may simply have grown from some first seed that had machinery in its blood, the way the gears are sealed inside the seedglass: complete, turning slowly, waiting to be warmed.

## Leaving

When you finally come down off the plateau, you find a husk-bundle stuck in the lining of your coat. You don't remember it landing there. It is warm. Something inside it is turning.
