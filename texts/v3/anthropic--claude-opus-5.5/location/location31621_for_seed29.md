# The Blister

Near the summit of a young volcano, well above the last thin grass, a lava flow poured out forty-one years ago and has not finished cooling. It came down the eastern shoulder as a black tongue two kilometres long. It never quite solidified from the inside out, and somewhere in its upper third a bubble of gas the size of a small chapel was trapped and sealed. The flow's skin cracked along one seam as it shrank, and the bubble opened to the air through a split no wider than a forearm.

That split is the only way in. From outside it looks like nothing: a dark line in dark rock, at 4,100 metres, in wind that smells of sulphur and pumice dust. Get your face close to it and the mountain breathes hot, dry air at you. The air is thick and ammoniac, and it roars.

---

## The chamber

Inside, the Blister is shaped like the gas that made it: a rough ovoid, taller than it is wide, its ceiling a dome and its floor a shallow bowl. It is perhaps twelve metres long and seven high. Nothing about it has been worn. No water has ever run here, and no root has ever pried at a joint. The walls are exactly as the gas left them when the lava around it thickened and set.

That makes the surfaces strange to the hand. The dome is lined with a skin of volcanic glass, black and faintly iridescent, stretched in places into ropy folds and in others blistered into smaller bubbles, a surface of frozen froth. Where the glass cracked on cooling it split into shards with edges as fine as obsidian blades. Nothing has dulled them. Brush the wall carelessly and it will open your skin.

Lower down the glass gives way to vesicular basalt, grey-black and pocked with thousands of holes where smaller gas bubbles were caught. Each hole is lined with its own film of glass, and many of them are still growing crystals. Sulphur is the most visible: pale, brittle needles, yellow-green in any light you bring. Alongside it are white crusts of gypsum and thenardite, and in the hottest corners the delicate orange of realgar, which slowly crumbles to powder when light touches it. Traces of gas still seep through the rock from the magma body far below. As they reach the chamber and cool, they deposit a few more atoms. The crystals grow perhaps a millimetre a decade. This is the room's only weather: mineral condensing, very slowly, out of the mountain's breath.

The floor tells you how young the place is. It is not level. It holds the last slump of molten rock that sagged down the inside of the bubble before freezing, a series of overlapping lobes like poured wax. In the lowest part of the bowl a hairline fissure runs down into the flow's interior. The heat comes up through it. Lay your palm near the fissure and the rock is almost too hot to touch, around sixty degrees. At the upper dome the air sits at about forty-four. The Blister is never cooler than that. Outside, the summit freezes most nights of the year. In here it is always a desert afternoon.

There is no water at all. The air is so dry that sweat evaporates before it can bead. Lips crack within an hour. Paper brought inside curls. Static builds on everything: in fur, on clothing, in the fine dust hanging in the air. In full darkness, a hand moved quickly through the air sometimes produces a faint blue crackle.

---

## The colony

The Blister is full.

It is not full of rock or crystal but of bodies. Between two and three hundred thousand bats roost here in the warm months, and in the peak weeks after the young are born the number may be closer to half a million. They are a small free-tailed species, dark brown, each weighing about as much as a few coins. They are built for heat and drought. They drink on the wing from the lakes far below the mountain, and they need almost no water in the roost. The chamber is the warmest dry cavity on the mountain, and warmth is what their pups need. Stable heat, no predators, one narrow entrance: from a bat's point of view the Blister is nearly perfect.

They cover the dome completely. There is no rock visible anywhere above head height, only a ceiling of fur that shifts and shivers constantly, bodies packed at densities of several thousand to the square metre. The pups cluster in the hottest part of the dome, directly over the fissure, in a nursery crèche so dense that a mother returning from the night's hunt must crawl across hundreds of other young to find her own by voice and smell. The adults hang in the cooler margins, overlapping like shingles.

In the darkness you feel them before you see them. The air moves with their breathing. The heat of their bodies adds to the heat of the rock, and the whole chamber runs several degrees warmer at midday, when they are all home, than at midnight, when most are out.

Below them lies what they have made.

---

## The floor

The floor of the Blister is not the frozen lava lobes it once was. For roughly thirty years, since the colony found the split, guano has been falling. In the centre of the bowl it is now more than a metre deep: a dark, crumbly, desiccated mound that crunches underfoot and gives off a sharp ammonia sting strong enough to burn the eyes. At the edges, where the rock rises, the guano thins to a dusting over the basalt, and the sulphur needles poke up through it like pale grass.

Because the chamber is so dry, the guano does not rot. It desiccates. It is mostly the chitin of millions of moths and beetles caught over the lowlands, broken into flakes and husks, and it builds up in fine layers season by season. Cut into the mound and you would see the colony's history as bands of slightly different colour, one for each summer, laid down in a cave that has only existed for four decades.

The guano is alive. This is the second population of the Blister, and it outnumbers the first by orders of magnitude.

Dermestid beetles, small and bristled, work through the upper layers by the million, eating the dried bodies of pups that fell from the crèche and did not survive. Guano moths flutter weakly just above the surface. Cockroaches the colour of the guano itself move beneath it, flat and fast. Mites are everywhere: in the guano, on the beetles, on the bats, a haze of minute bodies too small to see individually, visible as a faint crawling shimmer on any surface you shine a light on. Pseudoscorpions hunt the mites. Solifugids, pale, fast, and hairy, hunt the beetles. A few lizards have found their way in and live at the dry margin near the split, fat on insects, never needing to leave.

Beyond the insects and the lizards there is almost nothing. Nothing green grows here, and nothing ever has. No light reaches far enough in, and there is no water for anything to root in. At the surface of the guano, fungi might grow in a damper cave, but this one is too dry for them. What lives here is animal, and it lives on what falls from the ceiling. Energy comes in on the wings of the bats as the insects of distant farmland, is dropped as guano and corpses, and is eaten again and again by smaller and smaller things until what is left is dust. The whole ecology is a funnel, and it points downward.

---

## The sound

The Blister is loud.

Some of it you can hear directly. A roost this size produces a constant high chittering: mothers calling to pups, pups crying back, adults squabbling over space on the ceiling. Individually each voice is thin and small. Hundreds of thousands together make a noise like frying fat, or rain on a tin roof, or a stadium heard from outside, always with a hiss in it. It never stops. It is highest in the late afternoon, when the colony stirs before the evening flight.

Most of the sound is beyond human hearing. The bats echolocate even inside the roost, and the ultrasonic din in the dome is intense enough that recording equipment brought in overloads. You feel it as a pressure, a faint ache in the ears, a vague unease, as if the air were humming just beyond perception.

Underneath all that there is a third layer, and it is not from the Blister at all.

On the other side of the summit ridge, about four kilometres away through solid rock, a sulphur works and geothermal plant has been operating since before the lava flow came down. It is a large industrial operation. Diesel generators run the site. Drilling rigs bore into the steam field. A compressor station moves steam through insulated pipelines to turbines that feed the grid in the valley below. Trucks grind up the switchback road carrying sulphur blocks down and fuel up. None of it is visible from the Blister. None of it has touched the chamber. But young, unfractured volcanic rock carries vibration extraordinarily well, and the whole sealed bubble works like the body of a drum.

The industry arrives as sound through stone. There is a low throb at the rhythm of the compressors, about once a second, so steady that the bats seem to have tuned their own rhythm around it. A drill rig starting up comes through as a grinding moan that rises for a few minutes and settles to a steady drone. Every few hours a steam vent is opened for pressure relief, and the release comes through the rock as a deep, long exhalation you feel in your chest more than your ears. At the change of shift the generators stop and start, and in the moment between, when the throb cuts out, the whole colony falls briefly silent, as if listening.

To stand in the Blister is to stand inside the mountain's noise: bats above, beetles crackling below, and through the rock the heartbeat of machines you cannot see, in a room that has never been touched by any hand.

---

## The darkness

The only light the Blister receives comes through the split, and that light never reaches the floor. On a bright afternoon a single dim blade falls a metre or two into the chamber and dies against the glass of the far wall, where the iridescence turns it a faint oily violet. Everything else is black.

A torch changes everything, and not for the better. Light in the Blister shows you too much at once. The dome is alive and moving. Two hundred thousand pairs of eyes glint and disappear. The sulphur needles glow pale green in the beam, and the realgar begins, invisibly, to decay. The floor seethes. The air is a fog of dust and mites and the dry flakes of guano that the bats' wingbeats keep suspended. And everything, everywhere, glitters: the glass, the crystals, the chitin of beetle shells, the eyes. It is the most crowded place you will ever stand in. There is no square centimetre not covered by something alive or something growing.

Turn the torch off and the noise comes back to the front of your attention, and with it the heat, and with it the sense that the room is closing in.

---

## The evening flight

The chamber's great event happens once a day, in the warm months, at dusk.

The colony stirs for an hour beforehand. The noise rises. The bats begin crawling down toward the split, and the air in the dome thickens with warm, ammonia-soaked movement. Then the first ones drop into the narrow gap and are gone, and within minutes the flow has become a solid river of bodies pouring out of the split into the cold summit air. The outflow lasts nearly an hour. From outside it appears as a dark column rising from the flank of the mountain, twisting in the wind, visible from the valley and from the sulphur works on the far side of the ridge. Its sound is a roar of wings.

In the Blister, the flight leaves the pups behind. The crèche remains, a dense mass of young in the heat above the fissure, calling. The adults will not return until nearly dawn, their stomachs full of moths. For those hours the chamber is quieter, cooler, and emptier than at any other time, though still crowded by any ordinary measure. The beetles come higher up the guano mound. The lizards come out. The rock ticks as it cools by half a degree. Through the stone the compressors keep up their slow, patient throb.

Then, before sunrise, the river comes back in, and the dome fills, and the ceiling of fur settles, and the roar returns.

---

## The long cycle

The Blister changes, but so slowly that no one standing in it would notice.

Each season the guano rises by a few centimetres. Each decade the crystals grow a little longer in their gas holes. Each year the rock beneath the floor cools by a tiny fraction of a degree, as the lava flow slowly gives up its stored heat and the fissure's breath weakens. At some point in the next century or two the chamber will fall below the temperature that makes it valuable to the colony. The pups will need warmth it can no longer give, and the bats will go looking for another hot, dry hole. The guano will lie untouched, beetles eating the last of it. The sulphur will go on growing in the dark.

On the other side of the ridge, the geothermal plant will have its own cycle: the steam field slowly depleting, the drill rigs moving to new wells, perhaps one day the machines falling silent. The rock will stop carrying their heartbeat.

But for now, the Blister holds all of it at once: the youngest rock on the mountain, the densest population of animals within a hundred kilometres, the loudest silence of any enclosed space on earth, and the pulse of industry, sealed in a bubble of glass in the dark, high above the grass, too hot for water, too dry for anything green.
