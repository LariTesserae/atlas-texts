# The Condensing Room

It is a room about the size of a small chapel, and the first thing that fills it is sound, not light.

The sound is a single note, or what the body registers as a note. It is so low and so large that the ears only partly handle it; the rest arrives through the soles of the feet, the sternum, the fillings of the teeth. Above it, more audible, rides a hiss like surf that never draws back, and above that a constant fine chatter of dripping, clicking, and wingbeats. None of it pauses. Nobody could speak here and be heard. A shout comes out as a small warm pressure against the lips and is gone.

The room sits high in the body of a structure too large to see from inside. It is one cell among thousands in a regulator built along a mountain ridge, or built *as* the ridge, since there is no longer any clear line between the two. Somewhere below, the machine breathes in the valley's air: humid, heavy, rich with the lowlands. It draws that air upward through a honeycomb of chambers like this one and strips the water from it on the way. The note is the sound of that breathing. It has not stopped in a very long time.

---

## The Shape of It

The chamber is roughly lens-shaped, wider than it is tall. It is ribbed on every surface with a lattice of thin curved vanes set so close that a hand could not pass between them. The vanes are made of a dark, faintly iridescent material, somewhere between slate and oil, that does not behave quite like a solid. Pressed hard, it gives the way cold wax does, then slowly returns. Scratched, it closes over the scratch within a day. It is always cooler than the air around it, a few degrees cooler, by some means that uses no visible coolant, no pipes, no exhaust. It is simply cold in the way a stone at the bottom of a well is cold, except that it stays that way in a room that is otherwise hot as a greenhouse.

Because of that difference, the room weeps. Warm valley air pours in through a slot along one edge, a long mouth running the width of the chamber, and moves across the vanes in a steady, pressing wind. Water beads on every edge, gathers, and runs. The vanes are grooved to guide it, and it travels along them in thousands of fine silver threads. The threads converge toward the floor, where the lattice folds down into a shallow basin, then a channel, then a round drain darker than anything else in the room. The drain does not gurgle. It takes the water silently, which is strange given how much water there is, and given that everything else here makes noise.

The air is thick enough to feel in the lungs, and warm, perhaps thirty degrees, tasting faintly of iron and green things. Every surface is wet. It is less like a place after rain than a place where rain is constantly being assembled out of the air, one droplet at a time.

---

## What Time Has Done

The machine is old, and the room shows it, though not as ruin. Nothing is broken. It is more like the way an old cathedral's steps are worn into shallow cups.

The water that runs down the vanes carries minerals. Over the centuries those minerals have tried to build crust: flowstone, little stalactites, the pale frills that grow in any cave. The vanes will not entirely allow it. Their self-closing surface slowly swallows what forms on it, drawing deposits back into itself the way skin absorbs a splinter. The result is a standoff that has settled into a kind of truce. Near the intake slot, where the wind is strongest and the water freshest, the vanes are clean and black. Farther back, they carry a soft bloom of pale mineral, thinnest at the leading edges, thickest in the hollows, like frost that never melts. On the vanes nearest the drain, the absorbed minerals have changed the material itself. Those vanes are banded in rust, cream, and a dull green-gold, laminated like agate. They are subtly thicker and softer than the others, and slightly warmer.

The whole chamber has, in effect, a grain. It leans with the wind. The vanes near the intake have been worn by the air into a faint streamlined droop, their edges rounded like river pebbles. Nobody designed that. The machine accepted it and went on.

---

## The Light

There is very little. What there is comes from three places.

A dull wash comes in through the intake slot. It is daylight that has traveled a long way down some external throat of the structure, and by the time it arrives it is gray-blue and nearly spent. It lights the first few rows of vanes and turns the running water there into a moving sheen. Beyond that it gives up.

A second light comes from the vanes themselves. At irregular intervals, perhaps a few times a minute, a pulse runs through the lattice: a very faint violet shimmer that starts at the intake and ripples back toward the drain. It is the machine doing something, adjusting or tuning, and it coincides with a barely perceptible shift in the great note. It lasts less than a second. In the dark it is enough to show the whole room, briefly, like a photograph with the flash half-dead.

The third light is alive.

## The Inhabitants

The room is crowded. Nothing living here was part of the design, but the machine made a perfect habitat without meaning to: warm, wet, sheltered, high above predators, fed on a constant stream of valley air that carries seeds, spores, insects, and dust. So things came, and stayed, and changed.

**The swiftlets** are the most visible. They are small, sooty birds with long scythe wings, and there are hundreds of them. They nest in the angles where vanes meet the chamber wall, building cups of spit and down that the lattice cannot absorb fast enough to clear. They come and go through the intake slot at astonishing speed, flying straight into the wind and threading gaps no wider than their wingspan. Their calls are useless in the noise, so they have stopped calling. They navigate by the violet pulse. Each flash gives them a snapshot of the room, and they fly the dark between flashes from memory. If you watched long enough, you would notice their flights clustering just after each pulse, a burst of darting shapes, then scattered stragglers flying blind.

**The drip-shrimp** live in the water threads themselves. They are pale, translucent crustaceans no longer than a fingernail, and they cling to the grooved vanes in their thousands. Their fringed forelegs comb the running water for everything the valley air has dropped into it. From a distance they look like a fine gray fur on the vanes. They are blind and deaf, and the note means nothing to them except as a tremor in the metal. They feel the violet pulse, though; they all flinch at once when it passes, and the fur ripples.

**The lamp-slugs** are what give the room its living light. These are soft, finger-length gastropods that graze on the mineral bloom in the back half of the chamber, rasping it off the vanes before the lattice can take it back. Their bodies glow a sour yellow-green, bright enough to see by if you hold one close and nothing more. There might be forty or fifty at any time, scattered in slow trails across the dark rear wall like a map of some faint constellation. Their paths are patient, and over years they have grazed shallow clean lines into the bloom, a script of wandering loops that the lattice slowly erases behind them.

**The moths** come up with the air, pulled from the valley against their will, battered and exhausted. Many die on the vanes and feed the shrimp. Some survive long enough to be taken by the swiftlets. A few, a particular dusty brown species, have learned to ride the wind in and settle in the stillest pocket of the room, behind the drain lip, where they wait out the day in dense, quivering mats.

**The hunters**: along the chamber's upper curve, where the vanes are thickest with nests, live long-legged pale spiders that trap nothing. They wait on the vane edges for a swiftlet to fly the dark between pulses and misjudge a gap. When one strikes a vane and falls stunned, the spiders drop. It does not happen often, but it happens every day. The floor near the upper wall is littered with small bones that the lattice is gradually drawing down into itself, the way it takes everything, and the vanes there carry a faint chalky whiteness that is not mineral.

---

## The Garden, Such As It Is

Plants have only a thin foothold. There is no soil and no stone, only the lattice, and the lattice absorbs almost everything that tries to root in it.

Almost. Where swiftlet nests have accumulated for generations, the droppings build a dark rich crust faster than the machine can clear it. On these crusts, a few ferns have taken hold. They are tiny and wiry, with fronds no bigger than a thumbnail, uncurling toward the gray light of the intake. There are perhaps a dozen patches in the whole room. They grow very fast, and they die when the lattice eventually reaches up under their roots and swallows the crust they grew on. Then they grow again somewhere else. Their spores ride the wind back toward the drain, and most are lost.

Over everything near the intake lies a slick film of green algae, thin as paint, which the drip-shrimp graze and the running water constantly renews. When the violet pulse passes over it, the algae briefly shows its color, a startling wet emerald, before the dark closes over it again.

---

## Rhythms

The room is never still, but it has cycles.

The note rises and falls with the valley below. When a storm builds in the lowlands, the air thickens with moisture and the machine works harder. The note drops lower, until it seems to press against the chest like a hand, and the vanes run with so much water that the whole room sounds like the inside of a waterfall. The drip-shrimp feed frantically. The swiftlets stay home. The violet pulse quickens.

In the dry season the note climbs, thins, and grows almost melodic. The vanes dry toward the back of the room, the bloom thickens, and the lamp-slugs multiply. At those times the rear wall glows faintly with them all through the long dark.

Once in a long while, perhaps once a year, the note stops. Nobody who lives here can remember a reason or predict it. For a few seconds there is total silence, and the air is still, and every drop that falls can be heard striking the basin like a bell. The swiftlets freeze on their nests. The shrimp let go of the vanes and drift in the water. The spiders do not move.

Then the machine inhales again, and the room fills with noise, and everything resumes. Nothing that lives here seems to understand the silence as anything but the most frightening event in the world.
