# The Stair

## The ground that walks

The town sits in a cirque high above the treeline. It is a bowl of pale rock under a ring of snowfields so bright you cannot look at them. The floor of the bowl is not solid. It is a rock glacier, a tongue of broken stone about four hundred paces long and two hundred wide, with a core of old ice buried somewhere beneath it. The whole mass flows downhill.

It flows slowly, but you can see it. Set a pebble on a boulder and look away, and when you look back the boulder has shifted a finger's width past its neighbor. Over an hour it moves a hand's breadth. Over a day, the length of a sleeping body. The stones grind past one another with a sound like grain poured into a sack, heard through a wall.

At the bottom of the tongue is the lip, a steep front of loose rubble twice a man's height. Stones reach the lip one at a time and tip over into the tarn below. The tarn is milk-green with rock flour, too cold for anything larger than a water-flea, and it takes the falling stones with a soft *plock*, then closes over them.

The people built their town on this.

## The treadmill

The town is called the Stair, or sometimes the Climb. It has stayed in the same place for longer than anyone can count, and it has done so by never standing still.

Every house is dry-stone: fitted blocks with no mortar, since mortar would only crack. The houses are packed close enough that you can touch two walls at once in most lanes. Every house is also slowly riding downhill toward the lip. So every house is always being rebuilt. The people take stones from the downhill wall and carry them to the uphill side to lay a new course. A house crawls up the slope exactly as fast as the ground carries it down, and so it stays where it is.

This is the work of the town, and it never stops. At any hour, perhaps a third of the people are carrying stone. They use antler levers, sledges of lashed bone and hide, and slings of braided goat-hair across their foreheads. From the snowfield rim the town looks like an anthill: small dark figures moving in constant, crosshatched lines, each with a gray lump on its back.

Not everyone keeps up. A household that falls ill, grows old, or simply stops will find its house carried slowly downhill, lane by lane. It passes neighbors who were once uphill and arrives in quarters where it doesn't belong. This is how people say someone has declined: *they're drifting.* A family that works very hard can climb, and wealth here means height. The oldest and most honored hearths sit at the top of the tongue, against the living rock of the headwall, where the ground is thinnest and fastest. Holding position there takes a dozen hands.

At the bottom, near the lip, is a loose, shifting quarter of houses no one is maintaining. It holds the dead, the abandoned, and the departed. These houses come apart as they go. Their walls lean, their courses split, and one stone at a time they tip over into the tarn. People speak of this without sorrow, the way elsewhere people speak of leaves. *My grandmother's hearthstone went over the lip in the thaw.* Some people go down to watch their family stones fall. Most don't.

Nothing here lasts, and yet the town is very old. The lanes are fixed and have names. The shape of the Stair is remembered. Only its material is always passing through.

## Water

Everything is wet.

The buried ice sweats. Every stone in the tongue weeps a clear film that beads at the lower edges and drips. At noon, when the sun comes off the snowfields at full strength, meltwater runs everywhere beneath the rubble. You rarely see it, but it is always near: in the gaps between stones, down in the dark, cold enough to ache the teeth. Lift a slab from a wall and water wells up in the hole within a breath.

At night everything freezes. The weeping stones glaze over, and the town wakes to a glassed world. Every wall is sheathed, every lane a slick of clear ice, and each doorway is hung with a fringe of small icicles that the first stone-carriers knock loose. These are the only sharp sounds of the morning: tinkling, then quiet.

The tarn breathes fog. On most afternoons a white vapor rises off it, climbs the lip, and wanders up through the lower lanes. It condenses on hair, wool, and eyelashes, then burns off in the glare, then returns.

People drink from the stones. Every house has a drip-trough of carved soapstone under its north wall, and the wall fills it.

## Light

The Stair is one of the brightest places in the world.

It is high, so the air is thin and clear. The sky at noon is so deep a blue it is almost violet, and the sun is a small white hole in it. The snowfields that ring the cirque throw light back down from every side, so shadows are faint and colored: pale blue, pale violet. At midday the whole bowl is a vessel of reflected brilliance, and the wet stones shine like the backs of fish.

The rock itself is full of mica, and the wet surfaces flash. As the ground creeps, its stones tilt, and each tilting face catches the sun for a moment and releases it. The whole tongue glitters in slow motion, a field of small signals switching on and off.

Everyone squints. The people have deep creases at the corners of their eyes, even the children. They wear slit visors of bone or bark tied at the back of the head. The old people mostly have milky eyes, and they navigate by counting steps and by the feel of the lane under their boots.

## Silence

This is the strangest thing about the Stair: it is very, very quiet.

It is not empty. There is enormous activity everywhere, from the carrying, the rebuilding, the animals, and the water. But sound does not travel here. Part of it is the thin air. Part of it is the tongue itself, with its millions of voids between the stones, all of them wet and cold and dark, which drink sound the way moss drinks water. A shout dies within ten paces. Two people speaking at arm's length must lean in. Across a lane you might as well be across a valley.

So the people of the Stair hardly speak aloud. Close conversation is a murmur, faces nearly touching, and sometimes hands laid on each other's forearms to feel the voice. For anything farther they use light.

Every person carries a mirror. Some are polished slabs of black slate kept wet with the mouth. The better ones are sheets of clear mica set in bone frames. Messages cross the town as flashes: short and long, doubled and tripled, angled off the snow or the sky. At midday the Stair is full of these small, silent stabs of light. There are thousands a day, tiny and intense, sent between lanes, between rooftops, from the carriers at the lip to the hearths at the headwall. A child learns to flash before she learns to talk.

At night, with no sun, the town goes dark and mute. Fat lamps burn indoors, and families sit close.

## Life

For a place so high and cold and bare, the Stair teems.

**Pikas.** The rubble is their country. There are thousands of them, round and gray-brown, the size of a fist. They live in the voids of the tongue and ride it downhill as the people do, and they are constantly relocating uphill as the people are. Every stone wall is full of them. They go in and out of the gaps, move through the houses at will, and sit on windowsills in the sun. Their call, a thin high *eenk*, is one of the few sounds that carries a little. Even so, you hear it only close by, and it seems to come from nowhere. The people tolerate them and sometimes eat them. Mostly the pikas are simply part of the stone, as the lichen is.

**Snowfinches.** Flocks of small gray-and-white birds, hundreds together, wheel over the town in fast silent clouds and settle on the rooftops all at once. They pick at the insects that the melt brings up. They fly in total silence. If they sing, no one has heard it.

**Snow-fleas.** Springtails, tiny and black, cover the snow at the edges of the cirque in such numbers that the snowfields look sooted. When the sun is strongest the black patches move. They shift and ripple and drift in slow waves across the white, following no wind anyone can feel. The people read these patterns for weather, luck, and marriage.

**Ice-crawlers.** Pale wingless insects, thumb-long, live in the frozen seams at the base of the walls. They move only when it's very cold. At dawn, on the glazed stones, you can see them walking slowly across the ice, translucent as the ice itself.

**Goats.** The people keep small, dense, long-haired goats, which spend their days on the walls. They eat the lichens and the little tough sedge that grows in the drip-lines, and they give milk, hair, and dung for fuel. They are sure-footed on the moving rock and seem to enjoy it. They stand on a teetering block and ride it, chewing.

**Dippers.** Dark birds that walk underwater. There are dozens of them in the concealed meltwater of the tongue. They slip into the gaps between stones and disappear, then come up somewhere else, sometimes inside a house, through a hole in the floor, dripping and perfectly calm.

**Ravens.** A few, large and very black against the glare. They sit on the headwall and watch. They are the only animals that seem to make noise deliberately, and even their croaks die in the air. Some of the old people say the ravens flash too, that they angle their wet feathers to catch the sun, and that they are talking.

## What grows

Very little. The ground moves too much for roots.

On the tops of the most stable blocks there are crusts of lichen, orange and chartreuse and black, some of them centuries old. They are older than any house, riding downhill on stones the people carry back up. Some families have had the same lichen-stone in their walls for twenty generations, and they know its face as they know their grandmothers'.

In the drip-lines under the walls grow a thin, brilliant moss and a few tufts of a tough blue-green sedge. The pikas harvest it, and so do the goats.

On the headwall above the town, where the rock is solid, there are cushion plants: tight domes of tiny leaves, packed against the cold, dotted in the brief summer with white flowers the size of a lentil. The people climb up to look at them. They do not pick them.

## Weather

The Stair is never still in any sense.

Squalls come over the cirque rim without warning. The sky is violet, then there is snow, then violet again. The fog climbs from the tarn and burns off, climbs and burns off. Freeze-thaw cycles run faster here than elsewhere, several times in a day when clouds pass over the sun, so the town glazes and melts and glazes again. Small avalanches rush off the snowfields, loose white slides that you see and do not hear. Stones in the tongue suddenly heave upward as water freezes beneath them, or collapse into sudden holes when the ice melts out. A lane can change shape in an afternoon, and a house wall can split between one breath and the next.

The people move among all this carefully and constantly. They carry stone, lever blocks back into line, fill the new holes, and flash warnings across the lanes. The work does not end. They do not seem to expect it to. There is a saying, passed face to face in a murmur, hands on forearms:

*The ground goes down. We go up. That is where we live.*

## At dusk

The sun drops behind the western rim. The snowfields go pink, then gray. The flashes across the town slow and stop. The carriers set down their last stones. Across the whole tongue there is a sudden cessation of movement, except for the ground itself, which keeps going.

In the quiet you can hear it. In the dark houses, with the fat lamps lit, families lie on their felts and feel the floor move beneath them, a faint steady sliding toward the lip. From the voids between the stones comes the thin voice of a pika, then nothing. From very far down comes a single soft *plock* as something leaves the town forever and enters the tarn.

Then the frost comes and glazes everything, and the town is locked in clear ice until the sun returns.
