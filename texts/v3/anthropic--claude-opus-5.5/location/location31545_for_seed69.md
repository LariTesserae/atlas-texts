# The Stokemoor

Nothing here is visible. Understand that first, because everything else follows from it.

The Stokemoor is a highland the size of a small continent, lifted above the surrounding world on a shield of old basalt and older iron. It lies under a lid. From the plateau's edge you would only hear the lid: a ceiling of cloud so thick and so permanent that it thunders softly against itself, miles overhead, with no gap in it anywhere. No light has come through in longer than anything living here can reckon. Yet the Stokemoor is not cold, and it is not still. It is warm the way the inside of a mouth is warm. It is wet the way breath on glass is wet. And it is loud, loud everywhere, because in a world without light sound becomes the land itself.

---

## The Stroke

The first thing you learn is the Stroke.

Somewhere far below the plateau, a very large machine works. Something turns over in the rock, and every forty-one seconds the whole Stokemoor rises by the width of a fingernail and settles again. You feel it in your knees before you hear it. Then the sound arrives: a low, round *WHUMM* that comes up through the ground rather than through the air. Every stone repeats it, then every pipe, then every hollow stem, until the plateau rings like a struck bell and slowly goes quiet. Eleven seconds of near-silence follow, and then the next Stroke rises from underneath.

No one living knows what the machine is. The pump-houses scattered across the moor are clearly its lesser relatives. Their iron is the same grey-green alloy, and they share its rhythm, their pistons locked to the Stroke like the heartbeats of calves pressed against a sleeping mother. But whatever sits at the center is buried under forty miles of rock that has had ages to settle over it. The moor has grown around the Stroke the way a tree grows around a nail.

Everything here keeps time by it. Animals call in the quiet after the Stroke and fall silent before the next one. Plants open their pores on the rise and close them on the fall. Water moves through the soil in pulses. If you lay your ear against the ground you can hear the plateau's capillaries drinking, forty-one seconds at a time.

## Ground and Material

Under your hands the terrain is mostly rounded. Very little here is sharp. The basalt has been worn for so long that it has the feel of river cobbles even on the high ridges: smooth domes of rock and long whalebacked hills, their saddles filled with deep peat that gives underfoot and seeps. Running water has cut channels through everything, but even the channels are old. Their walls are soft, grooved, and fluted by uncounted years of flow, and their beds are polished so smooth that bare feet slide.

Iron runs through all of it. The old engineers laid pipe across the moor in vast networks: water mains a man could stand in, steam lines the width of a waist, bundled cables thick as a wrist. Over the ages the pipes have sunk. The rock has flowed around them, or mineral crusts have grown over them, or peat has buried them, and now they run through the landscape like the roots of some enormous plant. You find them by touch or by sound. A buried steam main is warm, and walking over it you feel heat rising through your soles in a long straight line that disappears over a hill. A buried water main gurgles. A cable hums, a thin electric whine that sets your teeth on edge if you press your jaw to the stone above it.

Where the pipes break the surface they have gone soft and knuckled with crust: sinter, iron-bloom, pale lime, all deposited by leaks that have dripped for millennia. Some have grown into columns, some into draped shelves like frozen waterfalls. Most are warm, and some are hot enough to burn. They are covered in life.

## Water

The Stokemoor is not a marsh, exactly, but it has no dry places. The heat that rises through the ground drives water upward through the rock, and it condenses the moment it meets air, so every stone weeps. Every leaf is beaded. The air is so saturated that when you breathe out, your breath returns to you as a film on your lips.

Down in the lower hollows the condensation gathers into pools and runnels, then streams, and the streams run toward the plateau's edges, where they drop off in waterfalls that may be miles high. From anywhere on the outer moor you can hear the waterfalls as a continuous roar beneath everything, like wind that never pauses.

Twice a day, by the reckoning of the old pump-clocks, the Exhale comes. Across the moor the great steam vents open together: stone-lipped shafts, iron chimneys, and the ruptured mouths of pipes too large to have been meant for anything but this. Pressure built up in the depths escapes in one enormous sigh. The sound is a white roar, hundreds of vents at once, carrying across the plateau in a front. The air goes hot and dense, the lid overhead booms, and rain falls. It comes all at once, torrential and warm as bathwater, pounding the broad leaves into a rattle like a drumline. For an hour the whole moor is sheeted water. Then the vents close, the rain thins to dripping, and the long slow condensation begins again.

The Stokemoor makes its own weather this way. It is large enough that the Exhale's fronts collide with one another, spin, and wander. Storms that began in the eastern vent-fields can take days to reach the west, still raining, still warm, thundering against the lid.

## Vegetation

Nothing here photosynthesizes. There has never been light enough.

The plants of the Stokemoor eat heat and mineral. Their roots follow the buried steam lines as if following scent, and they drink the leaking condensate, which is thick with dissolved iron and sulfur and stranger things. In the leaves, bacteria live in chambers and do the work that sunlight does elsewhere: they break down the chemistry of the water and turn it into sugar.

The result is lush, even crowded. The plants are broad, soft, and pale, though no one has ever seen that they are pale. Their leaves are large, some wider than a man's arms can stretch, and so thin and warm and damp that touching one feels like touching skin. They grow toward heat the way plants elsewhere grow toward the sun. Walk along a buried main and you pass through a corridor of leaves all angled inward, pressing close, heavy with water that sluices down on you at the slightest touch.

The flora falls into loose kinds:

- **Mantle-fern** carpets the open slopes in fronds as fine as hair. It grows so densely that the ground becomes a sponge, and in the quiet between Strokes it makes a faint rustling hiss as it breathes.
- **Stack-trees** grow where pipes break the surface. They are tall, soft-wooded, and hollow, and they wrap their roots around the crusted iron and rise twenty or thirty meters. Their trunks conduct sound. Every Stroke makes a stack-tree ring like an organ pipe, and a grove of them sounds a chord that varies with their heights: mournful, huge, and never quite in tune.
- **Bloom-lichen** encrusts every warm surface in layers built up over ages, thick as a mattress in places. It is spongy and sour-smelling and tastes of rust.
- **Cablevine** is stranger. It grows only along the electrified lines and seems to feed on the current itself. Its tendrils wrap the old cables in spirals, and its leaves buzz. A cablevine thicket is audible from a long way off as a thin, overlapping whine, and touching one gives a tingling shock that is not quite painful.

The undergrowth is dense everywhere, a layered tangle of stems, fronds, soft leaves, and hanging strands of fungus, all of it wet and warm and pressed together. You do not walk through the Stokemoor so much as push.

## Fauna

Everything here is loud, because everything here navigates by sound.

The moor is thick with animal life, and nearly all of it calls. In the quiet after each Stroke the air fills with clicks, pips, whistles, and chitters, and with the long bubbling ululation of the bellows-birds. The sound stops before the next Stroke falls, the whole moor going quiet together, so the Stokemoor's soundscape comes in pulses: a roaring chorus, silence, the Stroke, the chorus again.

A few of its creatures stand out.

**Bellows-birds** are heavy, flightless things the size of geese. They have enormous air sacs in their chests and call by inflating on the Stroke's rise and deflating slowly through a tube of horn in the throat. The sound is a deep hoot that carries for miles. Flocks spread across the moor and call back and forth, and from the pattern of echoes each bird builds a picture of the land between them. A bellows-bird knows its range as a kind of sung map.

**Rivetbacks** are armored herbivores, shelled and many-legged, that graze the lichen off the crusted pipes. Their shells are hard, round-plated, and faintly magnetic from the iron they eat, and they clack against the pipes as they feed. A herd of rivetbacks on a steam main sounds like distant workmen hammering.

**Condensate eels** live in the warm channels and hunt by feeling pressure waves in the water. They are long and slick, and large ones can reach several meters. They hum when they move, a sound you feel through your feet if you wade.

**Oil moths** swarm around leaking engine-joints, drawn to the smell of old lubricant that still seeps from buried machinery. Their wings are furred and nearly silent, but there are so many that a swarm makes a thick, soft roar like distant rain.

Then there is the **quietcat**, which nobody likes to talk about. It is the only large predator on the Stokemoor and the only animal that does not call. It moves in the silences. It has learned the gap before each Stroke, when the whole moor falls quiet and everything listening can hear its own heartbeat, and it moves only then, fast and soft on padded feet, eleven seconds at a time. It freezes when the Stroke comes and the chorus returns. Something that lives by listening cannot find a thing that makes no sound. You will know a quietcat is near only by a gap in the chorus: a patch of moor where the bellows-birds have stopped calling and the rivetbacks have stopped clacking, a silence with a shape.

## Fire Without Light

Here is the strangest thing about the Stokemoor: fire burns dark.

No one knows whether something in the air or the rock causes it, or something the engines did long ago. A lamp lit on the moor gives off heat and a crackle and a smell, but no glow. Where an old gas line has ruptured, the leak burns in a roaring column that you can feel from a hundred meters away as heat on your face, but it casts no light at all. Sparks leap from failing cables with a snap and a whiff of ozone and nothing else. Lightning in the Exhale-storms comes as thunder and a blast of heat.

The creatures here have adapted. Many of them sense heat directly, through pits on the face or patches of fine skin, and they "see" the land as a gradient of warmth. To them the buried mains are glowing rivers, the vents are fountains, and the fire-columns are beacons. The Stokemoor, lightless as it is, is full of this invisible glow, and much of its life steers by it.

## Structures

The builders are long gone, and the few things they left are scattered: a pump-house every few days' walk, iron towers along the ridgelines, fragments of rail sunk in their own grooves.

The pump-houses are the most common. They are squat and round, built of fitted basalt blocks worn smooth by condensate, and each shelters a machine that is still running. You hear them from a long way off: the piston's heavy rhythm locked to the Stroke, the hiss of valves, the clank of some mechanism that has worked unceasingly since before memory. Inside they are hot and full of steam, and wildlife crowds every one. Stack-trees grow up through their collapsed roofs. Cablevine smothers their walls.

The towers are rarer: tall iron frames on the high ground, cross-braced, hung with cables that sway and sing in the Exhale winds. Some still carry current. Their tops snap with dark sparks, and the air around them smells of burning.

There are other things, stranger ones. A great gear half-buried in a hillside turns once every few days, grinding against the stone. There is a door set flush in a cliff, iron and warm to the touch, with a steady thrumming behind it. And somewhere, reported by travelers who never quite agreed on where, there is a single tall chimney, standing alone in the open moor. It breathes out warm air in time with the Stroke, and if you stand at its base and listen, you can hear, very far below, voices.

## Living Here

To cross the Stokemoor you learn to listen.

You learn to hear the difference between a steam main and a water main underfoot, and between a stack-tree grove's chord and a pump-house's rhythm. You learn which way the waterfalls lie from the direction of their roar, and to read the bellows-birds' calls as distance and direction. You learn that silence is dangerous. And you learn to feel the land's warmth on your skin, to follow the buried heat-rivers, to find your way by the moor's invisible glow.

Above all you learn the Stroke. Forty-one seconds up, the moor rises. The chorus swells, the plants breathe, the water moves. Then comes the quiet, eleven seconds when nothing calls, when the quietcats hunt, when you can hear your own blood. And then, rising out of the deep rock, beneath the waterfalls and the rain and the singing trees, the Stokemoor's great heart turns over once more, and the whole plateau answers:

*WHUMM.*
