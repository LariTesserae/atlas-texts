# The Sinter Ward

It is smaller than you expect. That's the first thing. From the lip of the hollow it looks like a single mass of rooftops furred with green and breathing steam, no bigger than a village green. Once you go down the stair, which is slick, worn to a trough in the middle, and warm underfoot, the ward closes around you. You realize it is not small so much as *packed*. Everything that a city needs to make and move has been fitted into a space you could walk across in four minutes, if walking in straight lines were possible here. It isn't.

## Structure

The ward sits in a basin of old brick, a few storeys below the land around it, as if it had been set into a mould. Its buildings lean together until their eaves nearly touch. The alleys between them are a shoulder wide and roofed over by pipes: copper gone the green of pond-skin, iron gone the colour of dried blood, lead mains as thick as a torso. Some run straight and some coil. Some hang in shallow catenaries, sagging under their own warm weight, and drip steadily from every joint.

The buildings were once pump-houses, boiler sheds, a dye works, something with tall narrow windows that might have been a mill, and a squat domed structure full of relay cabinets. None of their edges is sharp anymore. Everything in the ward has been rounded, and not by wear. The ward was never eroded. It has been *coated*.

## The Water

The water here comes up warm from somewhere below. It carries so much dissolved stone that it leaves a skin on whatever it touches. The pipes weep it. The walls sweat it. The air is so saturated that it condenses on the undersides of the pipes and falls as a fine indoor rain that never quite stops. Wherever the water lingers, it lays down sinter: a pale, faintly translucent crust, cream and rust and bone-white, built up in layers thinner than paper.

Over a very long time this has buried the ward in itself. Door-hinges are sealed into frames that have become single pieces of stone. Stair-rails have swollen into smooth pale ropes. A handcart abandoned against a wall is now a low lump, its wheel still legible as a round shadow under the glaze, like a fossil that hasn't finished forming. The cobbles have vanished beneath terraced pools: shallow rimmed basins that step down the alleys, each brimming and spilling into the next. The water in them is clear, tea-warm, and very still at the centre, while it trembles constantly at the rims.

This is the ward's erosion, running backwards. Nothing is being worn away. Everything is being settled into place, closed up, finished. The longer a thing stays in the Sinter Ward, the more permanently it belongs to it.

## The Green

Plants love it here. Hart's-tongue ferns hang in ranks from every mortar line, their fronds beaded with condensate. Liverworts spread in flat, lobed sheets across the sinter, glossy as wet leather. Mosses climb the pipes in precise bands, thick on the cooler stretches and absent near the steam joints. If you know how to read them, the heat of the whole network is mapped in living green. Maidenhair clusters in the spray around valves. In the corners of the relay hall, where it is darkest and wettest, something like a club-moss has grown up the cabinets in stiff little spires.

Figs are the ward's trees. They root in the brickwork high up and send their roots down the walls in pale, braided cables that follow the mortar exactly, so each building seems to have grown a second skeleton outside its first. In places the sinter has overgrown the roots and the roots have overgrown the sinter, again and again, until neither can be said to be holding up the wall alone. The canopy they make overhead is patchy but thick. With the pipes, the leaning eaves, and the permanent haze, it means that daylight never reaches the ground here as daylight. It arrives as a greenish murk, like light at the bottom of a pond.

## The Machines

The ward is loud. It is loud the way a waterfall is loud: constantly, from every direction, in layers. You stop hearing it as noise after a while and start hearing it as a place.

The bottom layer is water. There is rushing in the mains, trickling in the terraced pools, and a gurgle in the drains, which are always running and always warm. Above that comes steam. Relief valves open and close with long hisses. Somewhere a whistle leaks a thin, unending note that has gone flat over the decades. Above that is the clatter of the lesser machines: little reciprocating pumps in basements, the ratchet of a float-valve, the relays in the domed hall snapping open and shut in patterns too irregular to be random and too dense to follow. A dynamo hums in a shed near the east wall. Its current runs out through cloth-wrapped cables to a scatter of arc lamps. These burn fitfully and violet-white, each in its own nimbus of fog, and they flicker in time with something.

Under all of it, felt more than heard, is the Engine.

It stands in the tallest building at the centre of the ward, in a hall whose roof glass has been glazed over by sinter until it glows a dim, even amber. It is a beam engine, enormous, its iron beam longer than the hall is wide, pivoting on a column furred with moss. Its stroke is very slow. From the bottom of one stroke to the bottom of the next takes about eleven minutes. The beam rises almost imperceptibly, groaning, drawing something up from far below. At the top it pauses. Then it falls, and the fall is the one sudden thing in the ward. The whole basin shudders. The terraced pools ripple outward in rings. Fig leaves shiver down every alley at once. A single deep *knock* goes through the stone, through the water, through your ribs.

Then the long rise begins again.

## The Stillness Inside the Noise

It takes a while to notice what's missing. Then it becomes the loudest thing in the place.

There are no animals. None at all. No rats in the drains, though the drains are warm and full of rotting figs. No midges over the pools, though the pools are still and warm and should seethe with them. No spiders, though the angles between the pipes are perfect for webs. No snails on the ferns, no woodlice under the fallen fronds, no birds in the fig canopy. The figs ripen, split, and fall uneaten into the terraces. There they soften, sink, and are slowly glazed over, so the floors of the deeper pools are studded with pale sinter lumps that were once fruit.

The plants don't seem to need animals. The figs set seed anyway. Some say the Engine's knock does it: every eleven minutes the pressure pulse shakes loose a fine gold haze from certain fronds and flowers, and the haze drifts through the alleys and settles where it will. Seeds move through the pipes as well. Cut open a length of old main and you'll find ferns growing *inside* it, pale and nearly colourless, rooted in the sinter lining and sustained by the warm dark water. So the ward plants itself, through its own plumbing.

Nothing here seems to be tended, yet nothing breaks down. When a joint fails and begins to spray, the sinter closes it within a season. When a valve seizes, water finds another way, and the moss shifts its bands to follow the new heat. The clocks mounted on several buildings have all stopped, each at a different hour and each glazed over, their hands fixed in stone. The ward keeps time anyway, by the knock.

## The Long Cycle

There is a slower rhythm than the Engine's, though you'd have to stay a long time to catch it.

A few times a year, never on a schedule anyone has worked out, the whole ward exhales. The pressure in the network builds over days. The hiss of the relief valves climbs in pitch. The indoor rain thickens. The arc lamps brighten and hold steady for once, and the relays in the domed hall fall into something like unison. Then, at some unmarked moment, every valve in the ward opens together. Steam pours up out of every alley and chimney and vent, so thick and white that the ward disappears from the lip of the hollow. It becomes a bowl of cloud with the Engine's knock beating inside it.

It lasts perhaps an hour. When the steam clears, every surface is running with fresh water. The pools have overflowed their rims and laid down a new layer. The ward is very slightly smaller inside, very slightly smoother, very slightly more sealed. And the green gleams as if it had been painted that morning.

## Standing There

What stays with you is the warmth: wet, mineral, faintly sulphurous, the air thick enough to drink. Then the dimness, green and amber and arc-violet, with no shadows sharp enough to stand in. Then the noise, which never lets up and never grows harsh, a whole city's worth of working packed into a few crooked streets. And under it, the pull of a place that is finishing itself. The Sinter Ward is slowly setting everything it holds into stone. It keeps its own time, grows its own garden, and runs its engine for no one. It seems neither to need anyone nor to mind the absence.

If you stand still long enough, water beads on your sleeves. It is warm, and a little chalky when it dries.
