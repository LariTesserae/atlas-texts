# The Seam

It begins as a crack you could miss: a lip of black rock at the bottom of a quarry pit, edged with iron railing and a signal lamp gone amber with mineral scale. Step through, go down forty iron stairs, and the air closes over you like bathwater. You are inside the Seam, a settlement no wider than a village square and no longer than a short street, packed into a fissure the earth opened less than a lifetime ago.

## The Rock

The walls are new, and you can see it. Nothing here has had time to soften. The stone is dark basalt shot through with veins of greenish glass, split so recently that the fracture faces still hold their conchoidal ripples, like water frozen mid-shudder. Edges are sharp enough to cut a sleeve. Where the fissure narrows overhead, the two walls lean toward each other and nearly meet, leaving a ragged ribbon of darkness where the quarry sky should be. Very little light comes down that way. Most of it rises from below.

The fissure is still moving, though too slowly for anyone to feel. It is closing. The walls creep toward each other at roughly the width of a thumbnail each year. The town was built to accommodate this. Every building is fitted with screw-jacks and sacrificial timbers along its wall-side face, and each autumn the crews go through and shave a little off, taking a course of brick from the back of a workshop or cutting down a gantry by one bolt-spacing. The Seam is a town that gets thinner as it ages. Old residents describe rooms that no longer exist.

## The Heat

Below the lowest walkway the fissure keeps going into a throat of rock that breathes. Heat comes up from it steadily, without wind or roar. It is a mineral, humid heat that smells of struck flint, wet slate, and faintly of eggs. Stand still anywhere in the Seam and within a minute your skin is slick. Iron is warm to the touch everywhere, and brass is hot. People here wear leather palms on their gloves and never lean on anything.

That heat is the reason the town exists. It is less a settlement than a works that people happen to live inside.

## The Works

Everything in the Seam is stacked. Buildings don't stand side by side, because there isn't room, so they sit on each other in shelves bolted into both walls, and the walkways run between them like the rungs of a ladder laid on its side. There are six levels, perhaps seven depending on how you count the half-floor above the chandlery. At no point can you stretch both arms out without touching something: a pipe, a railing, a wall of riveted plate, a hanging chain.

Pipes are the town's real architecture. They come up out of the hot throat in bundles as thick as tree trunks, lagged in cracked asbestos board and wrapped in canvas stiff with dried brine. They branch, loop, and double back. Some carry steam up to the condensers at the top of the Seam. Others carry brine drawn from deep pockets in the rock, water so saturated with minerals it feels oily between the fingers. There are also narrow copper lines that carry nothing anyone can name anymore. Nobody takes those out, because no one is certain.

The principal machines are three beam engines, one to a level, their great iron arms nodding in the dark with a patience that doesn't seem mechanical. They drive the brine pumps. Their stroke is slow, about one every eleven seconds, and the whole town unconsciously keeps time to them. People pour tea on the downstroke and hammer rivets on the up.

Above the engines are the evaporating pans, long shallow trays of black iron where the brine is spread thin and left to steam. The residue scraped from them each shift is the Seam's product, a pale, sparkling crust sold up top as "seam-salt." It is not really salt. It is a mixture of sulphates and something else the assay offices have never agreed on, and it is valued for glassmaking, for tanning, and for reasons people are vague about.

## The Damp

The Seam is hot, and it is also close to dry, but not quite.

Rain never reaches it. What water there is comes as condensation, which is everywhere and never pools. Steam rises, meets the cooler stone near the fissure's lips, and beads there. It gathers into drops that fall back down through the town in a slow, constant, scattered drizzle. Nothing is ever soaked, but nothing is ever fully dry. There is a film on every railing and a bead at the end of every bolt. Paper curls here. Books are kept in tins.

The water carries dissolved stone, and wherever it lands and evaporates it leaves some behind. That is the Seam's second slow process, the counterpart to the closing walls: the town is being built over. White sinter grows on everything. It laces the undersides of walkways in frilled curtains, drips into tiny stalactites from lamp brackets, and fills the grooves of screw-threads so that bolts must be chipped free before they can be turned. An unattended railing will have a knuckle of stone on it within a season. Unattended doors seal.

So the crews of the Seam do two kinds of work. They cut the town narrower as the walls close, and they chip it free as the stone grows over it. The rock takes from both directions, and the people keep scraping out room to exist.

## The Light

The Seam is dim, and its dimness has a warm color. Sodium lamps are rationed and caged, spaced at intervals along the walkways, and their orange light is diffused by the permanent haze into soft globes, each with a halo like a thumbprint on glass. Past about twenty paces, everything dissolves into amber murk.

From below there is a different light: the ember-red glow of the furnace mouths on the lowest level, where the steam is superheated for the engines. It comes up through the grating in long bars, so the underside of everything is lit faintly red and the top of everything faintly orange. Shadows here fall upward as often as down.

The glass veins in the walls catch both lights and hold them. In certain places, where a vein runs thick, the wall seems to glow from inside with a dull green shimmer, like light through a bottle. Everyone in the Seam knows that it isn't really glowing, and nearly everyone has at some point stood in front of a vein at the end of a shift and watched it anyway.

## The Growing Things

Plant life here is sparse but specific.

Where the lamps are and the drip is steady, pale ferns grow. They are thumb-sized, nearly white, and rooted in the sinter itself, taking what they need from the lamplight and the mineral water. They grow only on the lamp side of things, so every fern in the Seam leans toward a light. Following them, you could map every lamp in town in the dark.

On the warmest pipes, where the lagging has split, there are films of algae in a shocking copper-green, slick and fine-textured, which grow and die back with the engines' heating cycle. When the pumps shut down for maintenance and the pipes cool, the film goes grey within a day. When the engines resume, it greens again within an hour, as if it had been waiting.

That is everything. There is no soil, no grass, no weeds in cracks, only the ferns and the film, both of them pale, both clinging to the edges of the human systems.

## The Absence

Nothing in the Seam walks, flies, or crawls. There are no rats, although the town has everything rats usually want. There are no flies, beetles, or spiders at the lamps. Whether it is the heat, the sulphurous air, or something in the sinter, animal life does not come down the forty stairs. Dogs brought down refuse the last flight. Birds that stray into the quarry circle above the lip and turn away.

People notice it most at night, if a sleep-shift can be called night. A town should have small noises of scratching and fluttering and things living in its walls. The Seam has none. The walls are sinter and stone, and nothing lives in them but the slow chemistry.

## The Sound

The quiet of the Seam is dense rather than empty.

Underneath everything is the engines' eleven-second breath, the long sigh of the downstroke and the iron exhale of the return, felt through the soles as much as heard. On top of that are hundreds of small, overlapping sounds. There is the drip of condensation on iron, on stone, and on the fern leaves, each surface giving a different note. There is the tick of pipes expanding and the faint hiss of a valve somewhere always slightly leaking. Now and then comes the dry chink of a chisel as someone frees a bolt or a hinge from its casing of new stone.

People speak softly here. It started as habit, because in a space this tight and this hard, voices carry and ring back off the rock. Now it is closer to custom. Shouting in the Seam is considered not rude but alarming, since the only reason to raise a voice is a burst line or a jammed jack. So conversation is murmured, and the town sounds like a library built inside a boiler.

Once in a great while there is a deeper sound, a single, low, grinding crack from far back in the rock. It is the walls taking another fraction of their thumbnail. Everything goes still for a moment. Then the drip resumes, the engines take their next stroke, and someone somewhere notes the time in a ledger.

## The Rules

The Seam runs on a few principles, written nowhere and known to everyone:

**Nothing stays still unattended.** Sinter takes it. If you want to keep a thing, you must touch it, turn it, and use it.

**The town can only get smaller.** Nothing new is ever built. Only rearranging and cutting down are possible. What is lost to the closing walls does not come back.

**Keep time with the engines.** Work on the stroke. It is safer, because the whole Seam moves together, and a person out of rhythm is a person about to be caught by a swinging chain or a lurching gantry.

**Speak low. Listen to the rock.**

It is a young place. The stone is sharp, the fracture still fresh, and the town is dug into the wound before it has closed. It is hot and close and endlessly damp, never wet. It is small and dense and getting smaller and denser every year, and it is always slowly moving: the walls leaning in, the sinter growing, the engines nodding, the pale ferns turning toward their lamps. The people keep chipping out their small, busy, lamplit space in a crack that is healing shut.
