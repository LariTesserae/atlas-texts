# The Vaulting

## I. The shape of it

Start with the mountains. They are young: black, glassy, their ridgelines still sharp enough to cut a hand, their slopes skirted in fresh scree that has not yet had time to become soil. They rise out of a high cold plateau, already far above where trees should give up, and they keep rising into thin air that tastes of iron and snow.

Something has grown over all of it.

From a distance, if there were any distance to be had, which there mostly isn't, the growth would look like frost on a window: a pale, branching, crystalline haze laid over the whole range. Up close it is a lattice. Hollow canes, some thin as a finger and some wider than a house, rise from the rock, fork, lean against one another, fuse where they touch, and fork again. Every junction is a knuckle of hard, resinous tissue. Every span between junctions is empty air. The lattice climbs the mountains and then climbs past them, so that the true peaks are buried somewhere deep inside it like stones in a loaf. It rises in open tiers for what may be several kilometers more before it closes into a crown of matted fronds that no one alive has touched.

It is all one plant. Or it behaves as one. Cut a cane anywhere and the whole thing seems to know: sap stirs in the neighboring spans, and over the following days the wound is bridged by new growth angling in from three directions.

The vegetation is total, and the space is almost empty. Both are true at once. The Vaulting is a structure of pure plant, and it is ninety-nine parts air. You can stand on a cane-junction and look out along a horizontal gap a quarter-mile wide with nothing in it but falling mist, framed on all sides by the lattice going on and on, receding into gray, then darker gray, then nothing. The plant fills the world the way ribs fill a chest. It encloses; it does not crowd.

## II. What it is made of

The canes are young everywhere, because the Vaulting never stops growing. This season's shoots are a waxy green-black, almost reptilian, and they come to points: long sharp spurs of silica at every new tip, still translucent, still brittle. Older spans harden to a gray-brown like weathered horn, ringed every half-meter with growth nodes. Nothing here is rounded by time. The whole structure has the raw, unfinished look of a scaffold built last week. Break a young spur and it snaps clean along a glassy face.

Inside, the canes are hollow, partitioned at each node by a thin papery membrane. The hollows hold air, sometimes warm air: the plant breathes, slowly, and its respiration leaves the inner chambers a few degrees above the killing cold outside. Tap a cane and it rings. Pierce a membrane and a thread of faintly sweet vapor escapes.

At the junctions the plant lays down resin, amber-dark and slow-flowing, pooling in the crooks where spans meet. Resin is the closest thing to ground in the upper Vaulting. It is tacky when fresh, hard as bone when old, and it burns with a smoky, reluctant flame. Lichens and a fine gray moss colonize old resin in rosettes. The moss is the only thing here soft enough to sleep on.

Beneath it all, where the lattice roots into the mountains, there is basalt and pumice and ash so new it still crunches like sugar. Roots split it. Much of the "rock" in the lower tiers is actually root: great flattened buttresses, gray and lichened, gripping the stone so tightly you can't tell where one ends and the other begins.

## III. The dark

Light arrives filtered through kilometers of lattice and a crown of fronds, so almost none arrives at all. At midday in the upper tiers there is a dim, directionless gray, like the last ten minutes of dusk, and it thins steadily as you descend. By the middle tiers the gray is only a rumor at the edge of vision: the pale canes are just barely distinguishable from the black gaps between them. Below that is darkness proper.

The region is also far enough toward the pole that the year splits into a long half-light and a longer night. During the Night even the upper gray goes out.

The dark is not absolute, and this is one of the unusual things. The plant's newest growth, those black-green spurs, carries a faint phosphorescence at the very tips, a cold blue-white you can only see when your eyes have been in darkness for an hour. It is not enough to see by. It is enough to see the shape of the Vaulting: thousands upon thousands of tiny pinpoint lights marking where the lattice is still reaching outward, a constellation that drifts almost imperceptibly across the seasons as the structure grows. People who live here navigate by the growing-points the way sailors navigate by stars, except these stars are always moving outward and upward, and the maps have to be rememorized every year.

## IV. The sound

It is loud in the Vaulting. This surprises people who expect a place so dark and empty to be silent.

The mountains beneath make their own weather, and so does the lattice. Warm respiration rising through the hollow canes meets the cold high air and drives slow, enormous convection currents. Wind moves through the structure constantly, and the structure is made of pipes.

Every cane with a broken node, every split, every hollow open to the air becomes a flute. There are millions of them, of every length, so the Vaulting sounds in every register at once. Near you, whistles and reedy shrieks. Farther off, hooting, droning tones. From somewhere deep, down where the canes are as wide as towers, comes a bass so low it is felt in the sternum more than heard: a long moan that rises and falls over minutes as the great currents shift. People call these deep tones the Breathing, and they time their days by it. When a storm comes through, the whole lattice howls. The noise is physical, a pressure in the ears, and conversation is only possible by touch or by shouting mouth-to-ear.

Under the wind there are other sounds. The canes creak and pop as they flex and as they grow; a new spur lengthening in the cold gives off a thin crackling like ice forming. Old spans that finally fail do not fall quietly. They go with a gunshot crack, then a long clattering descent, ringing off junction after junction on the way down, and the echoes chase each other through the empty tiers for a full minute. And over everything is the noise of animals, of which there are a great many.

## V. Water

Water here is intermittent, and it hides.

It rarely rains in the conventional sense. The cold air is laden with mist, and the mist condenses on the canes. On the cold outer faces it becomes rime, feathery white crystals that build up overnight into delicate fringes; on the warmer spans near respiration vents it beads and runs. The Vaulting is always dripping somewhere. The drip is patchy and seasonal: one tier can be streaming while another a hundred meters over is dry as paper.

The drip collects. Resin pools at the junctions are shaped like cupped hands, and after a wet spell many hold a skin of clear cold water floating over the amber. The larger junctions cradle genuine ponds, a few meters across and suspended in the air, with nothing below them but half a mile of lattice and dark. These hanging pools freeze and thaw with the Breathing. They are full of life: tiny pale crustaceans, water-mites, the drifting larvae of things that will later fly.

When the half-light season warms the upper crown, meltwater comes down through the structure in bursts, cascading from junction to junction. For a few days each year the Vaulting is full of falling water. The fine threads and sudden curtains glint in the gray light, and the pools overflow, and the sound shifts from wind-howl to a vast hiss.

Then it dries again, down to rime and drip.

## VI. The living things

For a place of so little matter per cubic meter, the Vaulting is crowded with animals. They spread out through its volume the way birds spread through a sky, and the volume is enormous.

**The drift-moths** are the foundation. Pale, broad-winged, and soft as ash, they rise in the millions on the convection currents, feeding on the sweet vapor that leaks from pierced cane-nodes. They move in slow clouds that can take an hour to pass. In the phosphorescent dark you see them only as a flickering occlusion of the growing-point stars, a patch of sky that shivers.

**The skirlers** hunt the moths. They are leather-winged and fox-sized, and they navigate the lightless tiers by voice. Their calls are shrill clicking bursts that add a whole layer to the din. They roost head-down in the hollow canes, dozens to a chamber, and a roost disturbed will pour out of a split cane like smoke, screaming.

**Node-borers** are grubs as long as a forearm. They chew through the partition membranes of the canes and live in the warm inner chambers, and they are the reason so many canes whistle. Their holes are the pipes. The plant tolerates them, perhaps even needs them; their tunnels let its breath circulate.

**Climbers** move along the spans with hooked forelimbs. There are several kinds: some grazing moss off the resin, some prying borers out of canes, and the largest, gray and goat-heavy and silent, eating the young black spurs, spines and all. They leap gaps with a confidence that is terrifying to watch. Occasionally one misjudges, and the long clattering sound of a falling body joins the falling-cane sounds.

**Gulpers** are rarely seen and often heard. Nobody is certain what they look like. They are something large that lives in the deep tiers, among the tower-canes. Their call is a wet, cavernous boom, and the skirlers go silent when it sounds.

The whole system is fast. Moth clouds rise and are harvested. Skirler populations explode in the half-light and crash in the Night. Climbers strip a section of new growth, and the plant responds within weeks with thicker, sharper, more resinous spurs, so the climbers move on. Everything is feeding, fleeing, breeding, or growing, all the time, against the constant backdrop of the Breathing.

## VII. The people

A few people live in the Vaulting. Not many, and they are thinly spread, because there is so much space and so little ground.

Their marks are small. A notch-pattern cut into an old span marks a route: three short cuts and a long one means the next junction is sound. A cord of twisted cane-fiber is left strung between two junctions where the gap is too wide to jump. A resin bowl has been scraped smooth into a cistern. Beside an especially big hanging pool, there might be a shelter: a pod of split cane-slats woven into a basket-shape, lashed into a junction crook, lined with moss and skins, its door facing away from the prevailing wind. From outside, these shelters look like seed-cases, as if the plant had grown them itself. Some have hung in place so long that it has: the lattice grew around them, and now new spans run straight through old walls.

Their tools are of the lattice and what lives in it. Silica spurs, flaked and hafted, serve as knives and awls. Blowpipes are cut from thin canes and fire resin-tipped darts at skirlers and moths. Hooks are carved from climber horn. They cook over resin fires in stone pots carried up from the mountain roots, and the smoke hangs sweet and heavy in still air. Metal is almost unknown. A few families keep a single iron blade, handed down, its origin forgotten, sharpened to a sliver.

They talk in two ways. In calm air they speak aloud. When the Breathing rises and the storms come, they talk with their hands, pressing signs into one another's palms in the dark. Children learn palm-speech before voice.

They follow a few rules of the place, some learned and some believed.

**Never sleep on young growth.** Black spurs are brittle, and the plant sheds them without warning.

**Never cut a living cane without cause.** The plant knows. Cut carelessly, and the lattice around your shelter will thicken and close over the next seasons until the pool you drank from is sealed and your route is gone. Whether this is response or retribution depends on who is telling it.

**Follow the stars outward and you will find new growth, sharp and dangerous. Follow them inward and you will find old growth, strong and dark.** There is no down that is safe. There is only inward.

**No one goes to the crown, and no one goes to the roots.** The crown is too thin and cold to breathe, and the fronds there, some say, close like hands. The roots are where the Gulpers boom.

## VIII. Motion

Nothing in the Vaulting holds still long enough to be described twice. The lattice grows outward from its countless points and hardens behind them. Old spans die, crack, and fall, and the tiers below catch them, so there are drifts of dead cane piled in the crooks and slowly becoming new resin, new ground. Mist condenses, freezes, melts, falls. Moth clouds rise; skirlers pour out to meet them; climbers leap. The wind shifts, and a thousand pipes change their notes, and the Breathing rises from the dark.

The mountains underneath are still young and still restless. Now and then one of them shrugs. A tremor passes up through the roots, and the whole lattice rings like a struck bell, every cane at once, for a long ringing second. The moths lift, the skirlers scream, and a hundred dead spans let go and come clattering down through the dark. Then the echoes fade, and the growing points glitter, and the plant goes on building itself into the sky.
