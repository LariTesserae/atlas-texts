# The Held Chord

You would not find it by looking. The entrance is a seam in a limestone bank, a black mouth no wider than a doorway, half hidden behind a slump of wet scree at the bottom of a ravine that is itself hard to find. What gives it away is the sound. Standing at the seam, you feel it first in the sternum, a low pressure like a hand laid flat on the chest. Then it arrives in the ears. It is not a roar, exactly. It is a chord, many tones laid over one another, steady as held breath. It has been sounding, as far as anyone can tell, for longer than there have been people to hear it.

The passage runs down. The walls sweat. A skin of water covers every surface, cold enough to ache the fingertips, and in places the rock itself seems to weep, beading from pores too small to see and running in threads toward the floor. The floor has been cut into steps. They are shallow and many, and they are no longer sharp. Centuries of drip have glazed them with flowstone the color of old ivory, rounding every edge until the stairs look poured rather than carved. Your foot finds them easily. They were made for feet, and the stone has spent a long time agreeing with that.

The chord grows. By the fortieth step it is loud enough to take conversation away. By the hundredth it has taken thought's inner voice as well, or seems to, and you walk inside it the way you would walk in deep water.

---

## The Room

The passage opens into a chamber about the size of a large church. For a cave it is small, a pocket, a single bubble in the rock. It holds a city.

The walls are honeycombed from floor to vault with dwellings. There are hundreds of them, cut straight into the living limestone: cells, niches, rooms opening onto rooms, stacked twelve and fifteen deep up the curved walls like the chambers of a wasp nest or the seats of an amphitheater folded in on itself. Stairs climb between them, carved rather than built. Bridges of uncut rock were left standing when the stone around them was hollowed away, and they span from one side of the chamber to the other, thin as ribs, crossing and recrossing overhead. Nothing here was assembled. Everything was subtracted. The city is what remained after its makers spent generations taking the mountain away one flake at a time.

They were close to one another, those makers. Doorways sit shoulder to shoulder. Walls between rooms are a hand's width thick, sometimes less, and in some places they have worn through entirely, so that one family's niche opens into the next. Ledges run in every direction. Every surface that could hold something once did. The density is total. There is no plaza, no open ground, only the narrow gut of floor at the bottom where the water runs, and even that has been channeled and stepped and lipped with stone.

The tools lie where they were set down, and they have not moved. There are flint blades with edges still keen under a film of calcite, antler picks worn to stubs, and pounding stones shaped to the curve of a palm. A few copper pins have gone the green-black of deep verdigris and fused to the ledges where they rest. There is no iron here and no wheel. The whole city was cut by hand, by people working in the cold with stone against stone. Everywhere the walls carry the scalloped texture of ten thousand strikes, softened now under the same ivory glaze that covers the stairs.

---

## The Water

The chord comes from the ceiling.

Across the high vault runs a fissure, a crack in the roof from which water falls in a long, thin, unbroken sheet. It is not a torrent, and it does not need to be. It drops perhaps thirty meters through still air. Before it reaches the floor it meets the city.

The makers carved the upper tiers to catch it. Stone lips jut into the falling sheet at intervals, dozens of them, each cut to a particular angle and depth. Each one splits away a portion of the water and throws it against a particular surface: a hollow basin, a thin curved wall, a bell-shaped niche, a lattice of narrow slots. Every strike rings. Each basin and shell and slot has its own pitch, set by its size and the thickness of its stone. Together they make the chord. The deepest note comes from a great bowl near the floor, so large that three people could lie in it. The highest is a thin shimmer from a comb of slots near the vault, cut so finely that they whistle.

It is loud, loud enough that after an hour you stop hearing it as sound and start feeling it as weather. The chamber air vibrates. The film of water on the walls trembles in standing ripples that never move, fixed patterns like fingerprints pressed into every wet surface. Drops that fall from the ceiling in the quieter margins do not fall straight. The air shivers them into small arcs.

It does not change. The water that feeds the fissure comes from deep in the aquifer, from rock so far down that no rain and no drought reaches it. Its pressure is constant. Its flow is constant. Each lip has worn, over its long life, to precisely the shape the water gives it, and then stopped wearing. Erosion here is finished. The stone and the water came to an agreement long ago, and the agreement holds. The chord you hear is the chord the last maker heard, and the first.

---

## Light

There is light, barely.

The water glows. It is not bright. It gives off a faint cold blue-green, the color of a bruise beginning to heal, and it comes from a film of something living in the water. It is not a plant or anything so complicated, only a smear of single cells that thrive in the mineral dark and give off light as a byproduct of eating stone. They coat the falling sheet, the channels in the floor, and the inside of every ringing basin. Where the water runs thickest the light is strongest. The great bowl at the bottom glows like a shallow moon. The dry upper dwellings are nearly black.

The light does not flicker. It has no day and no night. It is the same dim, even glow it has always been, enough to see shapes and edges by, not enough to see color. Your hands look grey. The flowstone looks like bone. After a while your eyes open wide enough that the city seems almost lit, and then you look back up the entrance stairs and see that the passage is pitch dark, and you realize how little light you are living on.

The makers knew this light. In some of the dwellings the walls have been scored with fine grooves, and water was led through them in channels no wider than a straw. There, the glowing film traces patterns: spirals, lattices, rows of dots, running figures. It is writing in light, or decoration, or something between. The channels still flow, and the patterns still glow. No one is left to read them.

---

## The Still Room

At the heart of the city, where every bridge crosses, there is one room built differently from the rest.

It sits in the thickest bridge, near the center of the chamber, and you reach it by a stair so narrow you must turn sideways. It is round and small, just large enough for one person to sit. Its walls are cut in a series of stepped, nested hollows, like the inside of a shell.

When you enter it, the chord stops.

The silence is not complete. You can still hear your blood, and the faint tick of a single drop somewhere. But the great sound is gone, cancelled, as if a door had closed on it, though there is no door. The makers found the one place in the chamber where the chord's waves meet and erase each other, and they shaped the room to deepen it. In a city of unbroken noise, they carved a single seat of quiet.

The floor of the still room is worn deeper than any other surface in the city, a smooth dish polished by generations of sitting. Whoever came here came often, and came alone. It is the only place in the city where a person could have heard their own voice.

---

## What Lives Here

Very little, and slowly.

In the floor channels, pale salamanders the length of a finger drift among the glowing film and eat it. They have no eyes, only faint dark spots under the skin where eyes might once have been. They are the color of the flowstone, and they barely move. They may live a hundred years. You could watch one for an hour and see it take a single step.

In the great bowl there are shrimp, translucent and eyeless, lit from inside by the film they eat, so that each one is a small drifting spark. They hang in the water facing into the current that stirs off the falling sheet, and they hold position. There are perhaps a few dozen of them.

In the upper dwellings, where it is drier, a single species of cave cricket makes its way along the ledges on long pale legs, feeling ahead with antennae longer than its body. It makes no sound that could be heard over the chord. It may make none at all.

That is all. There are no bats, because the entrance is too far and too wet. There are no roots, no moss, no green thing. The rock grows nothing but its own slow skin of calcite, and the water grows nothing but its glowing film. Every creature here is pale, blind, patient, and sparse. The chamber holds as much life as the stone can feed, which is almost none, and it has held that same amount for as long as it has held anything.

---

## The Rules of the Place

It is always nine degrees, always wet, and always roaring. The air is saturated. Cloth left on a ledge would never dry; it would rot, and the makers' cloth did rot, and nothing remains of it. Breath does not fog, because the air is already fog, fine and cold, suspended in the vibration of the chord and never settling.

Nothing moves unless something moves it. The tools lie where they were left. The drip-glaze thickens by a hair in a lifetime. The salamanders drift. The water falls. The chord sounds, and the standing ripples hold their shapes on the walls, and the light holds its level, and the stillness of it is not the stillness of something asleep. It is the stillness of something that has finished becoming what it is.

The makers are gone. They left no bones here. They left no sign of leaving, no toppled doorways, no ash, no marks of haste. Perhaps they climbed out one generation and simply did not return. Perhaps they went deeper, following the fissure's water toward its source. Their city remains exactly as tuned as they left it. It plays their chord for no one, in a pocket of rock, under a ravine no one visits. Somewhere in the center of it there is one small round room where the noise stops. The stone floor there is worn smooth by all the people who came, one at a time, to sit in the only quiet they had ever made.
