# The Unfinished Range

## The Note

The first thing is the sound. It is not a wind, a roar, or a rumble of rockfall. It is a single note, sustained without pause and so enormous that it stops registering as sound. It sits in the sternum and the molars and behind the eyes. It has no source because the whole mountain is producing it. The rock sings the way a struck bowl sings, except nothing struck it, and it has never stopped.

The fundamental is too low to hear directly. You know it as pressure, a slow pulse in the gut, one throb every few seconds. Stacked above it are overtones, dozens of them, and those are what the ear catches: a vast chord, faintly sour, grinding against itself where the harmonics nearly align and don't quite. Close to bare rock the noise is total. You cannot hear your own voice, your breath, your footsteps, or the scrape of your boot. You shout and feel the shout only in your throat. Speech is not possible here, and never was.

Over long exposure the note seems to acquire texture. There are beats and swells where two overtones interfere, and occasionally a high thin harmonic, almost melodic, drifts in for an hour and fades. None of this is change. The note is the same note it was a thousand years ago, give or take a fraction of a tone.

That fraction matters, and we will come back to it.

## The Rock

The mountains are new. That is not a figure of speech: they are still being made.

There is no scree. There are no boulder fields, no talus fans, no rounded shoulders, no gullies. Nothing has broken yet. Every surface is a crisp, clean facet, the kind of face you would expect from a fresh fracture, except these faces were never fractured. They grew this way. The rock is dark and glassy, grey-black with a greenish depth where the light enters it, like obsidian with a mineral fog suspended inside. Run a hand along an edge and it cuts. Every edge cuts. The whole range is as sharp as the day it formed, because for most of it that day was recent, and for some of it the day is now.

The strata are incomplete. Look along a cliff and you see banding: layer upon layer of slightly different stone, each a few fingers thick, laid down horizontally the way sediment settles. But these layers weren't deposited from water. There is almost no water here, just a faint rime of frost in the deepest shadows that sublimates away without ever melting. The layers were deposited from the note. Each band is a period of slightly different pitch, frozen into the stone.

At the upper margins the strata simply stop. A ledge runs out along a cliff face and then thins, loses solidity, and becomes a grainy haze, a kind of shimmering static in the shape of rock. Beyond the haze the ledge continues as an outline: a faint suggestion in the air, visible mainly by the way light bends slightly around it. This is rock that has not yet arrived. It is intended rock. It will come in, grain by grain, over centuries.

## The Air and the Light

The elevation is extreme. The main basin, the part of the range where things live, sits so high that the air is thin enough to make the chest labor. It is cold, though not fiercely so: a steady, dry, still cold, like the inside of a stone cellar. There is almost no wind. The note seems to hold the air in place, packing it into standing layers.

The light is dim, and it is dim in a particular way. The sky is not dark. It is full. Suspended through the upper air is a fine haze of the same half-made matter that frays the edges of the unfinished strata, a glittering dust of not-yet-rock that has not decided where to settle. The sun, somewhere above it, becomes a diffuse brightness spread across a third of the sky, casting no sharp shadows. Everything is lit in a pewter-violet gloom, like the half hour after sunset stretched across the entire day.

There are shadows, though, and some of them are wrong. Some fall from things that aren't there. A long blue shadow lies across a snowless slope, cast by a spire that will not exist for another four hundred years.

## The Nodes

The mountain is vibrating, and any vibrating surface has nodes: lines where the motion cancels and the surface holds still. Scatter sand on a vibrating plate and it gathers along those lines into intricate geometric figures. These are Chladni patterns.

Here the vegetation is the sand.

From any high vantage the slopes of the basin are covered in green lattices: long curving lines, concentric arcs, star-points, nested ellipses, all traced in dense plant growth across the black glass. Between the lines is bare rock, humming too violently for anything to root. Along the lines, where the stone is still, it is lush. The growth is thick and deep and layered, and in places it is waist-high.

These plants do not drink. There is nothing to drink. Their bodies are mostly a waxy mineral substance, and their sap is a slow, amber-colored paste that doesn't flow so much as creep. They are cool-colored: deep bottle-green, green-black, a verdigris that shades toward copper at the tips. The textures are leathery and papery, dry to the touch, and faintly warm compared to the cold air. Most of them feed on the vibration itself, somehow drawing energy from the very edge of the node, where the stone barely trembles. The wide flat fronds of the most common species angle themselves toward the loudest nearby rock like sunflowers facing the sun, and they visibly quiver.

Nothing pollinates them. There are no insects, no birds, no animals of any kind. There never have been. The plants reproduce by shattering. Once a season, or what passes for a season, an overtone climbs briefly into some species' resonant frequency and every mature seed-head of that kind bursts at once. Across the basin you see puffs of glittering dust leaping up from the node lines in sequence, like a fuse burning along the lattice. You don't hear it, because you can't hear anything. The seeds drift and fall, and only the ones that land on a node survive.

The quiet lines are not fixed. As the note drifts by its fraction of a tone, the pattern of nodes shifts across the slopes, and the forests follow. It is very slow. A line of growth moves perhaps a hand's width in a lifetime. The trailing edge dies back into dry copper-colored husks that crumble to powder, and the leading edge pushes pale new shoots onto freshly stilled stone. Behind every living lattice lies a faint ghost lattice of dead stubble, and behind that, fainter still, another. Seen from above, a slope is a palimpsest of green figures drawn over older brown ones, a slow-motion record of the mountain changing its mind.

## How Height Works

Walking uphill here does not work the way it should.

You climb and you gain altitude, and the air thins as expected. But the note climbs with you. The fundamental stays where it is, and the overtones you hear most loudly shift upward, as though the mountain were a column of air and you were moving along it toward a narrower part. Height here is partly a distance and partly a pitch.

At a certain point the pitch outruns the distance. You can climb a ridge for a day and find yourself passing the same stone marker, the same bent frond, the same scatter of knapped glass. You have gained elevation, and your lungs and your ears both insist on it, but you haven't gone anywhere. The mountain is getting taller underneath you as fast as you can climb. Near the summits the rule becomes plain: a peak is not a place you can reach. It is where the note becomes too high for the rock to hold, and matter gives up and becomes haze.

Above that point the range continues as a sketch. Ridges and pinnacles are drawn faintly in the violet air, suggested by refraction and by the wrong shadows they cast. At the highest reaches you can see faceted peaks that are still mostly idea: vast hanging outlines, translucent, a few grains of real stone flickering in them like the first stars at dusk.

The echoes are stranger still. Since no one can hear ordinary sound here, it takes a while to notice. But if you strike two slabs of stone together hard, near a node where the ambient roar is slightly less, you feel an answer come back through your feet and hands. Sometimes it comes back from a cliff that is plainly there. Sometimes it comes back from empty air, from the outline of a cliff that hasn't formed yet. The sound goes out, finds the shape of the future, and returns. The echo is faint and a little late, as though it had to travel farther than the distance.

## The Makers

Someone lived here once, or passed through, or came repeatedly. The traces are scattered and few, with long empty distances between them, and none of them are recent.

They built only on the node lines, where the stone was still. Low ring walls of stacked glass-rock, knee-high, sit half-buried in the plant growth. Most of them now sit a little off the living green, stranded as the lattice moved on. Inside one there is a hearth-ring, although there is no trace of fire and nothing obvious to burn. Perhaps they burned the waxy husks. The stones are blackened only faintly.

They made tools from the mountain. Chips and flakes of knapped black glass lie in small scatters, easy to miss until the dim light catches an edge. There are scrapers, blades, and awls. This rock is so fresh and so homogeneous that it fractures beautifully, and the edges are finer than anything that has ever been ground. There is no metal.

They wove. Screens of plaited frond-fiber, stiff as bark and still intact because nothing here rots, stand propped against boulders facing the loudest rock. At first they look like windbreaks. There is no wind. They are sound-breaks. Behind each one there is a small pocket where the roar drops from unbearable to merely enormous.

They didn't speak, or couldn't. Instead they carved. Scattered across the basin are flat slabs incised with patterns: curving lines, nested arcs, and star-points. They are maps, but not of paths or landmarks. They are maps of quiet: charts of where the node lines ran when the carver lived. Compare one to the living lattice around it and the two almost match. The difference between them tells you roughly how long ago the carving was made. Some are nearly aligned. Others are so far off they must be many thousands of years old, from when the mountain was lower and sang a different chord.

## The Silence House

On the east side of the basin, on a spur of rock where three node lines cross, stands the one real building in the range.

It is a beehive of stacked stone about twice a person's height, corbelled inward to a narrow opening at the top. The stones are not random. Each has been chosen and shaped. Tapped with a knuckle, and felt rather than heard, each one rings at its own pitch, and the pitches are set against the mountain's note, a little flat or a little sharp, carefully placed so they cancel it.

Duck through the low doorway and the note stops.

It doesn't fade. It stops, all at once, as though a hand had closed over the world. For the first time you can hear your own breath and your heartbeat and the small dry rustle of your sleeve. The silence is so complete it feels like pressure. People who come here often weep, and they can hear themselves weeping, and that makes it worse, or better.

Scratched into the inner walls, low down where someone sitting would face them, are hundreds of small marks: tallies, simple glyphs, and a few crude outlines of hands. Nothing is written in any language you could read. There is only evidence that people sat here in the one quiet room on the mountain and wanted to leave something behind.

The house is failing, very slowly. Its builders tuned it to the note as it was, and the note has drifted. The cancellation is no longer perfect. At the center of the floor, if you hold very still, you can hear a faint high whine of mountain leaking through. In another few thousand years the silence will be gone and the house will sing along with everything else.

## The Whole

Step back far enough, to the rim of the basin, where the view opens across the whole region, and the place resolves.

Across the dim violet distance there are other peaks, each forming at its own rate and each singing its own note. You feel them as a vast slow chord, the local mountain's roar laid over fainter, more distant fundamentals, like standing in the middle of an organ that is being built while it plays. Their slopes carry their own green lattices, their own node-forests, each patterned to a different frequency. On one far range the figures are tight and star-shaped. On another they are long, loose spirals.

Between the peaks is a great deal of emptiness: bare black glass, humming slopes where nothing grows, a sky full of suspended half-matter, cold still air. Presences here are few and far apart. There is a line of green, a ring of stones, a carved slab, a single failing house. Then comes the long unmarked distance to the next.

Nothing here is eroding yet. Nothing has had time. The rain that would soften these edges has not come and may never come. The mountains are young and sharp and loud, still pouring slowly out of their own sound, still learning their shape from a note that is itself changing by a hair's breadth every century. Everything alive here is arranged around the few places where the singing briefly holds still.
