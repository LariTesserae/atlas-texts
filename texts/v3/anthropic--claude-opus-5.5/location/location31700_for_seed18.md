# The Crown of the Stack

## The Room

It is a room. You have to keep reminding yourself of that, because the first sensation is not of being inside anything but of standing at the lip of an enormous absence.

The chamber is a cylinder of riveted iron plate, perhaps sixty meters across and twice that tall, open at the top to the sky. There is no floor. What passes for ground is a narrow gallery, a ring of iron grating a few paces wide, bolted to the inner wall about a third of the way up. Above it the wall rises in curved plates toward a circle of daylight. Below the gallery's railing the wall simply continues down. It drops past the reach of the eye, past the reach of sound, into a warm dimness that does not end.

The middle of the room is nothing: a column of moving air as wide as a village square. That void is the most important thing here. Everything else is arranged around it, the way a town is arranged around a river.

## The Breath

The air comes up.

It rises from somewhere so far below that no one has measured it: a furnace, an engine, a buried industry that has run so long it has become weather. The updraft is steady and warm, the temperature of skin, and it carries water. It is not rain, only a thick mineral dampness. It smells of hot metal, wet ash, and faintly of something like bread.

As the column climbs, it meets iron plates that are cooler, especially near the rim, where the outside cold presses in. The moisture condenses there in beads, in sheets, in slow crawling films. So the Crown has two kinds of wet:

- **The rising damp** stays in the air, suspended.
- **The falling damp** gathers on the walls and runs back down in threads and beads toward the void it came from.

Walk the gallery and you pass through both. The air is thick and warm on one side. The wall on the other side sweats cold beads against your palm.

## The Sound

Nothing about the Crown can be understood without the sound, and the sound cannot really be described. It can only be survived.

The stack is a pipe. Air moving up a pipe makes a note, and this pipe is a hundred times taller than any cathedral organ's longest. The note is continuous and enormous: a low, grinding, harmonic roar that sits somewhere under hearing and somewhere inside the ribs at once. It does not echo, because there is nothing to echo off except itself. It fills the room completely, the way water fills a bottle.

You cannot speak here. You cannot hear your own footsteps or the rain on the plates. After an hour you stop hearing the note as sound at all and start experiencing it as a pressure, a condition, like altitude. Your teeth hum. Loose objects crawl slowly across the grating, walked along by vibration.

Nothing that lives here relies on hearing. Ecologically, the room is silent, because the roar has taken all the sound there is.

## The Garden

It is lush, astonishingly so, the way a place gets lush when it has been left alone for a very long time with warmth and water and something to hold onto.

Rust is the soil. Centuries of condensation have turned the iron's surface into flaking, layered crusts. It is a kind of metal shale, red-brown and ochre and nearly black, that peels in plates the size of hands. Behind the peeling layers is a thin rich substrate of rust powder, bird droppings, dead moss, and the corpses of insects carried up on the breath. Everything is rooted in this.

The garden sorts itself into belts by heat and wet:

- **Near the gallery**, where the air is warmest: tree ferns grow straight out of the wall at right angles and then curve upward, their fronds fanned open in the draft like hands warming at a fire. Fig trees have split rivets with their roots and braided themselves across the seams of plates. Their fruit, when it ripens and drops, falls *up* for a while before losing to gravity.
- **In the middle heights**: orchids pour off the wall in white and violet cascades. Pitcher plants drink the running condensation. Mosses a hand's-breadth thick grow in contour lines that exactly follow the rivet rows, so the wall looks mapped.
- **Near the rim**, where the metal is coldest and wettest: a pale lichen coats the iron like frost. Lower, curtains of hanging vine stream straight upward in the draft, pointing at the sky like weathervanes. Some of them are forty meters long and have never once hung down.

Seeds do not fall here; they rise. The breath carries spores and fluff and winged seeds up through the void. They circle in the slow vortex near the walls, and eventually they stick somewhere damp. The garden plants itself from below.

## The Machinery

The Crown is entirely made. There is no stone, no earth, no natural ground, only iron, brass, bolts, and grating. The plants are grown over the made thing, not in place of it.

The room still carries its apparatus:

- Pipes of green-furred brass run along the walls, some as thick as a person.
- Pressure gauges are set into the plate, their faces fogged. Their needles still move. They drift almost imperceptibly, pointing at numbers no one reads.
- A great valve wheel is mounted beside a hatch that no longer opens. The wheel turns on its own, a fraction of a degree a year. The ferns around it grow bent, pushed aside by the spoke that passes through them every few decades.
- A ladder of iron rungs climbs from the gallery to the rim. Most of the rungs are furred with moss, but the handholds on the uppermost few are polished bright, as though someone still climbs it. No one has seen who.
- Maintenance plates are stamped with numbers and a maker's mark worn nearly smooth. Under the moss, the mark shows a circle with a line through it. It might be a stack. It might be an eye.

Nothing here is decorative. It was all built for a purpose, and the purpose seems to have continued without anyone.

## The Living Things

There is a great deal of life in the Crown. It just isn't *dense*: it spreads across the walls or floats in the vast volume of empty air, never crowded.

**The swifts.** Thousands of swifts live in the column and have never landed. They sleep in the updraft, wings locked, rising slowly in their sleep, then waking near the rim and gliding back down in long spirals. From the gallery they look like ash on the breath. They mate on the wing. They build nests of nothing: little felted cups of spider silk and seed fluff, glued to the upward-streaming vines, swinging in the draft.

**The moths.** Pale, big as a palm, and blind, the moths navigate by the note. They feel it in their bodies and read its slight variations across the room, finding the walls by where the vibration changes. They gather in dense silent clouds around the gauges. Their wings smell faintly of hot iron.

**The frogs.** Small and translucent, they live in the pitcher plants and the moss. With no way to call to each other, they flash instead. Their throat sacs turn brilliant orange when they inflate. On the long evenings the wall flickers with thousands of tiny silent lanterns.

**The long spiders.** The spiders never build flat webs. They spin long threads and let them rise. The threads trail up into the draft, sometimes hundreds of meters, and catch whatever the breath brings: insects, seeds, flakes of rust, once a whole live bird. The threads catch the light, so the void is sometimes laced with rising silver lines, as though the room were being slowly strung like an instrument.

**The grating-snails.** They are heavy and iron-shelled. They feed on rust and move so slowly that a snail on the gallery is a landmark for a generation. The polished trails they leave on the iron are the only straight lines in the garden.

## Outside

Climb the ladder to the rim, and the room ends suddenly in a sky that is enormous, cold, and bright.

The Crown sits far above the clouds. The air outside is thin and freezing, and the wind moves sideways. Below the rim there is only cloud: a white floor stretching to every horizon, lit in ordinary daylight, broken here and there by other stacks.

There are other stacks. From the rim you can see perhaps a dozen, scattered at great distances across the cloud sea like the chimneys of a drowned city. Each breathes a faint plume of warm vapor into the sky. Each has its own garden at its crown: a green smudge on the black silhouette. And if you stand very still and let your body stop trembling from the roar beneath you, you can feel their notes. They are faint and far away, each at a slightly different pitch, all together forming a slow unresolving chord.

The cold does not come in. The warm column pushes it back at the rim, so the garden ends in a sharp line a few meters below the edge. Above that line is frost; below it, orchids. The boundary between them is as clean as a painted stripe.

## The Long Cycle

The Crown changes slowly, on the time scale of trees and valves.

**The furnace breathes.** Every seven years or so the draft slackens for a season. The heat drops and the note falls a half-step. The swifts must land for the first time in their lives, and they cling to the vines, bewildered. The rising seeds and threads and floating fluff slowly descend. Spider threads that had pointed straight at the sky sag and hang. For a few weeks there is a faint, almost-quiet stillness, and you can hear rain on the iron, the drip of condensation, a frog calling aloud. Then the heat comes back, the roar swells, and everything turns upward again.

**The note sinks.** Over centuries the pitch drops. Nobody knows why. Perhaps the pipe is lengthening, or the furnace changing, or the rust thickening. The moths track the note and adapt. The oldest orchids' growth rings, cut open, show bands where the vibration changed.

**The garden thickens.** Every year a thin new layer of rust and soil builds up. The ferns root deeper. One day, perhaps, the ring of green will close over the void, and the column will breathe up through leaves.

## The Rules of the Place

- Everything rises unless something holds it.
- The note is the ground. Things know where they are by feeling it.
- Wet comes up as warmth and falls back as water.
- Nothing is made anew. Everything is made, and has been made for a very long time.
- There is always far more space than life, and the life is dense only on the walls.
- Somewhere below, something is still burning, and it has never asked for anything, and it has never stopped.
