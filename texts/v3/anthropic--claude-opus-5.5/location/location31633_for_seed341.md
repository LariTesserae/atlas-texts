# The Sump at Hethering Low

## The Approach

You come to it over the embankment, and the embankment is the first thing that tells you where you are. It is old brick, laid in a long curve and grown over so thickly with fern and liverwort that it reads more like a hill than a wall. Only where the green has sloughed away, in wet brown scabs, do you see the courses underneath: soft red bricks worn to the roundness of river stones and weeping from every joint.

From the top of the embankment you look *down*. Hethering Low sits in a bowl beneath the level of the marsh around it, perhaps six hundred paces across, and the marsh leans against the embankment on every side. Outside, the water stands flat and brown to the horizon under a white-hot haze. Inside, the ground is lower than the water. The only thing that keeps it from being a lake is the noise.

## The Noise

The noise is the place. You feel it before you understand it: a slow, enormous *thoom* that comes up through the soles of your feet, then a long exhaling hiss, then a pause about as long as a held breath, then *thoom* again. Under that runs a continuous grinding, iron on iron, and over it, at irregular intervals, the shriek of a relief valve lifting somewhere in the steam.

There are seven engine houses in the Sump, scattered without much pattern across the bowl, with wide wet ground between them. Each holds a beam engine, a single iron beam as long as a barn, pivoted at its middle on a wall of dressed stone. One end rises and falls with the piston. The other end draws the pump rod from a shaft sunk deep into the ground. The engines do not stroke together. Each keeps its own tempo, and the tempos drift in and out of phase, so the combined sound is never twice the same. For minutes at a time the strokes scatter into a loose, lurching rhythm, like several people walking out of step on a wooden floor. Then, perhaps twice an hour, they converge. All seven beams come down within a heartbeat of one another, and the single blow is so heavy that every puddle in the Sump jumps into rings and the water beading on the leaves shakes loose in a brief falling rain.

People who have spent time here call this *the draw*. It is the loudest thing in Hethering Low, and it is the reason the plants are the way they are.

## The Air

It is hot, a heavy, standing, boiler-room heat. Part of it is the climate: this is low swamp country at the latitude of fevers, and the sun, where it can be felt at all, presses. The rest comes from the fireboxes. Each engine house vents its waste steam from a short iron stack, and the steam doesn't rise far. It spreads just above the rooftops, meets the hot wet air of the marsh, and hangs there as a low ceiling of vapour. The Sump lives under that ceiling permanently.

So the light is dim. At noon it is the colour of weak tea with milk in it, a warm grey-brown that throws no clear shadows. The steam ceiling glows faintly overhead without revealing the sun. Things at a distance go soft. The farthest engine house is only a darker shape with a pulsing orange seam at the base of its wall where the firebox door doesn't quite seal. Morning and evening hardly differ from midday. The gloom only deepens a little and turns more orange as the firebox glow becomes the strongest light.

Gas lamps stand along the old tram lines, iron posts with glass hoods cloudy from the inside. They are still lit. Their mantles burn a pale blue-green, fed by pipes that run down into the ground, and the gas that reaches them smells faintly of rot and eggs. It is marsh gas, drawn from the peat beneath the Sump, the same gas that feeds the fireboxes. Nothing here burns coal. The engines run on the breath of the ground they are draining.

Every afternoon it rains, and not from clouds. The steam ceiling thickens through the morning, sagging until the tops of the chimneys disappear into it. Sometime after what should be noon, it lets go. Warm water falls straight down, heavy and fat-dropped, drumming on the corrugated iron roofs loudly enough to compete with the engines. For half an hour the Sump is a roar of water on metal. Then it thins, stops, and the ceiling begins building again.

## The Water

Water is everywhere, and all of it is on its way somewhere.

It seeps through the embankment and runs down the inner face in a thousand threads, pooling at the foot in a moat of brown standing water choked with reeds. It rises in the low ground between the engine houses and fills the old cellars of the terraced houses to within a hand of the doorsteps. It condenses on every cool surface, which here means iron. The tram rails sweat, the lamp posts stream, and the great beams drip continually from their undersides, so that each engine house has a line of puddles beneath it matching the arc of its beam. It weeps from the brick, which is saturated through, and the brick has grown soft and fine-grained, like a biscuit left in tea. Press a thumb into an old wall and it leaves a mark.

And the engines lift it. From each pump shaft a cast-iron main rises, crosses the Sump on stone piers, climbs the embankment, and discharges over the top into the marsh outside. The outfalls are the clearest, fastest water in the place: seven thick brown ropes pouring warm over the lip of the wall into foaming pools on the marsh side, loud and ceaseless.

What leaves over the wall comes back through it. The embankment is not watertight and never was. The marsh presses in, the ground seeps, the rain falls, and the engines lift it out again. It is a closed circle, an argument that has gone on longer than anyone can say. The water has not won and the engines have not won. The Sump has settled at a level: wet always, flooded never, and it stays there.

## The Green

In the heat and wet and gloom, things grow.

Moss covers every horizontal surface and most vertical ones, thick and springy as felt, in a dozen shades from almost black to a startling acid yellow. Ferns root in the mortar joints of the engine houses and hang in skirts from the eaves. Fig trees, or something like them, grow out of the chimneys and gable-ends, their pale roots poured down the brickwork like wax, prising the courses apart a hair's breadth each year. Where the tramway ran, the rails are sunk in a green channel of sedges taller than a person. The roofs of the empty terraces have become gardens of their own. Each carries a small overhanging forest of shrubs whose roots have found the soft brick below and are slowly turning the houses into mounds.

The plants are sparse more than dense, though. The Sump is not a jungle. There is a lot of open ground: flats of black standing water, broad beds of moss, stretches of wet cobble where the traffic of the pump rods and the scouring of rain keep things clear. The green gathers on the buildings, on the embankment, and along the piers of the water mains. Between them are wide dim spaces where you can see from one engine house to the next across unbroken, gently steaming water.

## The Absence

The first strange thing is that nothing moves except the machines and the water.

There are no insects: no mosquitoes, no flies, no moths batting at the gas lamps, which in a hot wet place like this is almost unbearable to notice once you have noticed it. There are no frogs in the pools and no birds in the fig trees. No fish break the surface of the moat. The leaves are whole. Look at any of them, a fern frond or a fig leaf or a reed blade, and there is not a single chewed edge, not a single mine or gall or spot of mould left by anything that feeds. Fallen fruit lies where it drops, swelling and splitting and slowly dissolving into the moss. Nothing comes for it.

Something in the Sump keeps animals away. It might be the gas, the vibration, or something older in the ground. Whatever it is, the plants have had to manage without them, and they have found another way.

## The Plants That Listen

They have learned to use the noise.

The flowers of the Sump are pendulous: long drooping bells and tassels hanging from thin, springy stalks, pale cream or dull rust or a faint lamp-glow green. Brush one and nothing happens. But they are tuned. When the ground heaves under the stroke of a nearby engine, they swing, and on the heaviest strokes they shed their pollen in visible puffs, fine golden smoke that drifts in the heavy air and settles on the flowers of neighbouring plants. At the draw, when all seven beams come down together, the entire Sump exhales pollen at once. For a few seconds the dim air glitters, and then it settles.

Different plants answer different engines. The ferns that grow in the engine house walls have fronds that tremble at one particular tempo and hang still at others. Ivy-like vines climb the stone pivot walls and reach out along the beams themselves. Every stroke carries them up into the steam and down again, and over the years the vines have grown hinged, jointed stems that flex with the motion rather than tearing. On the fourth engine, the one with the slowest stroke, a vine has completely wrapped the outer end of the beam. It rises and falls, rises and falls, flowering heavily, and it looks for all the world as though it is doing the pumping.

Seeds travel by water. The plants of the Sump drop them into the drainage channels and pools, and from there they are drawn into the pump shafts, lifted up through the cast-iron mains, and flung out over the wall with the outfall into the marsh. The marsh outside the embankment is thick with these plants too, spreading outward in seven green fans from the seven outfalls, as if the Sump were slowly exhaling itself into the country around it.

## The Machines That Tend Themselves

The second strange thing is that nobody runs the engines.

The engine houses are empty of people. The doors hang open or have rotted off. Inside, it is dark, loud and hot, the firebox glowing, the great cylinder sweating and hissing, the parallel motion grinding through its arc overhead. There are stools and a bench and a rack of long-handled tools, all furred with a velvety orange rust and wrapped in fern. The engines keep themselves.

Close attention shows how, partly. There are floats in the pump sumps, copper globes on iron arms, and as the water rises they lift and open the gas valves wider, and the fire burns hotter and the engine strokes faster. When the water falls, the floats sink, the valves close, and the engine slows almost to stopping. Counterweights, chains and ratchets trip the valves and oil cups and dampers. The machines are governed by the water they drain. They speed and slow with the rain and the seep. That is why no two engines keep time together, and why, when the water rises everywhere at once after the afternoon rain, they converge toward the same frantic tempo and the draws come thick and fast in the hour after the storm.

Not all the self-tending makes sense. Some valve gear seems to trip without any float to trip it. Some oil cups stay full though no pipe feeds them. The tools on the racks are never where they were. It would be wrong to say anything tends the machines. But the machines are tended.

The plants do their part too, in their way. Roots grow into gear teeth and are crushed. Vines tangle the linkages and are snapped. Moss clogs the slide bars and is scoured away. The engines prune the green. The green, in turn, pries at the stonework, softens the brick, and drinks the condensate. Rust blooms, moss follows the rust, and the rust eats the iron beneath the moss. It is all slow and it is all happening. Somewhere in Hethering Low, at any given moment, something is being broken, something is growing, and something is being worn away.

## The Stillness Under the Noise

The Sump is loud, hot, wet, and never still. Yet it has the atmosphere of a place that has reached some kind of settlement with itself.

The brick has gone soft but hasn't fallen. The embankment leaks but doesn't breach. The engines have been rusting for a hundred years and are still pumping. The water rises every day and is lifted out every day. The plants grow into the machines and are cut back, and grow again, and have made themselves around the machines' rhythm the way the plants of other places make themselves around the seasons. It is not a ruin, and it is not a working town. It is something that has continued past the point of anyone meaning it to, and in continuing it has become its own kind of country: small, walled, sunk below the marsh, lit by the ground's gas, humming and thudding and hissing in the warm gloom.

Stand still in the middle of it, on the wet cobbles between two engine houses, and wait for the draw. You'll feel the strokes gathering, the stray beats drawing in toward each other, the pauses getting shorter. Then all seven come down together. The ground jumps and the water shivers. Every leaf lets go its drops at once, the pale bells swing, and the gold pollen goes up into the dim air and hangs there, turning slowly under the steam ceiling, in a place where nothing else alive is watching.
