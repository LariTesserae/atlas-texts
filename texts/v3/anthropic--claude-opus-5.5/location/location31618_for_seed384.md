# Sallow Bottom

## The Descent

The lake above is ordinary. It is a long, grey reservoir in a low valley, with a concrete dam at the southern end and a few sheep-cropped hills that are hardly hills at all. In winter a skin of ice forms along the margins and cracks at night with a sound like distant rifle shots. Nothing on the surface suggests anything underneath, except one detail. On still days there is a faint, slow swirl in the water near the middle of the lake, the kind a spoon leaves in tea. It never quite stops.

Go down through it. The first ten metres are brown-green and grainy with suspended life. Then the colour drains away. By twenty metres there is almost no light, only a dim sense of up. At about twenty-six metres the temperature drops sharply. It is not a gradual chill but a distinct floor of cold, and you pass through it the way you'd pass through a curtain.

Below that line the water is four degrees, the temperature at which fresh water is heaviest. It does not rise and it does not mix. It lies in the bottom of the valley like a lens of cold glass, and inside that lens is a town.

## The Shape of the Place

Sallow Bottom was never large: a mill, a pump house, a gasholder, a chapel, a public house, a short run of shops, and perhaps two hundred back-to-back terraced houses. They were packed into the crook of a river bend so tightly that the whole settlement could be walked end to end in ten minutes. The dam went in downstream, the valley filled, and the town was not demolished. It was simply left.

It is still packed tight. The streets are barely wider than a handcart. Two people standing in the middle of Cable Row could each touch a front door. Between the terraces the old ginnels are so narrow that the walls on either side have leaned together over the years and now touch at the eaves, making tunnels. Overhead, the town's wiring still hangs: tram cables, telegraph lines, the braided wires of the electric lamps, drive belts running between the mill and the outbuildings, and the iron pipes of the gas main strapped along the walls. Somebody's washing line still crosses between two upper windows. There is nothing on it, but it is still taut.

Everything is layered. Roofs sit against roofs. Chimney stacks cluster like reeds. The mill rises five storeys over all of it, and behind the mill stands the chimney, square in section and faced with blue engineering brick. It is so tall that it reaches up out of the cold lens into the warmer, livelier water above. Its top is furred with mussels, and the fish of the upper lake treat it as a reef. Its base stands in a silence they never enter.

## The Materials, Settled

A century underwater has not destroyed the town. It has settled it.

Every horizontal surface carries a layer of silt as fine as flour and as even as snow. It lies thickest in corners and doorways, rounding every right angle into a soft curve, so the town looks as if it has been gently sanded. Window sills have become little dunes. Steps have become ramps. The cobbles of the square are gone under a smooth grey floor that takes a footprint perfectly and holds it for weeks, until the current wipes it away.

The brick has gone dark and soft-edged. The mortar has leached out in places and been replaced, grain by grain, with fine sediment that has hardened into something close to stone. The walls are now in a sense a new material, half brick and half lakebed.

The iron has found its equilibrium. It rusted fast in the first decades and then slowed almost to nothing, because the cold dark water holds little oxygen. Every railing, bracket, and pipe now wears a thick orange-brown crust, and from the undersides hang rusticles: brittle, delicate stalactites of oxidised iron, some as long as a forearm. They sway slightly in the current. A careless hand will shatter one into a cloud of rust that hangs in the water like smoke and takes an hour to settle.

The glass has gone milky and opaque. Most windows are intact. The lake has frosted them from the outside in, so every house looks blind and has a soft, pale face.

## The Dark

It is dark here, but not completely.

At the edge of the square stands the pump house, a squat brick building with arched windows. Inside, behind the milky glass, are three filament lamps that are still lit. They glow a deep, low orange, the colour of embers just before they go out. The light is enough to see by within a few metres of the building and no further. From the square the pump house windows look like three banked coals floating in the black.

Elsewhere the lamps on their iron brackets along Cable Row flicker faintly once or twice a day, when the current is strongest. They glow for a minute, dull and brown, and fade. You learn to navigate by these brief lights, and by touch, and by the faint grey shapes of buildings that the eye can make out once it has been in the dark long enough.

In high summer, on the brightest days, a sickly green light comes down from the surface and reaches the top two storeys of the mill. It never reaches the streets.

## The Machinery Still Runs

This is the first rule of the place: nobody ever turned it off.

The dam's intake pipe takes water from the bottom of the reservoir and feeds the hydroelectric station below the dam. The engineers who built it laid its route along the town's old mill race. Water still flows through the race, drawn by the intake, a slow constant current that runs through the lowest part of Sallow Bottom and out the far end of the valley.

It still turns the mill's turbine.

The turbine turns the main shaft. The main shaft runs the length of the mill on every floor, and from it the leather drive belts, stiff as board now and furred with silt, still loop down to the machines. The looms still work, slowly. A shuttle moves across a loom perhaps once every forty seconds, then back again. On every floor of the mill, in the dark, rows of looms nod and shift in a patient, uneven rhythm.

The turbine also runs a small dynamo in the pump house, which feeds the three lamps and the electric clock in the square.

The current is not steady. The dam's sluices open and close on a schedule set a hundred years ago by a float-and-counterweight mechanism no one has touched since. Twice a day, at no hour anyone living would recognise, the flow through the race rises for about three hours, then drops to almost nothing for about nine. The town has tides, driven by machinery.

When the current rises, the silt lifts from the streets in slow grey plumes, as if the town were exhaling. The rusticles swing. The belts tighten with a shiver that travels the length of the mill. The looms quicken, the lamps along Cable Row flicker, and fine sediment streams down the streets toward the far end of the valley like fog pouring downhill.

When the current drops, everything settles. Silt drifts back down like snow and the town goes still. The looms slow until they seem to have stopped, though they never quite do.

## What the Looms Make

This is the second rule, and the strangest thing in Sallow Bottom.

The looms have no thread. The spindles emptied long ago. But the current flowing through the mill race carries whatever the lake gives it: long strands of filamentous algae from the shallows, fibres of waterlogged wood, horsehair and wool from drowned upholstery, fishing line lost from boats above, root hairs, the fine threads of fungal mats. All of it catches in the race and is drawn up through the grilles and the mill's old intake channels, and some of it snags on the empty heddles.

And the looms weave it.

The weaving is loose and irregular: a grey-green fabric, open-meshed, somewhere between felt and net. It grows at perhaps a hand's width a week. It hangs from the looms in sheets, and when a sheet grows long enough the current tears it free and carries it out through the broken windows of the mill.

The town is draped in its own cloth. The weaving hangs from gutters and chimney pots and drifts along the streets, snagging on railings and the corners of buildings. It lies over the roofs in layers, grey on grey, the old sheets silted and stiffened, the new ones soft and moving. In places it has closed over the gaps between houses entirely, and walking down a ginnel is like walking through a tent. In the square it has piled against the chapel wall in drifts as high as a person, some of it decades old and compressed into something like paper.

Hold a piece of new weaving up to the pump house lamps and you can see the pattern. It is not random. The looms are still set to the last job they were running: a plain twill with a small diamond repeat. It shows faintly in all the cloth, the old sheets and the new, the same diamond again and again.

## The Sound

The town is almost silent. Water does not carry sound the way air does, and there is very little to hear.

Mostly what you notice are pressures rather than sounds. When the main shaft turns, it produces a low throb felt through the soles of the feet and the teeth more than heard. When the current rises, the hum of the turbine comes up through the ground so faintly that you wonder if you've imagined it.

Close to the mill, in the quiet hours, you can hear the looms: a soft, deliberate knock, a pause, another knock. It is almost like someone slowly tapping a table in another room.

The electric clock in the square still keeps time, within a few minutes. Every hour its hands shift with a small click, and a hammer strikes the bell in the chapel tower. The bell is crusted and packed with silt and does not ring. It produces a dull, close thud that carries only a few metres. But the stroke travels through the water as a pressure pulse, and anywhere in the town you feel it on your skin: a gentle push, once for each hour.

Besides this, there is the tick of rust flakes falling on stone. There is the whisper of silt settling when the current drops. Occasionally a crayfish clicks in a gutter.

## What Lives Here

Very little. The cold lens is short of oxygen and food, and most of the lake's life stays in the warmer water above.

Plants barely hold on. In the square a colony of stoneworts has taken root in the silt: pale, jointed, candelabra-like plants, whorled and brittle, that smell faintly of garlic if crushed. They are almost colourless. On the pump house walls, where the lamps give some warmth and the least light, there is a thin rust-coloured film of algae in a rough halo around each window. On the turbine housing, where the current is strongest, freshwater sponges have grown in grey, knobbed crusts that feed on what the water carries past. There is nothing else.

Animals are rare, and each one is conspicuous.

Crayfish live in the gutters and drains, a few dozen of them, pale and slow. They pick through silt at night, though here it is always night.

Caddisfly larvae live in the mill race. They build their protective cases from rust flakes cemented with silk, so each larva carries a little cylinder of orange iron. In the three hours of high current they cling to the grilles by the hundreds.

Something lives in the gasholder, a vast drowned iron drum at the edge of town. It is an eel, and it is enormous. Nobody knows how old it is. It rarely leaves the gasholder. In the quiet hours it can sometimes be seen draped along the rim, grey and motionless, as thick as a man's leg.

Once a year the pike come. In late autumn the surface of the lake cools until it matches the four-degree layer below, and the reservoir turns over. For a few weeks the cold lens mixes with the upper lake, oxygen reaches the streets, and the town briefly takes a breath. The pike come down. They hang in the doorways and at the windows of the chapel, long, green and patient. They feed on the crayfish and the caddis. By midwinter the lake has stratified again and they are gone, back to the light.

## The Town Becoming Itself

This is what Sallow Bottom is now, after a hundred years.

It is not a ruin, and it is not quite a town. It is a settlement that has stopped changing its shape and started, instead, changing its substance. The walls are becoming lakebed. The iron is becoming rust and rusticle, very slowly. The looms are knitting the whole place together in their loose grey diamond twill.

Every year the weaving lies thicker on the roofs. The ginnels close over. Old sheets harden into felt, the felt compresses into something like paper, and the paper silts into something like stone. The drifts against the chapel wall have begun, at their base, to look like shale: thin laminated layers, each one a year of cloth.

The current runs, the turbine turns, the looms knock in the dark. The clock pushes the hours through the water. Three lamps glow like embers in the pump house windows. Above it all, the lake is grey and ordinary, and on still days the faint slow swirl in the middle never quite stops.
