# The Chorus Hollow

You find it by ear before you find it by eye.

The plain around it is baked, flat, the color of old bread crust, with no well or seep for days in any direction. Then there's a pressure in the air, a note so low it seems to come from your own ribs. It grows as you walk until it is no longer a note but a condition. The ground itself hums. Then the plain simply ends in a circular drop, perhaps three hundred paces across, and the sound pours up out of it like heat from a kiln.

Look down and there is a city.

---

## The Bowl

The Hollow is a sinkhole, very old, its lip softened by ages of wind into a smooth, lipped rim like the mouth of a jar. It isn't deep. Forty or fifty body-lengths down to the floor, no more. The pale stone is soft enough to carve with a harder stone and hard enough to hold the carving for centuries.

It has been carved everywhere.

The walls descend in terraces, dozens of them, each one a street. Each street is lined with doorways, and each doorway opens into a room, and each room opens deeper into others. Stairs cut from the living rock switch back and forth between levels. Bridges of fitted stone blocks cross narrow clefts. Chimneys, galleries, arcades, balconies no wider than a sandal. The honeycomb goes back into the rock farther than anyone has mapped. It is unmistakably urban: organized, layered, built by generations who knew each other's work and built on top of it. Newer square-cut doorways sit inside older round ones. Stairs are worn into dips by feet long gone.

And almost all of it is empty.

That is the first thing you understand and the last thing you get used to. The city could house thousands. On a given day you might see four people. The rooms are hollow, the streets are hollow, and the air in them holds nothing but light and noise. The emptiness is not ruin. The stonework is maintained, swept, sometimes freshly recut. The emptiness is deliberate. The city was not built to be lived in. It was built to be *hollow*.

---

## The Sound

Every opening in the Hollow is shaped. The doorways flare or narrow in careful proportions. The window-slits are cut at angles. The rooms behind them are sized like the bodies of instruments. When the hot wind comes off the plain and falls over the rim into the bowl, it crosses ten thousand mouths, and every one of them sings.

The result is deafening in the literal sense. People who stay long lose the top of their hearing within a season. The keepers speak with their hands. Standing on the lowest terrace at midday is like standing inside a struck bell that never stops ringing. Your teeth buzz. Loose grit walks across flat stones in slow patterns. If you press your palm to a wall, you can feel which note that wall is carrying.

The sound is not constant. It is weather.

- **Dawn** is the quietest hour: a deep, breathy drone from the largest chambers at the bottom, where the cool night air slowly drains out.
- **Mid-morning** brings the rising sun and the rising heat. The plain starts to breathe upward, and the middle terraces wake one register at a time, like an orchestra tuning, only never resolving.
- **Midday** is the roar. Thermal wind, full force, every aperture open. Harmonics stack until they produce phantom tones, voices that aren't there, a sound like crowds cheering from somewhere underground.
- **Afternoon** brings the dust-gusts, sudden, swirling, sending shrieks up individual chimneys like birds startled out of them.
- **Evening** is the long fall, the city exhaling as the stone gives back its heat and the air reverses. Visitors say it sounds like mourning. The keepers say it sounds like the city eating.

---

## The Light

The Hollow is bright to the point of pain. The stone is pale and faintly crystalline, and the bowl's shape gathers the sun and throws it back and forth between terraces until there are no shadows worth the name. Even deep rooms glow, lit by light that has bounced a dozen times off polished walls. The builders knew this. Many interior surfaces were rubbed smooth with sand and hide, and certain shafts were cut to carry noon deep into the rock. The effect is of a city lit from inside, every doorway a lamp.

At midday you cannot look straight across the bowl. The far side is a white blaze. You navigate by sound instead, which is its own kind of blindness.

---

## The Gardens

The Hollow is green. Lushly, riotously green, and there is no water.

There is no spring at the bottom, no cistern, no dew. The air is so dry it cracks lips in an hour. Yet every terrace spills over with growth. Vines hang in thick curtains across doorways. Broad-leaved shrubs crowd the streets. Mats of dense, velvety groundcover pad the stairs. Bulbous succulent-things, blue-green and faintly translucent, cluster in the corners of rooms like sleeping animals.

They drink sound.

It took visitors a long time to believe it, but the pattern is plain once you see it. The plants grow thickest where the city is loudest. A doorway that sings well is framed in vines. A doorway that has cracked and gone flat and breathy is bare. Leaves are thin and taut as drumskins and turn on their stems to face the strongest tones, the way flowers elsewhere turn to the sun. Put your ear to one of the bulbous succulents and you'll hear the city's roar inside it, softened, stored, as if it were holding a mouthful of noise the way a cactus holds water.

The roots don't go down. They go *in*, spreading along the stone's surface in fine pale networks that follow the vibration lines. Where the walls ring strongest, the roots are thickest. Some keepers believe the plants and the stone have grown into each other so thoroughly that the city's tuning now depends on them. A vine can dampen a chamber or brighten it. The gardens are part of the instrument.

This produces the Hollow's strangest rule: **silence is drought.** In the rare still seasons, when the plain wind fails for days, the gardens wilt within hours. Leaves go slack and gray. Bulbs shrink and wrinkle. If the stillness lasts long, whole terraces brown and drop their growth, and the dead vines muffle the doorways further, and the city grows quieter, and more dies. It is the one real catastrophe the Hollow knows.

---

## The Keepers

The few people here are called keepers, though they don't call themselves anything, since they rarely speak aloud.

Their tools are simple: chisels of harder stone and a few of soft bronze, bone scrapers, fiber cords, fired-clay plugs and baffles, leather bellows, abrasive sand kept in woven bags. Their work is tuning. They walk the terraces feeling the walls, listening with their hands and jaws, finding the chambers that have gone flat. A crack can be packed with clay. A doorway can be widened by a finger's breadth over a week of patient grinding. A new window can be opened into a dead room to wake it. They prune vines not for growth but for tone.

During still seasons they work the bellows by hand, great leather lungs set into the lowest chambers, pumped in shifts day and night, to keep the most important gardens alive until the wind returns. You can tell a still season is ending when the keepers collapse and sleep where they stand.

Why the city was built, they don't say, or don't know. Some older carvings show the bowl crowded with figures, all with their mouths open. Some show no figures at all, only doorways, repeated, forever.

---

## What Else Lives Here

Very little. The noise drives most animals off, and the dryness finishes the rest.

There is a small, flat, dust-colored lizard, earless, that basks on the loudest walls and seems to be the only creature entirely comfortable here. Keepers say it tastes the vibration through its belly and finds prey that way.

The prey is moths, pale, broad-winged, which feed on the sound-plants' flowers. The flowers open only in the midday roar, and they are shaped like tiny horns. When a moth is inside one, the flower's note changes. On a still, bright afternoon you can hear the moths feeding, a flickering shimmer layered over the city's drone.

Occasionally a hawk circles the rim, drawn by the green, and then veers away as though struck. None has ever been seen to land.

---

## Standing There

What stays with you afterward is not any single sight. It's the combination, which never quite resolves into sense: a city with no people, a garden with no water, a light with no shadows, a sound so large it becomes a kind of stillness. After an hour your own thoughts go quiet, because there is no room for them. After a day you begin to hear the city's moods, the way it strains in the afternoon gusts and settles at evening.

After a week, visitors report, the silence of the outer plain becomes unbearable. It feels like thirst.

Most of them come back.
