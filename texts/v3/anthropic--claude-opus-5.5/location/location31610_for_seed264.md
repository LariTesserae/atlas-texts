# The Workings

The basin opens out low and wide, a shallow bowl perhaps a day's walk across, its floor barely higher than the sea somewhere beyond the eastern rim. Nothing grows here. The ground is stone throughout: dark iron-bearing basalt worn to the smoothness of an old step, crossed by bands of pale sinter and seams of something that glints like pewter. The first impression is of a place long abandoned. The second, arriving through the soles of the feet before the ears have sorted it out, is that the place is running.

It is loud. The noise is not a roar or a storm but a working sound, layered and regular, the sound of a shop floor heard through a wall. There is a deep, spaced *thud* every eleven seconds or so that you feel in your ribs. Over it runs a continuous high hiss, and under everything is a hum so steady that after an hour you forget it, until you step past a certain seam and it cuts out and the silence it leaves is startling.

No one built any of this. There are no walls, no rails, no bolts, no rust-scabbed boilers. The machinery is geological.

## The Hammer

Near the center of the basin the basalt rises in a low dome, a blister of rock perhaps two hundred paces across and only the height of a person at its crown. Beneath it, far down, groundwater meets heat. The water flashes to steam, the pressure builds under the lid of rock, and then it vents all at once through a network of cracks around the dome's rim. The dome drops back onto its bed a fraction of a finger's width, and that is the *thud*.

It has done this for longer than anything living can reckon. The dome has settled into its seat the way a pestle wears into a mortar. Its underside and the rock it strikes are ground into matched faces, and the rim cracks have been widened and smoothed by steam into something like nozzles, clean-edged and slightly flared. Every eleven seconds (it is always eleven, never ten, never twelve) the rim breathes out a ring of white vapor that rises waist-high, thins, and is gone before the next.

Standing on the dome is possible. It is warm through the boots, and at each stroke the whole surface drops beneath you by an amount too small to see and impossible not to feel. People who have stood there long say they began to breathe in time with it without meaning to.

## The Whistling Ground

West of the Hammer the stone is riddled with vents: thousands of them, from pinholes to openings you could put an arm into. Each gives off a continuous thread of steam, and each has been shaped by that steam over uncountable years into a tube of hardened sinter. Wind moving across the tube mouths draws notes from them. There isn't much wind, and what there is always comes the same way at the same strength, so the notes are held and constant, a flat chord of perhaps forty tones that never resolves.

The sinter here is white and grey and faintly pink. It forms in terraces where the vent water trickles down and evaporates, and the terrace edges are thin as porcelain and ring when tapped. The air is warm and wet in this quarter and smells of struck matches and wet slate. Everything is beaded with condensation: every ledge and lip carries a row of drops that swell, fall, and are replaced at an even pace. If you listen close enough, under the whistling, you can hear them landing.

## The Seams

The hum is electrical.

Two kinds of water rise in the basin. From the north come brines heavy with iron and sulfur, orange-tinged and faintly sour. From the south come clear alkaline springs that taste of chalk. Where they meet, along a meandering line through the basin floor, they seep into the same fractured rock but do not quite mix. Between them lie the metallic seams, veins of magnetite and native copper exposed by weathering and polished by water. Two electrolytes and a conductor: the basin is, in the plainest sense, a battery. It is an enormous, slow, natural cell that has been discharging steadily since before there were eyes to see it.

The current is small at any one point but spread across leagues of rock. It makes the seams hum at a pitch just at the bottom edge of hearing. It keeps the metal bright, because nothing can corrode on a surface that current is constantly scrubbing. It gives the air above the seams a clean, sharp ozone edge. On the few overcast days the basin gets, a faint blue glimmer runs along the wettest stretches of seam, visible only if you crouch and shade your eyes. Put a hand flat on the copper where it crosses a damp patch and you feel a tingle, like a limb waking up.

Iron filings, if you brought some, would stand up in soft combs along the seams and point the same way no matter where you set them down.

## The Cisterns

Scattered through the basin, far apart, are pools, though "cisterns" suits them better. They sit in hollows the water carved for itself: smooth-walled, round, and flat-bottomed, some as small as a washbasin and some as wide as a village square. Each is fed from below and drains from below, and each holds exactly its level. The waterline on the stone is a hairline, never a band. Nothing overflows. Nothing dries.

The water is warm, somewhere between blood and bath. In the iron-fed cisterns it is rust-gold, and sunlight goes down into it and turns amber. In the chalk-fed ones it is a milky blue-white that the eye can't see into at all. A few cisterns sit right on the dividing line and are split down the middle, gold on one side and pale on the other, with a boundary that shimmers but never blurs. The hum is loudest beside these.

Mist sits on the cisterns in the early hours and lifts at the same hour every day.

## Weather, Such As It Is

It rarely rains, but when it does it is a warm, thin rain that makes no difference. The stone takes it and gives it back as steam within the hour. Moisture here comes mostly from below: from the vents, from the cisterns, from condensation settling out of the warm air onto the cooler basalt at dawn. The ground is damp in patches and dry in patches, and those patches are the same every day. Travelers learn them the way you learn the creaky boards in a house.

Light is plain daylight, slightly softened by the ever-present haze of vapor, so shadows have gentle edges. The sun crosses the basin, the steam rises into it, and the pale sinter throws it back. Nothing about the light is strange, and that makes the rest of the place stranger.

## Things That Live Here

Very little lives here, and nothing green does.

In the warm runoff channels below the sinter terraces there is a small crab, grey as the stone, with a shell furred in fine mineral crust that it cannot shed. It grazes films of iron-eating microbes off the channel walls. These films are the only true biology on the basin floor: thin, rust-colored, slick to the touch, layered like the pages of a wet book. The crabs are few, a handful per channel. They move very little. They seem timed to the Hammer: at each stroke they all flinch together, then carry on.

At the cisterns, now and then, a heron-like wader comes down from beyond the rim. It is long-legged and slate-colored and always alone. It stands in the gold water with its eyes half-closed, warming itself, and leaves without eating. Nothing in the cisterns is large enough to eat.

Swifts nest in the whistle tubes on the western ground. The steam keeps their eggs warm. You rarely see them come or go, but sometimes a tube's note wavers, drops a half-tone for a moment, and recovers. That is a swift going in.

## The Nature of the Place

What makes the basin uncanny is its steadiness more than its strangeness. Everywhere else, weathering is a story: rock breaks, washes away, and something new forms downstream. Here the erosion finished long ago. Every channel has been cut to exactly the shape its water needs, every vent to exactly the bore its steam demands, and every seat to exactly the fit of the stone that strikes it. The land has worn itself into tolerances. There is no slack left in it, no loose grit or half-formed feature. Everything is fitted.

So nothing changes. The Hammer keeps its interval. The chord keeps its tones. The cisterns hold their levels, and the dividing line in the split pools does not move by a hair in a lifetime. The current runs at one strength. It is loud, warm, wet, and working, and it is also as still as anything can be. It is an engine at perfect equilibrium, turning over forever and producing nothing but its own continuation.

People who spend a night there say the worst of it is not the noise. The worst is waking before dawn, hearing the thud, the chord, the drip, and the hum, and understanding that it was exactly the same when they fell asleep, and will be exactly the same when their grandchildren's grandchildren come to stand on the warm dome and feel it drop beneath their feet.
