# The Laying Gallery

You find it by going down, and then down again, past the point where the city's cellars stop pretending to be cellars. A service stair ends at an iron door with no handle on the outside, only a small brass plate worn smooth. Beyond it the air changes at once. It is colder and wetter, and it smells of lime and wet stone and faintly of hot metal, like the moment after a kettle is taken off the flame.

The place behind the door is not large. It is a single gallery perhaps four paces wide and just over head-height, running away from you into a dim amber haze. At first glance it looks like an old tunnel. It is not old. Nothing here is more than a few years old, and the far end is younger than the hour.

---

## The walls

Everything is made. The floor is cast slab, scored in a grid for traction. The walls are brick, but not fired clay. They are pale grey blocks of poured and pressed aggregate, each the size of two hands laid side by side. The ceiling is a shallow vault of the same blocks, keyed so tightly that the joints are hairlines. There is no bedrock visible anywhere, no natural surface, no seam where the made thing meets the found thing. If there was ever earth here, it has been entirely replaced.

The walls read like a timeline. Near the door they have darkened to the colour of wet slate, and a thin skin of mineral bloom has crept across them. White efflorescence gathers in feathered patches where the lime has sweated out. Walk further in and the blocks lighten. Their edges sharpen and the bloom thins away. Halfway down the gallery the joints still show a faint dampness, darker than the blocks, as if the mortar has not quite finished deciding to be stone. At the far end the walls are nearly white and still faintly warm. Put your palm flat against them and you can feel a slow, living heat, the curing.

This is the "erosion" of the place, run backwards. Nothing is being worn down. Everything is still arriving. The gallery has the raw, unhandled look of a quarry face or a fresh fracture, except that the fracture is being added to rather than opened.

## The face

At the far end is the machine. The people who know of it call it the Layer, or sometimes just *the hand*.

It fills the end of the gallery almost wall to wall, so the gallery seems to taper into it. It is a carriage of blackened iron on two rails set into the floor, and it carries a hopper, a mixing drum, a steam-fed press and a jointed arm with a gripping shoe at its tip. Conduits run back from it along the ceiling, bundled so densely that the vault is half hidden: steam lines lagged in grey felt, cable in braided sheaths, a water main sweating in heavy drops. Every surface near the machine is crowded. Valves sit shoulder to shoulder, and gauges face slightly different directions like a small anxious congregation. Chains hang in loops, and the counterweights are stacked like coins.

The Layer works slowly. The drum turns, and the press sighs down with a long exhalation of steam and lifts. Then the arm swings out and sets a single block into the face of the wall, pushing it home with a sound like a heavy book closed on a table. Mortar is extruded from a nozzle at the arm's wrist in a bead as neat as icing. Then the arm withdraws, and there is a long pause. The drum turns. The press sighs.

One block every four minutes or so, day and night.

It is not building a wall across the gallery. It is building the gallery itself, lengthening it. Each course of the vault is closed and then the carriage inches forward on its rails, a hand's width at a time, pushing the face of the unmade ahead of it. Beyond the machine there is nothing but a dark close pocket of loose, wet, cold fill, which the Layer consumes. A conveyor at its belly draws the fill in, and it is crushed, mixed and pressed into blocks. The gallery eats the ground in front of it and turns it into walls behind.

No one remembers commissioning it. The plans, if there were plans, are not here. The gallery does not run straight. It bends very gently to the left over its length, and the curve is not constant. It seems to follow something, a pressure line or a seam of cold, or a drawing only the machine has.

## The chute

Midway along the gallery, set into the ceiling, is an iron throat about the width of a drainpipe. It is the only connection to anything above apart from the door. Things fall down it, not often but steadily enough: grit and rainwater from some street gutter far overhead, and sometimes stranger things. A button. A coin worn blank. A key. A child's glass marble. Dried leaves, a bent nail, a sugar-paper wrapper, a hairpin, a small porcelain hand broken off a figurine.

The chute empties onto a sloped tray. A slow belt carries whatever lands there down the gallery's length to the Layer's hopper, and everything that goes into the hopper goes into the blocks.

So the walls carry fossils, made on purpose. Look closely at any block and you may find something pressed into its face, half-exposed where the mould released it. There is the ghost-curve of a spoon. A leaf skeleton is rendered in perfect negative, down to the finest veins. A ring of rust bleeds orange from a buried washer. Near the door, where the walls are oldest, these inclusions have begun to stain and weather in the damp. Near the machine they are crisp as a thumbprint in wax.

The gallery is making a geology, a stratum of the city's dropped and forgotten things laid down in order. A patient person could read the last few years of the streets above in these walls, the way one reads sediment. The leaf-fall of three autumns is visible as bands where leaf-prints cluster. One stretch is dense with bottle-caps, from a summer, perhaps, or a festival. A single course holds dozens of small brass buttons, all identical, and it is a mystery what happened up there.

## The weather

The gallery has its own weather, and the weather is a machine too.

The curing blocks near the face are warm, and the Layer breathes steam constantly. That warm, wet air drifts back along the ceiling toward the door, and as it goes it meets colder and colder walls. It condenses. The whole vault slowly beads. Beads grow into drops and drops follow the joints, so the water arranges itself in lines. It traces the grid of the masonry in silver, and each mortar seam becomes a tiny channel.

For most of the day this is just damp. There is a sheen, a cold glaze on the stone, the occasional isolated drip. But the air fills, and some time in what would be the late afternoon above the vault reaches saturation. Then, for twenty minutes or so, it rains.

It is a small, strange rain, falling from a ceiling you could touch. It comes down in fine, evenly spaced threads, because the drops are released along the joints. For a few minutes the gallery is strung with water like a loom. The floor's scored grid fills. Gutters along the base of each wall catch the runoff and carry it back, gurgling faintly, toward the Layer, where a sump draws it up to wet the next batch of mortar. Then the vapour is spent and the rain thins to a drip and stops. The walls go on shining.

Nothing here is wasted. The water that makes the blocks becomes the fog that becomes the rain that makes the blocks. The cycle is closed and tireless, and it is very cold everywhere except within a few paces of the face.

## The light

There is not much. Sodium lamps are fixed to the ceiling at long intervals. They are small caged bulbs, and they give a thick amber light that makes everything look like an old photograph. Between them are stretches of near-dark where the walls are only a suggestion of wet grey. The Layer carries its own lamps, brighter, fixed on the arm, and they swing with it. Every four minutes the far end of the gallery brightens and shifts as the arm reaches out, and the long shadows of the conduits sweep across the ceiling like the hands of a clock.

In the rain the lamps each wear a halo. When the water is falling you can see the individual drops passing through each cone of light, briefly gold.

## The plants

Nothing is supposed to grow here, and almost nothing does.

But seeds come down the chute with the leaves and the grit, and some of them survive the belt. Some even survive the hopper, lodging in the soft mortar at the face instead of in the crushed body of a block. For a few days the fresh mortar is warm, damp and rich with lime-dust and whatever organic slurry the gutters have washed in. In that brief window some of the seeds sprout.

You find them only near the face, and only along the joints. They are threadlike and nearly colourless, the pale yellow-white of things grown without sun. Here is a filament of cress with two tiny cotyledons. There is a grass-blade as thin as a hair, arched toward the nearest sodium lamp. Once in a while something more ambitious appears: a clover's first trefoil, or the curled head of a fern that has somehow made it all this way as a spore. They lean, every one of them, toward the lamps on the moving arm, and some of them seem to follow its swing.

They do not last. As the carriage moves on and their stretch of wall cools, the mortar hardens around their roots and the warmth leaves. They blacken and wither and finally become part of the stone. Further down the gallery you can see their remains as fine dark lines in the joints, a faint pencilling. The walls near the door are threaded with them, thousands of tiny drawings of plants that grew for a week in the wrong place.

The only green in the gallery lives at the moving edge, and it moves forward with it at the pace of the machine.

## The absence

There are no animals. No rats, though you might expect them in a warm tunnel under a city. There are no beetles in the gutters, no spiders in the conduit bundles, no moths drawn to the lamps. The lime and the cold keep them out, perhaps, or the iron door, or something about the ceaseless newness of the place. Every surface is too recent to have been colonised. Nothing has had time to find this room.

This gives the gallery a particular quality of attention. It is a place where processes happen without witnesses. The drum turns for no one. The rain falls on nothing that needs it.

## The sound

It is mostly quiet, which is to say it is quiet in a way that throws every small sound into relief.

The ground note is a low, steady hum from the cabling, felt more than heard. Over it, at intervals, the Layer's cycle goes on. The drum makes a grinding churn. The press gives a long, deflating sigh of steam and a click of pawls. Then the soft, final *thock* of the block set home, and the pause.

In between are the drips, arriving in irregular counterpoint, each one distinct. There is the faint trickle in the gutters. Very rarely there is a rattle high in the ceiling as something comes down the chute. It tumbles, pings against iron, and lands on the tray with a tick, and there is a small, curious silence afterward, as if the gallery were considering what it had been given.

When the rain comes, the sound changes entirely. It becomes a soft, pervasive hiss from every direction at once, remarkably gentle for being so close. It is like standing inside a waterfall made of sewing thread.

## The rules

These are things the few who come here have noticed.

The blocks never repeat. No two are cast from the same material, since what goes into the hopper is never the same, and each one is unique in its inclusions.

The carriage has never been seen to stop. The steam never falters. No one has found where it comes from.

The curve of the gallery changes when the city above changes. It bent sharply once the year a great building was pulled down several streets away, and it straightened after a flood. Whatever the Layer is following, it is related to the weight above.

Things that fall down the chute are never lost. They are only relocated, into the wall, in order.

Anything you set down in the gallery is gradually drawn toward the face, although the floor is level. A dropped pencil will be found, a week later, twenty paces further on. The belt is not the only thing that carries.

The warm end is always the same distance from the door when you arrive, no matter how long it has been since your last visit. The gallery grows and grows, but it never seems to get longer.

---

If you stand at the face long enough, beside the steaming carriage and the leaning, colourless sprouts, and watch the arm reach out and set another block into place, you begin to sense what the gallery is. It is not a tunnel to anywhere. It is a place still happening, a small room in the act of being born, a hand's width at a time. Behind you is its own slowly hardening memory, and ahead of you is the cold dark it has not yet become.

Then the drum turns. The press sighs. A drop of condensation falls from the ceiling onto the back of your hand, cold as a coin, and you realise you have been standing still for exactly four minutes.
