# Lamb's Sluice, Lower Works

## The approach

You come down into it the way water does, by the easiest route and without meaning to. There is a stairwell, iron-railed, with treads worn into shallow bowls by boots that stopped coming a very long time ago. Every tread holds a little water. The rail is cold enough that your hand aches, and it is furred along its underside with a pale rust, soft as moss, that comes away on your palm like pollen.

Forty-one steps. Then a landing, then a door that is no longer a door. It is a rectangle of wet black iron hung open on one hinge, and it has fused to the wall with its own corrosion so that it will never close again. Beyond it the air changes. It becomes thicker, cooler, mineral. It tastes the way a coin smells.

You are standing at the edge of a street.

## The street

It is a real street, cobbled, with a gutter down its center and a kerb on either side. It runs perhaps sixty paces before it ends against a wall of dressed stone. There are buildings along it: five of them, brick, two storeys each, with slate roofs that nearly touch the ceiling of the vault above. There was a town here once, or a piece of one. At some point the city above it rose, or this sank, and a single block of an industrial quarter was sealed beneath the new ground with its sky replaced by brick arches.

The vault is not high. Standing in the middle of the street you could, if you threw a stone, strike the ceiling. It is ribbed with iron girders, and between the girders the brick has taken on a glaze of mineral water: white where it has dried, amber where it is still moving, a slow sheet of seepage that never quite becomes a drip until it reaches the lowest point of each arch. There it gathers, hesitates, and falls.

The drips are the loudest thing here. They fall onto the roofs, the cobbles, the rim of the gutter, and each surface has its own note. Beneath all of them is the gutter itself, which carries a shallow, clear, nearly silent current toward the far end of the street. There the water enters a grated culvert and goes somewhere you can't follow.

The whole place leans very slightly toward that grate. After an hour you can feel the lean in your ankles.

## The light

There is electric light here, which is the first strangeness.

Along the kerbs stand iron lamp standards with glass globes, and in perhaps one globe in four a filament still glows. The filaments are not bright. They are the dull orange of a stove element just switched off, and they pulse very slowly, brightening and fading over the course of several minutes, like something breathing in its sleep. The glass around them is sweating. Inside some of the globes, snails have made their way up through the base and cling to the inner surface of the glass, and their bodies are lit from behind, amber and translucent. You can see the dark coil of their organs.

Nobody has replaced these bulbs. There are no spares, no stores, no workshop. The bulbs are simply the original bulbs, and they have not burned out, because they have never been asked to burn at full strength. The current that reaches them is thin, and it comes from somewhere below.

The windows of the buildings are dark except, at certain hours, one or two.

## The Pump House

The largest of the five buildings stands at the end of the street against the terminal wall, and it is the reason any of this exists. Its doors are twelve feet tall and have rotted to their iron frames, so you can walk straight in.

Inside is the engine.

It is a beam engine, a great rocking iron beam balanced on a central pillar the thickness of an old tree. One end connects to a piston in a cylinder and the other to a pump rod that descends through the floor into a shaft of black water. The beam is perhaps thirty feet long. Its iron has gone the color of a wet slate roof, and the ornamental cast-work along its flanks is softened almost past reading. There are leaves, or feathers, and a date, and a word that might be a name.

The engine is still running.

It is not running as it was built to run. There is no fire and no boiler pressure, and the stoking floor is a dry shelf of ash under a century of silt. But water comes into this chamber constantly, down the walls, up from the floor, condensing out of the cold air onto every iron surface. It collects in a copper cistern above the cylinder, a vessel the size of a small room. When the cistern is full it tips. The weight of the water drives the piston down. The beam lifts. The pump rod rises out of the shaft streaming, and holds, and then, very slowly, as the cistern drains, it descends again.

This takes about an hour.

You will be in the Pump House a long while before you realize the engine is moving at all. You'll notice the angle of the beam has changed against the brick behind it. Then you'll notice a sound so low it is less heard than felt: a single iron groan, rising out of the pivot, that lasts nearly a minute. Then the long descending sigh of the rod going home into the shaft. Then silence for most of an hour.

Linked to the beam by a belt of blackened leather is a small dynamo, and the dynamo is where the light comes from. Each stroke of the engine sends a faint surge of current out along wires strung beneath the eaves, and the lamps in the street brighten a little, then fade. The street's slow pulse of light is the engine's heartbeat, delivered through copper.

It is not clear what the pump is pumping, or to where. The shaft goes down into water that does not seem to fall. The outflow pipe leaves through the wall and doesn't come back. Perhaps it is keeping something dry that no longer exists. Perhaps it is keeping this place wet. The engine doesn't care. It has found an arrangement with the water that it can keep up forever.

## The animals

The place is sparse in almost every way. It has few objects, few plants, and wide empty stretches of wet cobble. Life is the exception. It is everywhere, and it is quiet.

**Eels.** The gutter holds them, and the pump shaft, and the cisterns, and they are the most numerous large creature in the Works. They are grey-green, as long as an arm, with the milky eyes of animals that have lived for many generations without needing much sight. They move against the current up the central gutter in the minutes before each stroke of the engine. Nobody taught them this. They gather beneath the Pump House floor in a slow knotted mass, and when the pump rod rises streaming out of the shaft they rise with it, and some of them slip over the lip into the cistern above and ride the next stroke down. You come to understand that the engine is, among other things, a kind of weir for them, a passage between two waters. They have been using it longer than anyone who built it was alive.

**Cave crickets.** Long-legged, pale, with antennae three times the length of their bodies, they cling to every vertical surface in loose constellations. They are spaced out with odd regularity, as if each keeps a territory the width of a hand. When the light brightens after a stroke they all turn, very slightly, toward the nearest lamp. When it fades they turn back. Walk the length of the street during a surge and you will see ten thousand antennae swivel together, like iron filings responding to a magnet somewhere beneath the floor.

**Bats.** A colony roosts in the roof void of the third building, which was once a counting house. You can see ledgers still, if you climb the stairs, swollen into blocks of pulp. The bats come and go through a gap in the slates. There is nowhere to go, no sky and no exit, but they leave all the same, fly the length of the vault in silence, touch the terminal wall, and return. They hunt the moths that hatch from the gutter weed. Their droppings have built a soft brown dune on the counting house's upper floor, and out of that dune grows most of the vegetation in the Works.

**The rest.** There are white crayfish in the culvert. There are water spiders that live inside the lamp globes with the snails and spin their webs across the lit glass, so that some globes cast a faint netted shadow onto the cobbles. There are rats, but fewer than you'd expect, and they're shy and silver-furred. There are moths with wings like wet paper. There are millipedes along the kerb, slow as clock hands. Something small and quick lives in the hollow iron of the lamp standards and occasionally taps.

Nothing here is loud. Even the bats make no sound you can hear. The abundance is dense but silent, and after a while it begins to feel like being in a crowded room where everyone has agreed not to speak.

## What grows

Very little. There is no sunlight here at all, only the thin orange pulse of the lamps.

What does grow is pale. Around each lit lamp there is a ring, perhaps a yard across, of a fine green-white moss on the cobbles. It is thickest directly beneath the globe and thins outward. The moss rings don't touch each other, so the street is patterned with them like a slow set of footprints. Between the rings the stone is bare and black.

In the counting house the bat guano feeds a stand of fungi on long translucent stems. They lean, all of them, toward the window facing the nearest lamp. There are ferns, stunted and ghost-colored, growing out of the mortar of the Pump House wall exactly where the engine's warmth reaches. The engine is warm, barely, from friction. It's the warmest thing for a mile, and the ferns know it.

In the gutter there is weed, long and hair-fine, streaming in the direction of the current. The eels rest in it. Out of it hatch the moths.

That is nearly all.

## The windows

This is the other strangeness, and the one people don't mention when they talk about Lamb's Sluice, if they talk about it at all.

The buildings are empty. Their floors are silted and their furniture has decayed into shapes. But some of their rooms still have electric lights, wired to the same dynamo as the street, and those lights come on at particular times.

They do not come on at random. The window of an upstairs room in the second building brightens every eleventh stroke of the engine and stays lit for three strokes. A ground-floor room in the fourth building lights for a single stroke, then goes dark for nineteen. There is a pattern to it, and if you stayed long enough and kept a careful tally, you might find that it has the shape of a working week. It looks like shift changes. It looks like a foreman's office opening in the morning, a mess room at midday, a watchman's lamp at night. The engine's slow strokes have become the hours of a day that no longer happens anywhere else.

The wiring was built to a timetable, perhaps. Some clockwork relay somewhere, still clicking over. Or perhaps not. The crickets, at least, have learned the timetable. When a window is about to light, they begin to gather on its sill.

Go into one of those rooms when it lights and you'll find it exactly as empty as the others. There is a chair, reduced to a suggestion of a chair. There is a tin cup in the silt. The bulb overhead glows its sleepy orange, and the damp in the air glitters around it, and for a moment the room feels occupied. It is not haunted, but it is used, the way a path is used, by something that doesn't need to be there to keep it open.

Then the engine groans. The rod sighs down into the shaft. The light fades, and the room goes back to being brick and water.

## The rules

What seems to be true here:

The water always wins, but slowly. Every edge in the Works is rounded. Every kerb is bowed. The cobbles are domed and polished like river stones. The water is not destroying the place; it has already finished doing most of that. It has worn everything down to the shape it prefers, and now it simply maintains it.

Nothing is repaired, and nothing fully fails. The bulbs dim but do not burn out. The engine slows but does not stop. The leather belt has not rotted because it is always wet, and it has not stretched because it is never asked to carry much. Everything has settled into the lowest effort at which it can go on existing. The whole Works is in a state of patient equilibrium, and it feels less like a ruin than like a machine that has figured out how to idle for ten thousand years.

Life follows the current. The eels follow the water through the pump. The crickets follow the light. The moss follows the lamps. The ferns follow the engine's faint heat. The bats follow the moths. Everything alive here moves along a gradient the engine creates, and the engine is driven by the water, and the water comes from the city above, which none of the animals have ever seen.

Time is kept by the beam. There is no day or night down here, only the slow tilt of iron and the pulse of the lamps. If you stay long enough you'll stop counting in hours and start counting in strokes. After a while the strokes begin to feel like breath, like something vast and patient at rest, breathing once an hour through iron lungs, while all of this small wet quiet life lives inside the rhythm of it.

## Leaving

Going back up the forty-one steps, you'll notice the drip-notes behind you begin to rearrange. It isn't that they change. It's that you've learned to hear them now, and they have a shape. They are clustered around the arches in patterns that, like the windows, seem almost to make a timetable.

At the top of the stairs the air warms, and thins, and takes on the smell of the city. Somewhere below, the beam has reached the top of its arc and is holding there, poised, the cistern emptying drop by drop. When it falls, you won't hear it.

But for a few days after, you will find yourself pausing, about once an hour, as if listening for something.
