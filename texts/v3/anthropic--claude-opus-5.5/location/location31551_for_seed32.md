# The Holding Room

You would not find it by looking. The plain above is a low, pale country of gypsum and wind-scoured limestone, barely a few meters above the dry basin it slopes toward. Nothing on it stands higher than your knee. The entrance is a crease in the ground, a slot between two tilted slabs where the surface seems to have folded in on itself. You hear it before you see it. Standing on the plain you might take the sound for a trick of the wind, a hum lower than hearing that arrives through the soles of the feet. Stepping down into the crease, it becomes enormous.

## The room

The room is small, no larger than a threshing floor, perhaps twelve paces across and roughly oval. Everything in it has been placed.

The walls are dry-laid stone with no mortar, no dressing, and no chisel mark anywhere. Each stone was chosen for its shape rather than cut to fit, and the fitting is so close that a blade of dry grass could not be slid between two courses. The builders, whoever they were, worked with what the plain gave them: gypsum in slabs and knuckles, white vein-quartz, a few rounded cobbles of grey flint that must have been carried from somewhere else, since none lie on the plain now. The courses lean inward as they rise, each ring overhanging the one below, until they meet a ceiling of thin translucent sheets. This is selenite, the clear crystalline gypsum, laid in overlapping scales like the underside of a shell.

The light comes through those scales. The room is very bright, far brighter than a hole in the ground has any right to be. The selenite takes the full sky above and scatters it, and the white walls take it again and throw it back, so the light has no direction. There are no shadows. Your hand held up casts nothing. The stones have no dark side. This is the first thing that unsettles visitors, more than the noise: an evenness that makes every surface equally present and flattens depth, so the far wall seems close enough to touch and the near wall seems to recede.

## The packing

There is no floor to speak of, because the floor is full.

Every surface that could hold a stone holds one. The ground is covered edge to edge with stacks: small cairns, knee-high or lower, set so close together that their bases touch and their upper stones lean into their neighbors for support. Where the stacks meet the walls they merge with them, and it is hard to say where the architecture ends and the offerings begin, or whether there was ever a distinction. Ledges in the walls hold rows of pebbles. The gaps between the overhanging courses hold pebbles pressed in sideways. A few of the selenite scales above have small stones resting on them, visible as dark flecks against the glow.

The only path is a narrow way of flat stepping-slabs that winds from the entrance to the center. It is barely a foot wide, and the stacks crowd it on both sides. Walking it means turning sideways, placing each foot exactly, and holding your arms in.

At the center, where you would expect some altar or pit, there is only more of the same: a denser knot of cairns, taller and older-looking, fused at their edges.

## The weathering

This place is old in the way things become old when nothing happens to them for a very long time.

The stones are not broken. They have softened. Every edge has been rounded by the slow grit carried in on the draft, and every stone that touches another has worn into it a little, so the stacks have settled into shapes that could not be taken apart without damage. They behave less like piles than like single objects now, each cairn a knobbled column of stones grown into one another. The gypsum has taken a waxy polish at the height of the wind's passage. The quartz has frosted. The flint cobbles alone keep their darkness, and they have been smoothed so thoroughly that they shine faintly even in shadowless light.

Nothing has fallen. Of the thousands of balanced stones there is no rubble and no toppled stack. Whatever instability they once had was spent long ago. They are finished.

## The sound

The sound is the room's true substance. The stone and the light only hold it.

Set into the walls, at every height and course, are holed stones: flints and chalky knuckles with natural perforations, the kind some people call hag stones, found where softer material dissolved out of a harder shell. Hundreds of them. None were drilled; each was found and kept. The builders placed them with their holes facing through the wall, into the ground outside.

Air moves through the ground here. The plain is riddled below with fissures and dissolution channels, and a steady draft passes from the higher country to the basin through the rock. It never stops and never gusts, a slow breath of the land. At the room it finds these hundreds of apertures and passes through them all at once.

Each hole sounds a note. The small ones whistle high and thin, the large ones moan, and the flint ones ring with a glassy edge. Together they make a chord that fills the chamber completely. It is loud enough to feel in the chest, loud enough that speech is useless and a shout vanishes into it like a stone into sand. The walls hum with it, and the stacks hum. Lay a palm on any stone and it is vibrating, faintly and endlessly.

What makes it strange is that the chord does not change. It does not swell or fade, and no note drifts. It is one sustained sonority, the same today as in whatever year the last stone was set. After a few minutes the mind stops hearing it as sound and begins to hear it as a condition, like pressure or temperature. Some visitors say it resolves into a voice if you stay long enough. Others say it resolves into silence, a silence so full it is indistinguishable from the roar.

## The cold

The draft that makes the sound also makes the cold. It comes from deep in the rock, where the temperature is the temperature of the ground, and it is the same in every season: a cool, mineral, cellar air that smells of chalk and dry stone and faintly of struck flint. Above, the plain may bake or freeze. Down here it is always the same coolness, the chill of a spring morning that never warms, never deepens, and never reaches frost.

There is no water. There has never been water here. The air is so dry it pulls moisture from the lips. No seep darkens the gypsum, no condensation forms on the selenite, and nothing drips. The whole room is a single dry breath.

## What grows

Life here is spare and flattened against the stone.

Lichens cover the upper faces of the stacks, pale as the rock itself: grey-green crusts, a dusty sulfur-yellow, a white leprose bloom that looks like a dusting of flour until you see it has edges. They grow only where the light is strongest, on the tops and outer shoulders, and they grow so slowly that a patch the size of a coin may be older than the stack beneath it.

In a few joints of the walls, where dust has gathered in a crack over centuries, there are cushion plants: tight silver-green domes the size of a fist, each a mass of tiny leaves packed so densely it feels like pumice to the touch. There are perhaps a dozen in the whole room. They do not seem to flower. Along the stepping-path, in the seams between slabs, a few blades of dry grass stand upright. They are the colour of straw and entirely still in the draft, too stiff to move, too dry to rot.

That is all. There are no mosses, which would need wetness, and no seedlings. Whatever grows here has been growing for a very long time and has reached its size.

## What does not live here

No animals. No insects cross the stones and no spiders web the corners. No droppings, no bones, no feathers blown in. Birds on the plain above give the crease a wide berth. Perhaps it is the sound, a pressure small bodies cannot bear. Perhaps it is simply that there is nothing to eat and nothing to drink. The absence is complete and conspicuous. Once you notice it, the room feels less abandoned than deliberately cleared.

## The rule of the place

The room's one law, if it has a law, is that it does not change.

The light does not move, because it comes from the whole sky at once and the selenite erases the sun's passage. Morning and afternoon look the same. Only full night dims it, and even then starlight and the pale stone keep a faint, directionless glow. The sound does not change, because the draft does not change. The cold does not change. The stacks do not fall. The lichens do not visibly spread. Everything has settled into an equilibrium so complete that time seems to pool here instead of passing.

Those who come here, and a few do, with no clear reason and no tradition anyone can name, bring a stone. They carry it along the narrow path, turning sideways between the stacks, and set it somewhere: on a ledge, in a gap, on top of a cairn. Nothing else is done. No words are said, because none could be heard.

And the stone stays. Within a season, the dust of the draft has begun to settle into the contact where it rests. Within some years it has worn a small seat for itself. Given long enough it becomes part of the cairn, then part of the room. There is no room left, and yet there is always room for one more. The packing absorbs it the way the chord absorbs a shout.

Climbing back out into the flat glare of the plain, the silence of the open air is the loudest thing you have ever heard. The hum stays in your bones for hours. Behind you the crease looks like nothing, a fold in the ground. Below it, the room goes on holding its single note, its single light, its thousands of stones, exactly as it was.
