# The Lantern Cistern

You come to it by sound first. Long before the passage opens, while you are still in the narrow stair cut down through limestone, the rock under your palm is humming. The deeper you go, the more the hum separates into parts: a low roar underneath, a hiss riding on top, and between them a tonal wobble like breath blown across the mouth of an enormous jar. By the last turn of the stair you have to shout to be heard, and there is no one to shout to.

Then the stair ends in a lip of stone, and there is light.

---

The chamber is not large. You could cross it in sixty paces, and its ceiling is perhaps the height of a village chapel's. But it is so full of brightness and motion that it seems to have no edges. Light comes from the center, from a point about two-thirds of the way up the air, and it is the white of noon on snow. It is too bright to look at directly, but it is not hot the way a flame is hot. It is warm the way a body is warm, and so is the whole chamber: the air is thick and close, the stone sweats, and your clothes are damp within a minute.

Water climbs toward the light.

From a round pool in the floor, maybe twelve paces across, a column of water rises in a slow, thick spiral. It is not a fountain. Nothing pushes it. It twists upward like rope being wound onto an invisible spindle, glassy and clear near the base and whitening as it rises, until at the top it tears into spray around the light-source. The spray then falls back down as rain over the whole chamber, uneven, gusting, and loud. The roar is the rain. The hiss is the column. The wobbling tone, you learn later, is the chamber itself.

The thing at the top is called the Wick, by whoever named it. You can only see it in glimpses when the spray thins: a knot of something that is not quite metal and not quite glass, the size of a clenched fist, with facets that seem to face more directions than a solid object should have. It does not hang from anything. It is simply there, in the air, and the water goes to it the way iron filings go to a magnet, and comes away from it spent.

---

**The stone remembers.**

The oldest thing about this place is not the Wick but the shape the Wick has made. Every surface in the chamber is fluted. Water, falling in the same patterns for a very long time, has carved the walls into vertical grooves as regular as organ pipes, some shallow as fingers and some deep enough to stand inside. Where the rain falls hardest, near the pool, the floor has worn into concentric terraces, low rimmed steps of flowstone that hold shallow water in overlapping crescents like the scales of a fish. Farther out, near the walls, the terraces dry into ridges of pale, mineral-crusted stone, soft and rounded, polished almost to porcelain.

Nothing here is sharp. Nothing is freshly broken. The erosion has gone on so long that it has reached a kind of truce: the stone is shaped exactly to the water's habits, and the water runs exactly where the stone lets it. Drains at the base of the walls, natural or once cut and long since smoothed, swallow the runoff and return it to the pool from below. The circuit is closed. It may have been closed for ten thousand years.

The flutes are why the chamber sings. As the spray-wind gusts, it blows across hundreds of stone grooves of different depths, and each one sounds. Mostly they blur into the roar. But sometimes the wind steadies and a chord surfaces, huge and unresolved, and holds for several seconds before the turbulence breaks it apart. Standing near the wall, you can feel the pitch in your teeth.

---

**The Wick is not steady.**

It is tempting to think of it as a lamp, but it behaves more like weather. The column wanders. It leans toward one wall for an hour, drenching that side of the chamber while the other dries. Then it swings, sometimes slowly and sometimes in a lurch that sends a sheet of water slapping across the terraces. The light pulses with it: bright, brighter, a flare that throws every flute into black relief. Then comes a dimming to the gold of late afternoon, when for a moment you can see the Wick's facets turning.

Every few hours there is what the marks on the walls call the Breath. The column thickens. The pool drops visibly, a hand's width, then an arm's, as the Wick draws more and more water into itself. The rain stops. The silence that follows is shocking; it lasts perhaps ten heartbeats, and in it you can hear drips, your own pulse, and a very high whine from the Wick. The light goes white-violet. Then everything it has taken comes down at once. It is not rain but a collapse, a warm deluge that floods the terraces, overtops the rims, and sends a knee-deep surge racing out to the walls and back. The chord in the flutes goes wild. For a minute the chamber is pure noise and foam.

Then the drains catch up, the column resumes its lazy spiral, and the rain settles back to merely very loud.

Near the center, the rules bend slightly. Drops falling close to the column fall slower than they should, drifting like snow. Shadows there come in pairs, one from the Wick and a second, fainter one cast in the opposite direction, as if the light also arrives from somewhere behind you. Water flicked upward from a hand sometimes hesitates at the top of its arc for a beat too long. None of this is dangerous. It is simply how things are, the way the chamber is warm.

---

**What lives here.**

Very little, and all of it strange in small ways.

On the outer terraces, where water pools shallow and the light is strongest, there are mats of algae in bands of color: copper-green nearest the water, then rust, then a pale lilac on the dry rims. The bands move over the seasons, if there are seasons here, following the column's wanderings. In the deep flutes, where spray condenses but the deluge rarely reaches, grow small ferns, no more than a dozen clumps in the whole chamber. Their fronds are almost translucent, veined with white, and they all turn slowly to track the Wick as it drifts, the way sunflowers track the sun.

In the pool live shrimp as clear as the water, visible mostly as the shadows they cast on the terraced bottom. They swim in loose orbit around the base of the column, never entering it. After a Breath they are briefly everywhere, stranded in the overflow, and they make their way back by flicking from crescent to crescent in the dark between pulses.

And there are birds. They are swifts, or something like them, small, slate-colored, with long scythe wings. You may see one or two in a day. They nest high in the flutes, and they hunt the insects that hatch from the algae mats. What makes them unusual is that they fly *into* the column. They ride the spiral upward, wings folded, and burst out at the top into the spray and light, then dive. No one knows why. It may be play. They do it most often just before a Breath, as if they can feel it coming.

---

**What others left.**

People have been here, though not many, and not recently.

Iron has been set into the rock in a few places. It is not quite iron, though: dark gray, unrusted after all this time, as smooth under the finger as wet soap. A line of these rungs climbs one wall to a narrow ledge. On the ledge stands a single small structure, a hut of fitted stone with a roof of the same gray metal, curved to shed the rain. Inside there is a stone bench and a niche. In the niche someone has left a row of lenses, dozens of them, from thumbnail-size to palm-size, cut from rock crystal and some other clear material that warms in the hand. They are arranged in careful order, largest to smallest. Maybe they are offerings. Maybe they are spares.

Low on the east wall, just above the drains, is a device fused into the stone. It is a panel with a curving slot and a bead of light that slides along the slot as the column moves. It has been tracking the Wick all this time. Next to it, scratched into the flowstone and half-buried now under new mineral crust, are tallies: hundreds of short marks in groups, then a gap, then a different hand's marks in a different style, then another gap. Beneath the oldest tallies, and furthest overgrown, is a single word in a script no one here could read. It has been carved so deeply that the water has had to go around it.

On the pool's rim, one of the terraces has a groove cut into it, a perfect square notch through which a thin stream runs to a small, separate basin. The basin is dry and cracked. The notch has long since silted up. Whatever that water was for, it is no longer being used.

---

**What it is for.**

It is easy, after a few hours in the roar and the heat and the white light, to think the Wick is the point of this place: a lamp, a machine, a relic someone lit and left.

But look at the flutes again. Look at the terraces, the drains, the smooth bowl of the chamber itself. None of it was dug. The chamber has the shape of something *made by* the Wick, not *made for* it. The Wick lifts water and drops it, lifts and drops, unevenly and restlessly, and the water carves. The chamber is the Wick's handwriting in the rock, the record of every swing and every Breath, laid down over ages. The room gets very slightly larger every year. The flutes deepen by the width of a hair. Perhaps the Wick was set here to hollow out this space, and it has never been told to stop. Or perhaps the space is simply what it leaves behind, the way a river leaves a canyon.

Either way, it is not finished. The light swings, the column leans, the swifts ride the spiral up into the brightness, and the whole chamber, loud and warm and streaming, keeps being made.
