# The Drowned Works at Saltcombe Pit

The first thing you notice is that the light comes from the wrong direction.

Far overhead, the surface of the pit is a faint lid of green, the colour of bottle glass held up to an overcast sky. It is thirty metres up, perhaps more, and what little daylight reaches down arrives tired and grey and gives up somewhere around the level of the chimney tops. Below that the light comes from the street lamps, and it comes sideways.

They still burn. Iron standards line the single cobbled street at intervals of twelve paces, each with a glass lantern-head gone milky with a century of film. Inside, the filaments glow a low sodium amber, never bright, like embers seen through a closed eyelid. Every surface in the town is lit from the side and a little from below, so shadows fall upward and outward. Doorways and gutters are bright. Eaves and ceilings are dark.

The whole place fits inside the pit. The walls are grey limestone, fluted and softened, and they curve in overhead like cupped hands. You could walk from one end of the town to the other in four minutes if the water let you walk. It doesn't, quite. It is warm, as warm as a bath that was drawn an hour ago, and heavy, and it holds you the way a crowd holds you: gently, from every side.

---

## The Street

Everything is packed together. The town was built by people who had very little ground and a great deal of work to do, and they put everything close. Terraced houses stand back to back and shoulder to shoulder, two storeys of brick with slate roofs, and the alleys between the rows are the width of one person's shoulders. Above the alleys, cables and pipes cross from wall to wall in such numbers that looking up is like looking into the underside of a net. There are telegraph wires, steam lines lagged in rotted felt, speaking tubes, drainpipes that now drain nothing, and heavy braided conduits that still carry current. When your hand brushes one, you feel a faint buzz.

The bricks have lost their corners. After a hundred years the water has worn every edge into a soft curve, so the houses look slightly melted, like soap that has been used. Mortar lines have become shallow grooves, and silt has settled in the grooves, and things grow in the silt. The ironwork rusted until the rust itself grew a stable crust. Railings, boot scrapers, and the doors of coal holes all wear rust like dark bark, knobbly and furred, and the rusting has more or less stopped. It reached an understanding with the water long ago. Copper downpipes have gone the deep blue-green of old roofs, and in places they are so thickly crusted with verdigris that they look like coral.

The windows are the strangest part. Most still have their glass, and the glass has grown a film on both sides, a thin living varnish of algae that is gold-green where it faces a lamp and nearly black where it doesn't. Every window is a stained-glass window now, though nobody designed the pattern. Where a pane is lit from behind by a lamp in the next street, the film shows its structure: branching veins, rosettes, tiny spirals. The film is slowly composing something.

## What Grows

The vegetation is lush, and it is organised entirely around the lamps.

Growth that would normally reach for the surface doesn't. The daylight up there is too thin to matter, and the plants have forgotten it. They reach for the amber filaments instead. Every lamp standard is the centre of a small, dense garden. Long ribbon-weed, brown-gold and translucent, roots in the gaps between cobbles and streams toward the nearest lamp. It leans at an angle, never straight up. It grows along the power conduits too, following the current as if it can taste it. Some of the ribbons are four or five metres long and have looped around the cables so many times that the wires look braided with hair.

Around the base of each lamp there is a cushion of moss so deep your hand sinks in to the wrist. It is dark olive, and near the glass it lightens to a pale lime. The lamp-glass itself is crowded with small stalked things, clustered close as spectators at a window, each tilting its tiny head toward the filament.

Between the lamps, in the dark stretches, other plants have adapted differently. Doorsteps grow pale feathery fronds that barely photosynthesise and seem to live mostly on what drifts past. Gutters are choked with a waterlily-like plant. It roots in the silt along the kerb and sends its stems up to the underside of the eaves, where the leaves spread flat against the slate. There they have found the air.

---

## The Air Rooms

The works at the bottom of the street still makes something, and what it makes is gas.

The works was an electrochemical plant: long brick sheds of electrolysis cells, a turbine hall, and a boiler house built over a seam where the ground runs hot. No one tends the boilers. The heat comes up through the rock on its own, as it always did. The water in the boiler house is noticeably warmer, almost uncomfortable, and it rises by convection through the old flues. The slow rising current turns the turbines, very slowly, one revolution every few breaths. The turbines make a trickle of current. The current lights the lamps and runs down the old bus-bars into the electrolysis cells. In the cells the water splits, patiently, into bubbles.

The bubbles rise, and the town catches them.

Every upper room, attic, and underside of an eave has a pocket of trapped gas. From below, these pockets look like ceilings of liquid mercury: silver, trembling, and perfectly flat, reflecting the lamplit street back at itself upside down. When you swim up a stairwell into an old bedroom, you find the top third of the room gone mirror-bright. The iron bedstead is reflected in it, and so is the washstand, and so are you, looking up.

The lily-leaves have grown into these pockets and breathe there. Their undersides press against the water and their upper faces lie in the gas, and they have made little inverted meadows under the roofs. Plants have grown into the meadows in turn: small creeping things, pale as bean sprouts, that live their whole lives in a hand's depth of trapped air, upside down, sustained by lamplight coming up through the silver.

The main chimney of the works still smokes. The smoke is a column of bubbles, and it rises in a slow, loose, silvery braid from the chimney's mouth, a steady exhalation that thins out toward the green lid far above. It is the only thing in the town that moves upward with any conviction.

---

## Sound

The town is very nearly silent.

What sound there is comes through your bones rather than your ears. The turbines produce a hum so low it is more a pressure than a note, like the sensation of standing near a very large animal that is asleep. Relays tick in the switch-boxes on the walls, click, then a long wait, then click. They still route current from street to street according to a logic nobody remembers. The bubbles make the smallest sound of all as they reach the eaves: a faint *tk* as each joins the pocket above, soft as a fingernail on a teacup.

Once per cycle the shift whistle blows. The whistle is steam-driven, and the steam forms in the hottest part of the boiler house and collapses almost at once in the cooler water, so the sound doesn't carry as a whistle. It carries as a long, dull, descending groan, felt in the sternum and heard mostly as an absence of the ordinary hum. Then the lamps change.

---

## The Cycle

The lamps are on a clock, and the clock has drifted.

Somewhere in the works a master clock regulates the town's lighting in shifts: a bright period for working, a dim period for sleeping. The clock runs a little slow, perhaps a few minutes each day. Over a century those minutes have added up, and the town's day has slid free of the surface day entirely. Up above it might be noon. Down here it might be the long amber dim of the night shift. Now and then, over months, the two line up. For a few days the faint green from above and the full working glow of the lamps arrive together, and the water brightens by a shade that is only just noticeable. The plants flush with new growth at those times. The growth rings in the ribbon-weed stalks, if you cut one, show a long, slow beat: thin, thin, thin, thick. The thick rings mark when the two clocks agreed.

Everything here happens slowly. The ribbon-weed grows perhaps a finger's width per cycle. Silt settles into corners at a rate you could measure only over a year. The biggest event in the town is when an air room overfills.

An attic's pocket of gas grows over weeks and months, a few bubbles at a time. It deepens and pushes the silver ceiling lower, and the upside-down meadow gets more room to spread. Then, eventually, the pocket's edge creeps down past the lintel of a window, or the lip of a broken slate, and the gas finds a way out. It leaves all at once, as a single enormous bubble, wobbling and pale, the size of a room. It rises slowly past the chimneys toward the surface and takes a long time to go. The lily-leaves that were floating in it fall back into the water, limp and startled. Within a few cycles the cells have begun to fill the room again.

People who have seen it say it looks as if a house breathed out.

---

## Fauna

Animals are rare here. You can spend a whole shift in the town and see nothing move that isn't plant or bubble.

There are a few. A kind of snail lives on the rust crusts, dark and ridged so that it is almost indistinguishable from the iron it grazes. You notice it only because a patch of railing is in a slightly different place than it was an hour ago. In the boiler house, where the water is hottest, there are small blind shrimp, colourless, with long trailing feelers. They cluster around the flue mouths as if warming their hands.

There is also one fish, or perhaps several, since nobody has ever seen more than one at a time. It is long and pale and moves with an eel's patience. It lives at the edges of the air rooms, just beneath the silver, and it seems to feed on whatever falls from the inverted meadows. When it drifts through a lamp's glow you can see its gut through its skin. When it passes beneath an air pocket, its reflection swims above it, upside down, perfectly matched. It is the only creature that seems to know the town, and it follows the same route every cycle: up the street, through the chapel window, around the works, and back.

---

## The Chapel and the Works

At the upper end of the street, where the pit wall curves in closest, there is a small chapel of the same brick as everything else. Its pews are still in rows, furred over with moss so that they look like long green loaves. Its roof holds the largest air pocket in town. The inverted meadow up there is thick and luxuriant and faintly luminous where a bus-bar runs along the ridge beam. Lying on the chapel floor and looking up, you see the whole interior doubled in the silver: pews above pews, the pulpit hanging downward, the lamplit door opening onto a street that hangs from the ceiling.

At the lower end is the works. Its sheds are long and low and packed tight with machinery: rows of cells in glazed ceramic tanks, copper plates gone blue, switchboards with brass knife-switches frozen at half-throw. A conveyor runs the length of the main shed and still moves, powered by the same slow turbines. It carries a centimetre a minute, perhaps less. It carries whatever settles on it, which is silt, fallen weed, shed snail-shells, and the fine white flakes that drift down from the air-room meadows. It carries all this to the end of the shed and tips it into a hopper that overflowed long ago. Beside the hopper a heap has grown, soft and pale and conical, taller than a person, perfectly regular. It is the only thing the works still visibly produces besides air and light, and it has been growing, one grain at a time, for longer than anyone has been counting.

---

## What the Place Is

It is a town that kept its appointments after its people stopped keeping theirs.

The heat comes up through the rock. The turbines turn. The lamps burn their amber. The cells make their bubbles and the houses hold them in their attics and the plants grow toward the lamps and into the air and the relays tick and the whistle groans and the conveyor carries its grains to the heap. None of it is quick. None of it is loud. It is dense and warm and dim and full, every surface occupied, every gap between the houses threaded with cable and weed. It is all held in the patient water like a ship in a bottle, a small, crowded, working world that has settled into its shape and intends to stay.

Now and then, when the clocks line up, the water brightens by a shade. The whole town, weed, moss, and silver ceilings, leans very slightly further toward its lamps.
