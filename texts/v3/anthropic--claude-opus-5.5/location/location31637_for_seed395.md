# The Mound Country

## The Ground

Begin with the ground, because nothing else here makes sense without it.

It is ash. Not soil, not sand, not anything a river ever touched. A few thousand years ago, which is yesterday in the life of land, the crust split along a seam longer than most coastlines. For a century it breathed out fine grey tephra and low sheets of basalt. The fissures closed. What they left behind is a basin the size of a continent, barely above the level of a sea that lies somewhere beyond the horizon and has never sent a single cloud inland to break.

The ash has not been worked. No water has sorted it into silt and gravel, and no stream has cut a channel into it. Pick up a pinch and look closely: the grains are still glass shards, sharp-edged and faceted, as crisp as the day they cooled. Where the basalt breaks the surface it shows its original ropy skin, the frozen wrinkles of flow, unsoftened. There are no valleys. There are no ridges except the low blister-lines where old fissures sealed. The land has had no time and no agent to become anything but what it was poured as.

It is almost perfectly flat, and it would be featureless, except that it has been built on from one edge to the other.

## The Mounds

The builders are termites. The shapes they make are the only relief this country has.

Stand anywhere and you are standing among towers. They are two, four, sometimes eight times the height of a person, fluted and buttressed. Their spires are pierced with chimneys, and their sides are thick with the ribs of old repairs. They are the colour of the ash they are made from, mixed with saliva and fungal paste and baked by the sun into something closer to fired clay than dirt. Some rise alone as needles. Most have fused. A single mound grows a shoulder, the shoulder meets its neighbour's, the gap between them roofs over, and the roof becomes a gallery. Across whole regions there are no separate mounds at all, only one continuous architecture: arcades, domes, and ventilation stacks running unbroken for days of walking.

There is no gap to rest in. Every open patch of ash, every slope of cooled basalt with a crack to grip, has been claimed. Colonies abut colonies. Their outer walls are shared the way row-houses share walls, and the boundaries between them are contested in slow, quiet wars. The builders make everything. Where the architecture thins, the land does not open into wilderness. It is simply where one city's suburbs are still being laid down, fresh grey courses of pellet-work still dark with moisture at the edges.

If you could see it from high enough, the pattern would look like a city grown under a microscope: dense cores, radiating avenues, and a vast honeycomb of districts. It has no plan, only the logic of ten trillion small bodies obeying heat, humidity, and each other.

## The Rule of Water

This is the law the whole country runs on: **nothing here drinks.**

There is no standing water anywhere. Rain has not fallen on most of the basin within living memory, not even the memory of the oldest tree. Yet the place is green, thickly and improbably green, because water here is not a thing that falls. It is a thing that is made.

The termites farm fungus deep inside the mounds, on combs of chewed plant matter. Metabolism releases water: every molecule of sugar burned by termite or fungus gives up a little. Multiply that by the population of a continent. The interior of every mound becomes humid, saturated, faintly warm, a damp heart sealed inside a hard shell. The mound breathes through its chimneys, and on cool nights the exhaled air carries that moisture up and out. It condenses on anything that touches it.

So the plants grow on the mounds, into them, and around them. Their roots are threaded through the walls to drink the inner damp. The builders tolerate this, and in places depend on it, because the roots bind the walls and the leaf litter feeds the fungus combs. Between the ash, the insects, the fungus, and the green, the water circulates in a closed economy. Almost none of it is ever loose.

Every animal eats its water. It comes out of prey, seeds, sap, or the fat bodies of termite queens-in-waiting.

## The Green

The vegetation is lush, but it is the lushness of hoarders.

The dominant trees are succulents. Tall candelabra forms rise in grey-green columns, their ribs fattened with stored sap, and they stand shoulder to shoulder with the spires they grow from. Bottle-trunked trees swell like wineskins. Vines with thick, waxy, coin-shaped leaves trail over the arcades and hang in curtains across the gallery openings. Low mats of fleshy groundcover, jade-coloured and purple-tipped, fill every ledge the builders leave bare.

It is not a forest in the usual sense, because there is no forest floor. The trees root into the architecture, so the canopy follows the shape of the city. It is highest over the old central cores, where the mounds are tallest and the damp is richest. It thins over the new suburbs, where the walls are still raw and only lichen and the first creeping succulents have arrived. From above, the green and the grey interleave like a mosaic. In a sense each colour is holding the other up.

Leaves here are thick and slow. Nothing wilts, because nothing can afford to. In the heat of the day the stomata close, the leaves tilt edge-on to the sun, and the whole green country goes still and patient. At night the pores open and the plants breathe in. Where the mound exhalations meet cold leaves, a fine dew beads along the edges: tiny, precious, and gone within an hour of dawn into a hundred small mouths.

## The Air and the Weather

The basin is so large that it makes its own weather, and all of that weather is dry.

By mid-morning the ash has heated and the mounds have heated. The whole continent of pale, sun-baked architecture begins to shed thermals. The air above the cities shimmers and lifts. By early afternoon the lift has organised itself into columns, and across the horizon towering clouds build, white and anvil-headed, hundreds of kilometres of them. They look like storms.

They are storms in every way but one. The rain that falls from them evaporates before it reaches the ground, hanging beneath the cloud bases in grey veils that trail and fade halfway down. That is virga, rain that exists only in the sky. Beneath it the air cools suddenly and drops, and a gust front runs out across the mound-country, lifting ash in long pale curtains. Dust devils walk between the spires. Sometimes there is dry lightning: silent, from this distance, a branching white flicker inside the cloud and occasionally a stroke to ground. When it strikes a dry vine-curtain, a small fire starts. It usually burns out against the damp walls, and it clears space that the builders immediately fill.

In the evening the clouds collapse. The sky goes deep and clear. The temperature drops fast, and the mounds begin their night-exhalation, breathing out the day's heat and moisture in slow, invisible plumes.

It is warm. It is not scorching. The mounds moderate it, the green shades it, and the dry air lets heat escape at night. It is warm the way the inside of a bread oven is warm an hour after the fire has gone out.

## The Quiet

For all of this, the country is hushed.

The ash swallows sound. It is porous, glassy, full of tiny voids, and a footstep lands on it like a footstep on snow. Thick walls and dense green break up the wind, so it hisses faintly at the chimney-tops and makes no sound at all lower down. The succulents do not rustle; their leaves are too heavy. The thunder of the dry storms rarely carries this far. The towering clouds work in pantomime.

The builders make no sound you can hear from outside. Press your ear to a mound wall and you will catch it: a faint dry ticking, like sand pattering on paper. That is soldiers striking their heads against the gallery walls in alarm, a signal passed through the architecture itself. Beneath it runs a softer continuous whisper, the sound of millions of feet in tunnels. Take your ear away and it is gone.

The birds here are mostly quiet birds. There is a soft churring near the communal nests, and the whirr of wings passing. At dusk, when the nuptial swarms rise, there is a sound almost below hearing: a vast, faint rustle, like a whole continent exhaling through its teeth.

## The Animals

It is crowded with life, and all of it is in motion, even when the motion is slow.

**The swarms.** On certain evenings after a dry storm's cool downdraft, the whole basin lifts. Winged termites pour from exit-slits that workers have opened in the mound walls, millions from each colony. They rise in glinting columns against the sunset, and from a distance the city seems to be smoking. It is the most important moment in the life of the country, and everything that eats is waiting for it.

**The takers.** Swifts and bee-eaters sweep through the columns in sheets. Small falcons stoop into them. Geckos crowd the exit-slits and snap the emerging fliers before their wings have unfolded. Spiders string nets across the gallery gaps in anticipation. Frogs are absent entirely, since no frog could survive here, but a species of burrowing lizard fills the same niche. It rises from the ash on swarm nights and gorges until it can barely move. Then it goes back underground to live for months on its own fat.

**The diggers.** There are long-snouted, heavy-clawed animals, some armoured in overlapping scales, some bare-skinned and pig-bodied, that break into the mounds at night. They tear a hole, lick up soldiers and workers with long sticky tongues, and move on before the colony's defense becomes too bitter. The builders repair the hole by morning. The breach and the repair leave a scar in the wall, a slightly different texture and colour. The older mounds are patchworks of these scars, a record of a thousand years of feeding.

**The ants.** The termites' oldest enemies move in raiding columns tens of metres long. They are red-brown rivers flowing silently along the arcade roofs, pouring into a breach, and flowing out again carrying the dead. When a column meets a column from a rival ant colony there is a war, fought in total silence, that can last for days and leave a gallery floor ankle-deep in severed bodies. The quiet is not peace.

**The weavers.** Small sparrow-like birds build communal nests in the crowns of the tallest succulents. They are huge thatched structures with hundreds of separate chambers opening downward, as heavy as a cart and as crowded as a tenement. This is a second city lodged in the first. Snakes climb up into it. Falcons perch on its roof. Pygmy owls take over the vacant chambers.

**The browsers.** Long-legged antelopes pick along the avenues between the mound-blocks. They are small, fine-boned, and never seen to drink, because they get everything they need from the succulent leaves and the night dew. In the densest districts there is barely room for them to pass. Their paths through the architecture are the nearest thing the country has to roads, and the builders sometimes wall them off overnight.

**The nocturnals.** After dark come jackal-sized hunters, bat-eared foxes listening for beetles in the leaf litter, and scorpions glowing faintly under the stars. Moths are drawn to the night-blooming flowers of the bottle trees. Those flowers open for a single night and are pollinated by bats, and the bats roost by the thousand in abandoned mound chambers.

## The Edges and the New Land

Toward the edges of the basin, where the last fissures closed most recently, the country is still being made.

There the ash lies bare, grey, and new. The first mounds are small and far apart, only knee-high domes, the first pioneer colonies. Lichen crusts the basalt in orange and pale green rosettes. One or two young succulents have rooted at the base of each mound, their roots already probing for the damp inside. There is space here, the only space in the whole country, and it is filling visibly. The crews are working, the courses are wet at the edges, and a generation from now it will be a city.

This is a country with no ruins. There has not been time. Nothing has worn down; everything is still going up. The land will not be carved by water, because there is no water to carve it. It will be carved by the builders, and by the plants, and by the diggers, in a slow, dry, silent, crowded work of construction that has only just begun.
