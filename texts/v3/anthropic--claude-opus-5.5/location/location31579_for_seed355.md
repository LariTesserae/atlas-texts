# The Precipitate City

It begins as a flatness. A wide, low basin under a sky with nothing in it, no moon, no stars, no cloud you could see if there were any. The air is the temperature of skin, then warmer. It sits on the body like a damp cloth and does not move. Underfoot the ground is soft, a silt of pale mineral that gives slightly and then holds, like wet sugar that has begun to set. Water stands everywhere in shallow sheets no deeper than a finger. It is warm too, warmer than the air, and faint threads of vapor lift from it and hang, going nowhere.

You would not call it dark at first, because a kind of seeing still happens. The mineral crust gives back a weak grey luminescence, too faint to be called light. It is more like the memory of light retained in a stone that sat all day in the sun, though there has been no sun here, possibly ever. By it you make out shapes at a distance. Low ones, then taller ones. Walls.

## The Buildings

There is a city here. Nobody built it.

The structures rise from the basin floor the way travertine rises around a hot spring: layer on layer of precipitated stone laid down by warm, mineral-heavy water welling up from below. The stone is cream-colored where it is fresh and amber-grey where it is old, and it has the fine banded grain of something deposited over a very long time. Put a hand on any wall and you feel it faintly wet, faintly warm, and very slightly *rough-then-smooth* in a repeating rhythm, like the ribs of a shell.

The shapes are wrong for geology. Springs make terraces, rimstone pools, domes, lumpy chimneys. These make *architecture*. There are walls with plumb verticals. There are lintels over doorways, and the doorways are about two meters tall and one wide, a size that implies a body. There are staircases, their risers a consistent hand-span high, climbing to landings that turn at right angles and continue. There are arcades, colonnades, and small roofed porches with a single step up. Windows are set at eye height, though no eye has been here to set them by.

Nothing is carved. Look closely at a column and there is no tool mark, only the growth-banding, curving smoothly around its circumference as if the water had known in advance what shape it was making. Where two walls meet at a corner the bands on each side flow continuously around it. It was all laid down at once, grain by grain, as a single intention.

The city is large, and it is empty in a way that feels designed. Buildings stand far apart, a hundred paces, two hundred, sometimes much more, with broad aprons of wet mineral flat between them. Taken together these are plazas: vast, faintly concave squares that hold a skin of standing water and reflect nothing overhead. A single building might have one room or twelve. Some are only a façade: a wall with a door and two windows, standing alone on the flat, nothing behind it. Walk through the door and you are on the same open ground you left, a little warmer.

## Erosion and Balance

The city is old, and it is not getting older in the usual way.

The same water that deposits the stone also dissolves it. Wherever the warm sheets run, over sills, down stairs, through the gutters that exist at the base of every wall, they carry mineral away as well as bringing it. Everything is being made and unmade at once, and over a long time the two processes have come into a balance so close that the city holds its shape. The shape is not frozen. It is *sustained*, a standing wave in stone.

You see it in the details. Stair treads are cupped in their centers, worn exactly as if by generations of feet, but it was water that wore them, pooling in the middle of each tread and slowly lifting the stone away. The edges of every doorway are rounded soft, the way thresholds are rounded by hands. Old walls have the look of old walls everywhere, slumped a little, their corners blunted, their bases thickened with a skirt of newer deposit. In places a wall has thinned so far that it is translucent, a dim amber membrane you could push a finger through. A pace away, a new one is rising from the floor, knee-high, still sharp-edged, the beginning of a room.

The city migrates. It does so very slowly, too slowly to see in a lifetime, but the evidence is everywhere: ghost foundations, low rectangles of mineral in the flats where buildings used to be, their outlines softening back into the basin floor. The city is not a place so much as a *weather*, a pattern that holds while its material flows through it.

## Water

The water table is right at the surface. Everywhere you step, the crust weeps. Walls sweat. The underside of every lintel carries a fringe of drops, each hanging until it lengthens and falls, and in falling leaves a tiny increment of stone behind. Over centuries these fringes grow into pale curtains, and in some older doorways the curtains have almost closed. The entrances are slowly being sealed shut by their own dripping.

The warm water moves in shallow tidal pulses. There is no moon to pull it. Every few hours, by no clock you can find, the sheets across the plazas deepen from a finger to a hand, and water flows along the gutters, down the stairs, and out through the doorways in thin quiet streams, all in the same direction. Then it slackens and draws back. The city seems to be breathing through its water.

Some channels run in closed loops. You can follow a gutter out of one building, across a plaza, along a colonnade, and back into the same room, never going downhill and never uphill, and the water keeps moving the whole way. It has no source and no outlet. It simply circulates, as if the city had a pulse and needed to keep it.

The heat rises from below. Stand still and you feel it through the soles of your feet. In a few places there are openings in the plaza floors, round and smooth-lipped like wells, where the water is almost too hot to touch and the vapor stands up out of them in pale columns. Nothing burns. The air stays thick and heavy and close, full of mineral smell: chalk, iron, a faint egg-edge of sulfur, and something clean underneath like wet slate.

## The Reflections

This is where the place stops pretending to be only strange geology.

Kneel by one of the standing sheets of water in a plaza and look into it. Above you there is nothing. The sky is a blank, black lid. But in the water there is a *dim, wavering light*, weak and brownish-gold, like late dusk through smoke. By that light the reflected city is visible: the same walls, the same colonnades, the same stairs, seen upside down and faintly lit.

The reflections are not quite accurate.

In the water the buildings are closer together. The long empty plazas are shorter, and there are more walls, more rooms, doorways where in the air there is only open ground. In some reflections the windows are lit from within, very faintly, a warm glow at eye height. You cannot see into them. You can see that the light is there.

Sometimes, in the reflection, a door is open that you know is closed, or closed that you know is open. Sometimes a stair in the water continues past where the real stair ends, climbing toward a landing that does not exist in the air above you. If you walk to that spot and look up, there is nothing, only darkness and hanging vapor. If you look down, you are standing in the reflected landing, on a stair that is not there.

The water does not show *people*. It never shows anyone. It shows light and density and the layout of a city that is fuller, more intimate, more inhabited, and it shows it only in the water.

One way to understand it is that the reflections are not showing what is. They are showing what the water *intends*. The deposition follows the reflection. Where a wall appears in the water and not in the air, a low sharp ridge of fresh stone is often rising from the floor. Watch a single pool for a long enough time and you might see the real city slowly turning toward its image, the way a plant turns toward light. The difference is that the image keeps changing, and the stone can never quite catch up.

## Vegetation

There is life, a little. No animals of any kind: no insects, no birds, no fish in the warm pools, not even a crawling thing in the silt. The stillness has an extra quality to it, the stillness of a place where nothing has ever needed to hide.

What grows grows from the heat. Along the edges of the hottest gutters there are mats of something between an algae and a bacterial film, thin slick skins in dark ochre and bruise-green. In the faint mineral glow they look nearly black, and when you press them they give off the smallest trace of luminescence, a greenish smear that fades in a breath. Some of the hot wells are ringed with pale reeds, a few dozen stalks at most, thin as wire, white where the mineral has crusted them, standing absolutely still in the unmoving air. On the oldest walls, in the dampest shadows (which here means everywhere and nowhere), a lichen-like bloom of rust and grey spreads in slow rings, and in some places the rings have the same banded rhythm as the stone beneath, as if the lichen had learned the grain.

That is all. There is less life than the heat and water would allow, as though the basin had room for more and simply never filled it.

## Sound

It is quiet. The quiet has a grain to it, made of small wet noises: the tick of a drop falling from a lintel into a pool, the thin trickle of a gutter during a high pulse, a soft bubbling from a hot well, like a pot at a very low simmer. Your own footsteps make a flat, muffled sound, a light *tck* of crust giving and resetting.

The strangeness is in how sound travels. Sometimes a drop lands and you hear it before it falls. You hear the tick, then look up and see the drop still lengthening on the lintel, and a moment later it drops silently into the pool. At the edges of the largest plazas, sound sometimes arrives from the wrong direction entirely. A trickle you know is on your left seems to come from the building across the square on your right. Stand in certain doorways and you hear, very faintly, what might be the sound of the reflected city: a muffled continuous murmur, like a room full of people talking softly in the next building over. Step out of the doorway and it stops.

## The Rules

The longer you stay, the clearer it becomes that the place follows rules, though not the ones you arrived with.

**Distance is not fixed.** Two buildings that were a hundred paces apart can be three hundred on the way back, or twenty. Nothing seems to move. The flats themselves stretch and contract, slowly, the way the water rises and falls. The city is breathing in space as well as in water.

**Inside is often older than outside.** Enter a small building whose walls look fresh and sharp from the plaza, and inside the stone is deeply worn, the stair treads hollowed almost through, the corners slumped, the drip-curtains in the doorways nearly closed. Some rooms are older than the city could possibly be.

**Rooms repeat.** Occasionally you walk through a doorway into a room you have already been in, one that was a quarter-mile away and facing a different direction. The pools on its floor still hold the ripples you made the first time.

**Darkness is not uniform.** In some places the dark is so thick it has texture, a soft pressure against the eyes and skin, and walking through it is like walking through warm water that doesn't wet you. In others the faint mineral luminance is almost strong enough to read by. These zones drift slowly across the basin like weather systems, and nothing seems to cause them.

**The city expects someone.** This is the rule underneath all the others. Everything here, the doors, the stairs, the windows at eye level, the cupped treads, the worn thresholds, the lit windows in the water, is scaled and shaped and worn for an inhabitant who has never existed. The city is not a ruin. There was never anyone to leave it. It is an *anticipation* that has been going on for a very long time, patient and warm, precipitating itself around an absence.

## The Edges

The basin goes on past what you can see in the dark. At its farthest reaches the buildings grow sparser, then stop. Only the ghost foundations remain, then only the flats, and eventually the standing water deepens into a shallow warm lake with no visible far shore. At the edge of that lake the reflections are strongest. The lit city in the water extends out across the surface, farther than the real city ever reaches, dense and golden and full of windows, with streets and bridges and towers climbing downward into the dim-lit depths of the image. Over the real lake there is only the black sky and the unmoving air.

At the edge of that water, knee-high and sharp and still warm, a new wall is beginning. It has a doorway in it, and the doorway faces the lake.
