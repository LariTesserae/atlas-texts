# The Level

It begins as a horizon that will not end.

From any point you might stand, and there is no point different enough from any other to call it a place, the ground runs out flat in every direction until the curvature takes it. It is not quite ground. It is floor: cast plates, each roughly the width of a small room, joined edge to edge by a seam of rivets. The rivet heads are domed and bright, their edges still crisp as if struck this morning. The plates are grey-brown iron with a faint bluish temper-sheen. Over all of them, everywhere, lies a skin of warm water about the depth of a fingernail.

The water does not flow. It sits on the plates in a single continuous film, and the film holds the sky.

## The Floor and the Film

The film is the first rule of the Level, and the one you learn through your feet. It is always the same depth. Where it gathers in the slight dish of a plate's center it is a little deeper, and where it crosses a seam it thins to a shimmer. It never pools past ankle height and never dries. It is blood-warm, the temperature of a bath someone drew an hour ago and forgot. Walking through it makes a sound like slow lips parting, and that sound is one of the loudest things here.

It comes from above, not from any spring. The air over the Level is saturated. It holds so much moisture that it beads on every cold surface and runs down every pipe, stanchion, and girder in beaded threads. Weeping is the right word for how the structures look. Iron sweats, and the sweat falls, and what falls spreads into the film. From the film the heat draws it up again, invisibly, at exactly the rate it falls. You could watch a single bead roll down a column for your whole life and never see the floor gain or lose a hair's depth.

A second, thinner skin lies on the iron beneath the water: a pale mineral scale, off-white, in patterns like frost ferns or the veining of leaves. It is the nearest thing the Level has to vegetation, and it isn't alive. It is salt and lime left by evaporation, flowering in the same fractal habit that living things use because the mathematics is the same. In places it grows thick enough to feel like felt underfoot. Nothing eats it. It does not spread. Each plate has its own pattern, and each pattern is, as far as anyone could tell, finished.

## What Stands on the Floor

The Level is not empty. It is flat in the way a city is flat when seen from very high up.

Across its whole extent stand the works. From a distance they rise in low, dense ranks, none very tall. The highest are perhaps ten stories, and most stand at two or three. Everything clusters close, so that from the floor you walk through corridors and courts rather than across open ground. There are boiler houses with curved iron flanks, riveted like the plates. There are pump stations whose pistons rise and fall inside glass-windowed cylinders. There are long sheds under sawtooth roofs, and inside them belts run on rollers the width of a street. Gantries cross overhead, trusswork laced so densely that the sky above some courts is a lattice. Pipes run everywhere: along the floor, up the walls, across the gaps between buildings at every height, in bundles of dozens. They are copper, iron, and something like ceramic, lagged in places with a grey fibrous wrap that drips.

Cables run with the pipes: braided, tarred, sagging in long catenaries from porcelain insulators. They carry current. You can feel it more than hear it, as a faint pressure in the fillings of the teeth near the larger conduits, and in the way the hair on the forearm lifts beneath the main lines.

Every edge is sharp. Nothing is worn. Stair treads keep their knurling. The teeth of the great gears are cut clean, their faces bright where they mesh. Rivets have not rounded, and the plate edges have not rusted through. The paint on the railings, which is green or oxblood depending on the district, has not chipped. It looks like a place finished the day before yesterday, and it has looked like that for as long as there has been anything to look.

This is the second rule. The Level replaces itself at exactly the rate it wears. Somewhere in the sheds, parts are cast, turned, and fitted, and somewhere wear takes them away. The two are so perfectly matched that no surface ever shows its age. The replacement can't be caught happening. You might notice, over months of attention, that a particular bolt head is very slightly brighter than its neighbours. But its neighbours were never duller before, and the brightness never fades. The erosion is always young because it is always just beginning.

## The Heat and the Weather

It is warm. It is not hot enough to hurt, but it is hot enough that the body stops trying to hold itself apart from the air. Skin stays damp, clothing clings, and breath feels thick. The heat rises off the boilers and furnace halls, off the water film, and off billions of small living bodies. Because the Level is continental, stretching thousands of miles in every direction, the rising heat makes weather.

About a mile up, the moisture condenses into a flat, even cloud deck that covers the whole sky. It is bright white-grey, lit from above, and it gives a light like the inside of a pearl. Few shadows fall under it, and those that do are soft and blue at the edges. It is ordinary daylight, generous and clear enough to read by, but it has no direction. You couldn't tell east from the look of it. The ceiling never breaks, never darkens, and never shows a sun.

The heat does not rise evenly. Over the largest furnace districts it climbs in columns, and where it meets the deck it pushes the cloud up into slow domes. Beneath those domes, rain falls. It falls in the same places always, in standing curtains that hang from the ceiling to the rooftops like grey muslin, a few miles wide, motionless at their edges. People who have seen them call them the pillars. They are landmarks, the only ones the Level has. A pillar stands where it stood before, raining at the same rate, with the same soft hiss as its drops strike iron. Walk into one and you are drenched in warm, heavy drops. Walk out and within a hundred steps you are merely damp again. The pillars are the reason the gutters exist. Wide channels run between the works and carry the pillar-rain out across the Level. It spreads thinner and thinner until it becomes the film.

There is no wind to speak of. The air moves in the slow convective breathing between pillar and plain, a drift you notice only because the midge-clouds lean with it.

## The Fauna

The Level is full of animals, and nothing else about it prepares you for that.

They are everywhere, at every scale and in every layer, so densely present that the eye stops sorting them after the first hour.

**In the film** live the tinkers: small crabs, thumbnail-sized, copper-brown with a verdigris bloom along the carapace seams. There are so many that the water seems to crawl. They move sideways across the plates in endless drifting sheets, picking at the seams between rivets with fine claws. No one knows what they take from there. Step anywhere and a few hundred of them part around your foot, closing behind it like water. Their movement is the Level's most constant sound: an extremely faint, dry, fizzing tick, like frying at a great distance.

**In the gutters**, where the water is deep enough to swim, are the lamprey-fish. They are pale, eyeless, and as long as a forearm, and they hang in the current with their mouths fixed to the channel walls. They cover the walls completely, a lining of soft white bodies, all facing upstream, all swaying at the same slow rate. When a pillar's rainfall pulses slightly (it has a rhythm, about once a minute, like breathing), the whole gutter lining ripples in unison, mile after mile.

**On every vertical surface** are the snails. They are small and glass-shelled, and the iron shows faintly through them, so a pipe coated in them looks frosted. They follow the weeping threads down the columns, grazing on something in the condensation, and they are so numerous that the bead-trails are paved with them. Some pipes carry snails on snails on snails, a crust three deep. The crust does not grow thicker.

**In the air, close**, are the midges. They are gnat-small and wing-bright, and they rise off the film in clouds that hang at head height or just above, turning slowly in the convective drift. A cloud might be the size of a house, or it might fill an entire shed from wall to wall. Walking through one is like walking through warm smoke that makes no sound. They do not bite. They settle briefly on skin, lips, and eyelashes and lift away. In the directionless light, a midge-cloud glints like mica suspended in water.

**Higher, among the gantries**, the swifts nest. They are small, dark, and sickle-winged, and they are uncountable. Every crossbeam of every truss has its row of mud-cup nests, made not of mud but of the white mineral scale, scraped from the plates and cemented with spit. The swifts feed on the midges. They pour through the upper lattices in streams that part and rejoin around the pipes, never colliding. From the floor, looking up through a gantry-roofed court, you see the lattice of iron and, threaded through it, a second lattice of moving birds, finer and faster. They do not cry out. Swifts elsewhere scream; these don't. Their wings make a sound like tearing paper, softly, very far off.

**In the sheds**, on the great belts, ride the herds. These are the strangest animals, and the ones that make the Level more than a factory with vermin. They are rounded, grey, and hairless, somewhere between a seal and a pig, the size of a large dog, and they lie on the conveyors in rows. The belts carry them slowly through the sheds and out, across the courts on raised runs, into other sheds, and on. The animals doze. Their flanks rise and fall, and their skins bead with the same condensation as the pipes. Now and then one slides off at a junction and waddles down a ramp to the film, where it lies soaking with the tinkers climbing over it. After a while another climbs on somewhere else. The belts never stop and are never empty. Where they go, whether they are being carried to something or simply carried, nobody has determined. The belts form loops, and some of the loops may be continental in circumference.

**Beneath the plates**, audible at certain seams if you kneel and lay an ear to the iron, something moves in the dark: a slow sliding, very large, or very many. The rivets over those places are sometimes warm.

The ecology closes. Snails graze the weep, tinkers pick the seams, midges rise from the film, and swifts eat the midges. The swifts' droppings fall into the film and feed whatever the lampreys filter. The herds ride and soak and ride. Nothing starves, and nothing multiplies past its place. When a swift dies, and they do, falling limp from the lattice into the water, the tinkers take it down to bright bone within an hour. Before its body is gone, a new swift has fledged from a nest somewhere and joined the stream. The numbers are not approximately stable. They are exact.

## The Quiet

For a place full of engines and animals, the Level is astonishingly hushed.

Partly the machinery makes little noise. The bearings run in oil baths and the gears mesh without chatter. The pistons in their glass cylinders rise and fall with a soft sigh of equalizing pressure. Current hums only where the lines are thickest.

Mostly the quiet comes from the air and the bodies. Sound here moves through saturated warmth, and the thick air takes the edge off everything. It lands on surfaces cushioned with snails, in courts roofed with birds, across water carpeted with crabs. Everything that might ring is muffled. A dropped wrench makes a thud. A shout doesn't carry past the next shed. What remains is a layered near-silence: the fizz of tinkers, the pillar-hiss in the distance, the paper-tearing of swifts, the slow lip-sound of your own feet in the film, and under all of it the faint electric pressure of the lines. After a day, you begin to hear your pulse. After a week, you begin to hear the Level's pulse, the once-a-minute swell of the pillars and the lamprey-ripple that answers it. After that, you are keeping time with it without meaning to.

## The Stillness

The last rule is the hardest to believe, and it holds all the others together. The Level does not change.

It moves constantly, all of it: belts, pistons, swifts, midges, crabs, rain, beads of weep. But the motion is circulation, not change. Nothing drifts. The film stays at its depth, the pillars stand in their places, and the cloud deck holds its height and brightness. There is no evening. The pearl light never dims, and it never brightens toward a noon. There are no seasons, no breeding flush, no die-off, no migration. The midge-clouds turn and remain the same clouds. The mineral ferns on each plate are complete. A bolt, when replaced, is replaced with the same bolt.

Every gradient that could drive anything has been run down to flatness and held there. Heat enters from the furnaces and leaves through the ceiling at the same rate. Water rises and falls at the same rate, and so do life and death, casting and wear. It is a machine for staying exactly as it is, running at full power, forever.

The strangeness is not that it works. The strangeness is how it feels to stand inside it: warm, damp, in soft sourceless light, surrounded by more living things than you have ever seen, all of them busy, all of them quiet. Slowly it becomes clear that none of this is heading anywhere. The Level is not waiting for anything. It was not begun and will not be finished. It is simply, entirely, and continuously made. Every sharp new rivet, every crab, every standing column of rain is exactly where it should be and will be there again tomorrow, which will be indistinguishable from today.

Most who come here stop noticing the difference between a minute and a year. The tinkers part around their feet, the swifts pour through the lattice overhead, and the belts carry the dozing herds on into the next shed and the one after that.
