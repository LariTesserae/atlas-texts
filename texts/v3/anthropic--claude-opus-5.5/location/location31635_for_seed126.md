# The Lathe

## First Sight

It looks, at first, like a salt flat.

A pale surface runs off in every direction with no break, under a sky so full of light it seems overexposed. It is almost level. The horizon isn't a line. It is a place where the ground's brightness and the sky's brightness become the same, and you cannot say where one stops. There are no mountains. The heat is dry and steady, like standing near an oven door someone has left ajar, and it comes up from below as much as down from above.

Kneel and the resemblance to salt ends. The ground is not crystalline. It is *worked*. What looked like a crust is a close, even stubble of structures. There are millions of them, each about as wide as a thumb, packed so tightly that their edges touch. Some are hexagonal and some are fluted. Some have hollow cores, and some split at the top into two or three smaller forms. They stand about a finger's length above a base that is harder to see. Every one of them has been rounded, blunted, and polished by an age of wear. You are looking at the stumps of something, and the something was once tall.

The whole place is like this. There is no natural ground anywhere. Nobody has found bedrock, or soil, or any layer that was not put there on purpose.

## The Depth of It

Surveyors call it the Lathe. The name comes from how its surface looks, turned smooth, as if some patient tool had spun the continent against a blade for longer than stars usually burn.

If you cut down into it, which is very hard to do, you find strata, and every stratum is architecture. Just under the stubble lies a compressed layer of lattice: struts and nodes crushed flat, with the geometry still readable, like a pressed flower. Under that is a layer of what may once have been rooms, flattened into sheets thin as paper and stacked a thousand deep. Under that are channels, hundreds of them in parallel, packed with a fine grey powder. Under the channels there is more lattice, and more rooms, all the way down, until the heat makes going further impractical.

A city was built. It wore down, and a new city was built on its flattened remains. That one wore down too. This happened again and again, and nobody knows the number of cycles. Now the heap of cities has been compressed into a plain less than a few hundred meters above whatever lies under it all. The Lathe has almost no elevation. Its age is enormous. It spans a continent and may span more, since no expedition has reached an edge.

## The Light

The brightness is not just sunlight. The sky has a sun, a white disc that is hard to find in the glare. The ground also gives off light. It isn't glowing exactly, but it is luminous the way paper held against a window is luminous. The polished tops of the stumps carry light down into the material. The lattice layers scatter it, and it comes back up diffused and warm-toned. It feels as though the whole plain is lit from inside.

Because of this there are almost no shadows. An object standing on the Lathe is lit from below as well as from above. A person walking across it looks strangely flat, and faces lose their depth. Travelers learn to wear hoods to feel their own shape again. The only true darkness is inside your mouth, and under your feet where your soles block the ground-light. You leave a moving patch of dimness wherever you step.

At night the sun goes down and the ground keeps shining. It gives back what it stored during the day, and it fades so slowly that the brightest stars never really show. Night on the Lathe is a long amber dusk that never finishes.

## The Silence

Very little here makes sound.

There's no wind to speak of, because the air is too evenly warmed. The heat rises everywhere at once in a slow, even column instead of rushing to fill gaps. Nothing flows, since there is almost nothing liquid. The structures are too dense to ring and too worn to whistle. Walking produces a soft dry scuff with no echo, because the stubble absorbs it.

With patience you can hear three things.

The first is a very faint ticking. It is irregular and seems to come from everywhere, like a building settling. It is the material expanding and contracting with the heat, and some say it is something more deliberate than that.

The second is a hiss. It rises and falls over hours as the powder moves.

The third is almost impossible to describe. It is a pressure at the edge of hearing that comes with the repair fronts, and it is covered below.

## The Powder and the Dew

The Lathe is not dead. It is running.

The grey powder in the buried channels is also on the surface, and it moves. In the morning, as the sun and the ground-light push the heat past some threshold, the powder rises out of the hollow cores of the stumps in fine plumes. From a distance they look like steam. From close up they are cleaner than smoke, and each mote catches the light like a spark. The powder drifts. It gathers in slow haze-banks that roll across the plain at walking pace. It settles, rises again, and settles again. Its currents do not follow the air. They follow something in the ground, a gradient only the powder can sense.

Just after dawn there is also the dew. It is not water. It is a thin clear film with a faint sweetness, halfway between an oil and a solvent, that beads on the tops of the stumps for about an hour and then evaporates without a trace. No one knows whether it condenses out of the air or seeps up from below. The plants depend on it completely, and so do a few of the animals.

## The Repair Fronts

This is the thing that makes the Lathe what it is.

Every so often, sometimes days apart and sometimes months, a line appears on the plain. It first shows as a slight difference in brightness, a band where the ground-light runs a little whiter. The band can be tens of kilometers long, and it advances at roughly the speed of a slow walk.

Inside the band, the stubble grows.

The stumps get taller. The powder streams toward them and they lengthen and sharpen and branch. Worn hexagons regain their crisp edges. Fluted columns rise to the height of a knee, then a waist, then a head. For a few hours, while the band passes, a forest of fine pale spires stands where the flat was. They are delicate, intricate, and almost translucent. Each carries the ground-light up into itself and glows like a filament. The rebuilt structures fork and join and form lattices, and inside the lattices small chambers appear. The chambers are too small for anything alive, but they are clearly *rooms*, with thresholds and alcoves and what might be windows.

This is the third sound: the pressure at the edge of hearing, like a large crowd holding its breath.

Then the band moves on, and behind it the spires start to relax. They don't crumble. They sink, over hours, the way a tide goes out, and their material flows back down into the cores. By the next morning the ground behind the front is stubble again, blunt and polished. It is not quite the same stubble, though. Each front leaves the surface a hair smoother than before, as if the rebuilding were a little less complete each time. The Lathe repairs itself, and each repair is a slight erosion. This is the blade the name refers to. The plain is being turned down by its own attempts to restore itself.

Those who have studied the fronts say the structures they raise are not random. Mapped over many passes, the lattices match the flattened layers below. The repair front is not inventing anything. It is *remembering*. It rebuilds a city that existed at some earlier stage, but only in part, only for an afternoon, and only as far as its degraded instructions allow. Different fronts seem to remember different eras, and one may be reaching for a much older version than another. It is possible that no front has rebuilt the same thing twice.

## The Rule

There is one law on the Lathe that every traveler learns quickly, often from someone else's loss.

**What stays still long enough, the Lathe takes in.**

A pack left on the ground overnight will have fine pale filaments growing up into its fabric by morning. Leave it for a week and it is fused to the stubble. The powder will have worked into it and repatterned it, and the next front that passes will rebuild it as part of the city, a pack-shaped room in the lattice. After a month it is just ground.

This happens to camps, to fallen tools, and to the dead.

It does not happen to anything that keeps moving, or that is picked up and set down again every few hours. So people who cross the Lathe never really stop. They sleep in hammocks slung between walking-frames. They shift their weight constantly and turn their gear at every rest. There are rumors of travelers who stood still on purpose, and of spires that rise during some fronts in the shape of a person with a raised hand. The rumors are hard to confirm and easy to believe.

## What Grows

Vegetation is sparse, but it exists, which is remarkable in itself.

The most common plant is the copper rosette, a flat whorl of leathery, metallic-bronze leaves pressed tight against the stubble. It is about the size of an open hand. It has no roots. It grips the tops of the stumps with thousands of tiny hooks, and it moves slowly across the ground, a few centimeters a day, which is fast enough that the Lathe never takes it in. Its leaves have grooves that catch the morning dew. Some botanists think it also feeds on the heat gradient between the warm ground and the slightly cooler air, drawing energy from the difference itself. Rosettes are spaced tens of meters apart, as if each one keeps a territory, and from above they look like a scatter of dropped coins.

Rarer are the threadvines. These are single stems as thin as hair that grow inside the channels of the upper lattice and put out only a few pale tips at the surface. When a repair front passes they grow very fast, climbing the temporary spires and flowering at their peaks. The flowers are tiny, white, and five-petaled. They last only as long as the spire lasts, and when the spire sinks the vine pulls back with it. The flowers drop a fine seed into the powder, and the powder carries it wherever it goes. The threadvine lives entirely in the Lathe's afternoons of memory.

In a few places, depressions a few meters deep and hundreds wide, where some old structure collapsed more completely than its neighbors, the dew collects slightly longer and the rosettes crowd closer together. Surveyors call these *gardens*, though a person could walk through one without noticing it was there.

## What Lives

Animals are rare. Most travelers see none.

The one most often reported is the stilt-reader. It is tall and thin, with a pale body the color of the ground, and so still when it stops that people take it for a front-spire that failed to sink. It walks on four long jointed legs with narrow feet that barely touch the stubble. It follows the repair fronts at a distance, and while the city stands it walks slowly among the spires, pausing and tilting its narrow head toward the small rooms. It seems to be reading them. It eats the threadvine flowers, and people say it eats nothing else. No one has seen one young, or dying, or taken in.

Smaller and far stranger are the grinders. They are flat, disc-shaped creatures about the width of a palm that live under the stubble in the crushed room-layers. Every few years they come up in great numbers during a front, spread out across the fresh spires, and chew at them. They leave neat grooves, perfectly regular, spiral patterns cut into the new structures. Then they go back down. When the next front passes, the spires it builds carry the same spiral grooves, as if the memory had been edited. Some believe the grinders are the Lathe's editors. Others think they are its disease. It is possible they are the only authors it has left.

## The Weather of a Continent

At this scale the Lathe makes weather, though not the kind that brings rain.

The heat rising evenly off the ground turns into a single enormous convection cell that covers the continent. It is too slow and gentle to feel as wind, but it lifts the powder into the upper air. There the powder forms a high, thin, brilliant veil. This veil is a large part of why the sky is so bright: sunlight scatters through billions of reflective motes. Sometimes the veil thickens, and then the light becomes a flat white glare from every direction at once, and the horizon disappears completely. On those days travelers say it is like walking inside a lamp.

Sometimes the veil forms lines. Long bright streaks cross the sky in parallel, each hundreds of kilometers long. They mirror the repair fronts moving across the ground beneath them, a pattern in the sky matching a pattern on the plain, and they show where memory will come next.

Now and then, very rarely, the powder in the high air starts making shapes. There are brief outlines in the veil: lattices, towers, the frame of something vast, drawn in light across half the sky. They last a few minutes and then dissolve. No one knows what the powder is remembering up there, or for whom.

## The Long Afternoon

This is what a person comes to understand after crossing the Lathe for a long time.

It is not a ruin. A ruin has been abandoned, and nothing here was abandoned. It is still doing what it was built to do. It maintains itself. It rebuilds what was lost, powders what is worn, gathers what stays still, and keeps the plain level. It still works. It has simply been working for longer than its instructions could last. Every cycle of care leaves it a little flatter, a little smoother, and a little quieter, and the cities it remembers are a little less detailed each time.

Someday, on some unimaginably distant afternoon, the fronts will stop. The stubble will be polished completely flat, and the Lathe will be a single seamless surface of warm light under a white sky, with the copper rosettes still moving across it very slowly.

That day has not come yet. For now a band of brightness moves across the plain, and behind it a pale city rises for an afternoon. A stilt-reader steps carefully between the towers, bends toward a room the size of a thimble, and eats a white flower.
