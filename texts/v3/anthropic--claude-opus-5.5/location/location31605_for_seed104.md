# The Standing Water

It begins with a wall.

You come up through the last of the highlands, a country of rounded stone worn so smooth it looks poured, and the air thins and warms in a way that air at altitude shouldn't. Then the slope levels, and ahead of you, across the whole width of the world, stands water.

It is not held by anything. There is no dam and no basin rim. The sea simply stops at a vertical face that rises out of sight, a sheer plane of dark green glass that trembles faintly, the way the skin of a drum trembles long after it's been struck. Small things drift inside it: seeds, filaments, the pale undersides of leaves. At the base, where the face meets the stone, the water does not spill. It *leans*, a finger's width over nothing, and stays.

Put your hand against it and it's warm, body-warm, the warmth of bathwater someone left an hour ago. Push, and your hand goes in without resistance. The wall accepts you the way sleep does.

---

## Inside

The first thing is the light.

It comes from above, but not from a sun. Far overhead, much farther than it should be, the surface of this sea is a ceiling of slow-moving silver, and light filters down through it in long, wavering columns the color of weak tea and old brass. By the time it reaches the floor it has been handed down through so much water and so much suspended life that it is barely light at all. It's more a permission to see shapes. Everything is amber-green and underexposed. Edges soften at ten paces and vanish at forty.

The water is crowded. This is the second thing. It is thick with presences, not murky exactly, but *inhabited*: motes, spores, drifting threads of something like pollen, translucent discs that turn slowly as they sink, rafts of seed-fluff that hang motionless in the thermoclines. You move through it the way you'd move through a room full of dust in a sunbeam, except the sunbeam is everywhere and the dust is alive. It brushes your face. It parts around your hands and closes behind you.

You can breathe. You don't know how. The water fills your chest, heavy and warm, and your body simply agrees to it, the way a body agrees to a dream's rules without argument. There's no drowning here. There may never have been.

---

## The Stone

The floor of this sea was once a mountain range.

You can tell by the way the land rises and falls in immense, gentle swells, each one miles across. These were peaks once. They are now barely hills, domes of dark basalt and banded granite, polished to a dull satin sheen by a length of time that has no useful number. There are no sharp edges anywhere. Every crack has been rounded into a groove, every groove into a shallow valley, every ridge into a long soft shoulder. The rock has the look of a thing that has been touched by many hands for a very long time: river stones, worry stones, the knee of a bronze statue in a busy square.

Erosion here is not finished. It is only very, very slow, and very, very patient. The warm currents move constantly over the stone, carrying fine grit, and if you lie still with your cheek against a boulder you can feel the faintest sandpaper whisper of it. The mountain is still being taken apart, one grain at a time. It has been taken apart for so long that it has forgotten what it was.

In the low places, between the old summits, the sediment has gathered into deep beds of pale silt so fine it rises in a slow, luminous cloud at the slightest disturbance and takes an hour to settle again.

---

## The Growing Things

Out of the stone, rooted in cracks and anchored in silt, grows a forest.

It isn't dense the way a jungle is dense. It's dense the way a cathedral full of hanging banners would be dense. The dominant plants are tall, slender stalks, some as thick as your wrist and some as thick as a house, rising up through the water column toward that distant silver ceiling. They sway. Everything sways. The whole forest leans one way, then slowly, over minutes, leans back, following currents too broad to feel directly.

Some have long ribbon leaves the color of wet bronze that trail out horizontally, flickering at their tips. Some are hollow and segmented like bamboo, and the currents moving through them produce a sound so low it is less heard than felt in the sternum. Some bear clusters of bladders, pale and glowing faintly from within with a dim sulfur light. These are the closest thing the forest has to lanterns, and around them the drifting motes gather thicker, as moths gather.

Lower down, carpeting the stone domes, are mats of soft, feathery growth, dense as moss but longer, rippling in fields. Where the current is strong they lie flat and silver-backed. Where it's slack they stand up and open like hands.

Everything here is lush in a subdued way. There are no bright colors. It is a wealth of olive, umber, rust, and deep teal, a palette of old paintings in a dim gallery. But it is *abundant*. Every surface is claimed. Every current carries seed.

---

## The Quiet

Sound behaves strangely here, or rather, it barely behaves at all.

The water swallows most of it. What remains arrives soft and from no particular direction: a long exhalation that might be current moving through a hollow stalk, the faint papery tick of seed pods splitting open far away, sometimes a single deep tone that rises and falls and is gone, like a far-off bell heard through a wall. There is no birdsong, no wind noise, no crash of anything. Even your own movements make no sound. You can clap your hands and hear only a muffled thump that seems to come from somewhere behind you.

Mostly you hear your own heartbeat, and the pressure of the water against your ears, which is a sound like the ocean in a shell. You are, you realize, inside the shell.

---

## The Weather

The sea has weather. It is large enough to need it.

There are fronts: great moving layers of warmer or cooler water that roll slowly across the old mountains like weather systems across a plain. You feel them as a change in temperature on your skin, a sudden thickening or thinning of the water, a shift in the direction the forest leans. When a warm front passes, the lantern-bladders brighten and the feathery mats open all at once, a ripple of opening that spreads outward across the domes like a field of grain in wind.

There are storms. You see them before you feel them: in the distance, the suspended matter begins to rotate, a slow gyre of motes and seed and silt forming an enormous turning column that reaches up toward the ceiling. The forest bends away from it. The storm moves across the land over days, stirring the silt beds into luminous clouds, scattering seed, tearing loose old stalks, which drift upward and are lost.

And there is the *rising*. This is the sea's strangest weather and its most constant. Everything that dies here goes *up*. Fallen leaves, the husks of seed pods, the shed skins of the few animals, the fine grit worn from the mountains: all of it lifts, slowly, and drifts toward the silver ceiling. It is snow falling the wrong way. On some days it is light, a scattering of pale flakes. On others it is a blizzard, and the whole water column fills with slowly ascending debris, and you stand in it looking up as it pours past you into the light.

No one knows what happens at the surface. The rising matter reaches the ceiling and does not collect there. It simply goes.

---

## The Animals

There are almost none.

You can travel for days and see nothing that moves of its own will. The forest is so full of drifting life that for a long time you mistake the motes for creatures, but they are only seed and spore, and they have no intentions.

Then, once, you see a grazer.

It is long, many times your length, pale as a peeled stick, and nearly transparent. You can see its gut, dark with half-digested moss, and the slow red pulse of something like a heart. It moves along the stone domes with a motion like a caterpillar's, lifting and setting down its body in waves, and it eats the feathery mats as it goes, leaving a trail of bare satin stone behind it. It doesn't notice you. It doesn't seem able to notice anything. It moves at the pace of a clock's minute hand.

You see perhaps three more in all your time here, always alone, always far apart, always grazing.

Once you see something else: a quick, glassy flicker at the edge of vision, a ribbon of refraction that darts between the stalks and is gone. Maybe an eel. Maybe a trick of the light. Maybe a place where the water briefly forgot to be water. You never see it clearly.

The emptiness is not dead. It feels more like a house whose occupants have stepped out and might return, or might have left a very long time ago.

---

## The Marks

Someone was here.

Not recently. Not in any time you can measure. But the evidence is scattered across the drowned mountains, faint and patient, worn nearly away.

A stairway cut into the flank of one of the old domes, its steps so smoothed by current that they are now only a series of shallow ripples in the stone, leading down into a silt bed and vanishing.

A ring of standing stones on a hilltop, each one worn to a rounded stub, the gaps between them grown over with feathery mat. At the center, a hollow in the rock, perfectly round, as if a great sphere had rested there.

A single lintel. Two upright stones and a crosspiece, a doorway, standing alone in the middle of a silt plain, with nothing on either side of it. Walking through it changes nothing that you can tell. Walking *around* it takes longer than it should.

Cairns. Hundreds of them, small, knee-high, scattered across the domes with no pattern you can find. Stones stacked by hands. The current should have toppled them long ago. It hasn't. They stand with their stones fused together at the joints, grown into each other, as if the patience of the water had made them one piece.

And carvings. Very rarely, on some sheltered underside of stone, you find grooves that are too regular to be erosion: a spiral, a set of parallel lines, what might be a hand. They are shallow. They have been almost erased. You think they were made by people who breathed air, and who stood on these mountains when they were mountains, and looked up at a sky.

---

## Where It Breaks

The longer you stay, the less the sea holds together.

It starts with small things. A current that should carry you downhill carries you up. A dome you walked over yesterday is gone, and in its place is a valley you remember from the day before that. Your shadow, in the dim columns of light, falls *upward* toward the ceiling, stretching like something reaching.

Then larger things. There are places where the water simply stops being water, not at a surface but gradually, over the space of a few steps. The density thins. Your breath changes. The motes, which were suspended, begin to *fall*. And you find yourself standing in warm, still air on a stone slope, with the sea somewhere above you or beside you or both, a pocket of the old world, a dry memory. The feathery mats here lie flat and brittle. The seeds lie scattered on the stone as if dropped. And the silence is different, the silence of air rather than water, thinner, more exposed. Then you take another step and the water is back, closing over you as if it had never left.

You begin to suspect the sea and the sky are not two things.

Sometimes, when the light is right, when the rising debris thins and the ceiling is clear, you look up through that distant silver surface and see, on the other side, *land*. Rounded domes, pale silt plains, the slender stalks of a forest hanging down. An inverted country, identical to this one, stretched across the underside of the sky. The rising matter drifts up toward it. Perhaps it falls onto it. Perhaps someone standing there looks down and sees you, hanging upside down in their sky.

And sometimes, far off, you glimpse the wall again: that vertical face of standing water you first walked into. But you are inside it now, and when you look through it from this side, the highlands you climbed are not there. What stands beyond the wall is *more water*, green and dim, with a forest in it, and domes, and far away another wall.

Distance itself grows soft. You walk toward a cairn for an hour and it stays the same size, then you take one step and you're touching it. The ring of standing stones appears on three different hilltops. The same grazer passes you twice, going the same direction, a day apart, and the second time its gut is empty.

Time goes too. You find a carving on a stone and the grooves are fresh and sharp. You come back, and they are worn to a whisper. You come back again, and the stone is a smooth rounded stub, and then there is no stone at all, only silt, rising slowly toward the light.

This, you begin to understand, is what the ancientness *is*. It isn't that this place is old. It's that this place is the *end* of oldness, the final settled state that everything is heading toward, and the erosion here is not happening over time. Time is happening *through* the erosion. Every grain of rock lifted by the current and carried up toward the ceiling is a moment being carried out of the world. The mountains are wearing down into the sea, and the sea is wearing down into the sky, and the sky is wearing down into another sea, and somewhere in all of it the difference between *high* and *deep* has simply worn away.

That is why you can breathe. There's no longer enough distinction between water and air for your body to object.

---

## The Last Thing

On your final day, if there is such a thing as days here, you come to the highest of the old summits. It's barely a rise now, a long gentle swell of dark stone polished to a mirror sheen, covered in the open hands of the feathery mat.

At the top is a single cairn, taller than the others. At its base, grown into the stone, is a small object: a bowl, carved from the same basalt, worn thin as a leaf. It holds water. Of course it holds water. Everything here holds water.

But the water in the bowl is still.

It is the only still thing you have seen in the entire sea. Around it the forest sways, the motes drift, the silt rises. The bowl's water doesn't move. It doesn't rise. Its surface is flat and dark, and when you lean over it you see, reflected, not your face, but a sky. An open sky, blue and empty, the sky someone saw from this summit when it was a summit, when the air was air.

You don't touch it.

The current leans the forest one way and then, slowly, back. Far overhead, the silver ceiling ripples. The rising goes on, patient and soundless, carrying the mountain up grain by grain into the light.

And the sea stands, warm and full and very quiet, at the top of the world, holding everything, waiting for nothing in particular, becoming, a little more each moment, the only thing it was ever going to be.
