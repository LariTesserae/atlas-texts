# The Contour City of Anhel

## The Line

The first thing to understand about Anhel is that it has a shoreline, and there is no sea.

The valley is a deep, steep-sided bowl, perhaps a day's walk across and three days around, cut into a range of mountains so young their ridgelines are still serrated. Filling the bottom of the valley, flat as poured milk, is a lake of cloud. It does not drift or thin. It does not rise with the morning or sink in the afternoon. It sits at one exact height, its surface faintly textured like fleece, and it has sat there for as long as anyone has kept count. Where that surface meets the valley walls there is a line, running unbroken around the whole bowl, and along that line lives a city.

Anhel is one house deep and many miles long. It follows the contour of the cloud the way foam follows a tide mark, a thin braid of stone dwellings, terraces, cisterns, and stairways looping around the valley and back to where it began. Standing on any rooftop, you can see the city curve away in both directions, grey roofs and green terraces strung across spurs and ravines, shrinking into the haze on the far side of the bowl, where a house is a pale fleck and a terrace is a thread.

## Stone That Does Not Soften

The mountains here are new. The rock is a dark, glassy basalt, fractured into columns and blades, and none of it has had time to wear down. Scarps drop in clean vertical faces. Talus slopes are made of shards with edges that will open a palm. Ridges above the city look as though they were broken off yesterday: every crest is a row of teeth, every gully a raw, straight cut.

Nothing in Anhel weathers them. There is no rain here, only condensation. The air is cool, and it is always the same cool: never warm enough to dry the stone, never cold enough for frost to pry it apart. Wind barely reaches down into the bowl. Water beads on the rock, runs in thin sheets, and gathers, but it never falls hard enough to carve anything. So the landscape keeps its first sharpness. The oldest carved lintel in the city has corners as crisp as the day they were chipped out, and the people say this is simply what stone does when it is left alone.

The Anheli wear leather palm-guards when they climb. Children learn early to walk flat-footed on the talus and to touch rock with the heel of the hand, never the fingertips.

## How the Water Comes

Everything wet in Anhel comes from the cloud's edge. Just above the cloud-surface, a band of air perhaps forty paces tall stays saturated, and moisture condenses out of it onto any cool surface it meets. Below the line, inside the cloud, everything drips. Above the band, the mountains are nearly bare: black rock, pale lichen crust, a few tough cushion plants in the cracks. The damp belt is where life lives.

The city is built to harvest this. Every house has walls of dry-laid basalt, and the outer faces are ribbed with protruding stones set at a downward angle, so that each wall becomes a drip-comb. Beads collect on the ribs, run to their tips, and fall into stone gutters that feed a cistern set into the house's foundations. Between houses, the Anheli string long nets of felted goat wool on wooden frames, angled into the faint movement of air off the cloud. By midmorning the nets are heavy and grey with caught water, and someone walks the line wringing them into clay jars.

There are no rivers, only ten thousand small channels. Water runs along the contour through chiseled troughs, pools in carved basins, overflows into terraced beds, and trickles back down toward the cloud, which takes it without sound.

## The Green Belt

Within the damp band the vegetation is startling, dense and soft against the hard rock. Moss grows in pillows a hand deep over every horizontal surface and in trailing curtains from every overhang. Ferns crowd the gullies, their fronds silvered with droplets. Small-leaved shrubs with papery red bark root in the crevices between basalt columns, and their branches are hung with grey-green beard lichen so thick that a single bush looks like a sleeping animal.

The Anheli farm terraces cut directly into the slope, faced with stone and filled with soil carried up in baskets over generations. They grow a squat, thick-leaved tuber, a climbing bean trained on the drip-walls, bitter greens, and a knee-high grain with heavy, drooping heads that holds dew in its awns. On the terraces nearest the cloud, the ground is always soaked, and there they grow a watercress-like herb in shallow flooded beds.

Nothing grows very tall. The lushness is all low, close, and saturated. It is a carpet, not a forest. The tallest thing in the belt is a kind of tree-fern, ringed and slow, that the Anheli plant beside their doors. They say each one was planted by the first person to live in that house, and none has ever died.

## Animals of the Edge

Anhel is full of animals, and almost none of them make noise.

Small long-haired goats, grey and black, range the slopes above and below the terraces, cropping moss and lichen and returning to the houses at dusk without being called. Their hooves are soft and wide, suited to wet rock; they pass on the stone stairs with a sound like someone brushing a sleeve.

The swifts are everywhere. They are dark, narrow-winged birds that live in the vertical cracks of the scarps and feed by skimming the cloud-surface in long, low passes, snapping up the pale insects that hatch in the damp band. At any moment you can look down and see a dozen of them tracing the edge of the cloud, occasionally dipping a wingtip into it and leaving a brief furrow that closes behind them.

In the moss live fat, slow snails the size of a thumb, their shells the same dull green as their food; the city's children collect them for broth. Moss-hares, small and round-eared, freeze motionless when approached and trust their colour to hide them. Ravens sit on the house-peaks in pairs, never calling, watching the terraces with a kind of patient ownership. Frogs, black with gold throats, sit in every cistern and basin. Their throats pulse, but they do not sing.

Then there are the waders. These are large, long-legged grazers, something like an elk built by someone who had only heard elk described. Their shoulders are high, their necks thin, and their coats hang heavy with moisture. Now and then one walks down out of the green belt, past the lowest terraces, and steps into the cloud. It goes in slowly, chest-deep, then neck-deep, then gone. Days later, sometimes somewhere else on the rim, one walks back out, dripping, with the fine fur of its legs matted with something pale that the Anheli call "floor-silt." Nobody follows them. Nobody has ever gone more than a few steps into the cloud and come back willing to talk about it. What is at the bottom of the valley is not a question the city asks aloud.

## The Shape of the City

Anhel is urban in every sense but crowding. It has a common law, a system of wardens for each spur, communal granaries, stone-paved stairways, a unified channel system, and a single continuous path, the Rim Road, that connects every house to every other. But the houses sit far apart, each on its own shoulder of rock or its own terrace shelf. Between them there is often a stretch of empty slope, moss and fern and a strung net or two, before the next roof. From across the valley the city looks less like a town than like a necklace with gaps between the beads.

This spacing is practical. Each house needs its share of condensing surface and air. Crowd them, and they rob each other's water. Over time the Anheli have settled on a distance, measured in paces and marked by carved stones along the Rim Road, and it is not broken.

The houses are round or oval, built low, with corbelled stone roofs that are then covered in turf and moss until they look like hillocks with doors. Their doors face the cloud. Inside, there are hearths burning shrub-wood and dried dung, stone sleeping benches padded with felted wool, and niches holding clay vessels, bone needles, and flaked-obsidian blades. Metal is known but scarce. There is a little bronze, cast in a few clay molds kept in the granary-houses and traded along the rim. It is used for chisels, needle-points, and the fittings of the net frames. Most work is done with stone, bone, wood, wool, and patience.

At intervals along the Rim Road stand larger structures: granaries shaped like beehives, open-sided meeting shelters roofed with slabs, and, at four points around the valley, tall cairns of stacked basalt columns that serve as landmarks. From any one of them, on a clear afternoon, you can see the next.

## Quiet

The quiet of Anhel is thick and specific. The cloud swallows sound; anything said near the edge seems to fall into it and not come back. There is no wind to speak of, no rushing water, no birdsong. What you hear is dripping, everywhere and constantly, a soft irregular ticking from ten thousand wall-ribs and leaf-tips. You hear the scuff of a goat on stone, the wet tearing of moss being grazed, the tap of a chisel from some distant house carried faintly along the slope, and a voice, if someone calls, which is rare. The Anheli speak low by habit. Children who shout are told that the cloud is listening, and they mostly believe it.

## The Rule of Stillness

Nothing here changes.

There are no seasons. The light comes up grey-gold over the eastern ridges each morning at the same pale angle, clears into an even, ordinary brightness by midday, and fades at the same hour each evening. The cloud-surface holds its level to the width of a finger. The damp band is exactly as tall today as it was when the oldest carved marker was set. The tree-ferns by the doors grow so slowly that no one has seen one change. Moss thickens, is grazed, thickens again, and settles at the same depth. The goats breed, the swifts nest, and the frogs sit in the basins, but the numbers stay balanced as if counted.

The Anheli do not have a word for "weather." They have a word for "the day," and it is the same word for every day. Their stories are not about change but about keeping: the house kept, the terrace kept, the net kept mended, the distance kept between neighbors, the line kept. The one thing they fear is the thought that the cloud might someday move, even a little. If it rose, it would swallow every house. If it sank, the green belt would slide downward, away from the terraces, and the city would be left stranded on dry rock with its cisterns empty.

It has never moved. Each morning someone at every house walks to the lowest terrace, looks at the white surface lying there at exactly the height of the old carved mark, nods, and goes back up to wring the nets.
