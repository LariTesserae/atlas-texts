# The Crown That Pours Itself

## The Approach

There is no road up. There is a cloud sea, and above it, for a long way, nothing. Then a darkness that is not sky rises through the white like the prow of a ship turned upright, and the darkness is stone, and the stone is warm.

The mountain has no name the lowland maps agree on, because it did not exist when the maps were drawn. The people who live on it call it the Crown, or the Pour, or simply *up*. It is steep past reason, a single cone so tall that the air at its summit is thin enough to make a stranger's heart knock and hands tingle. Its slopes have no scree fans, no gullies, no softening at all. Every edge is still sharp. The rock is black and oxblood and bottle-green glass, fractured in clean planes, shining where the fog has licked it. Nothing here has had time to crumble.

Built into that rock, clinging to it and made of it, is a city.

## The Rock That Is Still Being Born

The first thing to understand is that the mountain is not finished. It is not finished the way a loaf on the peel is not finished.

At the summit is a broad caldera, a lip of red-black glass perhaps two kilometers across, and from its center the mountain extrudes itself. It is not an eruption. Nothing here is violent. A column of molten stone rises with the patience of a glacier, about the height of a hand each year, and as it rises the older rock beneath is pushed outward and down. The whole cone is a slow conveyor turned inside out. New rock is born at the crown and travels, over centuries, down the flanks toward the clouds.

So the city moves.

A house cast at the crest this year will, in the lifetime of a grandchild, have drifted a few dozen meters down-slope, carried on its bedrock like a stone in a slow river. In six hundred years it will reach the cloud-line. Everything here begins at the top and ends at the bottom, and the ground itself sets the pace.

## The Foundries of the Crest

The people of the Crown did not simply settle on this rising stone. They learned to shape it as it emerges.

Ringing the caldera are the foundries: vast iron hoods and bore-collars set directly over the places where the upwelling breaches the surface. Beneath them the rock comes up soft and dull orange, too thick to flow and too hot to touch. Great slow presses meet it there, cast-iron molds the size of townhouses driven by steam drawn from the mountain's own heat. The rock is pressed into walls, lintels, stair-flights, arched vaults, and water channels while it is still yielding. Then it is released to cool and rise and be pushed outward, and it becomes the city.

No stone is quarried here. None is carried. The city is poured from below and set in place by the mountain's own growth.

The molds are old and much repaired. Some bear the marks of long-dead guilds, spiral stamps, stylized hands, rows of dots whose meaning is disputed. Because every mold presses the same shapes over and over, whole districts are repetitions. A door-arch cast four hundred years ago down near the cloud-line matches one cast last spring, and walking downhill is like walking through the same street at different ages. Near the crest, edges are crisp and glossy. Farther down, the walls have taken on a faint bloom of mineral salts, and the glass has begun, just barely, to cloud.

## Heat

It is hot on the Crown. The heat does not come from the sky. At this altitude the sun is fierce but the air holds little warmth. It comes from beneath.

The ground radiates like the floor of a bread oven an hour after the fire is raked out. Near the crest, streets must be crossed in wooden clogs. Farther down the heat eases to something bodily, a constant warmth underfoot that makes the whole city feel faintly alive, like the flank of a sleeping animal. Buildings are warmest at their foundations and coolest at their roofs. People sleep on the upper floors and cook on the lowest, often on nothing more than a bare slab of floor.

Vents open everywhere, round mouths in walls, slots along the stair-edges, chimneys that were never built but simply appeared as the rock cooled and cracked along the lines the molds left. They breathe a dry, faintly sulfurous warmth. On still days, heat-shimmer stands over the whole mountain, and the city wavers in it like something seen through water.

## Water

Water arrives as fog.

Most days, at some hour, the cloud sea rises. It climbs the flanks and pours through the lower districts, and sometimes, not often, it reaches all the way to the crest. When it meets the hot stone the city hisses faintly, a soft universal sound like breath through teeth. Steam lifts from every surface. Where the fog meets cooler things, it condenses. Iron railings, the undersides of eaves, and especially the great copper fog-sails bead with water and drip.

The fog-sails are the city's orchards. They are broad fins of hammered copper, hundreds of them, mounted on masts along every ridge and rooftop and angled to catch the drifting cloud. Beneath each one runs a gutter cast into the stone, and the gutters join into channels, and the channels feed cisterns sunk deep into the rock. The cisterns are placed where the stone is coolest, which is always lower down, so water in the Crown flows downhill toward the old districts, where the ground has had centuries to cool. A household's wealth is measured in sail-area and cistern depth.

Rain happens perhaps a dozen times a year. When it does, it falls on hot glass and much of it flashes to steam before it can run. For a few minutes the whole mountain vanishes inside its own white exhalation, and the only thing to see is the dark ghost of the nearest wall.

Everything metal in the city is green. The fog-sails, the mold-housings, and the railings all carry a bright verdigris bloom, blue-green and powdery. It is the only green on the Crown. Children who have never been down to the lowlands draw trees in that color.

## No Life That Grows

Nothing grows here. No moss, no lichen, not even the dark crust of bacterial film that colonizes bare rock elsewhere in the world. Nothing has had time to arrive, the stone is too hot, and the surface is too new. Every square meter of the Crown was, within living memory or a few lifetimes of it, molten.

Food comes up from below on cable-lifts that run from anchor-towers at the cloud-line down into a white void. The lowland end is never seen from the top. Hampers of grain and dried fish rise out of the cloud on slow, creaking wheels, and hampers of cast glass, sulfur, and forged iron descend. The cables are the city's umbilicus. The people of the Crown are quietly aware that they live entirely on the mercy of a world they cannot see.

## The Few Animals

Animal life is rare, and what there is has come up on the wind.

The mountain's heat drives a permanent updraft. Warm air pours upward along the flanks, and it carries the lowland's aerial drift with it: spiders on silk threads, winged seeds that will never find soil, gnats, tiny moths, and spores. This drift is the base of whatever food web the Crown has.

Feeding on it are the **vent-swifts**, small slate-grey birds with long sickle wings. They nest in the warm chimney-vents, where their eggs incubate on the stone's heat alone, and they almost never land except to enter a nest. They ride the updraft in slow spirals above the caldera, mouths open, straining the air. They are rarely heard. On a quiet evening you may see forty of them turning in the shimmer above the foundries, and not one will make a sound.

Stranger are the **glass-mites**, visible only as a faint moving dust in the corners of the oldest walls. They appear to eat the mineral bloom that forms on aging glass. They are the nearest thing the Crown has to decay. Where they gather, surfaces dull and pit. Some residents scrape them away. Others leave them, on the belief that a wall with mites is a wall that has lived long enough to matter.

There are stories of larger things: a pale, long-legged animal that walks the cloud-line at dawn, where the old districts crumble, and seems to eat the crumbling stone itself. People who claim to have seen it disagree about how many legs it has.

## Density

The city is packed tight. The mountain's flanks are so steep that level ground exists only where the molds have made it, and every mold-cast terrace is precious. Houses are stacked atop houses, and the roof of one is the street of the next. Stairs cut through buildings rather than around them. A single cast block may contain a foundry-worker's family, a sail-mender's shop, a cistern, a shrine, and a public passage, all within walls that were pressed out of one mold on one day three centuries ago.

Because the city is poured continuously, there are no gaps. Where two districts drift against each other, their walls simply fuse, and the seam is marked only by a change in stamp or a slight step in the paving. From the air, if anything could see it from the air, the Crown would look like a single structure, a fluted, windowed, terraced cone with no space between one building and the next, crowned with iron and fringed below with copper fins.

## Quiet

For all its industry, the Crown is quiet.

The air is thin and carries sound poorly. Voices fall dead a few paces off. The presses do not hammer. They close over the soft rock with the slowness of a tide, and what they make is less a sound than a pressure: a deep, subsonic groan felt in the soles and teeth, once every few hours. People wear felt-soled shoes against the heat and walk softly by habit.

What you hear, in the long stillness between fogs, is the stone itself. Glass cooling and rising makes tiny noises, clicks and ticks and, very rarely, a high ringing *ping* as some stress releases deep inside a wall. The residents call these sounds "the mountain counting." At night, lying on an upper floor with the warmth coming up through the house, you can hear it all around you: a scattered, patient ticking, as if the city were full of small clocks, each keeping different time.

## Descent and Ending

Walking down through the Crown is walking backward through time.

At the crest everything is glossy, hot, and sharp. The streets glow faintly at night where the rock is freshest. The foundry districts are loud by local standards, filled with the slow breathing of steam-valves and the occasional low grind of a mold being reset.

By the middle slopes, the stone has cooled. The glass has taken on the grey-green bloom of age, and the doorways sit a little lower than their stamped sills, where the fog-water has worn the thresholds by a finger's width in three hundred years. This is where most people live. It is warm but bearable, and the sails catch plenty.

In the lowest districts, just above the cloud sea, the Crown finally begins to erode. This is the only place on the mountain where the ordinary processes of the world have had time to take hold. Walls are cracked. Fog has seeped into fissures, and centuries of slow heating and cooling have split the glass along its casting lines. Houses here are abandoned one by one as they become unsafe. Their doors are marked with a chalk sign, and their families climb upward to new castings. The oldest buildings lean, then slump, then shed their facades in slow sheets of glass that slide down into the cloud without a sound.

This is the Crown's only talus, and its only soil: a thin band of broken glass and mineral dust at the edge of the world. Beneath the cloud sea, somewhere, it must pile up. Nobody has gone to see.

## What the People Believe

The people of the Crown hold that the mountain is not rising but *reaching*. They believe the upwelling at the summit is a hand pushing slowly out of the earth, and the city is the shape of the fingers. They believe that, given enough time, the mountain will grow tall enough to break through the thin blue into whatever lies above. They note that the sky here is already darker than it should be. At noon, overhead, it is nearly violet, and on the clearest days a few stars can be seen in daylight.

They do not hurry toward that day. The mountain sets the pace, and it is patient. A hand's height a year. The presses close, the stone ticks, the fog rises and hisses and falls back. The swifts turn silently in the heat. And the city, poured from its own foundations and walking by imperceptible steps down into the cloud, keeps building itself upward toward a sky it is not yet close enough to touch.
