# The Grown City of the Low Plain

## Approach

The plain is barely above the level of the sea that must once have made it. The land lies flat and warm under a high white sky. From a distance, before anything else, you hear it: a roar with no single source, like a waterfall that has been split into ten thousand smaller falls and scattered across forty miles. Then you see the green. It does not look like a forest. The skyline is too regular: flat tops, sheer faces, dark rectangles that can only be windows. It looks like a city seen through heat-shimmer, but the shimmer never clears, and the city never quite holds still.

There is no soil at the edge. There is no edge. The ground simply becomes more alive as you walk. Dry, springy turf gives way to a mat of fine roots, and the mat thickens into something like woven cable. Then you are walking on a floor made entirely of living wood, grain running in every direction, still pale and sappy where it has most recently grown. Nothing here is old. Everything has the raw, sharp look of a fresh cut: splinters that have not yet dulled, bark that has not yet furrowed, edges you could shave with. No weather has had time to soften anything, because nothing stands long enough to weather.

## The Substrate

Everything is plant: the streets, the walls, the stairs, the lintels, the dust in the air (pollen), the haze (spores and the fine exhalations of stomata), even the light, which comes through a million translucent leaf-panes and arrives tinted and dappled. There is no rock anywhere. If there was ever stone beneath the plain, it has been dissolved, taken up, and rebuilt into cellulose and lignin.

The floor of the city is a braided root-pavement, flat enough to walk on and roughly level, in the way a frozen river is level. Individual roots swell to the thickness of a thigh and carry pulses you can feel through your feet: slow, heavy surges as sap is pushed toward some quarter that is about to grow. Where the roots part there are gaps, and through the gaps you can look down into lower strata. There are older streets beneath the streets, already crushed and half-reabsorbed, and below those, darker layers of compressed green going down farther than the light can follow. The city has been built on top of itself many times. It is young at every level, because each layer was young when it was buried.

## The Architecture

Nobody built this, and yet it is unmistakably built.

Trunks grow in straight lines, set at intervals so exact they look surveyed. They rise and then branch at right angles, which no tree should do, and the branches meet the branches of the next trunk and graft together seamlessly into beams. Between the beams, sheets of bark and leaf stretch taut and harden into walls. Lianas grow in spirals around invisible columns and then thicken and lock, so that where the column should be there is only air, but a staircase stands anyway, climbing a void.

The buildings have rooms, and the rooms have doorways. The doorways are the most unsettling thing about the city, because they are sized for someone. Some are human-height. Many are not: some are twice as tall and very narrow, some waist-high and wide as a cart, some round, some shaped like a keyhole turned on its side. There are windows with sills, and on the sills grow small, tight rosettes of leaves arranged like objects set out on display. There are benches. There are what look like market stalls with awnings of broad fibrous leaves, their counters polished smooth by nothing, with no one behind them, and nothing ever sold there.

The street plan is dense and layered. Lanes are rarely wider than two people abreast. Buildings lean together overhead until their upper stories touch and fuse, and many streets are effectively tunnels. Bridges of grown timber cross between towers at every height, so the city is as much a lattice as a map. Look up and you see walkways at four, eight, fifteen levels, braided and crossing, all of them green, some of them visibly moving.

It extends across the whole plain, a regional sprawl, with districts that feel distinct from each other. Some are low and tangled, some tall and sparse, some built entirely of hollow cane, some of heavy dark heartwood. You can climb one of the taller towers and look out, and the green city goes to the horizon in every direction except one. To the south, faintly, there is a line of low gray-gold that may be dunes, or may simply be dryness.

## The Sound

The noise is total. It is not a background. It is the medium you move through, the way a swimmer moves through water.

There are no animals making it. There are no machines. Every sound comes from growth and strain.

- **Cracking.** Wood under stress, green wood splitting as new growth forces through old, beams shearing where two graft-lines disagree. The cracks come constantly and at every pitch, from the high snap of a twig to the sound of a whole trunk opening lengthwise, which is like cannon fire and arrives as a pressure on the chest.
- **Groaning.** Whole buildings flexing. The low, rising moan of a tower leaning into a new angle over the course of an hour. You feel these in your teeth.
- **Detonation.** Seed pods, some the size of barrels, exploding and hurling seeds hard enough to embed them in walls. On a hot day the pod-fields go off in chains, rolling across a district like artillery.
- **Piping.** The cane quarters are full of hollow stems of every length and bore, and the warm wind across the plain sounds them. The result is not music, or not quite. It is a vast drone chord that shifts as the canes grow and change length, a chord that is always almost resolving.
- **Rustling.** Too much leaf to count, moving all at once, a hiss so broad it becomes a kind of silence underneath everything else, the way the sea is silent under a storm.
- **Tearing.** Roots ripping through the pavement. Membranes splitting. Buds bursting, which here is audible: a soft wet pop, multiplied by millions into a sizzle like fat on a griddle.

You cannot speak to anyone here, if you had anyone to speak to. You cannot hear your own footsteps. After a while you stop trying to separate the sounds and begin to hear the whole, and then you notice that the whole has *structure*. It pulses. It builds in one quarter and falls away in another. There are districts that are distinctly louder, and they are always the districts that are growing fastest.

## The Rule

This is the law of the place, the one thing that seems to hold when everything else is failing: **the plants grow toward sound.**

Not toward light, not toward water. Toward loudness, toward vibration. A stem grows along the line of greatest resonance. A root follows the thrum in the ground. And every act of growth makes noise, so growth draws more growth. That is why the city is dense and why it is turbulent: each loud place pulls the city toward itself until it becomes too crowded, until the strain splits it apart with a sound louder still, which pulls in more.

The architecture follows from this. The straight lines are standing waves, the paths along which vibration travels cleanly. The right angles are where one resonance meets another. The rooms are resonant chambers; step into one and the noise of the city drops and changes, becomes a single tone, the room's own note. Every room has its note, and the size of a room is simply the size its note requires. The doorways are sized for their frequencies, not for any body. The benches, the sills, the market stalls, the staircases around invisible columns: all of them are shapes that sound makes when it is given something soft and eager to press itself into.

Pollination happens by vibration too. With no insects and no birds, the flowers rely on the roar. Pollen shakes loose in the loud quarters and hangs in golden clouds, and the flowers of the quieter districts open wide toward the sound and catch it. At certain hours, when the pod-fields fire in unison, the whole sky over the city turns yellow for a few minutes and then slowly rains back down.

## Water

Water is scarce, and the city holds it jealously.

It rains perhaps once in a week or two: short, warm, heavy rains that sweep in off the sea side and are over in minutes. When they come, the noise changes entirely. The drone of the canes drowns, the leaf-hiss goes up into a roar, the dry cracking softens into a wet creaking. The city drinks audibly, a vast slurping and gurgling through every pipe and gutter. And there *are* gutters. The roofs are shaped to channel rain inward, into cisterns grown from pitcher-leaves the size of rooms. Their walls are thin and translucent, and you can see the water in them sloshing and trembling, green-lit, vibrating with the city's sound so that its surface is always patterned with fine standing ripples.

Between rains there is condensation. In the hour before dawn the plain cools a little, and every surface beads. The leaves weep. Guttation drips from every edge, clear drops pushed out at the leaf-margins, and the whole city glitters and patters for an hour as though it were raining from the inside. There is a theory, if there were anyone to hold theories: that the noise itself wrings water out of the air, that the city is so loud it shakes the vapor into droplets. Certainly the loudest quarters are also the dampest, their walls always slick, their air close and steaming.

There is one river, or something like one. It is narrow, slow, and brown, and it does not seem to come from anywhere. It rises in the middle of a district and flows through the city in channels the city has grown for it, along a lined, grooved bed of living wood. Then it simply ends, soaked into the roots. The city has built embankments along it, and quays, and steps leading down into it, and arched bridges over it, as if it were the river of a great capital. No boats have ever used it.

## Heat and Light

It is warm, always, the heavy warmth of a greenhouse. The sun is high and ordinary and bright, and the light is clean daylight, but inside the city it arrives filtered, green-gold, shifting as the leaves shift. The shadows are sharp, because everything is sharp. The heat builds in the enclosed streets and makes the air thick with sap-smell: sweet and resinous, cut-grass green, with a sourness underneath from the crushed lower layers fermenting.

The light is the one thing that behaves normally, and so it becomes a kind of anchor. You watch it to know which way is up.

## The Breakdown

The rules fail, in places, and more often than you would like.

**Districts that grow backward.** Here and there you come across a quarter where everything is running in reverse: timber shrinking into saplings, saplings folding into seedlings, seedlings drawing back into seed. The seeds pop up from the pavement into the air and fly back into the pods, which seal themselves with a sucking sound. The noise in these districts is the city's noise played backward, and it makes you queasy in a way that has nothing to do with volume.

**Rooms larger inside than out.** The resonant chambers do not always respect the buildings that contain them. A small cane hut can open onto a hall whose ceiling you cannot see, humming a note so low it is below hearing and felt only as pressure in the gut. Close the door behind you and the hut outside is still small.

**Streets that disagree.** Two lanes meet at an intersection, and each is at right angles to the other, and each is also straight ahead. You can walk down either and arrive at the same place, or at different places, depending on the hour. The city rearranges itself so fast that maps are pointless anyway, but in some quarters the rearrangement happens *while you are in the street*: walls slide past, grow, split, and a lane you are standing in becomes a courtyard, then a room, then the inside of a trunk.

**Echoes of cities that were never here.** Sometimes a district grows into a shape that is too specific: a cathedral nave with rose windows of radial leaves, a covered bazaar with a hundred identical stalls, an amphitheater, a colonnaded forum, a street of tall narrow houses with a clock-face grown into the end wall, its hands of twisted twig showing a time. It is as if the sound of the city, left to itself, has discovered on its own the forms that other cities arrived at, or as if it remembers them from somewhere. These forms hold for a day, an hour, and then they are torn apart by the next surge. But they recur. The same clock-face shows up in different quarters, weeks apart, always showing the same time.

**Things that almost move.** There are no animals here. The city is purely plant, and the absence of animal life is so complete it becomes a presence: no insect hum, no birdsong, no rustle of anything alive in the leaf-litter, because there is no leaf-litter. Everything fallen is immediately regrown into. But in some districts the flowers grow in the shapes of animals, rooted and green and utterly still: a flock of birds grown in white petal along a cornice, each wing in mid-beat, or something with a long neck and eyes of dark seed, standing in a doorway as if waiting. They do not move. They are just flowers. They face the loudest direction, like everything else here. But you look away and look back and are never sure whether the neck was turned that way before.

## The Cycle

Nothing is stable. The whole city is in storm-state, permanently.

A quarter swells, grows louder and denser, pulls in growth from its neighbors until the lanes squeeze shut and the rooms crush each other and the strain becomes unbearable. Then it breaks. Sometimes this happens over days, as a slow collapse of groaning and cracking. Sometimes it is sudden, a whole district shearing apart in one long detonation that you can hear across the plain and feel as a shudder through the root-floor miles away. The broken wood does not rot. It is overgrown within hours, pressed down into the pavement, and on top of it the next city starts. The sound of the collapse draws the growth, and the growth makes the next sound.

From the tallest towers you can watch this happen across the whole plain at once: green waves swelling and breaking, cities rising and falling like surf, dozens of districts at different stages, the loudest always tallest, the quiet ones low and waiting. Pollen clouds drift between them. The canes drone. Somewhere a room is sounding its note, and somewhere a clock with no mechanism shows the same time it always shows.

## The Edge

To the south there is quiet. The dunes are dry and pale and the city does not grow toward them, because there is nothing there to hear. If you walk out far enough the roar drops behind you, and for the first time in days you hear your own breath and the dry sand shifting. It is unbearable, in its way: an absence so sharp it hurts. You notice that you have been leaning back toward the noise without meaning to, the way the flowers do. And you understand, a little, why every stem and root in the city chose to grow toward the sound. It is the only thing here that tells you that you are still somewhere.
