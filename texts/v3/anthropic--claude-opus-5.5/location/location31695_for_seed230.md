# The Town on the Saddle

The town sits in the notch between two summits, where the ridge dips and then rises again. It isn't large. You could walk from the lower gate to the upper gate in the time it takes a pot of water to boil, if the water would boil properly this high, which it doesn't quite. But in that short distance there is more wall than seems possible, and more stairs, and more doorways, than any number of people could need.

It is built entirely of the mountain. The stone is a grey-green schist that splits into flat plates, and the builders took what the mountain offered. The walls are dry-laid, with no mortar, the plates stacked in courses so tight that a knife blade finds no purchase between them. Lintels are single long slabs. Roofs are corbelled: each course of stone steps inward over the one below until the gap at the top can be closed with one capstone. Under these roofs the rooms are round or rounded-square, low, and dim. They are the shape of the stone's own reluctance to span distance.

## The layering

The town is old in a particular way. It was not built once and then abandoned. It was built continuously, over so many lifetimes that the building became a kind of sediment.

Walk any lane and you can read it. The lowest courses of a wall are rounded at the edges, softened by centuries of damp and frost until they look almost like river stones. Above them the plates are sharper. Above those, sharper still. A doorway has been filled in and a new one opened a hand's width to the left. A stair climbs to a landing that leads nowhere now, because the house it served was taken down and its stones went into the house beside it. Nothing here was ever carted away. Stone is too heavy to waste, and the mountain around the saddle is too steep to quarry easily, so every wall is made of older walls. The town is its own quarry, and has been for as long as there has been a town.

This gives the place its density. Structures crowd shoulder to shoulder and climb over one another. Lanes are the width of a person with a basket. Roofs serve as terraces for the houses above. The town has the feel of a city, compressed. There are wards and thresholds. There are small squares where four lanes meet around a well-head, and there is a sense of civic order. There is a lower town and an upper town, and the difference between them is understood even if no one says it.

## The emptiness

And yet it is nearly empty.

Not abandoned. Someone lives here, several someones, but they are few, and the town is sized for many more. Most of the rooms are closed. Some are used for storage: dried dung for fuel, bundles of cut heather, clay jars sealed with wax. Some are simply shut, their doorways stacked closed with loose plates. This is the local way of closing a house, and it can be undone in an afternoon. Nobody treats these rooms as ruins. They are rooms that are not currently needed.

So you can walk three lanes without seeing anyone. Then, at a turning, a woman sits in a doorway splitting reeds with her thumbnail, and she nods, and you go on, and there is no one again for a long while. The people here are spread through the town the way the plants are spread across the slopes: one here, one there, each with a wide margin of stone around it.

## Water

Water is the town's central problem and its central art.

The saddle is dry in the ordinary sense. It is too high and too exposed to hold a stream, and the rain comes rarely and briefly. But cloud comes often. It pours over the ridge from the windward side, silent and white, and for an hour or a morning the town sits inside it. Then everything beads. The stone sweats. Every surface grows a skin of droplets that swell, join, and run.

The builders learned to catch this. On the windward faces of the town stand low structures that look at first like cairns or rough towers. They are loosely stacked, full of gaps, open to the wind. They are air wells. The fog moves through their honeycomb of stone, the stones stay cold from the night, and the moisture condenses and trickles down inside to a sloped floor. From there it runs through a carved channel to a cistern. Each air well yields only a little on a good morning, a few jars. But there are dozens of them, some so old their stones are furred green on the inside with the only thick moss in the town.

The whole town drains to the cisterns. Roofs are canted. Lanes have a slight central groove. Stair treads tilt a finger's width toward the downhill side. When the rare rain does come, the town rings with running water for twenty minutes, every channel alive at once, and then goes quiet again. The cisterns are under the squares, their mouths capped with round stones worn smooth by hands and ropes. The water in them is very cold and tastes faintly of iron and slate.

Between the fogs the town is dry. Condensation lines are the only sign: faint tide-marks on the stone showing where the moisture gathers and where it runs.

## Plants

Almost nothing grows here on purpose, and not much grows at all.

Lichen is everywhere, in slow colors: sulfur yellow, ash grey, a rusty orange that favors the tops of walls where birds once perched. Some lichen colonies on the oldest courses are wider than a spread hand. At the rate lichen grows, that means the stones have sat undisturbed for longer than anyone can count.

In the cracks at the bases of walls, where the drainage channels keep things slightly damp, grow small cushion plants. They are tight green domes with tiny white flowers that open only in sun. A few stunted junipers cling to the upper terraces, their trunks twisted and silver, none taller than a child. On the slopes below the lower gate there are patches of wiry grass and heather, and these are harvested carefully, never all at once.

There are no gardens in the usual sense. There are a few stone-lined beds, sheltered in angles of wall, where someone grows bitter greens and a hardy grain. The beds are small and very old, and the soil in them was carried up from somewhere else, generations ago, one basket at a time.

## Animals

Animals are rare. Here and there, a few:

- **Choughs**, black with red bills, that ride the updraft over the saddle and sometimes land on the rooftops to pick at lichen or crumbs. Their calls are the sharpest sound the town regularly hears.
- **A lizard**, grey as the schist, that suns itself on south-facing walls and vanishes if you so much as look at it directly.
- **A cat or two**, belonging to no one in particular, lean and unhurried.
- **A few goats**, kept in a walled pen near the lower gate and taken out onto the slopes to graze. They are the town's wealth, and they are treated with a kind of formal respect.

Beyond that, the insects are small and quiet. On the slope there is a burrowing animal whose whistle you hear more often than you see it.

## Sound

Mostly the town is quiet. It is not silent. Wind is always moving across the saddle, and it finds the gaps in the stone and makes soft sounds, not quite whistles, more like breath. The air wells hum faintly when the wind is right. Water drips somewhere, even on dry days, from some channel still draining the last fog.

Human sounds come singly and carry far. The tap of a mallet on a stone chisel, dressing a plate to fit a wall. The rhythmic knock of a loom. A voice calling a goat. Because the lanes are narrow and the walls hard, these sounds bounce and travel, and you often hear someone long before you find them, or never find them at all.

At night it is quieter still. Then the only sound is the wind, and, very rarely, a stone somewhere settling a hair's breadth deeper into its bed.

## Tools and making

Everything is made by hand. The tools are stone, bone, wood, and a little soft metal: copper chisels hardened by hammering, bronze needles, a few prized iron points passed down through families. There is no wheel here. The lanes are too narrow and steep. Everything is carried: in baskets on the back, in jars on the head, on the goats when the loads are heavy.

Building is the great craft. Everyone knows how to lay stone, the way everyone elsewhere knows how to cook. Knowing how to read a plate is a matter of pride: where it will split, which face should go outward, how it will shed water. Weaving is the second craft, from goat hair and a coarse fiber stripped from the heather. The cloth is dense, brown and grey, windproof.

## Light and temperature

It is cool here, always. Not bitter. The saddle is high, but it is sheltered from the worst by the summits on either side. The air has the clean chill of altitude, and the stone holds the cold of the night well into the morning. In the sun, against a south wall, it is pleasant. Step into shadow and you feel the difference at once.

The light is plain daylight, clear when the cloud is gone. It is thin and bright at noon and slants long in the evenings across the terraces, picking out every course of every wall. When the fog comes the light goes soft and directionless, and the town becomes a set of grey shapes emerging one at a time from white.

## Stillness

What stays with you is how little the place changes.

The fog comes and goes, but the fog has always come and gone. A wall gets taken down and its stones go into another wall; the total amount of wall stays the same. The lichen grows so slowly it may as well be painted on. The people live and die and their houses are closed, then reopened for someone else, and the number of people stays roughly what it has been for a very long time. Not growing, not shrinking, just holding.

It is a town in equilibrium with its mountain. It takes the stone and gives it back. It takes the fog and drinks it. It sits in the notch between two summits the way a stone sits in the notch of a wall: fitted, settled, bearing its load, and not expecting to move.
