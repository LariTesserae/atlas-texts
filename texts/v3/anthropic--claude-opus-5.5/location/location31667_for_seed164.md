# The Held Plateau

## The Approach

The land rises in one long, steep step. Below is a lowland you can no longer see. Above is a tableland of black basalt so wide that its far edges drop behind the curve of the world. It sits nearly four kilometres up. The air is thin and cold, and it tastes of wet iron.

Nothing about the rock is old. The basalt fractured into its columns recently enough that the breaks are still bright. Run a hand along a column face and the edge will cut you. There are no rounded boulders and no talus fans softened by centuries, only crisp hexagons, glassy rinds where lava met air, and ridges of obsidian that hold a white reflection of the sky. The terrain looks newly cooled. By any clock you might bring, it has looked this way for a very long time.

The cloud base sits a few hundred metres above the ground, flat as a ceiling. It never lifts and never lowers. Under it the light is even and white, bright enough to read by and dim enough to look at directly. Shadows are soft and exist, but they don't move. The sun, somewhere above that lid, has no visible position. It is always the same hour of a morning that never advances.

## The Works

Across this emptiness the Works are spread out, and the first one is visible from the edge. It is a grey-green cluster on the horizon, trailing a white plume.

The stations stand twenty, thirty, sometimes fifty kilometres apart. Between them is bare black stone. Walking from one to the next takes days. Each station is a city built for machines. It has streets laid out on a grid, paved in basalt setts cut from the same rock the plateau is made of. Riveted iron halls stand along them, with roofs that pitch at the same angle, block after block. There are pump houses and boiler houses, and turbine sheds long enough to hold a small river. Above them rise cooling towers, flared concrete shells a hundred metres high, each exhaling a steady column of vapour straight up into the cloud.

The construction is layered. Newer iron is bolted over older iron, and gantries are built on gantries. In places a whole hall has been enclosed inside a larger one, like a seed inside a fruit. Someone, or something, kept building, and then stopped at one particular moment. That moment has been kept.

Pipelines connect the stations: huge lagged conduits running dead straight across the plateau on concrete saddles. They cross gorges on trestles and pass through ridges in bored tunnels. Beside them run tramlines. On some of these a tram car stands at a platform with its doors open and its brass handrails wet. Nobody boards, and it never leaves. The timetables posted in the shelters are legible, precise, and describe departures that are always about to happen.

There are no people. There were never any bodies, no abandoned belongings, no signs of flight. The rooms in the worker blocks have beds with folded blankets and iron stoves that are warm to the touch. The absence doesn't read as ruin. It reads as a vacancy held open, a building that is fully ready for its occupants and has been that way forever.

## The Sound

Before you see any of this, you hear it.

Every station makes a note. The turbines, flywheels, and pumps of each cluster run at a fixed speed, and together they sound one sustained pitch. Near a station it is enormous. The note is felt in the sternum and the teeth, and it sets the water on every surface trembling. From the plateau's edge you hear many stations at once, blended by distance into a single chord: low, wide, faintly dissonant in the middle, the way a cathedral organ sounds with every stop pulled out and a book resting on the keys.

The chord never modulates. It doesn't rise or fall, and it has no rhythm, because nothing turning in the Works speeds up or slows down. After a while the ear stops treating it as sound and starts treating it as pressure, the medium the place is made of. Quiet doesn't exist here. The closest thing to it is the lee of an obsidian ridge, where the chord drops to a roar you can almost talk over.

The loudness has consequences. Near the great halls the ground is patterned. Standing waves from the machinery shake grit and droplets into the nodes of the vibration, and the plateau is marked in the concentric figures you'd see if you drew a violin bow across a sand-covered plate. Because the sound never changes, the figures never change, and things have grown into them.

## The Water and the Green

The cooling towers breathe out warm vapour. The pipelines carry warm water across the cold stone and sweat through every flange and seam. At this altitude, under this lid of cloud, none of that moisture goes far. It condenses on the iron, drips from the rivets, beads on the concrete, and runs in thin sheets down the columnar faces. It is always raining a little near the Works, a fine drizzle that falls from nowhere, because the air cannot hold what the machines keep giving it.

The green follows the warmth.

It is lush in a way cold places rarely are. Deep cushions of moss, emerald and bronze and a nearly black olive, fill the sett joints of every street, climb the halls, and hang in dripping beards from the gantries. Ferns grow out of the lagging on the pipelines, cold-country ferns with stiff dark fronds and rusty undersides, packed so tight that the conduits look like hedgerows running to the horizon. Clubmosses cover the flanks of the cooling towers in scaled green armour. Liverworts spread across wet concrete in overlapping lobes like spilled paint. Near the warmest outfalls there are rushes and sedges, and even small stunted shrubs with leathery leaves.

From higher ground the vegetation looks like a map of the infrastructure. There are green grids where the stations stand and green lines ruled between them along the pipelines. Everywhere else is black: raw basalt with a skin of frost, bare, sharp, and untouched. Nothing grows more than a few metres beyond the reach of the Works' breath. The whole ecology of a continent-sized plateau is a lace the machines have drawn over it.

Around the loudest halls the moss grows in the vibration patterns. There are rings, lobes, and radiating spokes of green, with bare stone in the lines where the ground shakes hardest. These patterns are exact. Standing in a turbine yard and looking down, you see the note written out in moss.

## The Absence of Animals

Nothing moves in the green.

There are no insects in the moss and no beetles under the fern fronds. There are no birds on the gantries, no flies over the warm outfalls, no worms in the thin soil. Every plant here reproduces by spores, by fragments, or by wind. Flowers would be pointless and there are none. The ferns release their spores into the drizzle, and the drizzle carries them along the pipelines to wherever the warmth is.

Without animals, nothing grazes, nothing burrows, and nothing breaks down the dead. Old fronds don't rot. They blacken, stay intact, and lie under the living ones in dark layers like the pages of a book. The moss cushions are metres deep in places, a dense wet peat of preserved growth. Everything living here is growing on top of everything that grew before it.

## The Rule of the Place

At some point, in the third or fourth station, you notice the thing the place is built around.

Nothing wears.

The iron is wet and should rust, and it does, but only to a fine orange bloom a few microns thick that never deepens. The rivets have kept their heads. The basalt should weather: water in the cracks, frost, the scouring drizzle. But the edges stay knife-bright, and the moss that grows over them covers them without softening them. Pools collect in the street drains and stay at the same level to the millimetre, filled exactly as fast as they drain. The plumes rise from the towers at a constant rate. The temperature is two degrees above freezing and does not vary, day or night, if there is a night. The cloud never breaks.

A plateau this size should have weather. It should have fronts, storms, cold air draining off the edges, and snow on the high ridges. It does have weather, but only one. The heat of the Works drives a single enormous standing circulation. Warm air rises through the towers, spreads under the cloud lid, cools, and sinks at the plateau's rim, where it pours over the edge in a permanent downslope wind you can hear under the chord like breath. Rain falls near the stations, frost holds at the margins, and the clouds keep their positions. It is a continental climate that has found one state and stayed in it.

It is tempting to say the Works run because the plateau is stable. After enough time here, the opposite seems more likely. The machines are the reason the land stays young. They pump heat up through the plateau from some deep hot source that keeps the basalt from finishing its cooling. They hold the water at the dew point, the air at one pressure, the rock at the moment of its making. The chord is the sound of a world being held at a single instant, with the full weight of industry spent on keeping it from becoming the next one.

The engines turn. Their speed never changes, so their turning measures nothing.

## Standing There

At the centre of the largest station there is a plaza. It is a wide paved square ringed by turbine halls, their doors open, the flywheels inside spinning so steadily they look motionless. The moss here grows in a perfect rosette, forty metres across, a bare spot at its heart where the vibration is strongest. The drizzle falls straight down. Water weeps from the columns and fills the drains to exactly the brim. The ferns on the pipelines lean slightly toward the warmth and have always leaned that way.

There is a clock tower above the square. Its face is clean. Its hands are set a few minutes before the hour, and the whole mechanism is running. You can hear its escapement ticking inside the enormous chord, a tiny precise sound that repeats the same second over and over.

If you stand in the bare centre of the rosette, the note of the station passes through you completely. Water beads on your sleeves and gathers into lines. It is cold, bright, and deafening. There is so much space in every direction and so little in it: the halls, the green, the black stone, the plumes rising into a sky that stays where it is.

This is the one thing on the plateau that is not held in place: you, breathing. Even so, after a long time here you begin to suspect your own footprints in the moss won't still be there when you turn around.
