# The Tabletop Sea

## I. The Height of It

The sea sits higher than it should. It fills a plateau that the planet pushed up only recently, in geological terms: a single raised shelf of black rock the size of a continent, lifted into thin, cold air and then flooded to the brim. Seen from the edge, if anyone stood at the edge, the sea would seem to rest on a table. Its surface lies flat against a pale sky, and at its rim the water simply stops. It pours over the lip of the plateau in a sheet eleven hundred kilometers long, falling into cloud.

The air up here is too thin to hold much warmth. The water is a degree above freezing, sometimes half a degree, and has been for as long as anything has measured it. It is heavy and clean, with nothing dissolved in it that might soften it. Brine would at least taste of something. This tastes of stone.

Go under, and that is where the place truly begins.

## II. Clear Water, Sharp Stone

The first surprise is the light. There is no murk, no green haze of plankton, no drifting silt. The water is so clear that daylight reaches down unbroken, and at forty meters the world looks like a cold, bright afternoon rather than the underside of a sea. Everything is lit in the same even blue-white. There are no caustic ripples on the floor, because the surface barely moves. The light comes straight down and stays.

The floor below is new. That shows in every edge. Erosion has barely started. The basalt still holds the shapes it cooled into. There are columns with faces so crisp that their hexagonal corners could cut a hand. There are ridges of obsidian that fractured and were never smoothed, standing up like broken plates of black glass. There are pressure-ridges and blisters from the lava's last movements, frozen in the middle of a heave. Nothing has had time to round anything off. No sand has formed, because no current has ground stone against stone long enough to make it. There is no mud either. Mud needs death, and decay, and time.

Nothing grows here, and nothing ever has. No weed, no film of algae, no mat of green in the shallows. The rock is bare in a way that feels deliberate, like a floor that was just swept. Where it breaks, the glass shows fresh faces: black, bottle-green, and sometimes a smoky, iridescent bronze where the cooling happened at a strange rate. In the even light these faces shine without flickering.

The sea is vast, and most of it is simply space. Between the floor and the surface lie hundreds of meters of transparent water. Between one ridge and the next there may be forty kilometers of flat, clear void. A swimmer could hang in the middle distance and see nothing in any direction but blue, with black rock far below and the silver ceiling far above. The sea is big enough to have weather of its own. Up top, above the surface, slow fronts of freezing fog drift across the plateau for weeks. Underneath, there is almost nothing in it. It is volume, not contents.

## III. The Sound

You hear it before you see anything alive.

On first entry it feels less like a sound than a pressure on the whole body. It is a roar made of crackling: an unbroken, total static, like fat spitting in a pan the size of a continent, or ten million campfires at once, or radio noise turned up until it becomes a solid. It comes from every direction equally, so it has no source. It never pauses, swells, or falls. It is simply there, at a level that makes the skull hum and the teeth ache.

A diver cannot hear their own breath. They cannot hear their own heartbeat, or the clatter of their equipment. Shouting into a mask produces a small vibration in the jaw and nothing else. Within an hour or two the noise stops registering as sound and becomes the medium itself, as if the water were made of it. People who have spent time here say the strangest part is not the loudness but the steadiness. After a while the mind files it as silence, a very thick silence. Then, back in the air above, true quiet feels like falling.

The sound is made by the fauna.

## IV. The Snappers

Scattered through the whole volume of the sea, but too small and too clear to see at first, are the animals the old surveyors called *clickwrights*. Each is about the length of a thumbnail. They are crustacean in rough outline, but made almost entirely of transparent chitin, so that in the bright water a clickwright is visible only as a faint lens, a small kink in the light. Each one has a single oversized claw. That claw is the whole point of it.

The claw snaps shut so fast that it tears the water open. It leaves behind a cavitation bubble, a pocket of near-vacuum that collapses a fraction of a millisecond later with a crack and a pinpoint of heat. One snap is as loud as a gunshot at close range. The clickwrights snap constantly: to stun food, to signal, to hold territory, or for no reason anyone has found. There are uncountably many of them. Trillions is a guess, and probably low.

They are abundant, yet the sea is empty. Both are true. Each clickwright keeps a wide sphere of distance around itself, a meter or more of clear water it defends with its snapping. So the population is spread through the sea almost evenly, like a very fine and very sparse suspension. A cubic meter of water holds perhaps one animal, or none. But the sea has more cubic meters than there are words for, and every one of them is crackling.

Up close, a clickwright is unsettling to watch. It holds perfectly still in the water, oriented to nothing. It fires its claw. A tiny bead of light blooms and vanishes, too brief to be sure you saw it. Then it fires again, and again. The whole sea is full of these glints, never more than one or two in view, a slow, sparse glittering like frost catching the sun.

They eat something smaller still. There is a haze of mineral-feeding particles, not quite alive by any familiar standard, that forms on the fresh glass faces and drifts off in threads. Nothing eats the clickwrights except other clickwrights, and only rarely. There are other creatures, but they are fewer:

- **Longwhips.** Eel-like, glass-clear, and as long as a train. Each moves through the void on a single constant sine-wave and has no eyes at all. They navigate by the texture of the noise, reading the faint differences in crackle-density that mark a ridge, a hollow, a structure. They seem to feed on the particle-haze by simply moving through it, mouths open, for years at a time.
- **Stonebells.** Sessile, domed, and gray, they cling to the obsidian ridges in small groups. They ring. Or they are said to ring, anyway. The ring is at a pitch lower than the crackle, and it is felt in the chest when you swim near one. No one has heard it as sound.
- **Scatterflies.** Brief, bright, swimming things. They appear in sudden flocks near the old ironworks, turn once in unison, and disperse. Of all the animals here, they are the only ones that ever seem to be in a hurry.

## V. The Warmth Rule

This is the strange law of the place, and it is only a little strange: *the noise keeps the sea from freezing.*

At this altitude, in this thin air, a still, fresh sea should have frozen solid from the top down long ago. It has not. Every snap of every clickwright ends in a collapsing bubble, and every collapse releases a tiny spark of heat, briefly hotter than anything else for kilometers. One spark is nothing. Trillions of them, firing every second, spread evenly through the whole volume, add up to a gentle and perfectly distributed warmth. It holds the water exactly at the edge of freezing and no farther.

This is why the sea never changes. There are no warm currents or cold currents, because the heat is released everywhere at once. There is no convection, because no part of the sea is warmer than any other. There are no tides, because the plateau is too high and too enclosed for the planet's pulls to slosh it. No thermocline, no seasons, no storms below the surface. The whole volume sits at one temperature, lit by one light, loud with one roar, and it has done so for millennia. It is not calm. It is balanced. The deafening noise and the stillness are the same thing.

If the clickwrights stopped, the sea would freeze in a season. Everything in it, and above it, depends on that sound.

## VI. The Ironworks

People came here once, briefly, with engines.

They did not stay, and they did not build much. What they left is scattered so thinly across the plateau that you could swim for a week and find nothing. Then, in the middle of some black and empty plain, you come across a single made thing standing in the blue: square-edged, rust-colored, obviously foreign.

**The Lip Station** is the largest. It sits at the plateau's edge, where the sea spills over. The overflow is the one place in the whole sea with a steady gradient: water moving in one direction, forever, at a constant rate. The builders put turbines there. There is a row of fourteen iron housings, each the size of a church, bolted into the fresh basalt of the rim, with the outflow pouring through them into the cloud below. Eleven are still turning. They have turned for as long as the station has stood, never faster and never slower, because the overflow never changes. Their hum is swallowed entirely by the crackle, but you can feel it through the rock for a kilometer in every direction, like a second pulse.

From the Lip Station, **cables** run out across the sea floor. They are thick, armored, and furred with a gray crust of clickwright moults. They stretch for hundreds of kilometers across the bare stone, sagging over ridges and straightening across the plains, bright black lines leading into the blue. Nobody knows all of their destinations. The ones that have been followed lead to small things.

- **A sounding engine.** It is an iron frame on a basalt ridge with a great bronze diaphragm, built to send pulses through the water to map the floor. It still fires, once every forty-one seconds. Against the roar of the snappers its pulse is inaudible. The only evidence that it works is that the longwhips avoid it, bending their long sine-waves around it in a wide arc.
- **A lamp.** A single electric arc-lamp in a glass-and-iron cage, burning on a pole in the middle of an empty obsidian plain. There is no reason for it to be there and nothing near it to light. In the even blue daylight it is almost invisible, a small, hot, yellow-white star. The scatterflies gather around it most often.
- **A drowned railcar.** One wagon, with no rails, sitting on its side on a ledge of columnar basalt. The cable runs into it and stops. Inside there are racks of cold iron equipment whose purpose is unclear, and the stonebells have made a small colony on its roof.
- **A pump hall.** It is half built and doorless, an iron shell open to the water. Whatever it was meant to pump, it never started. The clickwrights have claimed it. Its interior is the only place in the sea where they crowd closer than a meter apart, and the noise inside is louder than anywhere else in the world, loud enough to shimmer the water visibly. The light inside flickers with sparks.

There are no bodies, no names, no signs, no rubbish. The builders left engines and nothing else, as though they came only to switch something on.

## VII. Standing in It

Imagine hanging in the open water halfway between the floor and the surface, somewhere far from any ridge and far from any ironwork.

Below you is the black glass of a young world, sharp and unworn, shining faintly green. Above you is the flat silver of a surface that does not move. Around you, in every direction, is clear, cold, brightly lit blue, and it goes on for longer than you could swim in a lifetime. There is nothing in it. There is nothing in it, and yet it is crowded: somewhere within arm's reach an invisible animal fires its claw, and a pinprick of light flares and dies. Then another does, farther off, and a hundred more past that. The roar is so complete you cannot hear yourself think in words. Very far away, just barely, a single yellow light burns on a pole that no one tends. Through the water, faintly, you feel the patient thud of turbines at the edge of the world.

Nothing will be different tomorrow. Nothing will be different in a thousand years. The noise will hold the cold at bay, and the cold will hold the noise in place. The water will keep spilling over the lip. The light will keep coming straight down. The whole vast, empty, deafening sea will stay exactly on the edge of freezing, held there by the sound of itself.
