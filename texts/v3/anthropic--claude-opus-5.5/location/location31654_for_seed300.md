# The Pile Field at Lower Scour

It lies below the level of everything around it, which is the first thing you notice and the last thing you understand.

Coming across the estuary flats you see nothing, just grey mud running to grey water and a low sky with the light evenly spread across it. Then the ground drops away in front of you into a hollow roughly the size of a village green, ringed by a retaining wall of old blue engineering brick. The wall is slumped in places and bellied outward in others, and it is furred everywhere with a fine orange bloom where iron has wept through the mortar for a very long time. You could walk the circuit of the rim in ten minutes. Most people who come here don't try, because the rim is soft and the hollow pulls at the eye.

## The Piles

The floor of the hollow is filled with iron.

There are thousands of piles, driven vertically into the silt so close together that in places you could not pass between them sideways. They are the stubs of some foundation or cofferdam, or a pier for a building that was either never built or was built and then taken back down to the waterline and no further. Nobody local agrees which. Some are round, some are H-section, some are riveted plate boxes the width of a chimney. Their heights vary from ankle to shoulder, as if something had grazed across the field at different depths over different centuries.

They are not rusted in the way you expect. The silt has worked them. Every tide, every pulse, every drain of water has carried fine abrasive sediment past them, and over a hundred-odd years this has scoured the iron into shapes. The upstream face of each pile is polished to a dull pewter sheen, smooth as a river stone. The downstream face is deeply fluted and hollowed, and on the oldest piles it is eaten through, so that from certain angles you look across the field through a lattice of iron lace. The top edges are rounded like the rims of old cups. There are no sharp corners anywhere in the pile field. Everything has been brought to a settlement with the water.

Standing among them is like standing in a crowd that is not paying attention to you.

## The Breathing

Here the stillness breaks. The pile field is never still.

The hollow sits below the water table, below high tide, and below the old channel of the river, which was diverted and buried under the flats generations ago but has not accepted this. Water comes up through the floor. It does not seep. It arrives in pulses.

A patch of silt between the piles darkens, then glistens, then lifts. A skin of brown water rises out of the ground and spreads, sliding between the piles faster than you could walk, filling the low places, joining other sheets that have risen elsewhere. Within four or five minutes the whole field can stand ankle-deep, the piles doubled in a shivering reflection. Then, just as quickly, it drains. It goes back down through the same vents or out through sumps at the base of the wall, and leaves the silt rippled in new patterns, with small fans and deltas and fresh scour-pits at the foot of every pile.

This happens dozens of times a day, on no schedule anyone has worked out. It is linked somehow to the tide, to rainfall miles inland, and to the pressure of the buried river. The sheets of water rarely rise in the same place twice running. The silt never holds a shape for long. Footprints last minutes. Channels form, deepen, braid, and are erased. Whole banks of sediment migrate across the hollow over the course of an afternoon, burying the short piles and uncovering others that have not seen air in years. Those come up black and gleaming and begin to redden before your eyes.

This is the turbulence of the place, and it is old turbulence. The churning is not a crisis. It is the equilibrium. The pile field has been in this state for longer than anyone living has known it, and the scoured iron shows it: every surface has been shaped by exactly this chaos repeated past counting. It is as settled as a thing can be while never holding still for a single hour.

## The Quiet

For all that movement, it is almost silent.

The silt absorbs sound. The fog absorbs sound. The rising water makes no rush or gurgle. It makes a faint sustained hiss, like breath let out through the teeth, and that is only when you crouch close. When a sheet of water reaches a pile it parts around it with a sound too small to name. When the field drains there is sometimes a single low sucking note from one of the sumps, and then nothing.

You notice your own sounds too much: the creak of your boots, your breathing, the rustle of your coat. Birds on the flats outside the wall cry and are cut off abruptly at the rim, as though the hollow has a lid.

## The Air

The air is cool, and it is always condensing. The rising water is a few degrees warmer than the estuary air, because the buried river comes from deep and far away, so every pulse throws up a low mist. The mist forms in the lee of the piles, streams sideways in threads, tears on the iron, and reforms. On a day without wind the whole hollow fills to the height of a man with a moving, fibrous fog that is densest at the base of the piles and thins upward. You can stand with your head in clear grey daylight and your hands invisible.

The light is ordinary estuary light, flat and honest, without drama. That is what makes the fog strange. Nothing is hidden by darkness here. Things are hidden in plain daylight, by moving water in the air.

## The Pump House

There is one building.

It stands inside the wall at the seaward side, a small square engine house of the same blue brick, with a pitched slate roof gone green and a squat chimney that no longer smokes. Its windows are mostly boarded, with one left clear. Inside, still running, is a pump.

It is electric now. Someone converted it long ago, and the conduit runs in along the top of the wall on rusted brackets from a line of wooden poles that march in from the landward side across the flats. The motor is housed in a cast-iron casing painted a green that has faded almost to grey. It turns slowly and constantly, drawing from the deepest sump and pushing the water out through a culvert under the wall to the estuary. It cannot keep up. It was probably never meant to keep up. It does just enough to stop the hollow from becoming a lake, and it has been doing just enough for decades.

Its sound is the only mechanical sound in the pile field: a low, even hum you feel in your sternum more than hear. After an hour you stop noticing it. When you leave, its absence rings in your ears.

A single bulb burns inside, day and night, behind the clear window, in a wire cage. At dusk it is the only warm colour in the hollow.

There is no keeper. There are signs that someone comes: a fresh-oiled grease nipple, a new padlock on an old door, a mug on the windowsill, clean. You never see them.

## Other Marks

Scattered through the field are the leavings of other purposes. There is a length of narrow-gauge rail curling up out of the silt and back down again, like the back of something swimming. A cast manhole cover lies flat on top of a pile, placed there deliberately by someone. There is a chain, each link as thick as a wrist, that runs taut from one pile to another across a gap, holding nothing. There is a set of concrete steps that goes down into the silt and doesn't come back up. There is a brass gauge on a post, its needle frozen at a reading with no units. There are maybe a dozen such things in the whole field, no more. They are not a ruin. They are more like words dropped in a long silence.

## What Grows

Very little grows here.

Glasswort grows in a few places where silt has lingered long enough to hold a seed. It is jointed and succulent, green in summer and turning a deep wine red in autumn. It clings to the upstream side of certain piles, in the smooth hollow where the scour eddies and pauses. There is a grey-green crust of lichen on the wall and on the tallest pile tops, the only surfaces that reliably stay dry. There is a slick olive film of algae that appears on the silt after a long drain and vanishes the moment the water returns.

And there is one alder. It stands near the landward wall, rooted in a crack in a collapsed section of brickwork, leaning at a steep angle over the field, its roots wrapped around an iron pile as though holding on. It is small and dense and very old. Its bark is stained orange to the height of the highest water.

## What Lives

Animals come rarely, and never many at once.

A heron sometimes stands on one of the short piles, motionless as the iron, and you do not see it until it moves. Eels live in the sumps and rise with the pulses. Occasionally you will see one crossing the silt in a skin of water barely deep enough to cover it, then find it stranded when the field drains, and then find it gone. Tiny translucent shrimp live in the scour-pits, dozens to a pit, and they survive the drains by burrowing. A pied wagtail visits in winter, walking the silt between pulses and picking at whatever the water has turned up. That is all, most days.

## The Unusual Thing

The locals call the last quality "the leaning."

When the field fills, the rising sheets of water do not spread evenly. They move between the piles in patterns, sliding along certain lanes and avoiding others, and the lanes change from pulse to pulse. Watch long enough and you begin to feel the patterns are close to legible. They are not writing, and nothing so obvious as a message. They have the quality of handwriting seen from too far away. The piles seem to guide the water, and over a century the water has shaped the piles to guide it better. The two have been negotiating for a very long time.

Some who come here say that if you stand still enough, the water starts choosing lanes around you, as though you were one more pile. It parts at your boots, flows smoothly past, and slows in your lee. It feels like being accepted into the crowd.

Then the field drains, the fog tears on the iron, the pump hums, and the silt is wiped clean, ready to be written over again.
